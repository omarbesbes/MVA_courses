§0Where this lesson sits

This is the first lesson. It sets up the objects that every later lesson manipulates: probability measures, the push-forward of a measure by a map, and the transport cost of a map. It asks the original question of Monge (1781): move a pile of mass onto another pile, point by point, at the smallest total cost. The answer is complete in three cases (the line, Gaussians, and the quadratic cost with a smooth source), and it fails in instructive ways elsewhere. The failures motivate the next lesson.

What it reuses, what it prepares

Reuses. Nothing from earlier lessons. It assumes basic probability (laws of random variables, expectations), the notion of a measure and of a density, convex functions of one and several variables, and symmetric positive semidefinite matrices and their square roots.

Prepares.

  • Lesson 2 (Kantorovich) relaxes maps into couplings. This repairs the three failures of §5 (no admissible map, an infimum that is not reached, non-uniqueness). The Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem explains why the assignment problem of §2 is a linear program in disguise. The 1D formula of §6 becomes the 1D Kantorovich solution.
  • Lesson 3 (metric properties) turns the transport cost into the Wasserstein distance $W_p$. McCann’s interpolation (§9) becomes a geodesic, and the Gaussian formula of §8 becomes a basic example of the metric. Two exercises of its sheet (Gaussian couplings) check Brenier’s theorem from the other side.
  • Lesson 4 (duality) proves Brenier’s theorem (§7), which this lesson only states and proves in the discrete case. The semi-discrete maps of §5 become Laguerre cells, and the 1D formula for $W_1$ (§6) becomes the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality.
  • Lesson 5 (sliced OT) applies the 1D quantile formula of §6 to every direction of projection.
  • Lesson 6 (dynamic OT and flow matching) reads McCann’s interpolation as a flow with a velocity field. Its exercises test whether flow matching recovers the Brenier map of §7–§8 (Lesson 6, Exercise 4).
  • Lesson 7 (entropic regularization) never produces a map. It recovers one by barycentric projection, and it has a closed form between Gaussians.
  • Lesson 8 (gradient flows) §2 uses the Brenier map to write the Wasserstein gradient of $W_2^2(\cdot,\beta)$ as $2(\Id-T)$.

Watch out: the factor ½ in the quadratic cost

This lesson uses $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2$. Later lessons and several sheets use $\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$ (the Sinkhorn and gradient-flow sheets, and OT4ML’s duality chapter). The optimal map is the same for both costs (Exercise 9(b)), but costs, potentials and gradients differ by a factor 2. CompOT mixes the two normalisations inside its proof of Brenier’s theorem (see §7).

A question that comes back

The question of this lesson

When can mass be moved by a map — without splitting any point — and what does the optimal map look like?

This is the first lesson, so no earlier lesson has asked it yet. The question returns in every lesson. Lesson 2 answers “always, for equal discrete weights” (the linear program has a permutation among its solutions). Lesson 3 shows that the best map and the best coupling have the same cost when the source has no atoms. Lesson 4 proves Brenier’s theorem and builds maps from potentials. In Lesson 6, a flow is a map in time, and the map it generates is optimal only in special cases. Lesson 7 gives up maps entirely and recovers them by averaging. In Lesson 8, particles move along maps.

§1Why transport? Comparing distributions with geometry

OT4ML ch. 1–2 introductions, pp. 9 (1), 20 (12); slides 2–4; CompOT ch. 1, pp. 7–10 (3–6); StatOT preface and §1.1, pp. 7–13, 15.

The problem

Machine learning keeps comparing probability distributions: a model with data, a source domain with a target domain, generated samples with real ones, a cloud of particles with its limit. A discrepancy $D(\al,\be)$ between two probability measures $\al$ and $\be$ turns such a comparison into a number. Fitting a model with parameter $\theta$ then means solving $\min_\theta D(\al_\theta,\be)$ (slide 3).

Many models are push-forward models: draw a latent sample $z$ from a fixed law $\zeta$ (for instance a standard Gaussian), and output $x=g_\theta(z)$. The law of $x$, written $\al_\theta=(g_\theta)_\sharp\zeta$ (defined in §4), usually has no density we can evaluate. Two empirical clouds of samples, or a cloud and a smooth density, are then all we can compare.

The intuition

Classical divergences compare densities point by point. The Kullback–Leibler divergence is infinite as soon as $\be$ puts mass where $\al$ puts none, and the total variation between two Dirac masses $\delta_x$ and $\delta_y$ is the same for $y$ next to $x$ and for $y$ far away. Optimal transport compares distributions by moving mass: it asks how much effort is needed to rearrange $\al$ into $\be$, where effort is measured with the geometry of the space (a distance, or a cost $c(x,y)$ of sending a unit of mass from $x$ to $y$). Two Dirac masses at distance $\varepsilon$ are then $\varepsilon$-close. This is what makes transport useful for point clouds, generative models and anything with a spatial structure.

Link: other ways to compare distributions

Lesson 4 compares transport with the KL divergence, φ-divergences and integral probability metrics; this lesson only needs the idea that transport uses the ground geometry. §8 shows the difference concretely on Gaussians (Wasserstein versus Fisher–Rao geometry).

A short history (from the outline)

  • Schrödinger (1931) and Sinkhorn (1964): entropic regularisation and matrix scaling. Lesson 7.

  • Cuturi (2013) makes transport fast with Sinkhorn; Wasserstein GANs (2017) bring it to generative modelling. Lessons 4 and 7.

Two ways to represent a measure

A discrete probability measure $\al=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\delta_{x_i}$ has positions $x_i$ and weights $a_i\ge0$ with $\sum_i a_i=1$. One can move the positions and keep the weights (a Lagrangian description, like tracking particles), or fix a grid of positions and change the weights (an Eulerian description, like a histogram). Monge’s problem is Lagrangian: it moves each point. Moving positions adapts to the data but destroys convexity; changing weights on a fixed grid keeps convexity (OT4ML §2.1, p. 20 (12)). This tension returns in every lesson (thread T5).

§2The discrete Monge problem: optimal assignment

OT4ML §1.1–1.2, pp. 9–19 (1–11); CompOT §2.2 and eq. (2.2), p. 13 (9); slides 6–7; StatOT §1.1.3, p. 19 (13).

The problem

Take two clouds of $n$ points, $x_1,\dots,x_n$ (sources, in red in all figures) and $y_1,\dots,y_n$ (targets, in blue), each point carrying the same mass $1/n$. Choose the cost $c(x,y)$ of sending a unit of mass from $x$ to $y$; the default is a power of the distance, $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^p$. Collect the pairwise costs in the cost matrix $C_{i,j}=c(x_i,y_j)$. We want to pair each source with exactly one target, and each target with exactly one source, at the smallest total cost.

Definition (optimal assignment problem)OT4ML Def. 1.1, p. 9 (1); CompOT eq. (2.2)

Let $\Perm(n)$ be the set of permutations of $\range{n}$, the bijections $\sigma:\range{n}\to\range{n}$. The optimal assignment problem is $$\min_{\sigma\in\Perm(n)}\ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n C_{i,\sigma(i)} .$$ A minimiser $\sigma^\star$ is an optimal assignment: source $i$ goes to target $\sigma^\star(i)$.

  • Every symbol. $n$ is the number of points on each side; $C_{i,j}$ the cost of pairing source $i$ with target $j$; $\sigma(i)$ the target assigned to source $i$. The factor $1/n$ is the mass of each point; it does not change the minimiser.
  • Existence. $\Perm(n)$ is finite and nonempty, so a minimiser always exists. It need not be unique (CompOT Remark 2.3, p. 13: four points at the corners of a square).
  • Size. There are $n!$ permutations. For the 36+36 points of the lab below, $36!\approx3.72\times10^{41}$. Exhaustive search is hopeless; efficient methods exploit either geometry (the line, §3) or duality (the Hungarian method, below).

Why it is hard, and the elementary move: swapping two pairs

The set of permutations is not convex, and a small change of the points can reorganise the whole optimal assignment. The one local tool is the swap: take two pairs $(x_i,y_{\sigma(i)})$ and $(x_j,y_{\sigma(j)})$ and exchange their targets. If swapping lowers the cost, $\sigma$ was not optimal. Exercise 2 of the sheet computes exactly when swapping helps for the quadratic cost:

$$\begin{aligned}&\norm{x_1-y_1}^2+\norm{z-y_2}^2\ \le\ \norm{x_1-y_2}^2+\norm{z-y_1}^2\\ \iff\ &\dotp{x_1-z}{y_1-y_2}\ \ge\ 0 .\end{aligned}$$

Expanding the squares, the norms of the four points cancel and only the cross terms remain. The pairing $x_1\mapsto y_1$, $z\mapsto y_2$ is better exactly when the displacement between the two sources and the displacement between their targets point in directions making an acute angle. The set of such $z$ is a half-space bounded by the hyperplane through $x_1$ orthogonal to $y_1-y_2$. This “pairs must be monotone” condition is the seed of Brenier’s theorem (§7).

The Monge cost in the plane: optimal segments do not cross

Monge’s own cost is the distance itself, $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}$ ($p=1$). A swap argument then shows a clean geometric property.

Proposition (no proper crossings)OT4ML Prop. 1.5, p. 15 (7)

Let $x_1,\dots,x_n,y_1,\dots,y_n\in\R^2$ and $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}$. If $\sigma$ is an optimal assignment, two matched segments $[x_i,y_{\sigma(i)}]$ and $[x_j,y_{\sigma(j)}]$ cannot cross properly: their relative interiors cannot meet at a point where their supporting lines are distinct.

Proof (the step that makes it work: the triangle inequality along the rewired paths)

Suppose the two segments cross at a point $z$. Rewire: send $x_i$ to $y_{\sigma(j)}$ and $x_j$ to $y_{\sigma(i)}$. By the triangle inequality, $$\begin{gathered}\norm{x_i-y_{\sigma(j)}}\le\norm{x_i-z}+\norm{z-y_{\sigma(j)}},\\ \norm{x_j-y_{\sigma(i)}}\le\norm{x_j-z}+\norm{z-y_{\sigma(i)}}.\end{gathered}$$ The two right-hand sides add up to the lengths of the two original segments. At least one inequality is strict because the crossing is proper (the points are not aligned). The swap strictly decreases the cost, which contradicts optimality. $\square$

  • When it fails. The proof uses that the cost of a segment is the sum of the costs of its two pieces, which is true for the length ($p=1$) and false for every other power. For $p\neq1$ crossings do occur: Lab 2 measures 3 for $p=\frac12$, 3 for $p=2$ and 14 for $p=6$ on the canonical clouds. For collinear overlaps the swap can have equal cost, so the statement excludes them.
  • Why it is not enough. Non-crossing is a necessary test, not a certificate. Colour the vertices of a convex $2n$-gon alternately red and blue: the number of non-crossing perfect matchings is the Catalan number $C_n=\frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}\sim 4^n/(\sqrt\pi\,n^{3/2})$ (OT4ML Remark 1.6, p. 15 (7)). Exponentially many matchings pass the test.

Lab 2What the exponent $p$ changes in a planar assignment

Question: on fixed point clouds, how does the optimal assignment for $c=\norm{x-y}^p$ reorganise when $p$ changes, and how good are simple heuristics?

Setting: the canonical clouds of the notebook matching-2d-cost-exponent (OT4ML Fig. 1.5, p. 15): 36 farthest-point samples of a disk of radius 0.5 and 36 of the annulus $0.85\le r\le1$, seed 2027, exported with the notebook’s own code. The optimal assignment is computed in the page by the Hungarian method.

What we found (measured)

  • Reproduction. For $p=2$ the sum of squared lengths of the optimal assignment is 12.13332760, the value printed by the notebook matching-hungarian-progression. The normalised optimal costs are 0.742241 ($p=\frac12$), 0.568034 ($p=1$), 0.337037 ($p=2$) and 0.047597 ($p=6$); SciPy’s linear_sum_assignment and POT’s ot.emd give the same values to $10^{-15}$.
  • Crossings. The optimal assignment has 0 proper crossings for $p=1$, as Proposition 1.5 predicts, but 3 for $p=\frac12$, 3 for $p=2$ and 14 for $p=6$. As $p$ grows the matching trades crossings for shorter long edges: its longest edge drops from 1.011 ($p=\frac12$) to 0.768 ($p=6$).
  • Greedy is nearly optimal for the concave cost and poor for large $p$. The greedy rule (repeatedly match the closest remaining pair) only uses the order of the distances, so it returns the same matching for every $p$ (13 crossings). Its excess cost is 0.4% for $p=\frac12$, 1.5% for $p=1$, 13.4% for $p=2$ and 256.6% for $p=6$. A random permutation is far worse (0.8906 against 0.337037 for $p=2$, with 113 crossings).
  • Certificate. The dual potentials returned with the assignment satisfy $\frac1n\big|\sum_i u_i+\sum_j v_j-\sum_i C_{i,\sigma(i)}\big|\approx2.0\times10^{-16}$ and $\min_{i,j}(C_{i,j}-u_i-v_j)\ge 0$ up to rounding: the page proves optimality of what it displays (Proposition 1.7 below).

Solving it: the Hungarian method and its certificate

The Hungarian method (Kuhn, 1955) solves the assignment problem exactly in $O(n^3)$ operations for any cost matrix. What makes it work is a certificate of optimality that is cheap to check.

Proposition (dual certificate for an assignment)OT4ML Prop. 1.7, p. 16 (8)

Let $u\in\R^n$ and $v\in\R^n$ satisfy $u_i+v_j\le C_{i,j}$ for all $i,j$ (dual feasibility). Then every permutation $\tau$ satisfies $\sum_i C_{i,\tau(i)}\ge\sum_i u_i+\sum_j v_j$. If a permutation $\sigma$ uses only tight pairs, $u_i+v_{\sigma(i)}=C_{i,\sigma(i)}$ for every $i$, then $\sigma$ is optimal.

Proof (one line: sum the constraints along the permutation)

Summing $C_{i,\tau(i)}\ge u_i+v_{\tau(i)}$ over $i$, and using that $\tau$ is a bijection so that $\sum_i v_{\tau(i)}=\sum_j v_j$, gives the lower bound. If all pairs of $\sigma$ are tight, its cost equals the lower bound, which no permutation can beat. $\square$

  • How the method uses it. It keeps feasible potentials $(u,v)$ and a partial matching made of tight pairs, and grows the matching one pair at a time along augmenting paths; when no tight edge leaves the current search tree, it shifts the potentials by the smallest slack, which keeps feasibility and creates a new tight edge (OT4ML Alg. 1.4 and Prop. 1.8, pp. 17–18). After $n$ augmentations the matching is perfect and certified.
  • Interpretation. $u_i$ and $v_j$ are prices: a source pays $u_i$, a target $v_j$, and no pair may be worth more than its cost. This is the discrete Kantorovich dual of Lesson 4 §1, and the potentials will reappear in §7 as a discrete Brenier potential.

§3The line: sorting solves the problem

OT4ML §1.1 (“Convex costs on the line”, “Concave costs on the line”, Example 1.3), pp. 9–12 (1–4); CompOT Remark 2.28, p. 34 (30); slides 8–10; StatOT §1.3, p. 27 (21).

The problem and the intuition

On the real line points can be ordered. Take $n$ sources $x_1,\dots,x_n$ and $n$ targets $y_1,\dots,y_n$ in $\R$, all distinct, and the cost $c(x,y)=h(x-y)$ with $h$ convex, for instance $h(s)=\abs{s}^p$ with $p\ge1$. The natural guess is to match the smallest source with the smallest target, the second smallest with the second smallest, and so on. Picture the sources on one line and the targets on a parallel line below: the guess is the only matching whose segments do not cross.

Proposition (monotone matching on the line)OT4ML Prop. 1.2, p. 9 (1)

Assume the $x_i$ are pairwise distinct and the $y_j$ are pairwise distinct. If $C_{i,j}=h(x_i-y_j)$ with $h:\R\to\R$ strictly convex, the unique optimal assignment is the order-preserving one: $(x_i-x_{i'})(y_{\sigma(i)}-y_{\sigma(i')})>0$ for all $i\neq i'$. This covers $h(s)=\abs{s}^p$ for $p>1$. If $h$ is only convex, the order-preserving assignment is still optimal, but other optima may exist.

Proof (the step that makes it work: a convex function has increasing slopes)

Suppose $\sigma$ has an inversion: two sources $x\lt x'$ matched to targets $y'\gt y$ (the segments cross). Put $d=y'-y\gt0$ and $D(s)=\frac{h(s)-h(s-d)}{d}$, the slope of $h$ over an interval of length $d$ ending at $s$. Strict convexity makes $D$ strictly increasing. The crossed pairs cost $h(x-y')+h(x'-y)$, the uncrossed pairs cost $h(x-y)+h(x'-y')$, and $$\text{crossed}-\text{uncrossed}=d\,\big(D(x'-y)-D(x-y)\big)\gt 0 ,$$ because $x'-y\gt x-y$. Uncrossing the two pairs strictly lowers the cost. Repeating the swap removes every inversion, and the only assignment without inversions matches equal ranks. With $h$ merely convex, $D$ is nondecreasing and the inequality is only $\ge0$: uncrossing never hurts, so the sorted assignment is optimal, but ties are possible. $\square$

  • Algorithm. Sort both clouds, $x_{\sigma_X(1)}\le\dots\le x_{\sigma_X(n)}$ and $y_{\sigma_Y(1)}\le\dots\le y_{\sigma_Y(n)}$, and return $\sigma=\sigma_Y\circ\sigma_X^{-1}$ (OT4ML Alg. 1.1, p. 10 (2)). Cost: $O(n\log n)$ with a comparison sort, against $O(n^3)$ for the Hungarian method.
  • The value. With sorted points, $\frac1n\sum_i h(x_{(i)}-y_{(i)})$. For $h=\abs{\cdot}^p$ this is the $p$-th power of a scaled $\ell^p$ distance between the sorted vectors (CompOT’s wording after eq. (2.33) omits the factor $n^{-1/p}$; see the errata).
  • Stability under change of coordinates. Sorting still solves the problem for $h(\abs{\phi(x)-\phi(y)})$ with $\phi$ increasing (OT4ML p. 12 (4)): only the order matters. This is why histogram equalization (§6) is a sorting problem.
  • Where it is used again. The 1D Kantorovich solution (Lesson 2 §3, §7), the quantile formulas of §6, and every projection of sliced OT (Lesson 5).

When convexity is not strict: flat directions for $p=1$

Example (discrete book-shifting)OT4ML Example 1.3, p. 12 (4)

Let $X=\{1,\dots,2m\}$ and $Y=\{m+1,\dots,3m\}$ with uniform weights and $c(x,y)=\abs{x-y}$. The monotone assignment $i\mapsto i+m$ has average cost $m$. The “book-shifting” assignment keeps $m+1,\dots,2m$ in place and sends $i\le m$ to $i+2m$; its average cost is also $m$. Both are optimal, because every assignment costs at least $\frac1{2m}\sum_i(y_{\sigma(i)}-i)=m$.

The lower bound is the key: $\abs{y-x}\ge y-x$, and the right-hand side sums to a constant over any assignment. Every assignment that moves each point to the right is optimal. For $p\gt1$ the tie breaks: with $m=4$ the monotone assignment costs 16 and the book-shifting one 32 $=2^{p-1}m^p$ for $p=2$, while both cost 4 for $p=1$. Exercise 5 of the sheet is the continuous version (§5).

Concave costs: order still helps, sorting does not

For $c(x,y)=g(\abs{x-y})$ with $g$ concave and increasing, such as $\abs{x-y}^p$ with $0\lt p\lt1$, one long and one short displacement can cost less than two medium ones. Optimal matchings then create long exchanges and nested pairs (OT4ML p. 10 (2), Fig. 1.1). The line is still special: the problem splits into independent alternating chains that an exact $O(n^2)$ algorithm solves by “local matching indicators” (Delon, Salomon & Sobolevski 2012; OT4ML Alg. 1.2, p. 11 (3)).

§4Measures and the push-forward

OT4ML §2.1–2.2, pp. 20–24 (12–16); CompOT §2.1–2.2 (Remarks 2.1–2.2, 2.5–2.6, 2.8–2.9, Definition 2.1), pp. 11–17 (7–13); slides 12–14; StatOT §1.1.1, p. 15 (9).

Measures, in the amount of generality the course needs

The outline says “for now we stay vague”. What is needed:

  • A probability measure $\al$ on a space $\X$ assigns a mass $\al(A)\in[0,1]$ to measurable sets $A\subset\X$, with $\al(\X)=1$, additively over disjoint countable unions. We write $\Pc(\X)$ for the set of probability measures, and $\Pc_p(\X)$ for those with a finite $p$-th moment, $\int\norm{x}^p\dd\al(x)\lt\infty$.
  • The Dirac mass $\delta_x$ puts all the mass at $x$: $\delta_x(A)=1$ if $x\in A$, $0$ otherwise. A discrete measure is $\al=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\delta_{x_i}$ with weights $a_i\ge0$ summing to one (OT4ML Def. 2.2, p. 20 (12)); its integral is a weighted sum, $\int f\dd\al=\sum_i a_if(x_i)$. The empirical measure of a cloud is $\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$.
  • A measure has a density $\rho_\al$ with respect to Lebesgue measure when $\int h\dd\al=\int h(x)\rho_\al(x)\dd x$ for all bounded measurable $h$ (OT4ML Def. 2.6, p. 22 (14)). It is then absolutely continuous: it gives zero mass to every Lebesgue-null set. A measure with no atom ($\al(\{x\})=0$ for all $x$) is called atomless; absolutely continuous measures are atomless, but not conversely (the uniform law on a segment in $\R^2$ is atomless and singular).
  • The support $\supp(\al)$ is the set of points all of whose neighbourhoods have positive mass (OT4ML Def. 2.4).
  • A random variable $X:\Omega\to\X$ has a law $\Law(X)$, the measure $A\mapsto\Prob(X\in A)$ (OT4ML Def. 2.9, p. 23 (15)); then $\int f\dd\Law(X)=\E[f(X)]$.

Beyond the course (from the resources)

OT4ML §2.1 also defines Polish spaces, Radon measures and the total variation norm through the duality $\Mc(\X)=\Cc(\X)^*$ (Riesz–Markov; Prop. 2.8). The course only needs these when proving existence and duality theorems (Lessons 3–4).

The push-forward: how a map moves mass

Definition (push-forward)OT4ML Def. 2.10, p. 23 (15); CompOT Def. 2.1, p. 15 (11)

For a measurable map $T:\X\to\Y$ and $\al\in\Pc(\X)$, the push-forward $\be=T_\sharp\al\in\Pc(\Y)$ is defined by $$\be(B)=\al\big(T^{-1}(B)\big)=\al\big(\{x\in\X:\ T(x)\in B\}\big)$$ for all measurable $B\subset\Y$, or, equivalently, by $\displaystyle\int_\Y h(y)\dd\be(y)=\int_\X h(T(x))\dd\al(x)$ for all bounded measurable $h:\Y\to\R$.

  • Discrete measures. $T_\sharp\big(\sum_i a_i\delta_{x_i}\big)=\sum_i a_i\delta_{T(x_i)}$: move the points, keep the weights. Weights of points sent to the same place add up.
  • Random variables. If $X\sim\al$ then $T(X)\sim T_\sharp\al$ (OT4ML Remark 2.13; CompOT Remark 2.9). Sampling from $T_\sharp\al$ is sampling from $\al$ and applying $T$ — this is how push-forward generative models are sampled. StatOT defines the push-forward only this way (§1.1.1, p. 15 (9)).
  • Linearity. $\al\mapsto T_\sharp\al$ is linear on (signed) measures, even when $T$ is nonlinear: the map acts on points, its push-forward acts linearly on measures. This “lifting” is the source of every convex relaxation in the course.
  • Composition. $(S\circ T)_\sharp\al=S_\sharp(T_\sharp\al)$.

Link: push-forward and pullback are adjoint

The test-function form says $\dotp{h\circ T}{\al}=\dotp{h}{T_\sharp\al}$: push-forward on measures is the adjoint of the pullback $h\mapsto h\circ T$ on functions (OT4ML Remark 2.11, p. 23 (15); CompOT Remark 2.8, p. 16 (12)). Measures move forward, test functions move backward. This duality between measures and functions is the engine of Lesson 4.

Densities: the Jacobian is not cosmetic

Proposition (push-forward formula for densities)OT4ML Prop. 2.12, p. 24 (16); CompOT Remark 2.6, p. 15 (11)

Let $U,V\subset\R^d$ be open, $T:U\to V$ a $C^1$ diffeomorphism, and $\al,\be$ with densities $\rho_\al,\rho_\be$. Then $T_\sharp\al=\be$ if and only if, for almost every $x\in U$, $$\rho_\al(x)=\abs{\det T'(x)}\;\rho_\be\big(T(x)\big),$$ equivalently, for almost every $y\in V$, $$\rho_\be(y)=\frac{\rho_\al\big(T^{-1}(y)\big)}{\abs{\det T'\big(T^{-1}(y)\big)}} ,$$ where $T'(x)\in\R^{d\times d}$ is the Jacobian matrix of $T$.

Proof (change of variables in the test-function form)

For a continuous compactly supported $h$, the definition and the change of variables $y=T(x)$, $\dd y=\abs{\det T'(x)}\dd x$, give $$\begin{aligned}\int_V h\,\rho_\be\dd y&=\int_U h(T(x))\,\rho_\al(x)\dd x\\&=\int_V h(y)\,\frac{\rho_\al(T^{-1}y)}{\abs{\det T'(T^{-1}y)}}\dd y .\end{aligned}$$ Two densities that integrate every such $h$ to the same value agree almost everywhere. $\square$

Intuition. Where $T$ compresses space ($\abs{\det T'}\lt1$), the same mass occupies less volume and the density goes up. A naive guess $\rho_\be=\rho_\al\circ T^{-1}$ forgets this and is not even a probability density in general. The formula in the source variable, $\rho_\al=\abs{\det T'}\,\rho_\be\circ T$, becomes the Monge–Ampère equation when $T=\nabla\varphi$ (§9).

Watch out: the formula needs a bijection

For a map that is not injective, sum over all preimages. Exercise 12: $T(x)=x^2$ on $\al=U[-1,1]$ has two preimages $\pm\sqrt y$, each contributing $\frac12\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt y}$, so $\rho_\be(y)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt y}$ on $(0,1]$ — a density that blows up at $0$ but still integrates to one. Using a single branch gives half the mass. Lab 4 shows both mistakes numerically.

§5The Monge problem between measures, and three ways it fails

OT4ML §2.3, pp. 25–28 (17–20); CompOT Remarks 2.4 and 2.7, pp. 13–16 (9–12); slides 15–16; StatOT §1.1.1, pp. 15–16 (9–10).

The problem

Definition (Monge problem)OT4ML Def. 2.14, p. 25 (17); CompOT eq. (2.9)

For $\al\in\Pc(\X)$, $\be\in\Pc(\Y)$ and a measurable cost $c:\X\times\Y\to[0,+\infty]$, $$\Monge_c(\al,\be)=\inf_{T}\Big\{\int_\X c\big(x,T(x)\big)\dd\al(x)\ :\ T_\sharp\al=\be\Big\},$$ the infimum being over measurable maps $T:\X\to\Y$. An admissible $T$ (one with $T_\sharp\al=\be$) is a Monge map or transport map; a minimiser is an optimal Monge map. The value is $+\infty$ if no admissible map exists.

The unknown is a map; the constraint $T_\sharp\al=\be$ prescribes a whole measure, and it is nonlinear in $T$ (slide 15). The cost $\int c(x,T(x))\dd\al$ is the average price paid by a particle of $\al$.

Proposition (empirical Monge maps are assignments)OT4ML Prop. 2.15, p. 26 (18); CompOT Remark 2.4

If $\al=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$ with distinct $x_i$ and $\be=\frac1n\sum_j\delta_{y_j}$ with distinct $y_j$, then $T_\sharp\al=\be$ holds exactly when $T(x_i)=y_{\sigma(i)}$ for a permutation $\sigma$, and $\int c(x,T(x))\dd\al=\frac1n\sum_ic(x_i,y_{\sigma(i)})$. The discrete Monge problem is the assignment problem of §2.

Watch out: repeated points and unequal weights

If source points coincide, merge them into one atom of larger mass first: a map cannot split an atom (OT4ML Prop. 2.15). The slides’ statement (slide 16) assumes distinct points implicitly. CompOT writes the discrete Monge problem as $\min_T\sum_ic(x_i,T(x_i))$ (eq. (2.5), p. 14 (10)), without the weights $a_i$; the correct objective is $\sum_ia_ic(x_i,T(x_i))$, which agrees with (2.5) only for uniform weights.

Failure 1: there may be no admissible map

A map sends each point to one point. So $T_\sharp\delta_x=\delta_{T(x)}$ is always a Dirac mass, and $\al=\delta_0$ cannot be pushed to $\be=\frac12\delta_{-1}+\frac12\delta_1$ (OT4ML Remark 2.17, p. 26 (18); StatOT p. 16 (10)). More generally a discrete source can only produce a discrete target: $T_\sharp\big(\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i}\big)$ is supported on the finite set $\{T(x_i)\}$, so it never has a density (Exercise 3(1)). The problem is asymmetric: from a density to a discrete measure there are always maps — partition the space into cells $C_j=T^{-1}(y_j)$ of masses $\al(C_j)=b_j$ (Exercise 3(2); OT4ML Example 2.19, the semi-discrete case, whose optimal cells are the Laguerre cells of Lesson 4 §5).

Proposition (atomless sources always admit maps)OT4ML Prop. 2.16, p. 26 (18)

If $\al$ and $\be$ are probability measures on Polish spaces and $\al$ is atomless, there exists a measurable $T$ with $T_\sharp\al=\be$.

The proof identifies $(\X,\al)$ with $([0,1],\Leb)$ and uses the quantile function of the target (§6). Feasibility is solved; optimality is a different question.

Failure 2: the infimum may not be attained

Example (a splitting obstruction)OT4ML Example 2.18, p. 26 (18); Continuous-Kantorovich sheet, Ex. 6

Let $\al$ be uniform on the segment $\{0\}\times[0,1]$ of $\R^2$ and $\be=\frac12U(\{-1\}\times[0,1])+\frac12U(\{1\}\times[0,1])$, with $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2$. Every admissible map has cost $\gt1$, admissible maps have costs arbitrarily close to $1$, so $\Monge_c(\al,\be)=1$ is not attained.

Why (a lower bound, a construction, and an impossibility)

Lower bound. Every target point is at horizontal distance 1 from the segment, so any map (indeed any way of moving the mass) costs at least $1$.

Almost optimal maps. Cut $[0,1]$ into $n$ cells of length $1/n$. In each cell, send the lower half affinely onto the whole cell on the left segment and the upper half onto the whole cell on the right segment. Each image cell receives half its mass from each side, so $T_\sharp\al=\be$. A point at relative height $r\in[0,\frac12)$ of its cell moves vertically by $r/n$, and the vertical cost of one half cell is $\int_0^{1/(2n)}s^2\dd s=\frac{1}{24n^3}$. With $2n$ half cells the total cost is $$1+\frac{2n}{24n^3}=1+\frac{1}{12n^2}\ \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\ 1 .$$

No map reaches 1. Cost $1$ would force zero vertical motion: the point $(0,t)$ must go to $(\pm1,t)$. Let $A\subset[0,1]$ be the levels sent to the right. The right half of $\be$ requires $\abs{A\cap B}=\abs B/2$ for every interval $B$; taking $B=A$ gives $\abs A=\abs A/2$, so $\abs A=0$, and then $\abs{A\cap[0,1]}=0\neq\frac12$. A contradiction. $\square$

The optimum exists as a relaxed object: send each point $(0,t)$ half to the left and half to the right. That object is a coupling, not a map; it is the subject of Lesson 2 §6. The source here is not absolutely continuous in $\R^2$ (it lives on a segment), which is exactly the hypothesis of Brenier’s theorem that fails (§7).

Failure 3: optimal maps need not be unique

Example (book-shifting in the Monge problem)OT4ML Example 2.21, p. 28 (20); Monge sheet, Ex. 5

With $\al=U[0,2]$, $\be=U[1,3]$ and $c(x,y)=\abs{x-y}$, the translation $x\mapsto x+1$ and the book-shifting map ($x\mapsto x+2$ on $[0,1]$, identity on $(1,2]$) are both optimal, with cost $1$.

The argument of §3 again: $\int\abs{T(x)-x}\dd\al\ge\int(T(x)-x)\dd\al=\int y\dd\be-\int x\dd\al=1$, with equality for every admissible $T$ with $T(x)\ge x$. For $p=2$ the translation costs 1 and book-shifting costs 2 (measured in Lab 5): strict convexity selects the translation.

The Monge value as a (directed) distance

Definition (directed Monge distance)OT4ML Def. 2.20 and Prop. 2.22, pp. 27–28 (19–20)

On a metric space $(\X,d)$ and for $1\le p\lt\infty$, set $\Wt_p(\al,\be)^p=\inf\{\int d(x,T(x))^p\dd\al(x):\ T_\sharp\al=\be\}$ ($+\infty$ if no map exists). Then $\Wt_p$ is nonnegative, vanishes only when $\al=\be$, and satisfies the triangle inequality: it is a directed extended distance. It is not symmetric and can be infinite.

Proof of the triangle inequality (compose the maps, then Minkowski)

If $S_\sharp\al=\ga$ and $T_\sharp\ga=\be$, then $(T\circ S)_\sharp\al=\be$, and Minkowski’s inequality in $L^p(\al)$ gives $$\begin{aligned}\Big(\int d\big(x,T(S(x))\big)^p\dd\al\Big)^{\frac1p}&\le\Big(\int d\big(x,S(x)\big)^p\dd\al\Big)^{\frac1p}\\&\quad+\Big(\int d\big(S(x),T(S(x))\big)^p\dd\al\Big)^{\frac1p}.\end{aligned}$$ The last integral equals $\int d(z,T(z))^p\dd\ga(z)$ by the definition of $\ga=S_\sharp\al$. Take near-optimal $S$ and $T$. $\square$

Asymmetry is concrete: from a density to a discrete measure $\Wt_p$ is finite, in the reverse direction it is $+\infty$. Symmetrising and making the value always finite is exactly what the Kantorovich relaxation achieves; on a compact space and for an atomless source it gives back the same value (OT4ML Prop. 4.15, p. 79 (71); Lesson 3 §2).

§6One dimension: quantile functions solve the Monge problem

OT4ML §2.6, pp. 37–41 (29–33) and the histogram paragraph p. 12 (4); CompOT Remarks 2.29–2.30, pp. 35–36 (31–32); StatOT §1.3, pp. 27–29 (21–23), and Prop. 1.18, p. 44 (38); slides 20–24.

Cumulative and quantile functions

Definition (cumulative and quantile functions)OT4ML Def. 2.39, p. 37 (29); CompOT eqs. (2.34)–(2.35)

For $\al\in\Pc(\R)$, the cumulative distribution function (CDF) is $F_\al(x)=\al\big((-\infty,x]\big)$ and the quantile function is its generalised inverse $$F_\al^{-1}(r)=\inf\{x\in\R:\ F_\al(x)\ge r\},\qquad r\in(0,1).$$

  • The one fact to remember. For every $r\in(0,1)$ and $x\in\R$: $\ F_\al^{-1}(r)\le x\iff r\le F_\al(x)$ (StatOT eq. (1.5), p. 27). It follows from right-continuity of $F_\al$, and it replaces “inverse” when $F_\al$ has jumps (atoms) or flat parts (gaps in the support).
  • Picture. A mode of the density is a steep rise of $F_\al$; a region of low density is a flat part of $F_\al$ and a steep part of $F_\al^{-1}$ (OT4ML Fig. 2.10, p. 37). An atom of mass $m$ at $x$ is a jump of height $m$ of $F_\al$ and a flat step of $F_\al^{-1}$ at level $x$ of length $m$.

Proposition (quantile push-forward)OT4ML Prop. 2.40, p. 37 (29); StatOT eq. (1.6)

(i) $(F_\al^{-1})_\sharp\Leb_{(0,1)}=\al$: if $U$ is uniform on $(0,1)$, then $F_\al^{-1}(U)\sim\al$. (ii) If $\al$ has no atoms, $(F_\al)_\sharp\al=\Leb_{(0,1)}$: if $X\sim\al$, then $F_\al(X)$ is uniform.

Proof (use the equivalence above)

(i) $\Prob\big(F_\al^{-1}(U)\le x\big)=\Prob\big(U\le F_\al(x)\big)=F_\al(x)$, so $F_\al^{-1}(U)$ has CDF $F_\al$. (ii) If $F_\al$ is continuous, for each $r\in(0,1)$ the set $\{F_\al(X)\ge r\}$ equals $\{X\ge F_\al^{-1}(r)\}$ up to a null set, whose probability is $1-F_\al(F_\al^{-1}(r)^-)=1-r$ because $F_\al$ is continuous at $F_\al^{-1}(r)$ and $F_\al(F_\al^{-1}(r))=r$. Continuity is used exactly here: with an atom, $F_\al$ jumps over some levels, and $F_\al(X)$ never takes them (Exercise 11). $\square$

(i) is inverse transform sampling, the simplest push-forward generative model: a uniform latent variable and the quantile map as generator. (ii) is the probability integral transform.

The 1D Monge map: send each quantile level to the same level

Theorem (one-dimensional Monge solution)OT4ML Thm. 2.41, p. 38 (30); CompOT eq. (2.39); StatOT eq. (1.7), Prop. 1.18

Let $\al,\be\in\Pc(\R)$ with $\al$ atomless, $c(x,y)=h(x-y)$ with $h\ge0$ convex, and assume the cost below is finite. Then $$T=F_\be^{-1}\circ F_\al$$ satisfies $T_\sharp\al=\be$, is nondecreasing, and minimises $\int h(x-S(x))\dd\al(x)$ over all maps $S$ with $S_\sharp\al=\be$. Its cost is $$\int h\big(x-T(x)\big)\dd\al(x)=\int_0^1 h\big(F_\al^{-1}(r)-F_\be^{-1}(r)\big)\dd r .$$

Intuition. $F_\al(x)$ is the fraction of the mass of $\al$ to the left of $x$. The map sends $x$ to the point of $\be$ that has the same fraction of mass to its left: it is sorting, for a continuum of points. Feasibility is Proposition 2.40: $F_\al(X)$ is uniform and $F_\be^{-1}$ of a uniform variable has law $\be$.

Proof of optimality for the quadratic cost (self-contained; it proves more than asked)

Take $h(s)=s^2$ and $\al,\be\in\Pc_2(\R)$. For $X\sim\al$ and any admissible $S$, set $Y=S(X)\sim\be$. Then $\E(X-Y)^2=\E X^2+\E Y^2-2\E[XY]$, and the first two terms do not depend on $S$: we must maximise the correlation $\E[XY]$. Hoeffding’s covariance identity writes $$\begin{aligned}&\E[XY]-\E X\,\E Y\\&\quad=\iint_{\R^2}\Big(\Prob(X\le s,\,Y\le t)-F_\al(s)F_\be(t)\Big)\dd s\,\dd t .\end{aligned}$$ Only the joint term depends on the coupling, and it is bounded by the Fréchet–Hoeffding bound $\Prob(X\le s,Y\le t)\le\min\big(F_\al(s),F_\be(t)\big)$. For $Y=T(X)$ and $U=F_\al(X)$ (uniform, since $\al$ is atomless), $\{X\le s\}=\{U\le F_\al(s)\}$ up to a null set and $\{T(X)\le t\}=\{U\le F_\be(t)\}$ by the equivalence $F_\be^{-1}(u)\le t\iff u\le F_\be(t)$. Hence $$\begin{aligned}\Prob\big(X\le s,\ T(X)\le t\big)&=\Prob\big(U\le\min(F_\al(s),F_\be(t))\big)\\&=\min\big(F_\al(s),F_\be(t)\big)\end{aligned}$$ for all $(s,t)$ at once. The monotone map attains the upper bound everywhere, so it maximises $\E[XY]$ and minimises the quadratic cost. $\square$

What the proof teaches. It never used that $S$ is a map: the monotone pairing is optimal among all ways of pairing $X\sim\al$ with $Y\sim\be$, which is the 1D Kantorovich theorem of Lesson 2 §7 for the quadratic cost. For a general convex $h$, OT4ML proves Theorem 2.41 by that Kantorovich statement (its Theorem 3.39, p. 68 (60)); StatOT Exercise 1.8-8 does it with $c$-cyclical monotonicity.

Proposition (one-dimensional transport costs)OT4ML Prop. 2.44 and eq. (2.12), pp. 38–39 (30–31); CompOT eqs. (2.36)–(2.37); StatOT Thm. 1.7

For $1\le p\lt\infty$ and $\al,\be\in\Pc_p(\R)$ with $\al$ atomless, the optimal Monge cost for $\abs{x-y}^p$ is $$\int_0^1\abs{F_\al^{-1}(r)-F_\be^{-1}(r)}^p\dd r=\norm{F_\al^{-1}-F_\be^{-1}}_{L^p(0,1)}^p ,$$ and, for $p=1$, it is also equal to $$\int_\R\abs{F_\al(x)-F_\be(x)}\dd x .$$

Why the two $p=1$ formulas agree (layer cake)

For numbers $a,b$, $\abs{a-b}=\int_\R\abs{\mathbf 1_{a\le x}-\mathbf 1_{b\le x}}\dd x$. Take $a=F_\al^{-1}(r)$, $b=F_\be^{-1}(r)$, integrate in $r$ and swap the integrals. By the equivalence of Definition 2.39, $\mathbf 1_{F_\al^{-1}(r)\le x}=\mathbf 1_{r\le F_\al(x)}$, and $\int_0^1\abs{\mathbf 1_{r\le F_\al(x)}-\mathbf 1_{r\le F_\be(x)}}\dd r=\abs{F_\al(x)-F_\be(x)}$. $\square$

  • Vocabulary ahead of time. These quantities are $W_p(\al,\be)^p$, the Wasserstein distance of Lesson 3 §1; on a compact space and for an atomless source the Monge value and the Kantorovich value coincide (OT4ML Prop. 4.15, p. 79 (71)). We use the name now for the value of the problem.
  • Geometry. The map $\al\mapsto F_\al^{-1}$ is an isometry from one-dimensional $W_p$ into $L^p(0,1)$; its image is the convex cone of nondecreasing functions (OT4ML Remark 2.46, p. 39 (31)). For $p=2$ the space of 1D laws is flat: it sits isometrically inside a Hilbert space. This is why 1D barycenters are averages of quantiles and why sliced OT (Lesson 5) is cheap.
  • Interpolation. The displacement interpolation of §9 is linear in quantile coordinates: $F_{\al_t}^{-1}=(1-t)F_\al^{-1}+tF_\be^{-1}$ (OT4ML Fig. 2.11, p. 40; slide 21). Its density follows from $\rho_{\al_t}\big(Q_t(r)\big)=1/Q_t'(r)$ with $Q_t=F_{\al_t}^{-1}$.
  • Composition. In 1D, optimal maps compose: $T_{\be\to\ga}\circ T_{\al\to\be}=T_{\al\to\ga}$ (OT4ML Remark 2.42). In dimension $d\ge2$ this fails already for linear maps: $A_2A_1$ is symmetric only if $A_1,A_2$ commute (OT4ML Example 2.43; see §8).

Watch out: the formula $T=F_\be^{-1}\circ F_\al$ needs an atomless source

If $\al$ has an atom, $F_\al$ jumps and $T$ sends the whole atom to one point, so $T_\sharp\al\ne\be$ in general. Example: $\al=\delta_0$, $\be=U[0,1]$ gives $T\equiv F_\be^{-1}(1)=1$. The quantile coupling $(F_\al^{-1},F_\be^{-1})_\sharp\Leb_{(0,1)}$ is still optimal, but it is a coupling, not a map (Lesson 2 §7). CompOT states eq. (2.39) without the hypothesis (p. 36 (32)); OT4ML (Thm 2.41), StatOT (eq. (1.7)) and the slides (slide 20) include it.

Application: histogram equalization is 1D transport

A grey-level image is a cloud of $N$ intensities $I_1,\dots,I_N\in[0,1]$. Watch out: ties make it a coupling, not a map

Digital images have repeated intensities. The stable sort decides which pixel gets which value; it changes the image but not the coupling (OT4ML p. 12 (4)). In the lab’s image, 193 of the 201 grey levels are split.

§7Brenier’s theorem: the optimal map is the gradient of a convex function

OT4ML §2.4, pp. 28–30 (20–22), proof in Remark 5.13, p. 102 (94); CompOT Remark 2.24 and Theorem 2.1, pp. 31–32 (27–28); StatOT §1.4, pp. 29–35 (23–29), Theorem 1.16, p. 42 (36); slide 17.

The problem and the intuition

In dimension $d\ge2$ there is no order to sort by. Which maps are optimal for the quadratic cost? The two-point exchange of §2 says: for every two pairs, $\dotp{x-x'}{T(x)-T(x')}\ge0$, i.e. $T$ is a monotone map. In 1D, monotone maps are exactly the derivatives of convex functions. In $\R^d$, the natural candidates are gradients of convex functions, which are monotone: $\dotp{\nabla\varphi(x)-\nabla\varphi(x')}{x-x'}\ge0$.

Two-point exchanges are not all the exchanges: three sources can pass their targets around in a cycle. Asking that no cyclic exchange lowers the cost gives cyclical monotonicity: for every cycle $x_1,\dots,x_k$ of sources with targets $y_1,\dots,y_k$, $$\sum_{i=1}^k\norm{x_i-y_i}^2\ \le\ \sum_{i=1}^k\norm{x_{i+1}-y_i}^2\qquad(x_{k+1}=x_1),$$ that is, $$\sum_{i=1}^k\dotp{x_i-x_{i+1}}{y_i}\ \ge\ 0$$ (StatOT Def. 1.9 and eq. (1.12), p. 31 (25)). Rockafellar’s theorem (StatOT Thm. 1.10) says that cyclically monotone sets are exactly the subsets of graphs of subdifferentials of convex functions. Brenier’s theorem is this picture, made rigorous for measures.

Theorem (Brenier)OT4ML Thm. 2.23, p. 29 (21); CompOT Thm. 2.1, p. 31 (27); StatOT Thms. 1.8 and 1.16, pp. 29 (23), 42 (36)

Let $\al,\be\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$ and assume $\al$ is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure. For $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2$ there is a convex function $\varphi:\R^d\to\R\cup\{+\infty\}$ such that $$T=\nabla\varphi\quad\text{satisfies}\quad T_\sharp\al=\be,$$ and $T$ is the unique optimal Monge map, up to $\al$-null sets. Moreover every convex $\psi$ with $(\nabla\psi)_\sharp\al=\be$ has $\nabla\psi=\nabla\varphi$ $\al$-almost everywhere.

  • In one sentence. For the quadratic cost and a source with a density, the optimal transport is a map, it is unique, and it is characterised by a single property: being the gradient of a convex function that pushes $\al$ onto $\be$.
  • Every hypothesis. Finite second moments make the cost finite (the slides, slide 17, omit this). Absolute continuity (or the weaker “does not charge hypersurfaces”, OT4ML Remark 2.26) makes $\nabla\varphi$ defined $\al$-almost everywhere. Nothing is asked of $\be$: it can be discrete, which is the semi-discrete case.
  • How it is used. To prove that a candidate map is optimal, exhibit a convex potential. This is how §8 (Gaussians), Exercise 6 (affine maps), Exercise 13 (radial laws) and OT4ML Prop. 2.27 are solved.

Why it is true (1): the easy half, fully proved

Proposition (a gradient of a convex function is optimal)StatOT Thm. 1.14 (ii)⇒(i) and Thm. 1.16, pp. 40–42 (34–36)

If $\varphi$ is convex, $T=\nabla\varphi$ satisfies $T_\sharp\al=\be$, and $\al,\be\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$, then $T$ minimises $\int\norm{x-S(x)}^2\dd\al$ among all maps $S$ with $S_\sharp\al=\be$.

Proof (the step that makes it work: the Fenchel–Young inequality, with equality on the graph of ∇φ)

Let $\varphi^*(y)=\sup_x\dotp{x}{y}-\varphi(x)$ be the convex conjugate. For all $x,y$, $\dotp{x}{y}\le\varphi(x)+\varphi^*(y)$ (Fenchel–Young), with equality when $y=\nabla\varphi(x)$. Since $\norm{x-S(x)}^2=\norm x^2+\norm{S(x)}^2-2\dotp{x}{S(x)}$ and the first two terms integrate to constants ($S_\sharp\al=\be$), it suffices to show $\int\dotp{x}{S(x)}\dd\al\le\int\dotp{x}{T(x)}\dd\al$. For any admissible $S$, $$\begin{aligned}\int\dotp{x}{S(x)}\dd\al&\le\int\varphi\dd\al+\int\varphi^*\big(S(x)\big)\dd\al(x)\\&=\int\varphi\dd\al+\int\varphi^*\dd\be .\end{aligned}$$ The same computation with $S=T$ is an equality, and $T_\sharp\al=\be$ gives the same right-hand side. Hence $T$ achieves the maximum. (Integrability of $\varphi$ and $\varphi^*$ holds under finite second moments; StatOT handles it with affine minorants.) $\square$

This half needs no density: it shows optimality of any gradient-of-convex map, even among couplings. The density is needed for the converse — that an optimal map exists and is a gradient.

Why it is true (2): the discrete theorem, fully proved

For $n$ points everything is finite, and the whole mechanism can be seen. Fix an assignment $\sigma$ of $x_1,\dots,x_n$ to $y_1,\dots,y_n$ with $C_{i,j}=\norm{x_i-y_j}^2$. Build the exchange graph: its nodes are the targets, and for every source $i$ and target $j\ne\sigma(i)$ it has an edge $\sigma(i)\to j$ of weight $w=C_{i,j}-C_{i,\sigma(i)}$, the extra cost of giving target $j$ to source $i$. Following a cycle of edges and applying all its exchanges changes the total cost by the sum of the weights along the cycle.

Theorem (discrete Brenier theorem)assembled from OT4ML Prop. 1.7, StatOT §1.4.2 (discrete case, p. 32 (26)) and Thm. 1.10

For an assignment $\sigma$, the following are equivalent:

  1. $\sigma$ is optimal;
  2. the exchange graph has no cycle of negative weight ($\sigma$ is cyclically monotone);
  3. there are potentials $v_1,\dots,v_n$ with $v_j-v_{\sigma(i)}\le C_{i,j}-C_{i,\sigma(i)}$ for all $i$ and $j$;
  4. there is a convex function $\varphi$ with $y_{\sigma(i)}\in\partial\varphi(x_i)$ for all $i$ — one can take the piecewise-affine $\varphi(x)=\max_j\big(\dotp{x}{y_j}-\psi_j\big)$ with $\psi_j=\frac12(\norm{y_j}^2-v_j)$.
Proof (each step is one line)

1⇒2. A negative cycle is an improving cyclic exchange.

2⇒3. With no negative cycle, shortest-path distances from a virtual node linked to every node with weight $0$ are finite (Bellman–Ford), and shortest paths satisfy the triangle inequality $d_j\le d_{\sigma(i)}+w(\sigma(i)\to j)$. Take $v=d$.

3⇒4. Expand $C_{i,j}=\norm{x_i}^2+\norm{y_j}^2-2\dotp{x_i}{y_j}$ in the inequality of 3: it becomes $\dotp{x_i}{y_j}-\psi_j\le\dotp{x_i}{y_{\sigma(i)}}-\psi_{\sigma(i)}$ for all $j$. So at $x=x_i$ the affine piece of index $\sigma(i)$ is the largest one: $\varphi(x_i)=\dotp{x_i}{y_{\sigma(i)}}-\psi_{\sigma(i)}$, and for all $x$, $\varphi(x)\ge\varphi(x_i)+\dotp{y_{\sigma(i)}}{x-x_i}$, which is the definition of $y_{\sigma(i)}\in\partial\varphi(x_i)$.

4⇒1. For any permutation $\tau$, the subgradient inequality gives $\varphi(x_{\tau^{-1}(j)})\ge\varphi(x_{\sigma^{-1}(j)})+\dotp{y_j}{x_{\tau^{-1}(j)}-x_{\sigma^{-1}(j)}}$. Summing over $j$, the $\varphi$ terms cancel and $\sum_i\dotp{x_i}{y_{\sigma(i)}}\ge\sum_i\dotp{x_i}{y_{\tau(i)}}$: $\sigma$ maximises the correlation, hence minimises the quadratic cost. $\square$

If the minimum mean weight of a cycle, $\mu^\star$, is positive, subtracting $\mu^\star$ from every weight still leaves no negative cycle. The potentials of step 3 then have a margin, and each $x_i$ lies strictly inside the region where the piece $\sigma(i)$ wins. There $\varphi$ is differentiable and $\nabla\varphi(x_i)=y_{\sigma(i)}$. These regions are Laguerre cells (power cells), the cells of semi-discrete OT in Lesson 4 §5. The continuous theorem replaces “finitely many points” by “the support of an optimal plan”. It replaces “no negative cycle” by the cyclical monotonicity of that support (StatOT Prop. 1.11), and Bellman–Ford by Rockafellar’s theorem. The density of $\al$ removes the points where $\partial\varphi$ has several elements, since convex functions are differentiable almost everywhere.

When it fails, and what each hypothesis is for

  • Singular source. The segment example of §5 (Example 2.18) has an atomless source living on a line. The optimum splits mass, so no optimal map exists. Brenier’s theorem does not apply: the source charges a hypersurface. The Continuous-Kantorovich sheet, Exercise 6(d), makes the same point.
  • The sharp condition. It is enough that $\al$ gives zero mass to every countably $(d-1)$-rectifiable set, i.e. to sets covered by countably many $C^1$ hypersurfaces (OT4ML Def. 2.25, Remark 2.26, p. 29 (21)). Convex functions are non-differentiable only on such sets.
  • Monotone is not enough. The rotation $x\mapsto R_\theta x$ with $\abs\theta\le\pi/2$ is monotone, $\dotp{R_\theta(x-x')}{x-x'}=\cos\theta\norm{x-x'}^2\ge0$. For $\theta\ne0$ it is not a gradient, since its Jacobian is not symmetric (OT4ML Remark 2.24, p. 29 (21)). Two-point monotonicity is necessary, but not sufficient, in $d\ge2$.
  • Other costs. For $\abs{x-y}$ (the $p=1$ case) optimal maps exist but need not be unique (§5). For $h(x-y)$ with $h$ strictly convex there is a unique optimal map, of the form $x-\nabla h^*(\nabla f(x))$: a map, but no longer a gradient (OT4ML §2.5; StatOT §1.7).

How it is used: radial laws (Exercise 13)

Proposition (optimal transport between radial measures)OT4ML Prop. 2.27, p. 30 (22); Monge sheet, Ex. 13

Let $\al,\be\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$ be radial (invariant under orthogonal maps), with $\al$ absolutely continuous. With radial CDFs $F_\al(r)=\al(\{\norm x\le r\})$, $F_\be$, and $\tau=F_\be^{-1}\circ F_\al$, the Brenier map is $T(x)=\tau(\norm x)\,\frac{x}{\norm x}$, $T(0)=0$.

Why. The radius $\norm X$ has an atomless law, and $\tau$ is the 1D monotone map between the laws of the radii (§6). Given its radius, the direction of a radial law is uniform on the sphere, so moving only the radius produces $\be$. The map is the gradient of $x\mapsto\Psi(\norm x)$ with $\Psi(r)=\int_0^r\tau$. $\Psi$ is convex and nondecreasing because $\tau\ge0$ is nondecreasing, so $x\mapsto\Psi(\norm x)$ is convex, and Brenier’s theorem identifies the map. For uniform laws on balls, $T(x)=\frac{R_\be}{R_\al}x$ — a dilation, as one would guess.

§8Gaussians: an affine Brenier map and the Bures metric

OT4ML §2.7, pp. 44–50 (36–42); CompOT Remark 2.31, pp. 36–38 (32–34); StatOT Example 1.19 and eq. (1.20), pp. 44–45 (38–39), Exercise 1.8-3; slides 29–35.

One dimension: a Euclidean half-plane

For $\al=\Gauss(m_\al,\sigma_\al^2)$ and $\be=\Gauss(m_\be,\sigma_\be^2)$ with $\sigma_\al\gt0$, the affine map $$T(x)=m_\be+\frac{\sigma_\be}{\sigma_\al}(x-m_\al)$$ pushes $\al$ to $\be$ (an affine image of a Gaussian is Gaussian, and the mean and variance match). It is increasing, so it is the 1D monotone map of §6, and it is the derivative of the convex function $\varphi(x)=\frac{\sigma_\be}{2\sigma_\al}(x-m_\al)^2+m_\be x$. Its cost is $$W_2^2(\al,\be)=(m_\al-m_\be)^2+(\sigma_\al-\sigma_\be)^2 .$$ So the transport geometry of 1D Gaussians is the Euclidean geometry of the half-plane $\{(m,\sigma):\sigma\ge0\}$ (OT4ML p. 44 (36), Fig. 2.14). Geodesics are straight segments, and a Dirac mass ($\sigma=0$) is at finite distance. The same formula holds for any location–scale family, for example uniform laws on intervals (Exercise 4), because only the standardised shape is transported.

Dimension $d$: look for an affine map with a symmetric matrix

Take $\al=\Gauss(m_\al,\Sigma_\al)$ and $\be=\Gauss(m_\be,\Sigma_\be)$ with $\Sigma_\al\succ0$. Brenier’s theorem says: find a convex potential whose gradient pushes $\al$ to $\be$. Try a quadratic potential $\varphi(x)=\dotp{m_\be}{x}+\frac12\dotp{A(x-m_\al)}{x-m_\al}$; its gradient is the affine map $T(x)=m_\be+A(x-m_\al)$ when $A$ is symmetric, and $\varphi$ is convex exactly when $A\succeq0$.

Proposition (affine push-forward of Gaussians)OT4ML Prop. 2.54, p. 46 (38)

$T(x)=m_\be+A(x-m_\al)$ satisfies $T_\sharp\al=\be$ if and only if $A\Sigma_\al A^\top=\Sigma_\be$.

Proposition (Gaussian $W_2$ and the Bures metric)OT4ML Def. 2.55 and Prop. 2.56, p. 46 (38); CompOT Remark 2.31, eqs. (2.40)–(2.42); StatOT eq. (1.20)

If $\Sigma_\al,\Sigma_\be\succ0$, the equation $A\Sigma_\al A=\Sigma_\be$ has a unique symmetric positive definite solution, $$A=\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}\big(\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\Sigma_\al^{-1/2},$$ the map $T(x)=m_\be+A(x-m_\al)$ is the Brenier map from $\al$ to $\be$, and $$W_2^2(\al,\be)=\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+\Bures(\Sigma_\al,\Sigma_\be)^2,$$ $$\Bures(\Sigma_\al,\Sigma_\be)^2=\tr\Big(\Sigma_\al+\Sigma_\be-2\big(\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\Big).$$ $\Bures$ is the Bures metric on positive semidefinite matrices.

Proof (the step that makes it work: symmetry turns a quadratic matrix equation into a square root)

Solving the equation. Multiply $A\Sigma_\al A=\Sigma_\be$ on both sides by $\Sigma_\al^{1/2}$: $\big(\Sigma_\al^{1/2}A\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\big)^2=\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al^{1/2}$. If $A\succ0$ is symmetric, $M=\Sigma_\al^{1/2}A\Sigma_\al^{1/2}$ is symmetric positive definite, hence it is the positive square root of the right-hand side; solving for $A$ gives the formula, and conversely that $A$ is symmetric positive definite and solves the equation. (This is an algebraic Riccati equation, slide 31.)

Optimality. $T$ is the gradient of the convex quadratic $\varphi$, and $T_\sharp\al=\be$ by Proposition 2.54, so Brenier’s theorem (or the easy half of §7) gives optimality.

Cost. With $X\sim\al$ and $Z=X-m_\al$, $X-T(X)=(m_\al-m_\be)+(\Id-A)Z$ and $\E Z=0$, so $$\begin{aligned}&\E\norm{X-T(X)}^2\\&\quad=\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+\tr\big((\Id-A)\Sigma_\al(\Id-A)\big)\\&\quad=\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+\tr\Sigma_\al\\&\qquad+\tr(A\Sigma_\al A)-2\tr(A\Sigma_\al).\end{aligned}$$ Now $\tr(A\Sigma_\al A)=\tr\Sigma_\be$, and $A\Sigma_\al=\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}M\Sigma_\al^{1/2}$ is similar to $M$, so $\tr(A\Sigma_\al)=\tr M=\tr\big((\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al^{1/2})^{1/2}\big)$. Singular covariances follow by adding $\eta\Id$ and letting $\eta\to0$. $\square$

  • Check of dimensions and special cases. In 1D, $\Bures^2=\sigma_\al^2+\sigma_\be^2-2\sigma_\al\sigma_\be=(\sigma_\al-\sigma_\be)^2$. For $\Sigma_\be=\Sigma_\al$, $A=\Id$ and $\Bures=0$. For $\Sigma_\be=0$ (a Dirac target), $\Bures^2=\tr\Sigma_\al$. The trace term is symmetric: the eigenvalues of $\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al^{1/2}$ are those of $\Sigma_\al\Sigma_\be$.
  • Commuting covariances. If $\Sigma_\al\Sigma_\be=\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al$, then $A=\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}\Sigma_\be^{1/2}$ and $\Bures(\Sigma_\al,\Sigma_\be)=\norm{\Sigma_\al^{1/2}-\Sigma_\be^{1/2}}_F$; for diagonal matrices this is the Euclidean distance between the vectors of standard deviations (OT4ML Remark after Prop. 2.61; CompOT Remark 2.31 calls it a Hellinger distance). The naive guess $A=\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}\Sigma_\be^{1/2}$ is wrong in general: it is not symmetric (StatOT p. 45 (39)).
  • Monotone maps do not compose. The Brenier maps $\al\to\be$ and $\be\to\ga$ have matrices $A_1,A_2$; their composition $A_2A_1$ is symmetric only if they commute, so the composition is in general not the Brenier map $\al\to\ga$ (OT4ML Example 2.43).

Three other faces of the Bures metric

  • A matrix Procrustes distance. $\Bures(\Sigma,\Lambda)^2=\min_{R\in O(d)}\norm{\Sigma^{1/2}-\Lambda^{1/2}R}_F^2$: compare square roots up to a rotation (OT4ML Prop. 2.60, p. 48 (40)). Taking $R=\Id$ gives $\Bures(\Sigma,\Lambda)\le\norm{\Sigma^{1/2}-\Lambda^{1/2}}_F$ (StatOT Exercise 1.8-3(b)).
  • The best case among all laws with given moments (Gelbrich’s bound). For any $\al,\be\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$ with means $m_\al,m_\be$ and covariances $\Sigma_\al,\Sigma_\be$, $$W_2^2(\al,\be)\ \ge\ \norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+\Bures(\Sigma_\al,\Sigma_\be)^2 ,$$ with equality for Gaussians (OT4ML Prop. 2.59, p. 48 (40), in raw-moment form; CompOT Remark 2.32; StatOT Exercise 1.8-3(c)). The proof bounds the cross-covariance $K=\E[XY^\top]$ of any coupling through the positive semidefinite block matrix $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\Sigma&K\\K^\top&\Lambda\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ — exactly Exercise 5 of the Continuous-Kantorovich sheet (Lesson 3 §2, which also corrects the sheet’s formula).
  • A metric with a convex square. $\Bures$ is a distance on positive semidefinite matrices and $\Bures^2$ is jointly convex (OT4ML Prop. 2.61, p. 49 (41)).

The Gaussian displacement interpolation

Interpolating with the Brenier map (§9) keeps everything Gaussian: $\al_t=\Gauss(m_t,\Sigma_t)$ with $$\begin{gathered}m_t=(1-t)m_\al+tm_\be,\\ \Sigma_t=\big((1-t)\Id+tA\big)\,\Sigma_\al\,\big((1-t)\Id+tA\big)\end{gathered}$$ (slide 32; OT4ML Fig. 2.16). This path is not the straight line $(1-t)\Sigma_\al+t\Sigma_\be$ in the space of matrices. A short computation (ours, not in the resources) compares them at $t=\frac12$: expanding and using $A\Sigma_\al A=\Sigma_\be$, $$\tfrac12(\Sigma_\al+\Sigma_\be)-\Sigma_{1/2}=\tfrac14(\Id-A)\Sigma_\al(\Id-A)\ \succeq\ 0 .$$ The displacement midpoint is never wider than the averaged covariance, in every direction. The averaged covariance is the covariance of the mixture $\frac12\Gauss(0,\Sigma_\al)+\frac12\Gauss(0,\Sigma_\be)$, which fades one law into the other instead of moving mass. Lab 9 measures the gap.

Wasserstein versus Fisher–Rao on Gaussians

The KL divergence induces another geometry on Gaussians: the Fisher–Rao metric (OT4ML Remark 2.53, p. 44 (36)). For 1D Gaussians it reads $\frac{\dd m^2+2\dd\sigma^2}{\sigma^2}$, a hyperbolic half-plane in which the boundary $\sigma=0$ is infinitely far away. For centred Gaussians it gives $d_{\mathrm{FR}}(\Sigma,\Sigma')^2=\frac12\norm{\log(\Sigma^{-1/2}\Sigma'\Sigma^{-1/2})}_F^2$ (OT4ML p. 49 (41)). Wasserstein geodesics are straight in $(m,\sigma)$ and reach degenerate Gaussians at finite distance; Fisher–Rao geodesics bend upwards (the variance grows mid-way) and never reach the boundary.

§9Displacement interpolation, Monge–Ampère and regularity

OT4ML §2.4 (“Displacement interpolation”, “Regularity and the Monge–Ampère equation”), pp. 31–34 (23–26); CompOT Remark 2.25, p. 33 (29); slides 18–19, 25–28.

Moving particles along straight lines

Definition (Monge and McCann displacement interpolation)OT4ML Def. 2.29, p. 31 (23)

If $T_\sharp\al=\be$, set $T_t(x)=(1-t)x+tT(x)$ and $\al_t=(T_t)_\sharp\al$ for $t\in[0,1]$. When $T$ is the Brenier map, $(\al_t)$ is McCann’s displacement interpolation.

Each particle keeps its identity and moves at constant speed from $x$ to $T(x)$ (a Lagrangian picture). This is not the mixture $(1-t)\al+t\be$, which leaves every particle in place and changes weights (an Eulerian picture). Between two Diracs, the mixture has two atoms of varying weights, while the displacement interpolation is one Dirac moving from $x$ to $y$.

Proposition (constant-speed geodesics)OT4ML Prop. 2.30, p. 31 (23)

If $T$ is optimal for $\Wt_p(\al,\be)$ and $T_t$ is injective on a set of full $\al$-measure for $t\lt1$, then $\Wt_p(\al_s,\al_t)=(t-s)\,\Wt_p(\al,\be)$ for $0\le s\le t\le1$. This applies to Brenier maps with $p=2$.

Proof idea (particles give an upper bound, the triangle inequality forces equality)

The map $T_t\circ T_s^{-1}$ pushes $\al_s$ to $\al_t$ and moves each particle by $(t-s)\norm{T(x)-x}$, so $\Wt_p(\al_s,\al_t)\le(t-s)\Wt_p(\al,\be)$. The same bound holds for $(\al,\al_s)$ and $(\al_t,\be)$. The triangle inequality of Proposition 2.22 along $\al\to\al_s\to\al_t\to\be$ then turns all three inequalities into equalities. For a Brenier map, $T_t$ is the gradient of the $(1-t)$-strongly convex function $(1-t)\frac{\norm x^2}{2}+t\varphi$, hence injective: particles never collide before $t=1$. $\square$

Where it is used again. It becomes the geodesic of the Wasserstein space in Lesson 3 §3 and the solution of the Benamou–Brenier problem in Lesson 6 §2, whose velocity field is $v_t=(T-\Id)\circ T_t^{-1}$. It is also the path along which Lesson 8 §3 measures convexity (displacement convexity). In 1D it is the linear interpolation of quantiles (§6); for Gaussians it is the path $\Sigma_t$ of §8.

The Monge–Ampère equation

If $\al,\be$ have densities and the Brenier potential is smooth, the density formula of Proposition 2.12 with $T=\nabla\varphi$ and $T'=\nabla^2\varphi$ (symmetric, positive semidefinite, so $\abs{\det}=\det$) gives the Monge–Ampère equation $$\det\big(\nabla^2\varphi(x)\big)\,\rho_\be\big(\nabla\varphi(x)\big)=\rho_\al(x)$$ (OT4ML eq. (2.7), p. 33 (25); CompOT eq. (2.32); slide 18), with the “second boundary condition” $\nabla\varphi(\supp\al)=\supp\be$ and the constraint that $\varphi$ be convex. It is a fully nonlinear, degenerate elliptic PDE. Convexity selects the right branch: without it, $\det\nabla^2\varphi=\rho_\al/\rho_\be\circ\nabla\varphi$ has many non-transport solutions (flip the sign of an even number of eigenvalues).

Proposition (linearisation)OT4ML Prop. 2.34, p. 34 (26)

If $\rho_\ep=\rho_0+\ep r+o(\ep)$ with $\int r=0$ and $T_\ep(x)=x+\ep\nabla u(x)+o(\ep)$ transports $\rho_0$ to $\rho_\ep$, then $-\nabla\cdot(\rho_0\nabla u)=r$ to first order; for constant $\rho_0$, $-\Delta u=r/\rho_0$.

Near a reference density, Monge–Ampère is a Poisson equation. Expand $\det(\Id+\ep\nabla^2u)=1+\ep\Delta u+o(\ep)$ in the change of variables. (CompOT Remark 2.25 writes the perturbation as “$\varphi=\Id+\ep\psi$”. Since $\varphi$ is a scalar potential, this should read $\varphi=\frac12\norm\cdot^2+\ep\psi$, i.e. $\nabla\varphi=\Id+\ep\nabla\psi$.)

Regularity is geometric

Proposition (Caffarelli regularity)OT4ML Prop. 2.31, p. 32 (24)

If $\al,\be$ are supported on bounded uniformly convex domains with $C^2$ boundaries, with densities bounded above and below and Hölder continuous ($C^{0,a}$), then the Brenier potential is strictly convex and $C^{2,a}_{\mathrm{loc}}$, so the map $\nabla\varphi$ is $C^{1,a}_{\mathrm{loc}}$.

Brenier’s theorem gives a map that is only defined almost everywhere. Smoothness needs geometry: if the target is not convex, the map can be discontinuous. The classical picture is a disk sent to a dumbbell: the disk tears along a curve whose two sides go to different lobes (OT4ML Fig. 2.7, which the book calls “a finite-sample stress test rather than a counterexample”). The outline mentions “the regularity theory of Figalli” here. The book states Caffarelli’s theorem for the Euclidean quadratic case (Prop. 2.31); Figalli appears in its §2.5, which is beyond the course: regularity on products of spheres (Figalli, Kim & McCann 2013) and transport on non-compact manifolds (Fathi & Figalli 2010).

Exercise 2 — Two points to two points: when does each permutation win?§2 · Lab 1

With $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2$, compare $\mathrm{Id}\colon x_1\mapsto y_1,\ z\mapsto y_2$ and $\mathrm{Swap}\colon x_1\mapsto y_2,\ z\mapsto y_1$. Show that Id is better on a half-space bounded by a hyperplane orthogonal to $y_1-y_2$; give its equation.

Hint 1
Write cost(Swap) − cost(Id) and expand every square: which terms cancel?
Hint 2
Only the inner products $\dotp{x_1}{y_j}$ and $\dotp{z}{y_j}$ survive; group them as one inner product.
Solution

The norms $\norm{x_1}^2,\norm z^2,\norm{y_1}^2,\norm{y_2}^2$ appear once on each side and cancel, leaving $$\begin{aligned}&\text{cost(Swap)}-\text{cost(Id)}\\&\quad=2\big(\dotp{x_1}{y_1}+\dotp{z}{y_2}-\dotp{x_1}{y_2}-\dotp{z}{y_1}\big)\\&\quad=2\dotp{x_1-z}{y_1-y_2}.\end{aligned}$$ Id is at least as good iff $\dotp{z-x_1}{y_1-y_2}\le0$: a closed half-space. Its boundary is the hyperplane $H=\{z:\dotp{z-x_1}{y_1-y_2}=0\}$, through $x_1$ and orthogonal to $y_1-y_2$. On the other open half-space Swap is strictly better, and on $H$ they tie. In words: the two sources must be ordered, along the direction $y_1-y_2$, like their targets — the two-point monotonicity that §7 upgrades to cyclical monotonicity.

Exercise 3 — Discrete → continuous and continuous → discrete§5–§6 · checked

(1) $\al=\sum_{i=1}^Na_i\delta_{x_i}$ on $\R$ and $\be$ absolutely continuous: show that no map pushes $\al$ to $\be$. (2) $\al$ absolutely continuous, $\be=\sum_{j=1}^Mb_j\delta_{y_j}$: build many maps. (3) $\al=U[0,1]$, $\be=\sum_jb_j\delta_{y_j}$, $b_j\gt0$, $y_1\lt\dots\lt y_M$: compute the $W_p$-optimal Monge map for $p\ge1$. (4) $b_j=\frac1n$, $y_j=\frac{j-1/2}{n}$: compute $W_2^2(\al,\be)$.

Hint 1
(1) What is $T_\sharp\delta_x$? (2) A map to a discrete measure is a partition of the line into $M$ cells.
Hint 2
(3) $F_\al(x)=x$ on $[0,1]$, so the monotone map is the quantile function of $\be$. (4) Each cell of length $1/n$ is sent to its midpoint.
Solution

(1) $T_\sharp\al=\sum_ia_i\delta_{T(x_i)}$ is carried by the finite set $\{T(x_1),\dots,T(x_N)\}$, which has $\be$-measure $0$ because $\be$ has a density. So $T_\sharp\al\ne\be$ for every $T$.

(2) Choose measurable sets $A_1,\dots,A_M$ partitioning $\R$ with $\al(A_j)=b_j$ and set $T=y_j$ on $A_j$; then $\al(T^{-1}(\{y_j\}))=b_j$. Such partitions exist in abundance because $t\mapsto\al((-\infty,t])$ is continuous: consecutive intervals cut at quantile levels, in any of the $M!$ orders of the targets, or unions of many small intervals.

(3) The cost $\abs{x-y}^p$ is convex, so Theorem 2.41 applies: $T=F_\be^{-1}\circ F_\al=F_\be^{-1}$ on $[0,1]$. With $B_0=0$ and $B_j=b_1+\dots+b_j$, $T(x)=y_j$ for $x\in(B_{j-1},B_j]$: the intervals are filled in increasing order. It is optimal for every $p\ge1$, and it is the unique optimal map for $p\gt1$.

(4) Each interval $\big(\frac{j-1}n,\frac jn\big]$ is sent to its midpoint, so $W_2^2=n\int_{-1/(2n)}^{1/(2n)}s^2\dd s=\frac{1}{12n^2}$.

Exercise 4 — Uniform laws on intervals§6, §8 · checked

$\al=U[a,b]$, $\be=U[c,d]$, $c(x,y)=\abs{x-y}^2$: (i) find the optimal map; (ii) compute $W_2^2(\al,\be)$.

Hint
$F_\al(x)=\frac{x-a}{b-a}$ and $F_\be^{-1}(u)=c+(d-c)u$. For (ii), integrate in the quantile level $u$.
Solution

(i) $T(x)=F_\be^{-1}(F_\al(x))=c+\frac{d-c}{b-a}(x-a)$, an increasing affine map (the derivative of a convex quadratic).

(ii) Integrating in the quantile level, $$\begin{aligned}W_2^2&=\int_0^1\big((a-c)+((b-a)-(d-c))u\big)^2\dd u\\&=\Big(\frac{a+b}2-\frac{c+d}2\Big)^2+\frac{\big((b-a)-(d-c)\big)^2}{12}.\end{aligned}$$ It is $(\Delta m)^2+(\Delta\sigma)^2$ with the standard deviation $\sigma=\text{length}/\sqrt{12}$ of a uniform law: uniform laws form a location–scale family, like Gaussians (§8).

Exercise 5 — Many Monge solutions for $\abs{x-y}$§5 · Lab 5

$\al=U[0,2]$, $\be=U[1,3]$, $c(x,y)=\abs{x-y}$. (i) $T_1(x)=x+1$ is optimal and $W_1(\al,\be)=1$. (ii) $T_2(x)=x+2$ on $[0,1]$, $x$ on $(1,2]$, is optimal too. (iii) Build more.

Hint 1
Lower-bound $\int\abs{T(x)-x}\dd\al$ by $\int(T(x)-x)\dd\al$. What is this last integral for an admissible $T$?
Hint 2
Equality holds for every admissible $T$ with $T(x)\ge x$. Look for translations of pieces that tile $[1,3]$.
Solution

(i) For any $T$ with $T_\sharp\al=\be$, $$\begin{aligned}\int\abs{T(x)-x}\dd\al&\ge\int T\dd\al-\int x\dd\al\\&=\int y\dd\be-\int x\dd\al=2-1=1 .\end{aligned}$$ $T_1$ is admissible (a translation of a uniform law) and $T_1(x)-x=1\ge0$, so its cost is $1$: it is optimal and the Monge value is $1$. The same bound for couplings shows that the Kantorovich value (Lesson 2) is also $1$.

(ii) $T_2$ maps $[0,1]$ onto $[2,3]$ and fixes $(1,2]$, each piece by a translation; both pieces carry mass $\frac12$, which is what $\be$ puts on $[2,3]$ and $(1,2]$. So $T_{2\sharp}\al=\be$, $T_2(x)\ge x$, and its cost is $\int_0^1 2\cdot\frac12\dd x=1$.

(iii) For $s\in[0,1]$ let $T_s(x)=x+1$ on $[0,s]$, $x+2$ on $(s,1]$, $x+1$ on $(1,1+s]$, $x$ on $(1+s,2]$. The four images $[1,1+s]$, $(2+s,3]$, $(2,2+s]$, $(1+s,2]$ tile $[1,3]$, each piece is a translation, and $T_s(x)\ge x$: every $T_s$ is optimal; $s=1$ is $T_1$ and $s=0$ is $T_2$. More generally, replace $x\mapsto x+2$ on $[0,1]$ by any measure-preserving map of $[0,1]$ onto $[2,3]$, for instance $x\mapsto3-x$ (OT4ML Example 2.21). For $\abs{x-y}^2$ only $T_1$ is optimal (strict convexity): $T_2$ costs 2 (Lab 5).

Exercise 6 — Affine images§7–§8 · checked

(1) $\nu=(x\mapsto ax+b)_\sharp\mu$ with $a\gt0$ on $\R$: the optimal map, and $W_2$ in terms of $m_\mu$ and $\Var(\mu)$. (2) $\nu=(x\mapsto Ax+b)_\sharp\mu$ on $\R^d$ with $\mu$ absolutely continuous: a simple condition on $A$ for $T(x)=Ax+b$ to be the Brenier map, and $W_2$ in terms of $m_\mu$ and $\Sigma_\mu$.

Hint 1
(1) An increasing map on the line is optimal. (2) When is $x\mapsto Ax+b$ the gradient of a convex function?
Hint 2
Compute $\E\norm{X-AX-b}^2$ by splitting $X=m_\mu+Z$ with $\E Z=0$.
Solution

(1) $T(x)=ax+b$ is increasing, so it is the monotone map of §6 (it is the derivative of the convex $\frac a2x^2+bx$). With $m_\nu=am_\mu+b$ and $\sigma_\nu=a\sigma_\mu$, $$\begin{aligned}W_2^2(\mu,\nu)&=\E(X-aX-b)^2\\&=\big((1-a)m_\mu-b\big)^2+(1-a)^2\Var(\mu)\\&=(m_\mu-m_\nu)^2+(\sigma_\mu-\sigma_\nu)^2 .\end{aligned}$$

(2) Condition: $A$ symmetric positive semidefinite. Then $T=\nabla\big(\frac12\dotp{Ax}{x}+\dotp bx\big)$ is the gradient of a convex function pushing $\mu$ to $\nu$, hence the Brenier map (§7). Exercise 13 — Radial symmetry (optional)§7 · checked

$\al,\be$ radial on $\R^d$, $\al$ absolutely continuous, quadratic cost. (i) The Brenier map is radial, $T(x)=t(\norm x)\frac{x}{\norm x}$ with $t\ge0$ nondecreasing. (ii) $F_\al(r)=F_\be(t(r))$, hence $t=F_\be^{-1}\circ F_\al$ (radial CDFs). (iii) Uniform laws on balls.

Hint 1
Instead of proving that $T$ is radial first, guess the answer: move only the radius, by the 1D monotone map between the laws of $\norm X$ and $\norm Y$.
Hint 2
Check that your guess pushes $\al$ to $\be$ (the direction of a radial law is uniform and independent of the radius), and that it is the gradient of $x\mapsto\Psi(\norm x)$ for a convex nondecreasing $\Psi$. Then invoke uniqueness.
Solution (the book’s route, OT4ML Prop. 2.27, which avoids a gap in the sheet’s route)

Let $\tau=F_\be^{-1}\circ F_\al$; $F_\al$ is continuous because $\al$ has a density. Set $T(x)=\tau(\norm x)\frac x{\norm x}$ and $T(0)=0$.

$T_\sharp\al=\be$. Write $X=R\Theta$ with $R=\norm X$. By radial symmetry, $\Theta$ is uniform on the sphere and independent of $R$. By §6, $\tau(R)$ has the law of $\norm Y$ for $Y\sim\be$, and $\be$ is the law of $\norm Y\Theta'$ with $\Theta'$ uniform and independent. So $T(X)=\tau(R)\Theta\sim\be$.

$T$ is a gradient of a convex function. $\Psi(r)=\int_0^r\tau$ is convex and nondecreasing ($\tau\ge0$ is nondecreasing), so $\Phi(x)=\Psi(\norm x)$ is convex, and $\nabla\Phi(x)=\tau(\norm x)\frac x{\norm x}$ wherever $\tau$ is continuous at $\norm x$. $\tau$ is monotone, so it is continuous outside a countable set of radii, i.e. outside countably many spheres, which are $\al$-null. By Brenier’s theorem $T$ is the Brenier map. This proves (i) with $t=\tau$, and (ii) follows since $\tau(R)$ has the law of $\norm Y$.

The sheet’s route. It shows that $Q\circ T\circ Q^{-1}$ is also optimal, so $Q\circ T\circ Q^{-1}=T$ $\al$-a.e. It then evaluates this identity at a fixed $x$. That step needs care: the null set depends on $Q$, and there are uncountably many $Q$. A fix is to apply the identity to a countable dense set of $Q$’s, or to argue with the potential, since $\varphi\circ Q$ is also a Brenier potential. Two slips in the sheet: the statement writes “$Q\circ T\circ Q=T$” (it should be $Q\circ T\circ Q^{-1}$); and an axial symmetry is an orthogonal map, not a rotation. Radial laws are invariant under all orthogonal maps, so the argument is still valid. Also, the Hessian $\nabla^2\Phi=\Psi''(r)u\otimes u+\frac{\Psi'(r)}r(\Id-u\otimes u)$ computed in the sheet’s solution is correct: convexity requires $\Psi''\ge0$ and $\Psi'\ge0$.

(iii) $F_\al(r)=(r/R_\al)^d$ and $F_\be(\rho)=(\rho/R_\be)^d$ give $t(r)=\frac{R_\be}{R_\al}r$: the dilation $T(x)=\frac{R_\be}{R_\al}x$. One could guess it: it is the gradient of the convex $\frac{R_\be}{2R_\al}\norm x^2$ and pushes one uniform ball onto the other.

unit disk → disk of radius 2 in $\R^2$ ($W_2^2=\E\norm X^2=\frac12$): the empirical optimal assignment between $n$ samples of each sits at mean distance 0.380, 0.213, 0.118 from $x_i\mapsto2x_i$ for $n=100,300,1000$ — shrinking roughly like $n^{-1/2}$.

Lineconvex $h$: sorting is optimal; strictly convex: unique; $p=1$: ties; $p\lt1$: not sorting.
Push-forward$T_\sharp\al(B)=\al(T^{-1}(B))$; $\int h\dd T_\sharp\al=\int h\circ T\dd\al$; $T_\sharp\sum a_i\delta_{x_i}=\sum a_i\delta_{T(x_i)}$; $X\sim\al\Rightarrow T(X)\sim T_\sharp\al$.
Densities$\rho_\al(x)=\abs{\det T'(x)}\,\rho_\be(T(x))$ for a diffeomorphism; sum over preimages otherwise.
Monge problem$\inf\{\int c(x,T(x))\dd\al: T_\sharp\al=\be\}$; may be infeasible (atoms), unattained (singular source), non-unique ($p=1$); asymmetric.

Brenier$\al\ll\Leb$, $\Pc_2$, $\norm{x-y}^2$: unique optimal map $T=\nabla\varphi$, $\varphi$ convex; any convex gradient pushing $\al$ to $\be$ is it.

§15Self-test

  1. Why can $\delta_0$ not be pushed onto $\frac12(\delta_{-1}+\delta_1)$, while $U[-1,1]$ can be pushed onto it?
    Answer
    A map sends a point to one point, so $T_\sharp\delta_0=\delta_{T(0)}$ is a single Dirac. From $U[-1,1]$, send $[-1,0)$ to $-1$ and $[0,1]$ to $1$: each half has mass $\frac12$. An atomless source always admits maps (OT4ML Prop. 2.16).
  2. How many assignments must brute force examine for 36 points? What does the Hungarian method cost?
    Answer
    $36!\approx3.72\times10^{41}$ permutations; the Hungarian method needs $O(n^3)$ operations, about $5\times10^4$ basic steps here, and returns a certificate.
  3. State the dual certificate of an assignment and prove it in one line.
    Answer
    If $u_i+v_j\le C_{i,j}$ for all $i,j$ and $u_i+v_{\sigma(i)}=C_{i,\sigma(i)}$, then $\sigma$ is optimal: for any $\tau$, $\sum_iC_{i,\tau(i)}\ge\sum_iu_i+\sum_iv_{\tau(i)}=\sum u+\sum v=\sum_iC_{i,\sigma(i)}$.
  4. For $c=\norm{x-y}^2$, when is the pairing $x_1\mapsto y_1$, $z\mapsto y_2$ better than the swap?
    Answer
    Iff $\dotp{x_1-z}{y_1-y_2}\ge0$ (Exercise 2): a half-space bounded by the hyperplane through $x_1$ orthogonal to $y_1-y_2$.
  5. Why do optimal segments never cross for $c=\norm{x-y}$ in the plane, and why is that not enough to find the optimum?
    Answer
    Rewire the two crossing segments: the triangle inequality at the crossing point makes the new pair strictly shorter. But exponentially many non-crossing matchings exist (Catalan numbers), so non-crossing is a necessary test, not a certificate.
  6. On the line with $c=\abs{x-y}^p$, for which $p$ is sorting optimal, and when is it the unique optimum?
    Answer
    Optimal for $p\ge1$ (convex cost), the unique optimum for $p\gt1$ (strictly convex) with distinct points. For $p=1$ other optima can exist (book-shifting). For $p\lt1$ sorting is generally not optimal (Lab 3: 24.98% excess at $p=\frac12$).
  7. Give the two equivalent definitions of $T_\sharp\al$ and compute $T_\sharp\big(\frac13\delta_0+\frac23\delta_1\big)$ for $T(x)=x^2-x$.
    Answer
    $T_\sharp\al(B)=\al(T^{-1}(B))$, or $\int h\dd T_\sharp\al=\int h\circ T\dd\al$. Here $T(0)=T(1)=0$, so the result is $\delta_0$: masses of points sent to the same place add up.
  8. What is the density of $T_\sharp\al$ for $\al=\Gauss(0,1)$ and $T(x)=2x+1$? Check with the Jacobian formula.
    Answer
    $\rho_\be(y)=\rho_\al\big(\frac{y-1}2\big)\cdot\frac12$, the density of $\Gauss(1,4)$: the factor $\frac12=1/\abs{T'}$ is the Jacobian.
  9. Describe the three ways the Monge problem can fail, with one example each.
    Answer
    No admissible map ($\delta_0\to\frac12\delta_{-1}+\frac12\delta_1$); infimum not attained (segment to two segments, cost $1+\frac1{12n^2}\to1$); non-unique minimisers ($U[0,2]\to U[1,3]$ with $\abs{x-y}$: translation and book-shifting).
  10. Why is the directed Monge distance not symmetric? Give an example where one direction is finite and the other infinite.
    Answer
    Maps can merge mass but not split it. From $U[0,1]$ to $\frac12(\delta_0+\delta_1)$ there are maps; in the reverse direction none exists, so $\Wt_p=+\infty$.
  11. Prove that $F_\al^{-1}(U)\sim\al$ for $U$ uniform on $(0,1)$.
    Answer
    $\Prob(F_\al^{-1}(U)\le x)=\Prob(U\le F_\al(x))=F_\al(x)$, using $F_\al^{-1}(r)\le x\iff r\le F_\al(x)$.
  12. Why does $T=F_\be^{-1}\circ F_\al$ fail when $\al$ has an atom? What replaces it?
    Answer
    The whole atom goes to one point, so $T_\sharp\al\ne\be$ in general (e.g. $\delta_0\to U[0,1]$). The quantile coupling $(F_\al^{-1},F_\be^{-1})_\sharp\Leb_{(0,1)}$ is optimal among couplings (Lesson 2).
  13. Compute $W_2^2(U[0,1],U[0,2])$ and give the map.
    Answer
    $T(x)=2x$; $W_2^2=\int_0^1(x-2x)^2\dd x=\frac13$. Exercise 4’s formula: $(\frac12-1)^2+\frac{(1-2)^2}{12}=\frac14+\frac1{12}=\frac13$.
  14. What does the Fréchet–Hoeffding argument show, and why does it prove more than Theorem 2.41?
    Answer
    The monotone pairing attains $\Prob(X\le s,Y\le t)=\min(F_\al(s),F_\be(t))$ for all $(s,t)$, the largest possible value. Hoeffding’s identity then says it maximises $\E[XY]$, hence minimises $\E(X-Y)^2$, among all pairs with the given laws — maps or not.
  15. State Brenier’s theorem with all its hypotheses. Which hypothesis fails in the segment example?
    Answer
    $\al,\be\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$, $\al$ absolutely continuous, cost $\norm{x-y}^2$: there is a unique optimal map, and it is $\nabla\varphi$ with $\varphi$ convex. In the segment example the source lives on a line (a hypersurface of $\R^2$): it is not absolutely continuous.
  16. Prove that if $\varphi$ is convex and $(\nabla\varphi)_\sharp\al=\be$, then $\nabla\varphi$ is optimal.
    Answer
    Fenchel–Young: $\dotp{x}{S(x)}\le\varphi(x)+\varphi^*(S(x))$ with equality for $S=\nabla\varphi$. Integrate against $\al$: the right-hand side is $\int\varphi\dd\al+\int\varphi^*\dd\be$ for every admissible $S$. So $\nabla\varphi$ maximises $\int\dotp{x}{S(x)}\dd\al$, which is equivalent to minimising the quadratic cost.
  17. Is the rotation $x\mapsto R_{\pi/4}x$ an optimal map from $\Gauss(0,\Id_2)$ to its image?
    Answer
    No. The image is $\Gauss(0,\Id_2)$ itself, and the optimal map is the identity (cost 0), while the rotation moves points. The rotation is monotone but not symmetric, hence not a gradient.
  18. Give the Brenier map between $\Gauss(0,\diag(1,4))$ and $\Gauss(0,\diag(4,1))$ and the value of $W_2^2$.
    Answer
    The covariances commute: $A=\diag(2,\frac12)$, and $W_2^2=\norm{\Sigma_\al^{1/2}-\Sigma_\be^{1/2}}_F^2=(1-2)^2+(2-1)^2=2$. (A rotation by $90^\circ$ also pushes one to the other, but it is not a gradient.)
  19. Why is the Bures midpoint covariance smaller than $\frac12(\Sigma_\al+\Sigma_\be)$?
    Answer
    $\frac12(\Sigma_\al+\Sigma_\be)-\Sigma_{1/2}=\frac14(\Id-A)\Sigma_\al(\Id-A)\succeq0$. The mixture of the two laws keeps both spreads; the displacement interpolation moves the mass.
  20. Derive the Monge–Ampère equation from the density formula.
    Answer
    With $T=\nabla\varphi$, $T'=\nabla^2\varphi$ is symmetric positive semidefinite, so $\abs{\det T'}=\det\nabla^2\varphi$, and $\rho_\al=\abs{\det T'}\,\rho_\be\circ T$ becomes $\det\nabla^2\varphi\,\rho_\be(\nabla\varphi)=\rho_\al$.
  21. Why is $W_2^2$ between two independent $n$-samples a biased estimate of $W_2^2(\al,\be)$, and in which direction?
    Answer
    $W_2^2$ is jointly convex and $\E\al_n=\al$, $\E\be_n=\be$. By Jensen, $\E W_2^2(\al_n,\be_n)\ge W_2^2(\al,\be)$: the bias is upwards (Lab 6: +0.0585 at $n=100$).
  22. In the discrete Brenier theorem, what object certifies that an assignment is optimal, and what certifies that it is not?
    Answer
    Optimal: potentials $v$ (equivalently a convex $\varphi$ with $y_{\sigma(i)}\in\partial\varphi(x_i)$), which exist iff the exchange graph has no negative cycle. Not optimal: a negative cycle, i.e. an improving cyclic exchange.
