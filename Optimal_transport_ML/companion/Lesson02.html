§0Where this lesson sits

Lesson 1 asked Monge’s question, move each point to one point, and ended with three failures: some problems have no admissible map, some have an infimum that no map reaches, and optimal maps need not be unique. This lesson makes Kantorovich’s move of 1942: let the mass sitting at a point be split among several destinations. The unknown becomes a coupling, a joint distribution of source and target with the prescribed marginals. The problem becomes a linear program. It is always feasible, it always has a solution, and its solutions are sparse. For uniform weights (the assignment problem of Lesson 1), in one dimension, and under Brenier’s hypotheses, it loses nothing: an optimal coupling is a map.

What it reuses, what it prepares

Reuses (all from Lesson 1):

  • The assignment problem and its certificate $u_i+v_j\le C_{i,j}$ (Lesson 1 §2). Here it becomes the dual of a linear program (§5), and permutations become the vertices of a polytope (§4).
  • Sorting on the line (Lesson 1 §3). With weights it becomes the north-west corner rule (§3).
  • The push-forward (Lesson 1 §4). The marginals of a coupling are push-forwards by the two projections (§6).
  • The three failures of the Monge problem (Lesson 1 §5), which §1 and §7 repair.
  • Quantile functions (Lesson 1 §6): the 1D Kantorovich solution couples equal quantile levels even when there are atoms (§7).
  • Brenier’s theorem (Lesson 1 §7) and its discrete proof by cyclical monotonicity. Here it becomes “Monge = Kantorovich” (§7) and the $c$-cyclical monotonicity of optimal plans (§8).

Prepares.

  • Lesson 3 (metric properties) takes $c=d^p$ and turns $\mathcal L_{d^p}^{1/p}$ into the Wasserstein distance $W_p$. The plan-induced interpolation of §9 becomes a geodesic, and the existence result of §6 is revisited with weak convergence.
  • Lesson 4 (duality) develops the dual linear program of §5 into Kantorovich duality and $c$-transforms. It proves Brenier’s theorem, and with it the equivalence of §7.
  • Lesson 7 (entropic regularization) replaces the vertex-seeking linear program by a strictly convex problem solved by Sinkhorn. The central-path comparison of Lab 1 is a first look at it.

§1From maps to couplings: letting mass split

The problem

Take two discrete measures $\al=\sum_{i=1}^na_i\delta_{x_i}$ and $\be=\sum_{j=1}^mb_j\delta_{y_j}$ with $a_i,b_j\gt0$ and the same total mass. A Monge map must send the whole mass $a_i$ of $x_i$ to a single point $T(x_i)$. That is impossible as soon as one atom must feed two targets — for instance when $m\gt n$, or when $\al=\delta_0$ and $\be=\frac12(\delta_{-1}+\delta_1)$ (Lesson 1 §5). Even when maps exist, they form a combinatorial, non-convex set. The formulation is also asymmetric: two atoms can be merged into one, but one atom cannot be split into two.

The intuition, and the definition

Kantorovich’s idea is to record how much mass goes from $x_i$ to $y_j$, rather than where each point goes. Call this amount $P_{i,j}\ge0$. The mass leaving $x_i$ must be $a_i$, and the mass arriving at $y_j$ must be $b_j$.

Definition (discrete couplings)OT4ML Def. 3.1, p. 51 (43); CompOT (2.10), p. 18 (14)

$$\Couplings(a,b)=\big\{P\in\R_+^{n\times m}:\ P\mathbb 1_m=a,\ P^\top\mathbb 1_n=b\big\},$$ that is $\sum_jP_{i,j}=a_i$ for every row $i$ and $\sum_iP_{i,j}=b_j$ for every column $j$.

A coupling is a non-negative matrix with prescribed row sums and column sums. Its entries need not be $0$ or $a_i$: mass can be split. The set is defined by linear equalities and inequalities, so it is a convex polytope, the transportation polytope. It is symmetric: $P\in\Couplings(a,b)$ if and only if $P^\top\in\Couplings(b,a)$.

The smallest cases

If $n=1$ or $m=1$, the constraints fix every entry: the only coupling of $\delta_x$ with $\be$ sends each $b_j$ from $x$ to $y_j$. The first genuine choice appears for $n=m=2$, with $a=(p,1-p)$ and $b=(q,1-q)$ (OT4ML Remark 3.2, p. 51 (43)). Once $s=P_{1,1}$ is chosen, the marginal constraints force the other three entries: $$\begin{gathered}P(s)=\begin{pmatrix}s&p-s\\ q-s&1-p-q+s\end{pmatrix},\\ \max(0,p+q-1)\le s\le\min(p,q).\end{gathered}$$ So $\Couplings(a,b)$ is a segment. In general, when all masses are positive, the polytope has dimension $(n-1)(m-1)$: an $n\times m$ matrix has $nm$ entries, and the $n+m$ marginal constraints have rank $n+m-1$ (their sum over rows equals their sum over columns). Lab 1 draws the case $n=2$, $m=3$, a polygon.

A coupling that always exists

Definition (product coupling)OT4ML Def. 3.3, p. 52 (44)

$(a\otimes b)_{i,j}=a_ib_j$. It belongs to $\Couplings(a,b)$: its row sums are $a_i\sum_jb_j=a_i$ and its column sums $b_j$.

It sends each source label independently of the target label, so it is the most diffuse coupling: it has $nm$ positive entries. It is a feasibility witness, not a candidate optimum.

Proposition (product optimality is degenerate)OT4ML Prop. 3.4, p. 52 (44)

With $a_i,b_j\gt0$, the product plan minimises $P\mapsto\dotp CP$ over $\Couplings(a,b)$ if and only if every coupling does.

Proof (an interior point of a polytope can be optimal only if the cost is flat)

If every coupling is optimal, so is $a\otimes b$. Conversely, let $a\otimes b$ be optimal and $Q\in\Couplings(a,b)$ be arbitrary. All entries of $a\otimes b$ are positive, so $R=(1+t)(a\otimes b)-tQ$ is non-negative for small $t\gt0$. It has the right marginals, so $R\in\Couplings(a,b)$, and $a\otimes b=\frac1{1+t}R+\frac t{1+t}Q$. Both $\dotp CR$ and $\dotp CQ$ are at least $\dotp C{a\otimes b}$, and their average with weights $\frac1{1+t},\frac t{1+t}$ equals it. Hence $\dotp CQ=\dotp C{a\otimes b}$. $\square$

The Kantorovich problem, and why it relaxes Monge

Definition (discrete Kantorovich problem)OT4ML Def. 3.5, p. 53 (45); CompOT (2.11), p. 18 (14)

For a cost matrix $C\in\R^{n\times m}$ ($C_{i,j}$ is the price of moving one unit of mass from $x_i$ to $y_j$), $$\mathrm L_C(a,b)=\min_{P\in\Couplings(a,b)}\dotp CP=\min_{P\in\Couplings(a,b)}\sum_{i,j}C_{i,j}P_{i,j}.$$ A minimiser is an optimal coupling, or optimal transport plan.

A Monge map $T$ with $T_\sharp\al=\be$ defines the coupling $P_{i,j}=a_i\mathbf 1_{T(x_i)=y_j}$, with the same cost. So every Monge competitor is a Kantorovich competitor, and $$\mathrm L_C(a,b)\le\min\Big\{\sum_ia_iC_{i,T(i)}:\ T_\sharp\al=\be\Big\}.$$ The inequality can be strict (§7), and the right-hand side can be $+\infty$ (no admissible map), while the left-hand side is always a minimum over a non-empty compact polytope. The price of the relaxation is that an optimal coupling may split mass; the next sections show when it does not have to.

Key idea: maps are the vertices you hope for

The Monge problem minimises a linear function over a combinatorial set (maps, permutations). The Kantorovich problem minimises the same linear function over its convex hull, a polytope. A linear function on a polytope is minimised at a vertex. The whole lesson is about what the vertices of $\Couplings(a,b)$ look like: sparse trees in general (§2), permutations for uniform weights (§4), a monotone staircase on the line (§3).

Link: probability language

For random variables, a coupling is the law of a pair $(X,Y)$ with $X\sim\al$ and $Y\sim\be$, and $\mathrm L_C(a,b)=\min\E[c(X,Y)]$ over all joint laws with these marginals (OT4ML Remark 3.33, p. 66 (58); StatOT (KOT), p. 17 (11)). The product coupling makes $X$ and $Y$ independent, and a Monge map makes $Y$ a function of $X$. §6 states this for general measures.

§2A linear program: vertices, sparsity and the north-west corner

Convex in the marginals, concave in the cost

The value $\mathrm L_C(a,b)$ depends on two kinds of data, and it has opposite curvature in each.

Proposition (convexity in the marginals and concavity in the cost)OT4ML Prop. 3.6, p. 53 (45)

For fixed $C$, $(a,b)\mapsto\mathrm L_C(a,b)$ is jointly convex. For fixed $(a,b)$, $C\mapsto\mathrm L_C(a,b)$ is concave.

Proof (two lines: mixing plans, and a minimum of linear functions)

If $P_0$ is optimal for $(a_0,b_0)$ and $P_1$ for $(a_1,b_1)$, then $(1-t)P_0+tP_1$ couples $\big((1-t)a_0+ta_1,(1-t)b_0+tb_1\big)$, so $\mathrm L_C$ at the mixed marginals is at most $(1-t)\mathrm L_C(a_0,b_0)+t\mathrm L_C(a_1,b_1)$. For the cost, $\mathrm L_C=\min_P\dotp CP$ is a minimum of linear functions of $C$, hence concave. $\square$

Convexity in the marginals is what later makes transport losses usable for fitting distributions (Lesson 4 §9, Lesson 7). Concavity in the cost is what makes learning the cost a delicate problem (OT4ML §12.5, beyond the course).

Where a linear program attains its minimum

$\Couplings(a,b)$ is compact (entries lie in $[0,1]$) and convex, and the objective is linear. Two elementary facts of convex geometry then say where to look for minimisers.

Proposition (linear programs have extreme minimisers)OT4ML Props. 3.14 and 3.16, pp. 56–57 (48–49)

A non-empty compact convex set $\mathcal C$ in finite dimension has extreme points: points that are not the midpoint of two other points of $\mathcal C$. For every linear form $\ell$, some extreme point of $\mathcal C$ minimises $\ell$ over $\mathcal C$.

Proof idea: the set of minimisers is itself compact and convex, so it has an extreme point; an extreme point of the set of minimisers is extreme in $\mathcal C$, because if it were the midpoint of two points of $\mathcal C$, linearity would make both of them minimisers. Compactness matters: the line $\R\times\{0\}$ is closed and convex but has no extreme point (OT4ML Example 3.15).

Vertices are sparse: at most $n+m-1$ non-zero entries

What does an extreme point of $\Couplings(a,b)$ look like? Draw the support graph of a coupling: a bipartite graph with the $n$ sources on one side, the $m$ targets on the other, and an edge $(i,j)$ whenever $P_{i,j}\gt0$.

Proposition (sparse optimal plans)OT4ML Props. 3.7–3.8, p. 54 (46); CompOT Prop. 3.4, pp. 46–48 (42–44)

If a coupling $P$ is an extreme point of $\Couplings(a,b)$, its support graph has no cycle, so $P$ has at most $n+m-1$ non-zero entries. Consequently the Kantorovich problem always has an optimal coupling with at most $n+m-1$ non-zero entries.

Proof (a cycle in the support is a direction along which one can move in both senses)

Suppose the support graph contains a cycle $i_1\to j_1\to i_2\to j_2\to\cdots\to i_k\to j_k\to i_1$, whose edges $(i_1,j_1),(i_2,j_1),(i_2,j_2),\dots,(i_1,j_k)$ all carry positive mass. Let $E$ be the matrix equal to $+\varepsilon$ on $(i_1,j_1),(i_2,j_2),\dots,(i_k,j_k)$, to $-\varepsilon$ on $(i_2,j_1),(i_3,j_2),\dots,(i_1,j_k)$, and $0$ elsewhere. Every row and every column of the cycle contains one $+\varepsilon$ and one $-\varepsilon$, so $E$ has zero row and column sums. For $\varepsilon$ smaller than the smallest mass on the cycle, $P+E$ and $P-E$ are couplings, and $P$ is their midpoint: $P$ is not extreme. So an extreme coupling has an acyclic support graph, a forest on $n+m$ nodes, which has at most $n+m-1$ edges. The last sentence follows from the previous paragraph. $\square$

OT4ML proves the same statement with linear algebra (Prop. 3.7): a minimiser with the fewest non-zero entries has at most $\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal A)$ of them, where $\mathcal A(P)=(P\mathbb 1_m,P^\top\mathbb 1_n)$ is the marginal operator, of rank $n+m-1$. The alternating matrix $E$ above is exactly a non-zero element of the kernel of $\mathcal A$ supported on the cycle.

“At most” is essential. With uniform weights and $n=m$, an optimal vertex is a permutation matrix (§4), with only $n$ non-zero entries instead of $2n-1$: the vertex is degenerate. With random, generic weights the bound is attained.

Lab 1The transportation polytope of a 2 × 3 problem

Question: what does $\Couplings(a,b)$ look like, where is the optimum, and how do the two ways of staying inside the polytope — a log-barrier (interior-point methods) and an entropy (Lesson 7) — approach it?

Setting: two sources $x=(0,1)$ with masses $a=(a_1,1-a_1)$, three targets $y=(0.15,0.55,1.2)$ with masses $b$, cost $\abs{x-y}^p$. By §1 the polytope has dimension $(2-1)(3-1)=2$; it is drawn in the coordinates $(s,t)=(P_{1,1},P_{1,2})$, the four other entries being affine in $(s,t)$. The central paths are computed for 12 values of $\varepsilon$ from $1$ to $0.001$; the barrier problem is $\min\dotp CP-\varepsilon\sum_{i,j}\log P_{i,j}$ (OT4ML eq. (3.5), p. 63 (55)), solved by Newton’s method, and the entropic one $\min\dotp CP+\varepsilon\sum P_{i,j}\log P_{i,j}$, solved by Sinkhorn.

What we found (measured, at the default setting $a=(0.55,0.45)$, $b=(0.3,0.45,0.25)$, $p=2$)

  • The polytope is a pentagon: 5 vertices, with 3, 4, 4, 4, 4 non-zero entries. Four vertices have the maximal number $n+m-1=4$; one is degenerate, with 3.
  • The optimum is a vertex with 4 non-zero entries, the plan $P^\star=\big($(0.30, 0.25, 0.00), (0.00, 0.20, 0.25)$\big)$ (rows), of cost 0.13288. The network simplex of §5 returns the same value, 0.13288. The product plan costs 0.41962, more than three times as much.
  • The barrier path approaches the vertex linearly in $\varepsilon$: its distance to $P^\star$ is 0.0126 at $\varepsilon=0.01$ and 0.00133 at $\varepsilon=0.001$, about 1.33$\,\varepsilon$. At the optimum each vanishing entry balances its reduced cost $r$ against the barrier, $P_{i,j}\approx\varepsilon/r$.
  • The entropic path approaches it exponentially: distance 1.1e−4 at $\varepsilon=0.1$ and below 10⁻¹⁴ (rounding level) at $\varepsilon=0.01$, because a vanishing entry now behaves like $e^{-r/\varepsilon}$. It is the minimiser of a different problem, which is the subject of Lesson 7.
  • With $p=1$ the optimum is still a single vertex here (1 optimal vertex, cost 0.32250). Dragging $b$ until two vertices have the same cost makes a whole edge optimal: then the linear program has infinitely many solutions, all on that edge.

A vertex for free: the north-west corner rule

Finding some vertex is easy. Sweep the matrix from the top-left (“north-west”) corner, filling each cell with as much mass as the remaining row and column allow.

Algorithm (north-west corner)OT4ML Alg. 3.1 and Prop. 3.9, p. 55 (47); CompOT §3.4.2, p. 48 (44)

Start at $(i,j)=(1,1)$ with residual masses $r=a$, $s=b$. Repeat: set $P_{i,j}=\min(r_i,s_j)$, subtract it from $r_i$ and $s_j$; if row $i$ is exhausted move down, otherwise move right; stop at $(n,m)$. The result is a coupling with at most $n+m-1$ positive entries and an acyclic support.

Each step exhausts a row or a column, so the sweep takes at most $n+m-1$ steps. It moves only down or right, so its support is a staircase, which contains no cycle. The rule ignores the cost: it is a starting point, not a solver — except on the line, where §3 shows that it is optimal once the points are sorted. CompOT’s worked example, $a=(0.2,0.5,0.3)$ and $b=(0.5,0.1,0.4)$, gives $P_{1,1}=0.2$, $P_{2,1}=0.3$, $P_{2,2}=0.1$, $P_{2,3}=0.1$, $P_{3,3}=0.3$; the page’s implementation reproduces it.

Watch out: degenerate staircases

When a row and a column are exhausted at the same time, OT4ML’s and CompOT’s versions advance both indices, and the staircase then has fewer than $n+m-1$ cells. The simplex method of §5 needs a spanning tree of exactly $n+m-1$ cells, so implementations keep a cell with zero mass (a “degenerate basic variable”; OT4ML p. 55 (47)). The page’s code moves down in that case and keeps the zero cell.

§3One dimension: the north-west corner rule is optimal

The weighted version of sorting

In Lesson 1 §3, sorting both clouds solved the uniform assignment problem on the line for convex costs. With weights, the same idea reads: sort both supports, then sweep masses from left to right. That is exactly the north-west corner rule applied to sorted atoms.

Proposition (one-dimensional weighted sweep)OT4ML Prop. 3.10, p. 55 (47); compact notes Prop. 3.9, p. 9

Let $x_1\le\dots\le x_n$ and $y_1\le\dots\le y_m$ be points of $\R$, and $c(x,y)=h(x-y)$ with $h$ convex. The north-west corner plan between the sorted weighted atoms is optimal. Unsorted inputs are solved in $O(n\log n+m\log m)$ operations: sort, then sweep once.

Proof (move mass to the corner without increasing the cost, then induct)

Let $P$ be any coupling. If $P_{1,1}\lt\min(a_1,b_1)$, row 1 sends some mass to a column $j\gt1$ and column 1 receives some from a row $i\gt1$. Move $\eta=\min(P_{1,j},P_{i,1})$ from the cells $(1,j),(i,1)$ to $(1,1),(i,j)$: the marginals are unchanged. The cost changes by $\eta\big[h(x_1-y_1)+h(x_i-y_j)-h(x_1-y_j)-h(x_i-y_1)\big]\le0$, by the Monge inequality for convex $h$: with $u=x_1-y_j$, $\delta=x_i-x_1\ge0$ and $\eta'=y_j-y_1\ge0$, it reads $h(u+\eta')-h(u)\le h(u+\delta+\eta')-h(u+\delta)$, which says that the increments of a convex function increase. Each move empties cell $(1,j)$ or cell $(i,1)$, so finitely many moves give $P_{1,1}=\min(a_1,b_1)$, the first north-west assignment. It exhausts row 1 or column 1; removing it leaves a smaller ordered problem, and induction on $n+m$ turns $P$ into the north-west plan without increasing its cost. $\square$

  • Uniqueness. If $h$ is strictly convex and the points are distinct, the Monge inequality is strict. Then an optimal plan cannot contain a crossed pair — positive mass on $(i,j)$ and $(i',j')$ with $i\lt i'$ and $j\gt j'$ — because uncrossing would lower the cost. The only coupling without crossed pairs is the north-west plan (induction as above), so it is the unique optimum. For $h=\abs\cdot$ ($p=1$) the Monge inequality can be an equality, and other optima exist (the book-shifting example of Lesson 1 §3).
  • The quantile picture. The north-west plan is the discrete case of the quantile coupling of Lesson 1 §6: the level $r\in(0,1)$ goes from the source atom where $F_\al$ crosses $r$ to the target atom where $F_\be$ crosses $r$. §7 states this for arbitrary measures, atoms included.
  • Concave costs. The proof uses convexity once, in the Monge inequality. For $h=\abs\cdot^p$ with $p\lt1$ the inequality reverses for some configurations, and the sweep is no longer optimal — Lab 3 measures by how much.

§4Permutations as couplings: the Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem

The assignment problem is a linear program in disguise

Take $n=m$ and uniform weights $a=b=\mathbb 1_n/n$: the assignment problem of Lesson 1 §2. A permutation $\sigma$ defines the permutation matrix $(P_\sigma)_{i,j}=1$ if $j=\sigma(i)$ and $0$ otherwise, and the coupling $P_\sigma/n$, of cost $\dotp C{P_\sigma/n}=\frac1n\sum_iC_{i,\sigma(i)}$ (OT4ML Def. 3.11, p. 56 (48)). So the assignment problem minimises a linear function over the finite, non-convex set of permutation matrices. Its convex hull sits inside the Birkhoff polytope of bistochastic matrices, $$\begin{gathered}\mathcal B_n=\big\{P\in\R_+^{n\times n}:\ P\mathbb 1_n=\mathbb 1_n,\ P^\top\mathbb 1_n=\mathbb 1_n\big\},\\ \Couplings\big(\tfrac{\mathbb 1_n}n,\tfrac{\mathbb 1_n}n\big)=\tfrac1n\mathcal B_n,\end{gathered}$$ and the permutation matrices are exactly the bistochastic matrices with entries in $\{0,1\}$ (OT4ML Def. 3.12). Before knowing more about $\mathcal B_n$, all one can say is that the relaxation is a lower bound: $\min_{P\in\mathcal B_n}\dotp CP\le\min_\sigma\dotp C{P_\sigma}$.

The theorem

Theorem (Birkhoff–von Neumann)OT4ML Thm. 3.17, p. 57 (49); compact notes Thm. 3.15, p. 9; StatOT p. 20 (14)

The extreme points of $\mathcal B_n$ are exactly the permutation matrices.

Proof (a fractional entry lies on an alternating cycle, along which the matrix can be moved both ways)

Permutation matrices are extreme. Entries of a bistochastic matrix lie in $[0,1]$, whose only extreme points are $0$ and $1$. If $P_\sigma=\frac12(Q+R)$ with $Q,R\in\mathcal B_n$, every entry $0$ of $P_\sigma$ forces $Q_{i,j}=R_{i,j}=0$ and every entry $1$ forces $Q_{i,j}=R_{i,j}=1$, so $Q=R=P_\sigma$.

Every other bistochastic matrix is not. Let $P\in\mathcal B_n$ have a fractional entry. Consider the bipartite graph whose edges are the fractional entries, $0\lt P_{i,j}\lt1$. A row containing one fractional entry contains another one: the row sums to 1 and cannot be completed by an entry equal to 1. The same holds for columns. So every vertex of this graph has degree 0 or at least 2, and a walk that never immediately backtracks must eventually close a cycle. Take a shortest one, $i_1\to j_1\to i_2\to j_2\to\dots\to i_p\to j_p\to i_1$; its rows are distinct and so are its columns. Add $+\varepsilon/2$ on the edges $(i_s,j_s)$ and $-\varepsilon/2$ on the edges $(i_{s+1},j_s)$, with $\varepsilon$ the smallest of the numbers $P$ and $1-P$ on the cycle: this gives $Q$; the opposite signs give $R$. Each row and column of the cycle gains and loses $\varepsilon/2$, so $Q,R\in\mathcal B_n$, $Q\ne R$, and $P=\frac12(Q+R)$. $\square$

This is the same cycle argument as in §2. On a support that is a union of disjoint cycles and isolated unit entries — the $7\times7$ matrix of OT4ML Fig. 3.4 — it is visible at a glance, and Lab 4 starts from that matrix.

The relaxation is tight for uniform weights

Corollary (Kantorovich for matching)OT4ML Cor. 3.18, p. 58 (50); CompOT Prop. 2.1, p. 19 (15); compact notes Cor. 3.16

If $m=n$ and $a=b=\mathbb 1_n/n$, the Kantorovich problem has an optimal solution of the form $P_\sigma/n$, and $\sigma$ solves the assignment problem. Discrete Monge and discrete Kantorovich have the same value.

Proof: the feasible set is $\frac1n\mathcal B_n$; §2 gives an optimal extreme point; the theorem says it is $P_\sigma/n$. The Kantorovich value is then at least the assignment value and at most it (relaxation), so they are equal. Two warnings. First, other optimal couplings may be fractional when several permutations tie (Lesson 1’s $p=1$ examples). Second, nothing like this holds for non-uniform weights: there the Monge set can be empty, while optimal vertices are cycle-free trees with up to $n+m-1$ entries (OT4ML Remark 3.19; Lab 2). This is why Lesson 1’s Hungarian method, a special linear-programming algorithm (CompOT §3.6, the primal–dual method), solved an assignment problem that looks combinatorial.

Decomposing a bistochastic matrix

Corollary (Birkhoff–von Neumann decomposition)OT4ML Cor. 3.20, p. 58 (50) and Alg. 3.2, p. 59 (51)

Every $P\in\mathcal B_n$ is a convex combination $P=\sum_{r=1}^L\lambda_rP_{\sigma_r}$ of $L\le(n-1)^2+1$ permutation matrices.

Existence follows from the theorem (a compact convex set is the convex hull of its extreme points), and the bound from Carathéodory’s theorem: $\mathcal B_n$ has dimension $n^2-(2n-1)=(n-1)^2$, and a point of a polytope of dimension $d$ is a convex combination of $d+1$ of its vertices. The construction is greedy. Take a perfect matching $\sigma$ inside the support of the residual $R$ (initially $R=P$), subtract $\lambda P_\sigma$ with $\lambda=\min_iR_{i,\sigma(i)}$, and repeat. A perfect matching always exists by Hall’s theorem. For a set $I$ of rows with neighbour set $N(I)$ in the support, and row and column sums all equal to $s$, $$s\,\abs I=\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{j\in N(I)}R_{i,j}\le\sum_{j\in N(I)}\sum_iR_{i,j}=s\,\abs{N(I)},$$ so $\abs{N(I)}\ge\abs I$. Each step empties at least one entry, and the residual moves to a proper face of the face it was in, so the procedure stops after at most $(n-1)^2+1$ steps.

Proposition (the bound is attained)an observation made for this page, proved below; checked in Lab 4

For a bistochastic matrix in general position — for instance with random positive entries — every Birkhoff–von Neumann decomposition has exactly $(n-1)^2+1$ terms. The greedy procedure also takes exactly that many steps.

Proof (fewer vertices span a lower-dimensional set)

A convex combination of $k$ permutation matrices lies in the convex hull of $k$ points, a set of dimension at most $k-1$. There are finitely many choices of at most $(n-1)^2$ permutation matrices, and each hull has dimension at most $(n-1)^2-1$. Their union is a set of measure zero in the $(n-1)^2$-dimensional affine space of $\mathcal B_n$. A matrix drawn with a density (or a matrix in “general position”) avoids it, so it needs at least $(n-1)^2+1$ terms, and Carathéodory gives at most that many. $\square$

So the Carathéodory bound is not pessimism: it is the typical case. For sparse matrices the picture changes. A matrix built as a mixture of $k$ permutations has a decomposition with $k$ terms, but the greedy rarely finds it. Finding a decomposition with the fewest terms is a hard combinatorial problem (see §10).

Rational weights: back to an assignment

If the weights are rational, $a_i=k_i/N$ and $b_j=\ell_j/N$ with integers $k_i,\ell_j$, replace $x_i$ by $k_i$ copies and $y_j$ by $\ell_j$ copies. The result is a uniform assignment problem between two clouds of $N$ points.

Proposition (rational weights as duplicated uniform matching)OT4ML Prop. 3.21, p. 60 (52); compact notes Prop. 3.18

The duplicated assignment problem and the Kantorovich problem between $\al=\sum_i\frac{k_i}N\delta_{x_i}$ and $\be=\sum_j\frac{\ell_j}N\delta_{y_j}$ have the same value. Some optimal coupling has entries $P_{i,j}=n_{i,j}/N$ with integers $n_{i,j}$ (the number of copies of $x_i$ matched to copies of $y_j$).

Proof idea: an assignment between the duplicated clouds collapses to integer counts $n_{i,j}$ with row sums $k_i$ and column sums $\ell_j$, and conversely. That integer optima exist is total unimodularity: the constraint matrix of the transportation problem is the incidence matrix of a bipartite graph, so every vertex of $\{Q\ge0: Q\mathbb 1=k,\ Q^\top\mathbb 1=\ell\}$ is integral when $k,\ell$ are. Birkhoff–von Neumann is the special case $k=\ell=\mathbb 1$. This is also how the sheet’s Exercise 5 characterises the patterns of forbidden cells that still admit a finite-cost coupling.

§5Solving the linear program

In standard form

Stack the entries of $P$ into a vector $p\in\R^{nm}$. The marginal constraints become $Ap=(a,b)$ for a $(n+m)\times nm$ matrix $A$ of zeros and ones: row $i$ of the first block sums the entries of row $i$ of $P$, row $j$ of the second block sums column $j$. With row-major stacking, $p_{(i-1)m+j}=P_{i,j}$, one has $A=\begin{pmatrix}I_n\otimes\mathbb 1_m^\top\\ \mathbb 1_n^\top\otimes I_m\end{pmatrix}$, and the problem is $\min\{c^\top p:\ Ap=(a,b),\ p\ge0\}$ with $c$ the stacked costs (CompOT §3.1, p. 42 (38); the formula printed there has a stacking error, see §10). One constraint is redundant: the rows of $A$ in the first block and in the second block both sum to $\mathbb 1_{nm}^\top$. So $\mathrm{rank}\,A=n+m-1$, which is the $n+m-1$ of §2.

The dual, in brief: prices that certify optimality

Lesson 1 certified an assignment with numbers $u_i,v_j$ such that $u_i+v_j\le C_{i,j}$, with equality on the matched pairs. The same certificate works for couplings, and it is the dual of the linear program; Lesson 4 §1–§2 develops it.

Proposition (weak duality and complementary slackness)CompOT Props. 2.4, 3.2–3.3, pp. 27, 45–46 (23, 41–42); OT4ML Ch. 5

If $P\in\Couplings(a,b)$ and $u\in\R^n$, $v\in\R^m$ satisfy $u_i+v_j\le C_{i,j}$ for all $(i,j)$, then $\dotp CP\ge\dotp ua+\dotp vb$. If moreover $u_i+v_j=C_{i,j}$ wherever $P_{i,j}\gt0$ (complementary slackness), then $P$ is optimal and $(u,v)$ maximises $\dotp ua+\dotp vb$ under the constraint. The maxima and minima are equal (strong duality), and optimal pairs always satisfy complementary slackness.

The first part is one line: $\dotp CP=\sum_{i,j}C_{i,j}P_{i,j}\ge\sum_{i,j}(u_i+v_j)P_{i,j}=\dotp ua+\dotp vb$, using the marginals; equality holds exactly under complementary slackness. Strong duality is the linear-programming duality theorem (CompOT cites Bertsimas–Tsitsiklis, Thm. 4.4). An economist’s reading (CompOT Remark 2.21): $u_i$ and $v_j$ are prices at which a carrier offers to buy the goods at $x_i$ and sell them at $y_j$; the constraint says the carrier is competitive with every shipment, and at the optimum its revenue equals the shipping bill.

The network simplex

The simplex method walks from vertex to vertex of the polytope, lowering the cost at each step. On the transportation polytope, every step is a graph operation (Dantzig 1951; OT4ML §3.2, p. 62 (54); CompOT §3.5, pp. 49–53 (45–49)).

Algorithm (transportation (network) simplex)OT4ML §3.2; CompOT §3.5

  1. Start from a basis: a spanning tree $\mathcal T$ of $n+m-1$ cells carrying a feasible coupling, for instance the north-west corner staircase.
  2. Price. Solve $u_i+v_j=C_{i,j}$ on the cells of $\mathcal T$, with $u_1=0$. There are $n+m-1$ equations and $n+m$ unknowns, so the solution is unique once $u_1$ is fixed: walk the tree from its root. Compute the reduced costs $r_{i,j}=C_{i,j}-u_i-v_j$; they vanish on $\mathcal T$.
  3. Test. If every $r_{i,j}\ge0$, the pair $(u,v)$ is dual feasible and complementary to $P$, so $P$ is optimal. Stop.
  4. Enter a cell with $r_{i,j}\lt0$. Adding it to the tree closes a unique cycle, which alternates between cells that gain and cells that lose mass.
  5. Pivot. Push the largest amount $\theta$ that keeps the losing cells non-negative. The cost changes by $\theta\,r_{i,j}\le0$. A losing cell that reaches zero leaves the tree. Go to step 2.

The cost change is exact: along the cycle the costs of the basic cells cancel in $u_i+v_j$, leaving only the reduced cost of the entering cell (CompOT §3.5.3). When $\theta=0$ the pivot is degenerate: the tree changes but the plan does not. Degenerate pivots are the rule for assignment problems, and a careless choice of pivots can cycle forever. The page’s implementation uses the most negative reduced cost (Dantzig’s rule) and switches to the smallest-index rule (Bland’s rule, which provably terminates) after a run of degenerate pivots. On adversarial inputs the simplex method can take exponentially many pivots. Strongly polynomial minimum-cost-flow algorithms exist (Orlin 1997), but in practice network simplex codes are the standard exact solvers for problems up to a few thousand points; POT’s ot.emd, used for the checks on these pages, is one.

Interior points, and a first look at entropy

Interior-point methods approach the same linear program from inside the polytope. They minimise $\dotp CP-\varepsilon\sum_{i,j}\log P_{i,j}$ over $P\mathbb 1=a$, $P^\top\mathbb 1=b$ (OT4ML (3.5), p. 63 (55)) for a decreasing sequence of $\varepsilon$, with Newton steps. The minimisers form the central path, which tends to a solution of the linear program as $\varepsilon\to0$. The method is robustly polynomial, but each Newton step solves a linear system, which is expensive for dense couplings, and it never returns an exactly sparse plan. Lab 1 shows the path approaching the vertex linearly in $\varepsilon$. Sinkhorn’s algorithm (Lesson 7) also keeps every entry positive, but with a fixed $\varepsilon$ that defines a different problem, the entropic one; its minimiser approached the vertex exponentially fast in Lab 1.

§6The general Kantorovich problem

Couplings of arbitrary measures

For measures that are not discrete — densities, or mixtures of atoms and densities — a coupling is a probability measure on the product space whose two marginals are $\al$ and $\be$. The marginals are push-forwards (Lesson 1 §4) by the coordinate projections $P_\X(x,y)=x$ and $P_\Y(x,y)=y$.

Definition (marginals and couplings)OT4ML Defs. 3.25–3.26, pp. 63–64 (55–56); CompOT Remark 2.13, pp. 21–22 (17–18); StatOT p. 17 (11)

$$\Couplings(\al,\be)=\big\{\pi\in\Pc(\X\times\Y):\ (P_\X)_\sharp\pi=\al,\ (P_\Y)_\sharp\pi=\be\big\},$$ that is $\pi(A\times\Y)=\al(A)$ and $\pi(\X\times B)=\be(B)$ for all measurable $A,B$; equivalently $\int f(x)\dd\pi(x,y)=\int f\dd\al$ and $\int g(y)\dd\pi(x,y)=\int g\dd\be$ for bounded continuous $f,g$.

  • Probability. $\pi\in\Couplings(\al,\be)$ is the law of a pair $(X,Y)$ with $X\sim\al$ and $Y\sim\be$; the coupling is the dependence between them (OT4ML Remark 3.27).
  • Discrete measures. If $\al=\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i}$ and $\be=\sum_jb_j\delta_{y_j}$, every coupling is $\sum_{i,j}P_{i,j}\delta_{(x_i,y_j)}$ with $P\in\Couplings(a,b)$: the discrete problem is a special case, not an approximation.
  • Maps. A map $T$ with $T_\sharp\al=\be$ gives the graph coupling $(\Id,T)_\sharp\al$, characterised by $\int h(x,y)\dd\pi=\int h(x,T(x))\dd\al(x)$. Monge maps are exactly the couplings concentrated on a graph.
  • Semi-discrete. If $\al$ has a density and $\be$ is discrete, a coupling lives on the slices $\X\times\{y_j\}$. When an optimal one is a map, the slices come from a partition of $\X$ into cells — the Laguerre cells of Lesson 4 §5.

The product $\al\otimes\be$, defined by $\int h\dd(\al\otimes\be)=\int\!\!\int h(x,y)\dd\be(y)\dd\al(x)$, is always a coupling. As in the discrete case it is optimal only if all couplings are: for compact spaces and a continuous cost, this happens exactly when $c(x,y)=u(x)+v(y)$ on $\supp\al\times\supp\be$ (OT4ML Prop. 3.29, p. 64 (56)). Continuity matters: for $\al=\be=U[0,1]$ and $c(x,y)=\mathbf 1_{x=y}$, the product has cost $0$ (the diagonal has product measure zero), while the identity coupling costs $1$.

A whole face of optimal couplings: book-shifting again

Lesson 1’s book-shifting example (Lesson 1 §5) has a clean coupling explanation (OT4ML Remark 3.30, p. 65 (57)). Let $\al=U[0,2]$, $\be=U[1,3]$ and $c(x,y)=\abs{x-y}$. For every coupling, $$\int\abs{y-x}\dd\pi\ \ge\ \int(y-x)\dd\pi\ =\ \int y\dd\be-\int x\dd\al=2-1=1,$$ with equality exactly for couplings concentrated on the half-plane $\{y\ge x\}$. The set of optimal couplings is a face of $\Couplings(\al,\be)$ containing the translation, the book-shifting map and many couplings that split mass. It is the continuous version of the optimal edge of Lab 1.

The problem, and existence

Definition (Kantorovich problem)OT4ML Def. 3.31, p. 66 (58); CompOT (2.15), p. 22 (18); StatOT (KOT), p. 17 (11)

For a Borel cost $c:\X\times\Y\to[0,+\infty]$, $$\begin{aligned}\mathcal L_c(\al,\be)&=\inf_{\pi\in\Couplings(\al,\be)}\int c(x,y)\dd\pi(x,y)\\&=\inf_{X\sim\al,\,Y\sim\be}\E\big[c(X,Y)\big]\ \in[0,+\infty].\end{aligned}$$

It is an infinite-dimensional linear program. It is jointly convex in $(\al,\be)$ and concave in $c$, with the same proof as in the discrete case (OT4ML Prop. 3.32). Its decisive gain over Monge is existence.

Proposition (existence for lower semicontinuous costs)OT4ML Prop. 3.34, p. 66 (58); StatOT Prop. 1.1, p. 17 (11)

If $\X,\Y$ are Polish spaces (complete separable metric spaces, e.g. closed subsets of $\R^d$) and $c\ge0$ is lower semicontinuous, the infimum is attained. In particular it is attained for every continuous cost on compact spaces.

Proof sketch (the direct method: a minimising sequence has a convergent subsequence, and the cost can only drop in the limit)

Non-empty: $\al\otimes\be$ is a coupling. Tight: choose compact sets $K,K'$ with $\al(K),\be(K')\ge1-\frac\varepsilon2$; then every coupling gives $K\times K'$ mass at least $1-\varepsilon$. By Prokhorov’s theorem, a minimising sequence $\pi_k$ has a subsequence converging weakly to some $\pi$. Closed: the projections are continuous, so the marginals of $\pi$ are the limits of those of $\pi_k$, that is $\al$ and $\be$. Lower semicontinuous: for $c\ge0$ lower semicontinuous, $\pi\mapsto\int c\dd\pi$ is weakly lower semicontinuous (Portmanteau). So $\int c\dd\pi\le\liminf_k\int c\dd\pi_k=\mathcal L_c(\al,\be)$. $\square$ Lesson 3 §4–§5 studies weak convergence in detail.

This is where the Monge problem breaks: a weak limit of graph couplings need not be a graph coupling. In Lesson 1’s segment example (Lesson 1 §5), maps that alternate between the two target segments on finer and finer strips converge to a coupling that splits every point in two. The Kantorovich infimum is attained by that limit, which no map realises.

Beyond the course: why exactly this relaxation?

On compact spaces with a continuous cost, $\mathcal L_c$ is the largest functional of $(\al,\be)$ that is jointly convex, weakly lower semicontinuous, and at most $c(x,y)$ on pairs of Dirac masses (OT4ML Thm. 3.35, p. 67 (59), after Savaré and Sodini). So Kantorovich’s problem is not one relaxation among many: it is the convex, stable extension of the ground cost to probability measures.

§7When the relaxation loses nothing: Monge = Kantorovich

Three cases where an optimal coupling is a map

  • Uniform discrete weights (§4): Birkhoff–von Neumann gives an optimal permutation.
  • One dimension, atomless source: the quantile map of Lesson 1 is optimal among couplings (below, and OT4ML Thm. 3.39).
  • Quadratic cost, source with a density: Brenier’s theorem.

Corollary (Monge–Kantorovich equivalence under Brenier)OT4ML Cor. 3.36, p. 67 (59); compact notes Cor. 3.27; CompOT Thm. 2.1, p. 31 (27)

Let $\al,\be\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$, $\al$ absolutely continuous, and $c=\norm{x-y}^2$. If $T=\nabla\varphi$ is the Brenier map, then $(\Id,T)_\sharp\al$ is the unique optimal coupling. In particular the Monge and Kantorovich values are equal.

The proof belongs to Lesson 4 §4. It shows that every optimal coupling is concentrated on the graph of the subdifferential $\partial\varphi$ of a convex function. A convex function is differentiable almost everywhere, hence $\al$-almost everywhere when $\al$ has a density, so the graph of $\partial\varphi$ is $\al$-almost surely the graph of $\nabla\varphi$. Where $\varphi$ is not differentiable, mass can split: $\delta_0\mapsto\frac12(\delta_{-1}+\delta_1)$ is carried by $\partial\abs\cdot(0)=[-1,1]$, but by no map (OT4ML Remark 3.37). In probabilistic terms: if $(X,Y)$ is optimal then $Y=T(X)$ almost surely. The analogy with Birkhoff–von Neumann is imperfect: Birkhoff–von Neumann gives an optimal map, while Brenier gives uniqueness (OT4ML Remark 3.38).

Without these hypotheses: atoms make the Monge value jump

For atomless sources and continuous costs on compact spaces, the infimum over maps equals the minimum over couplings, even when no map attains it (Pratelli 2007; OT4ML Prop. 4.15, p. 79 (71), Lesson 3 §2). Atoms break this. Take $$\begin{gathered}\al=\tfrac14\delta_{-1}+\tfrac14\delta_{+1}+\tfrac12\delta_0,\qquad \be=\tfrac12\delta_{-1}+\tfrac12\delta_{+1},\\ c(x,y)=\abs{x-y}^2.\end{gathered}$$ A map must send the atom at $0$ as a whole to $-1$ or to $+1$. Say to $-1$: then $\be$’s atom at $-1$ is full, and both $\pm1$ atoms of $\al$ must go to $+1$. The cost is $\frac12\cdot1+\frac14\cdot4=1.5$. A coupling splits the atom at $0$ in two and leaves the outer atoms in place, for a cost of $\frac12\cdot1=0.5$. Maps exist, and still the Monge value is three times the Kantorovich value.

Lab 7Monge against Kantorovich when the source has atoms

Question: if the atom at $0$ is replaced by $k$ smaller atoms spread over $[-w,w]$, how do the Monge and Kantorovich values behave as $k$ grows?

Setting: $\al_k=\frac14\delta_{-1}+\frac14\delta_{+1}+\frac12\cdot\frac1k\sum_{l=1}^k\delta_{z_l}$ with $z_l$ equispaced on $[-w,w]$ ($z=0$ for $k=1$), $\be=\frac12(\delta_{-1}+\delta_{+1})$, $c=\abs{x-y}^2$. The Kantorovich value comes from the network simplex. The Monge value comes from an exact formula: a map sends $e\in\{0,1,2\}$ outer atoms and $j$ central atoms to $-1$, with $\frac e4+\frac j{2k}=\frac12$, and for fixed $(e,j)$ the cheapest map sends the $j$ leftmost central atoms. The formula agrees with a brute-force search over all $2^{k+2}$ maps for $k\le10$ (largest difference: 0 (exactly)).

What we found (measured, $w=0.2$)

  • $k=1$: Monge 1.500000, Kantorovich 0.500000. The example above.
  • Even $k$: the two values coincide, 0.320000 for $k=2$, 0.397037 for $k=10$, 0.404444 for $k=40$. Half of the central atoms can go left and half right, which is what the optimal coupling does.
  • Odd $k$: the Monge value stays near 1.5 — 1.508333 for $k=9$, 1.507018 for $k=39$, still 1.5067 for $k=999$ — while the Kantorovich value is 0.397222, 0.404453 and 0.4066. With an odd number of central atoms of mass $\frac1{2k}$, no map can send exactly half of the central mass, $\frac14$, to each side, so one outer atom must cross to the far side.
  • Going from $k$ to $k+1$ changes $\al_k$ very little (atoms of mass $\frac1{2k}$ move by about $\frac wk$). From $k=9$ to $k=10$ the Monge value drops from 1.508333 to 0.397037, while the Kantorovich value moves from 0.397222 to 0.397037. The Kantorovich value tends to $\frac12\cdot\frac{1-(1-w)^3}{3w}=0.406667$, the value for a uniform central part. The Monge value is not a continuous function of the measures. The Kantorovich value is, as Lesson 3 §5 shows.

One dimension, with atoms: couple equal quantile levels

In Lesson 1 §6 the 1D Monge map $F_\be^{-1}\circ F_\al$ required an atomless source. The atoms were a limitation of maps, not of optimality.

Theorem (one-dimensional Kantorovich solution)OT4ML Thm. 3.39, p. 68 (60); compact notes Thm. 3.28, p. 12

Let $\al,\be$ be probability measures on $\R$, $h\ge0$ convex, $c(x,y)=h(x-y)$, and assume $\int_0^1h\big(F_\al^{-1}(r)-F_\be^{-1}(r)\big)\dd r\lt\infty$. Then the quantile coupling $$\pi^\star=\big(F_\al^{-1},F_\be^{-1}\big)_\sharp\Leb_{[0,1]},$$ the law of $\big(F_\al^{-1}(U),F_\be^{-1}(U)\big)$ with $U$ uniform on $[0,1]$, is optimal. So $\mathcal L_c(\al,\be)=\int_0^1h\big(F_\al^{-1}(r)-F_\be^{-1}(r)\big)\dd r$, and for $h=\abs\cdot^p$ with $p\ge1$ this is the formula for $W_p^p$ of Lesson 1, now for arbitrary measures.

Proof idea (OT4ML p. 68): on two ordered grids the uncrossing step of §3 turns any plan into the ordered one without increasing the cost. For general measures, lift any coupling to a coupling of two uniform variables, approximate the (truncated) quantile functions by step functions, apply the discrete statement, and pass to the limit by dominated convergence. An atom of $\al$ of mass $m$ at $x$ corresponds to an interval of quantile levels of length $m$, which the coupling spreads over the matching levels of $\be$.

Example. $\al=\frac12\delta_0+\frac12U[1,2]$ and $\be=U[0,2]$, $c=\abs{x-y}^2$. The levels $r\in(0,\frac12]$ go from the atom at $0$ to $F_\be^{-1}(r)=2r\in(0,1]$: the atom is spread uniformly over $[0,1]$. The levels $r\in(\frac12,1]$ go from $1+2(r-\frac12)=2r$ to $2r$ and cost nothing. Hence $\mathcal L_c(\al,\be)=\int_0^{1/2}(2r)^2\dd r=\frac16$, and no map attains it, since the atom must split.

§8$c$-cyclical monotonicity: what optimal plans look like

The exchange test

Restrict an optimal plan to finitely many points of its support: the restriction must still be optimal for those points, otherwise exchanging targets among them would lower the cost. This is the finite test that Lesson 1 used for permutations (Lesson 1 §7).

Definition (c-cyclical monotonicity)OT4ML Def. 3.45, p. 72 (64); StatOT Def. 1.9, p. 31 (25)

A set $\Gamma\subset\X\times\Y$ is $c$-cyclically monotone if, for every finite family $(x_1,y_1),\dots,(x_k,y_k)\in\Gamma$ and every permutation $\sigma$, $$\sum_{i=1}^kc(x_i,y_i)\le\sum_{i=1}^kc(x_i,y_{\sigma(i)}).$$ Since permutations are products of cycles, it suffices to test $\sigma(i)=i+1$ (with $y_{k+1}=y_1$).

Theorem (optimal plans are c-cyclically monotone)OT4ML Thm. 3.46, p. 72 (64); StatOT Prop. 1.11, p. 33 (27)

If $c$ is continuous and $\mathcal L_c(\al,\be)\lt\infty$, the support of every optimal coupling is $c$-cyclically monotone.

Proof idea: if the inequality failed for some points of the support, it would fail, by continuity, for all points in small neighbourhoods of them, and these neighbourhoods carry positive mass. Removing a little mass from each neighbourhood product $U_i\times V_i$ and reinserting it on $U_i\times V_{\sigma(i)}$ keeps the marginals and lowers the cost — a contradiction.

For discrete problems it is also sufficient

For discrete measures, the exchange test characterises optimality, and it can be checked on a graph. The residual graph of a coupling $P$ has an arc $x_i\to y_j$ of weight $C_{i,j}$ for every pair (mass can always be added there), and an arc $y_j\to x_i$ of weight $-C_{i,j}$ whenever $P_{i,j}\gt0$ (mass can be removed there). A cycle alternates the two kinds of arcs, and pushing $\theta$ units of mass around it keeps both marginals.

Proposition (discrete optimality by cycles)classical (min-cost flow; Klein 1967); stated for this page, proved below

A coupling $P\in\Couplings(a,b)$ is optimal if and only if its residual graph has no cycle of negative total weight, if and only if its support $\{(x_i,y_j):P_{i,j}\gt0\}$ is $c$-cyclically monotone.

Proof (the difference of two couplings is a sum of cycles)

A residual cycle $x_{i_1}\to y_{j_1}\to x_{i_2}\to y_{j_2}\to\cdots\to x_{i_1}$ uses the added pairs $(i_1,j_1),(i_2,j_2),\dots$ and the support pairs $(i_2,j_1),(i_3,j_2),\dots$. Its weight is $\sum_kC_{i_k,j_k}-\sum_kC_{i_{k+1},j_k}$. It is negative exactly when the support points $(x_{i_{k+1}},y_{j_k})$ violate the exchange test for the cyclic shift of their targets. So “no negative cycle” and “$c$-cyclically monotone support” are the same statement.

If there is a negative cycle, pushing a small mass around it lowers the cost, and $P$ is not optimal. Conversely, suppose $P$ is not optimal and let $P'$ be a coupling with $\dotp C{P'}\lt\dotp CP$. The matrix $D=P'-P$ has zero row and column sums. Read it as a flow: positive entries are arcs $x_i\to y_j$, negative entries are arcs $y_j\to x_i$, and the latter only occur where $P_{i,j}\gt0$. A flow with zero balance at every node decomposes into cycles with positive amounts (flow decomposition), all made of residual arcs of $P$. Since $\dotp CD\lt0$ is the sum of the cycle weights times their amounts, one of the cycles is negative. $\square$

Cancelling negative cycles until none is left is an algorithm in its own right. Choosing at each step the cycle of minimum mean weight (Karp’s algorithm, as in Lesson 1) makes it strongly polynomial (Goldberg and Tarjan 1989).

What it says about maps

  • Quadratic cost. If an optimal plan is induced by a map $T$, the two-point test with $c=\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$ gives $\dotp{T(x)-T(x')}{x-x'}\ge0$: optimal maps are monotone (OT4ML p. 72 (64)). The converse fails in dimension $d\ge2$: a small rotation is monotone but not a gradient (Lesson 1 §7). The full cyclical condition is what forces a gradient of a convex function (Rockafellar’s theorem, StatOT Thm. 1.10, proved in its Appendix A; Lesson 4 §4 compares this route with the dual one).
  • The line. For $\abs{x-y}^p$ with $p\gt1$, a reversed pair $x\lt x'$, $T(x)\gt T(x')$ violates the two-point test by strict convexity, so every optimal map is non-decreasing. For $p=1$ uncrossing is not strict and other optimal maps and plans exist (§6, book-shifting).

§9Using a plan: interpolation, barycentric maps, applications

Interpolating along a plan

A coupling says which pieces of mass travel together. Moving each piece along a straight line at constant speed gives an interpolation between $\al$ and $\be$ (sheet Exercise 4; notebook 1-linprog): $$\begin{gathered}\al_t=(P_t)_\sharp\pi,\qquad P_t(x,y)=(1-t)x+ty,\\ \al_t=\sum_{i,j}P_{i,j}\,\delta_{(1-t)x_i+ty_j}\ \text{ for discrete measures}.\end{gathered}$$ Then $\al_0=\al$ and $\al_1=\be$, because $P_0$ and $P_1$ are the two projections. For an optimal plan and the quadratic cost, this is McCann’s displacement interpolation of Lesson 1 §9, extended to plans that split mass, and Lesson 3 §3 shows that it is a geodesic for $W_2$.

  • Number of atoms. $\al_t$ has at most as many atoms as $P$ has non-zero entries: at most $nm$, and at most $n+m-1$ for a vertex.
  • No collision before the end. Along a quadratic optimal plan, two different pieces never meet for $0\lt t\lt1$. By the two-point test, $\dotp{x-x'}{y-y'}\ge0$ for pairs $(x,y),(x',y')$ of the support, hence $$\begin{aligned}\norm{(1-t)(x-x')+t(y-y')}^2&\ge(1-t)^2\norm{x-x'}^2\\&\quad+t^2\norm{y-y'}^2\ \gt\ 0\end{aligned}$$ unless the pairs coincide. So $\al_t$ has exactly as many atoms as the support for $0\lt t\lt1$; at $t=0$ pieces of a split source coincide, and at $t=1$ pieces of a merged target do.

Barycentric maps and applications

A plan that splits mass does not define a map, but it defines an average destination for each source, the barycentric projection $\bar T(x_i)=\frac1{a_i}\sum_jP_{i,j}\,y_j$. It is the standard way to turn a plan into a map, and it reappears with entropic plans in Lesson 7 (OT4ML Def. 12.32). The book gives three applications of discrete couplings (OT4ML Examples 3.22–3.24, pp. 61–62 (53–54)):

  • Domain adaptation. A plan between labelled source samples and unlabelled target samples transfers labels by the backward barycentric rule $\tilde y_j=\frac1{b_j}\sum_iP_{i,j}\,y_i^s$ (OT domain adaptation, JDOT).
  • Visual distributions. Colour histograms and image descriptors compared by the Earth Mover’s Distance (Rubner et al.); the colour-transfer demo of Lesson 1 is a map version of this.
  • Single-cell population dynamics. Cells sequenced at successive times are atoms of empirical measures. A plan $P^{[k]}$ between consecutive snapshots, normalised row by row into $K^{[k]}_{i,j}=P^{[k]}_{i,j}/\sum_{j'}P^{[k]}_{i,j'}$, gives transition probabilities from ancestors to descendants; products of these matrices give lineage probabilities. Waddington-OT (Schiebinger et al. 2019) adds growth and unbalanced transport. This is the “application in genomics” of Lesson 8; the notebook kantorovich-waddington-ot (Fig. 3.7) displays the snapshots.

Exercise 1 — 1D discrete ⇒ optimal Kantorovich plan§3 · checked

$n=m=3$, two 1D discrete measures with sorted supports and weights $a=(1.1,0.8,1.1)$, $b=(1,1,1)$. For $c(x,y)=\abs{x-y}^2$, compute the optimal coupling $P\in\R_+^{3\times3}$.

Hint 1
The positions are not given, and they are not needed: which result gives an optimal plan from the order of the points alone?
Hint 2
Run the north-west corner rule: fill $(1,1)$ with $\min(a_1,b_1)$, then move right or down according to which marginal is exhausted.
Solution

The weights are not probability vectors, but both total $3$, which is all that $\Couplings(a,b)$ needs. By the weighted sweep (§3), the north-west plan on sorted supports is optimal for every convex $h(x-y)$, whatever the positions. Sweep: $P_{1,1}=\min(1.1,1)=1$ leaves $0.1$ in row 1, so $P_{1,2}=0.1$; column 2 still needs $0.9$, and row 2 gives $P_{2,2}=0.8$; column 2 still needs $0.1$, from row 3: $P_{3,2}=0.1$; finally $P_{3,3}=1$. $$P=\begin{pmatrix}1&0.1&0\\0&0.8&0\\0&0.1&1\end{pmatrix}.$$ It has $5=n+m-1$ non-zero entries. If the points are distinct, $\abs\cdot^2$ is strictly convex and this is the unique optimal plan (§3). The mass of the heavy outer atoms spills into the middle target, which receives from all three sources.

the page’s sweep gives $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1.0 & 0.1 & 0.0 \\ 0.0 & 0.8 & 0.0 \\ 0.0 & 0.1 & 1.0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$; on the supports $x=(0,1,2)$, $y=(0.5,1.5,2.5)$ its cost minus the simplex optimum is 0 (exactly).

Exercise 2 — Parametric 1D cases§3 · checked

(1) $a=(1+\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon)$, $b=(1,1)$, $\abs\varepsilon\le1$: compute the optimal $P\in\R_+^{2\times2}$ according to the sign of $\varepsilon$. (2) $a=(1+\varepsilon,1+\eta,1-\varepsilon-\eta)$, $b=(1,1,1)$ with $1+\varepsilon,1+\eta,1-\varepsilon-\eta\ge0$: compute the optimal monotone $P\in\R_+^{3\times3}$ in the regimes (i) $\varepsilon\gt0,\eta\gt0$ and (ii) $\varepsilon\lt0,\eta\gt0$.

Hint 1
Same tool as Exercise 1: the answer is the north-west sweep, and only the order in which rows and columns are exhausted depends on the parameters.
Hint 2
In (2)(ii), after $P_{1,1}=1+\varepsilon$ and $P_{2,1}=-\varepsilon$, row 2 still holds $1+\eta+\varepsilon$. Compare it with the capacity $1$ of column 2.
Solution

(1) If $\varepsilon\ge0$, row 1 overflows column 1: $P=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&\varepsilon\\0&1-\varepsilon\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. If $\varepsilon\le0$, row 1 is exhausted first and row 2 completes column 1: $P=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1+\varepsilon&0\\-\varepsilon&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. Both formulas give the identity at $\varepsilon=0$.

(2)(i) $\varepsilon,\eta\gt0$: $P_{1,1}=1$, $P_{1,2}=\varepsilon$; column 2 needs $1-\varepsilon$ from row 2, whose remaining $\varepsilon+\eta$ goes to column 3, which row 3 completes: $$P=\begin{pmatrix}1&\varepsilon&0\\0&1-\varepsilon&\varepsilon+\eta\\0&0&1-\varepsilon-\eta\end{pmatrix}.$$ Feasibility requires $1-\varepsilon\ge0$, which follows from $1-\varepsilon-\eta\ge0$ and $\eta\gt0$.

(2)(ii) $\varepsilon\lt0\lt\eta$: $P_{1,1}=1+\varepsilon$ and $P_{2,1}=-\varepsilon$. Row 2 has $1+\eta+\varepsilon$ left, and the answer depends on the sign of $\varepsilon+\eta$, which the sheet does not separate: $$\varepsilon+\eta\ge0:\quad P=\begin{pmatrix}1+\varepsilon&0&0\\-\varepsilon&1&\varepsilon+\eta\\0&0&1-\varepsilon-\eta\end{pmatrix},$$ $$\varepsilon+\eta\lt0:\quad P=\begin{pmatrix}1+\varepsilon&0&0\\-\varepsilon&1+\varepsilon+\eta&0\\0&-(\varepsilon+\eta)&1\end{pmatrix}.$$ In the first case row 2 overflows into column 3. In the second, row 2 cannot fill column 2, and row 3 completes it. At $\varepsilon+\eta=0$ both formulas agree.

on 400 random pairs $(\varepsilon,\eta)$ in the feasible region with random sorted supports, the monotone plan exceeds the simplex optimum by at most 1.1e−16.

Exercise 3 — Couplings in basic cases§1, §6

(a) What are the couplings between $\delta_x$ and $\delta_y$? (b) Between $\delta_x$ and $\frac12(\delta_y+\delta_z)$, $y\ne z$? (c) If $\al,\be$ are finite positive measures with different total masses, what is $\Couplings(\al,\be)$?

Hint 1
Use the set form of the marginal constraints: $\pi(A\times\Y)=\al(A)$ and $\pi(\X\times B)=\be(B)$. Take $A=\X\setminus\{x\}$.
Hint 2
For (c), evaluate both constraints on the whole space.
Solution

(a) $\pi\big((\X\setminus\{x\})\times\Y\big)=\delta_x(\X\setminus\{x\})=0$, so $\pi$ lives on $\{x\}\times\Y$; likewise on $\X\times\{y\}$. So $\pi$ is concentrated on the point $(x,y)$ and $\Couplings(\delta_x,\delta_y)=\{\delta_{(x,y)}\}$. More generally, the only coupling of $\delta_x$ with any $\be$ is $\delta_x\otimes\be$: a single source point must send its mass everywhere $\be$ requires.

(b) By the same argument $\pi$ lives on $\{x\}\times\{y,z\}$, and the second marginal fixes the masses: $\Couplings=\{\frac12\delta_{(x,y)}+\frac12\delta_{(x,z)}\}$. This coupling splits the atom at $x$. No map achieves it, which is Lesson 1’s first failure of Monge, repaired.

(c) For $\pi\in\Couplings(\al,\be)$, $\pi(\X\times\Y)=\al(\X)$ and $\pi(\X\times\Y)=\be(\Y)$. Different masses make this impossible: $\Couplings(\al,\be)=\emptyset$. Balanced transport requires equal masses; the unbalanced transport of Lesson 7 relaxes that.

Exercise 4 — Discrete $\al_t$ from a coupling§9 · Lab 9

$\al_0=\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i}$, $\al_1=\sum_jb_j\delta_{y_j}$, $\pi=\sum_{i,j}P_{i,j}\delta_{(x_i,y_j)}$ an optimal coupling, $P_t(x,y)=(1-t)x+ty$ and $\al_t=(P_t)_\sharp\pi$. (a) Show $\al_0=(P_0)_\sharp\pi$ and $\al_1=(P_1)_\sharp\pi$. (b) Show that $\al_t$ is discrete and bound its number of atoms; deduce $\#\supp(\al_t)\le nm$. Bonus: why can one choose an optimal coupling with at most $n+m-1$ non-zero entries? (c) Give $\al_t$ explicitly in terms of $P$. (d) If $n=m$, when can one choose an optimal coupling such that $\al_t$ has exactly $n$ atoms for all $t$? Give an example.

Hint 1
Push-forward of a discrete measure: $T_\sharp\sum_kw_k\delta_{z_k}=\sum_kw_k\delta_{T(z_k)}$.
Hint 2
For (d): a coupling with exactly $n$ non-zero entries and positive marginals on $n$ columns is a permutation plan. For the quadratic cost, can two pieces of an optimal plan collide at some $0\lt t\lt1$? (§9.)
Solution

(a) $P_0(x,y)=x$ and $P_1(x,y)=y$ are the two coordinate projections, so $(P_0)_\sharp\pi$ and $(P_1)_\sharp\pi$ are the marginals of $\pi$, namely $\al_0$ and $\al_1$.

(b)–(c) By Hint 1, $\al_t=\sum_{i,j}P_{i,j}\,\delta_{(1-t)x_i+ty_j}$, a discrete measure whose atoms are among the points $(1-t)x_i+ty_j$ with $P_{i,j}\gt0$. So $\#\supp(\al_t)\le\#\{(i,j):P_{i,j}\gt0\}\le nm$; fewer if some points coincide. Bonus: the linear program attains its minimum at a vertex of $\Couplings(a,b)$ (§2), and the support graph of a vertex is a forest on $n+m$ nodes, with at most $n+m-1$ edges. Such an optimal plan gives at most $n+m-1$ atoms.

(d) Assume the quadratic cost and distinct points. Then $\al_t$ can have exactly $n$ atoms for every $t$ if and only if some optimal coupling is a permutation plan, $P_{i,\sigma(i)}=a_i=b_{\sigma(i)}$. If: such a plan gives the $n$ points $(1-t)x_i+ty_{\sigma(i)}$. They are distinct at $t=0$ and $t=1$, and also for $0\lt t\lt1$ by the no-collision inequality of §9. Only if: an optimal plan with more than $n$ non-zero entries gives more than $n$ distinct atoms for $0\lt t\lt1$, again by §9, and a plan with exactly $n$ entries is a permutation plan (each column needs an entry). A permutation plan requires $b$ to be a rearrangement of $a$. Uniform weights always allow it: Birkhoff–von Neumann provides an optimal permutation (§4). Example: $a=b=\frac1n\mathbb 1$ with the optimal $\sigma$, $\al_t=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{(1-t)x_i+ty_{\sigma(i)}}$.

on the 24 + 24 uniform clouds of Fig. 3.1, $\al_t$ has 24 distinct atoms for every $t=0,0.05,\dots,0.95$; on the 12 → 8 weighted clouds it has 19 atoms at $t=\frac12$ (Lab 9).

Exercise 5 — When does a finite-cost coupling exist? (optional)§4 · checked

$\al=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$, $\be=\frac1n\sum_j\delta_{y_j}$, and $C\in(\R\cup\{+\infty\})^{n\times n}$, where $+\infty$ forbids a pair. (i) For $n=2$, determine exactly when a coupling of finite cost exists, and give one. (ii) With $E=\{(i,j):C_{i,j}\lt\infty\}$, prove: a finite-cost coupling exists $\iff$ there is a permutation $\sigma$ with $(i,\sigma(i))\in E$ for all $i$.

Hint 1
A finite-cost coupling puts zero mass on forbidden cells. Multiply it by $n$.
Hint 2
Birkhoff–von Neumann: $nP=\sum_k\lambda_kP_{\sigma_k}$ with $\lambda_k\gt0$. Which cells can the $\sigma_k$ use?
Solution

The sheet gives a solution; it is correct, and here it is with two remarks. (i) The two permutation supports are $\{(1,1),(2,2)\}$ and $\{(1,2),(2,1)\}$. A finite-cost coupling exists if and only if one of them is entirely finite, and then $\frac12P_\sigma$ on that support works. The sheet lists two obstructions, an entirely forbidden row or column, and one forbidden cell in each support. For $n=2$ they are the same: two cells taken one from each support always share a row or a column (the four possible pairs are row 1, row 2, column 1 and column 2).

(ii) ($\Leftarrow$) $P_{i,\sigma(i)}=\frac1n$ is a coupling that uses only allowed cells. ($\Rightarrow$) If $P$ is a finite-cost coupling, $Q=nP$ is bistochastic with $Q_{i,j}=0$ on forbidden cells. Write $Q=\sum_k\lambda_kP_{\sigma_k}$ with $\lambda_k\gt0$ (Birkhoff–von Neumann decomposition, §4). For a forbidden cell, $0=Q_{i,j}=\sum_k\lambda_k(P_{\sigma_k})_{i,j}$ is a sum of non-negative terms, so every $\sigma_k$ avoids it. Any $\sigma_k$ is an allowed permutation. Remark. The condition “the allowed cells contain a perfect matching” is equivalent, by Hall’s theorem, to $\abs{N(I)}\ge\abs I$ for every set $I$ of rows, where $N(I)$ is the set of columns allowed for some row of $I$: a finite-cost coupling exists exactly when no group of sources is confined to fewer targets than its size.

on 500 random forbidden patterns ($2\le n\le7$), “there is a perfect matching in the allowed cells” (Kuhn’s algorithm) and “the linear program with cost 1 on forbidden cells has value 0” (simplex) agree 500 times out of 500; 294 patterns admit a finite-cost coupling. SciPy matching and HiGHS feasibility give the same answers on 300 other patterns.

§14Cheat sheet

Couplings$\Couplings(a,b)=\{P\ge0:P\mathbb 1_m=a,P^\top\mathbb 1_n=b\}$; dimension $(n-1)(m-1)$; $a\otimes b$ always feasible; $P\in\Couplings(a,b)\iff P^\top\in\Couplings(b,a)$.
Kantorovich LP$\mathrm L_C(a,b)=\min_{\Couplings(a,b)}\dotp CP$; convex in $(a,b)$, concave in $C$; $\le$ Monge value (maps give couplings $a_i\mathbf 1_{T(x_i)=y_j}$).
Verticessupport graph of a vertex is a forest $\Rightarrow$ at most $n+m-1$ non-zero entries; an optimal vertex exists; equality for non-degenerate data.
North-west cornerfill $\min(r_i,s_j)$, move down if the row is exhausted, else right; staircase, $\le n+m-1$ cells; ignores the cost.
Linesorted supports + north-west sweep is optimal for convex $h(x-y)$, unique if strictly convex and points distinct; $O(n\log n)$; not for concave costs.
Birkhoff–von Neumann$\mathrm{Extr}(\mathcal B_n)=$ permutation matrices; uniform weights $\Rightarrow$ an optimal permutation (Monge = Kantorovich); every $P\in\mathcal B_n$ is a mixture of $\le(n-1)^2+1$ permutations, exactly that many for generic $P$.
Rational weights$a=k/N$, $b=\ell/N$: duplicate and solve an assignment; an integral optimum $n_{i,j}/N$ exists (total unimodularity).
Duality$u_i+v_j\le C_{i,j}\Rightarrow\dotp CP\ge\dotp ua+\dotp vb$; equality iff $u_i+v_j=C_{i,j}$ on $\{P\gt0\}$; strong duality.
Network simplextree of $n+m-1$ cells; potentials $u_i+v_j=C_{i,j}$ on the tree; reduced costs $r=C-u-v$; enter $r\lt0$, push $\theta$ around the cycle, cost $+\theta r$; stop when $r\ge0$.
General problem$\Couplings(\al,\be)$: $(P_\X)_\sharp\pi=\al$, $(P_\Y)_\sharp\pi=\be$; $\mathcal L_c=\inf\int c\dd\pi=\inf\E c(X,Y)$; minimiser exists for $c\ge0$ l.s.c. on Polish spaces.
Monge = Kantorovichuniform discrete (BvN); 1D atomless (quantile map); $\norm{x-y}^2$ with $\al\ll\Leb$ (Brenier, unique plan $(\Id,\nabla\varphi)_\sharp\al$); atomless + compact: equal values (Pratelli). Atoms: gap possible (1.5 vs 0.5).
1D Kantorovich$\pi^\star=(F_\al^{-1},F_\be^{-1})_\sharp\Leb_{[0,1]}$ optimal for convex $h(x-y)$, atoms allowed; $\mathcal L_c=\int_0^1h(F_\al^{-1}-F_\be^{-1})$.
$c$-cyclical monotonicity$\sum_ic(x_i,y_i)\le\sum_ic(x_i,y_{i+1})$ on the support; optimal $\Rightarrow$ c.c.m.; discrete: optimal $\iff$ no negative cycle in the residual graph.
Interpolation$\al_t=\sum P_{i,j}\delta_{(1-t)x_i+ty_j}$; atoms $\le\#\{P\gt0\}$; no collision for $0\lt t\lt1$ along a quadratic optimal plan; barycentric map $\bar T(x_i)=\frac1{a_i}\sum_jP_{i,j}y_j$.

§15Self-test

  1. Why does the Kantorovich problem between $\delta_0$ and $\frac12(\delta_{-1}+\delta_1)$ have a solution, while the Monge problem has no admissible map?
    Answer
    A map sends $\delta_0$ to a single Dirac mass. A coupling may split: $\frac12\delta_{(0,-1)}+\frac12\delta_{(0,1)}$ is the only coupling (Exercise 3(b)), hence optimal.
  2. Describe $\Couplings(a,b)$ for $a=(p,1-p)$, $b=(q,1-q)$. What is its dimension for general $n,m$?
    Answer
    The segment $P(s)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}s&p-s\\q-s&1-p-q+s\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, $\max(0,p+q-1)\le s\le\min(p,q)$. In general $(n-1)(m-1)$: $nm$ entries minus $n+m-1$ independent constraints.
  3. When is the product coupling optimal?
    Answer
    Only when every coupling is optimal (positive weights, Prop. 3.4). For continuous costs on compact spaces this means $c(x,y)=u(x)+v(y)$ on the product of the supports.
  4. Prove that $\mathrm L_C(a,b)$ is jointly convex in $(a,b)$.
    Answer
    Mix optimal plans: $(1-t)P_0+tP_1$ couples the mixed marginals, so the value there is at most the mixed values.
  5. Why does the transport linear program have a solution with at most $n+m-1$ non-zero entries?
    Answer
    A linear function on a polytope is minimised at a vertex. A vertex has no cycle in its support graph, since a cycle gives a direction $E$ with zero marginals along which the plan moves both ways. A forest on $n+m$ nodes has at most $n+m-1$ edges.
  6. What does the north-west corner rule compute, and when is it optimal?
    Answer
    A vertex of $\Couplings(a,b)$ (staircase support, $\le n+m-1$ cells), ignoring the cost. It is optimal when the points lie on a line, are sorted, and $c=h(x-y)$ with $h$ convex.
  7. Show that the sorted plan is not optimal for $h=\sqrt{\abs\cdot}$ with sources $\{0,1\}$ and targets $\{1,2\}$ (uniform weights).
    Answer
    Sorted: $0\to1$, $1\to2$, cost $2h(1)=2$. The nested plan $0\to2$, $1\to1$ costs $h(2)+h(0)=\sqrt2\approx1.41$. Concave costs favour one long move over several short ones.
  8. State the Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem and the key step of its proof.
    Answer
    The extreme points of $\mathcal B_n$ are the permutation matrices. A bistochastic matrix with a fractional entry has a cycle of fractional entries (rows and columns with one fractional entry have another); alternating $\pm\varepsilon/2$ along it writes the matrix as a midpoint of two bistochastic matrices.
  9. Why does the theorem imply that Lesson 1’s assignment problem is solved by a linear program?
    Answer
    With uniform weights the feasible set is $\frac1n\mathcal B_n$; the linear program has an optimal vertex, which is $P_\sigma/n$; its value is at most, hence equal to, the assignment value.
  10. How many permutation matrices does a random dense $10\times10$ bistochastic matrix need? Why?
    Answer
    Exactly $(n-1)^2+1=82$. Carathéodory gives at most $\dim\mathcal B_n+1$. Hulls of fewer permutation matrices are finitely many lower-dimensional sets, which a matrix in general position avoids. Lab 4 measures 82.
  11. How do you solve a Kantorovich problem with rational weights by an assignment?
    Answer
    Write $a_i=k_i/N$, $b_j=\ell_j/N$, duplicate $x_i$ $k_i$ times and $y_j$ $\ell_j$ times, solve the $N\times N$ assignment, and count the matched copies: $P_{i,j}=n_{i,j}/N$ (Prop. 3.21).
  12. Give the certificate of optimality of a coupling and prove it.
    Answer
    $u,v$ with $u_i+v_j\le C_{i,j}$ everywhere and equality where $P_{i,j}\gt0$. For any coupling $Q$: $\dotp CQ\ge\sum(u_i+v_j)Q_{i,j}=\dotp ua+\dotp vb=\dotp CP$.
  13. In the network simplex, what is a reduced cost, and what does the algorithm do when one is negative?
    Answer
    $r_{i,j}=C_{i,j}-u_i-v_j$, with $u,v$ solved on the basis tree. A negative $r_{i,j}$ means that adding cell $(i,j)$ and pushing $\theta$ around the cycle it closes changes the cost by $\theta r_{i,j}\lt0$. If all $r\ge0$, $(u,v)$ certifies optimality.
  14. What is a degenerate pivot, and why does it matter?
    Answer
    A pivot with $\theta=0$: the basis changes, the plan does not. Sequences of degenerate pivots can cycle; an anti-cycling rule such as Bland’s is needed. They are the rule in assignment problems (Lab 6: 188 of 202 pivots for $n=40$).
  15. Why does the Kantorovich problem between two measures on a compact space, with a continuous cost, always have a minimiser?
    Answer
    The set of couplings is non-empty (product), weakly compact (tight, and closed because the marginal constraints pass to weak limits), and $\pi\mapsto\int c\dd\pi$ is continuous: a minimising sequence has a limit point, which is a minimiser.
  16. For $\al=U[0,2]$, $\be=U[1,3]$ and $c=\abs{x-y}$, which couplings are optimal?
    Answer
    $\int\abs{y-x}\dd\pi\ge\int(y-x)\dd\pi=1$, with equality iff $\pi$ lives on $\{y\ge x\}$: all couplings concentrated there, including the translation and the book-shifting map.
  17. State the Monge–Kantorovich equivalence under Brenier’s hypotheses. What is absolute continuity used for?
    Answer
    For $\norm{x-y}^2$, $\al,\be\in\Pc_2$, $\al$ with a density: $(\Id,\nabla\varphi)_\sharp\al$ is the unique optimal coupling. Optimal plans lie in the graph of $\partial\varphi$; convex functions are differentiable almost everywhere, so $\al$-almost everywhere when $\al$ has a density.
  18. Compute $\mathcal L_c(\al,\be)$ for $\al=\frac12\delta_0+\frac12U[1,2]$, $\be=U[0,2]$, $c=\abs{x-y}^2$.
    Answer
    Quantile coupling: levels $r\le\frac12$ go from $0$ to $2r$, the others from $2r$ to $2r$. Value $\int_0^{1/2}4r^2\dd r=\frac16$; the atom at $0$ is spread over $[0,1]$, so no map attains it.
  19. In Lab 7, why is the Monge value about 1.5 for every odd number $k$ of central atoms?
    Answer
    A map sends $e$ outer atoms (mass $\frac14$) and $j$ central atoms (mass $\frac1{2k}$) to $-1$, with $\frac e4+\frac j{2k}=\frac12$. For odd $k$, $e=1$ would need $j=k/2$, not an integer, so $e\in\{0,2\}$: one outer atom crosses to the far side, at cost $\frac14\cdot4=1$, and the central half costs about $\frac12$.
  20. Define $c$-cyclical monotonicity. For discrete problems, how is it related to optimality?
    Answer
    $\sum c(x_i,y_i)\le\sum c(x_i,y_{i+1})$ for finitely many points of the support. For discrete measures: optimal $\iff$ c.c.m. support $\iff$ no negative cycle in the residual graph (the difference of two couplings decomposes into cycles).
  21. How many atoms does $\al_t$ have for an optimal vertex plan with $n=12$, $m=8$, and why do they not collide for $0\lt t\lt1$?
    Answer
    At most $n+m-1=19$, the support size (Lab 9 measures 19). The two-point test gives $\dotp{x-x'}{y-y'}\ge0$, so $\norm{x_t-x'_t}^2\ge(1-t)^2\norm{x-x'}^2+t^2\norm{y-y'}^2\gt0$.
  22. Why do interior-point methods never return an exactly sparse plan, and how does that show in 1-linprog?
    Answer
    The barrier keeps every entry positive, with vanishing entries of order $\varepsilon/r$. The notebook counts entries above $10^{-5}$; a smaller threshold counts more than $n+m-1$.
