Dynamic OT and flow matching

Transport can be computed as a motion: a curve of densities pushed by a velocity field, of least kinetic energy. The same language describes the generative models that learn a velocity by regression. They produce a valid transport map, which is optimal only in special cases.

§0Where this lesson sits

So far a transport plan was a static object: a map or a coupling. McCann’s interpolation (Lesson 1 §9) and the geodesics of $W_2$ (Lesson 3 §3) already showed it in motion. This lesson makes the motion the primary object. A curve of measures is described by a velocity field through the continuity equation, and $W_2^2$ is the least kinetic energy needed to go from one measure to the other (Benamou–Brenier). The second half applies the same language to generative models. Flow matching and diffusion models prescribe a path from noise to data and learn its velocity by least squares; integrating it gives a transport map, but in general not the optimal one.

  • Push-forwards and the density formula (Lesson 1 §4): a flow map moves a measure by push-forward (§1).
  • Brenier’s theorem and McCann’s interpolation (Lesson 1 §7, §9): the Benamou–Brenier minimizer moves each point straight to its Brenier image (§2).
  • Gaussians, the Bures formula and the Gaussian Brenier map (Lesson 1 §8): closed forms for the labs of §1 and §7.
  • Couplings and their interpolation (Lesson 2 §9), and the geodesics of $W_2$ (Lesson 3 §3).
  • Kantorovich duality and $c$-transforms (Lesson 4 §2, §3): the dual of Benamou–Brenier is a Hamilton–Jacobi inequality whose endpoint values form a Kantorovich pair (§3).
  • Semi-discrete transport (Lesson 4 §5): Lab 5 compares the cells of a diffusion map with the Laguerre cells of the optimal map.

  • Lesson 8 (gradient flows) §2: a gradient flow is a curve of measures whose velocity is the gradient of a first variation; the continuity equation and the least-squares velocity of §1 are its language.

Watch out: two conventions that change every formula

Some sources halve the action and some do not. With the action $\int\!\!\int\norm v^2$ (OT4ML, CompOT, StatOT, this page) the Hamilton–Jacobi inequality reads $\partial_t\varphi+\frac14\norm{\nabla\varphi}^2\le0$ and the optimal velocity is $\nabla\varphi/2$. With $\frac12\int\!\!\int\norm v^2=\frac12W_2^2$ (slides 27 and 33) it reads $\partial_t\varphi+\frac12\norm{\nabla\varphi}^2\le0$ and $v=\nabla\varphi$. The direction of time also differs. The sheet and the slides go from noise ($t=0$) to data ($t=1$); OT4ML’s diffusion propositions go from data to noise. Then $v_t$ changes sign and $t$ becomes $1-t$; for instance slide 18’s $v_t=\frac xt+\frac{1-t}t\nabla\log\rho_t$ is OT4ML’s Prop. 16.7 read backward.

A question that comes back

When a path joins two distributions, what does it say about the map at its end? A path determines its velocity only up to motions that leave the measure in place; the least-squares choice is a gradient (§1). Among all paths, the cheapest is McCann’s, and its cost is $W_2^2$ (§2–§3). Flow matching and diffusion models choose a convenient path and learn its velocity by regression (§5–§6). Integrating that velocity always gives a valid transport map, but in general not the optimal one (§7): in dimension 1, or with commuting Gaussian covariances, the map is optimal, and otherwise it can carry a hidden rotation. Lesson 8 §5 asks the same question for gradient flows: among the flows it compares, only that of $W_2^2$ follows the geodesic.

§1Moving mass: particles, densities and velocities

The problem

A generative model must turn samples of a simple law into samples of the data (slides 4–5). Autoregressive models factor the law into conditionals. Transport models construct a map $T$ with $\push T\al_0=\al_1$, or a flow $\Phi_t$ of an ordinary differential equation with $\push{(\Phi_1)}\al_0=\al_1$. To build and compare such flows one needs to describe how a measure moves, and to say what it costs.

Lagrangian and Eulerian descriptions

In the Lagrangian description, particles follow a time-dependent vector field, $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}x(t)=v_t(x(t))$, with flow map $T_t$, and the measure is transported: $\al_t=\push{(T_t)}\al_0$. In the Eulerian description the same motion is a partial differential equation on the measure, the continuity equation $$\partial_t\al_t+\operatorname{div}(\al_tv_t)=0,$$ understood in the weak sense: $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\int\varphi\dd\al_t=\int\dotp{\nabla\varphi}{v_t}\dd\al_t$ for smooth test functions $\varphi$ (OT4ML (14.2)–(14.4), pp. 328–329 (320–321); slides 6–7; StatOT Prop. 5.1, p. 142 (136)). The weak form makes sense for empirical measures: $\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i(t)}$ solves it when every particle follows the field.

Proposition (Lagrangian flows solve the continuity equation)OT4ML Prop. 14.2, p. 329 (321); compact Prop. 14.2, p. 100; StatOT Prop. 5.1, p. 142 (136)

Let $(T_t)$ be a $C^1$ family of diffeomorphisms with $T_0=\mathrm{Id}$, set $\al_t=\push{(T_t)}\al_0$, and define $v_t$ by $v_t(T_t(y))=\partial_tT_t(y)$. Then $(\al_t,v_t)$ solves the continuity equation.

Proof (the chain rule under the push-forward)

For a smooth test function $\varphi$, $\int\varphi\dd\al_t=\int\varphi(T_t(y))\dd\al_0(y)$. Differentiating under the integral, $$\begin{aligned}\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\int\varphi\dd\al_t&=\int\dotp{\nabla\varphi(T_t(y))}{\partial_tT_t(y)}\dd\al_0(y)\\&=\int\dotp{\nabla\varphi(T_t(y))}{v_t(T_t(y))}\dd\al_0(y)\\&=\int\dotp{\nabla\varphi}{v_t}\dd\al_t,\end{aligned}$$ the last step by the push-forward relation again. This is the weak form. $\square$

One path, many velocities

The converse problem, recovering a velocity from a curve $(\al_t)$, is ill-posed: if $w$ satisfies $\operatorname{div}(\al_tw)=0$, then $v_t+w$ moves the measure exactly as $v_t$ does. Such fields exist: a rotation $x\mapsto Jx$, $J$ antisymmetric, keeps the standard Gaussian in place while every particle turns (StatOT Fig. 5.1, p. 144 (138); OT4ML p. 329 (321); StatOT Exercise 1 of §5.10, Exercise 6 here). Dacorogna and Moser select the velocity whose flux $\rho_tv_t$ is a gradient (OT4ML (14.6)). The choice used by optimal transport, flow matching and gradient flows is the velocity of least kinetic energy.

Proposition (least-squares velocities are gradients)OT4ML Prop. 14.3, p. 330 (322); compact Prop. 14.3, p. 101; slides 8–9

Let $\al_t=\rho_t\dd x$ be a smooth positive curve. Among the velocities with $\partial_t\rho_t+\operatorname{div}(\rho_tv_t)=0$, the one minimizing $\int\norm{v_t}^2\dd\al_t$ is a gradient, $v_t=\nabla\psi_t$, where $\psi_t$ solves the weighted Poisson equation $-\operatorname{div}(\rho_t\nabla\psi_t)=\partial_t\rho_t$.

Proof (orthogonality to the divergence-free fields)

The admissible velocities at time $t$ form an affine space $v^\star+\mathcal H$, where $\mathcal H=\{w\in L^2(\al_t):\operatorname{div}(\rho_tw)=0\}$ is closed. The minimizer of the norm is the element orthogonal to $\mathcal H$ in $L^2(\al_t)$. Gradients are orthogonal to $\mathcal H$: $\int\dotp{\nabla\psi}w\rho_t\dd x=-\int\psi\operatorname{div}(\rho_tw)\dd x=0$ when boundary terms vanish. Conversely, the orthogonal complement of $\mathcal H$ is the closure of the gradients. Hence the minimizer is a gradient $\nabla\psi_t$, and substituting it in the continuity equation gives the Poisson equation. OT4ML writes the same argument with a Lagrange multiplier. $\square$

For Gaussian curves this is explicit. If $\al_t=\Gauss(m_t,\Sigma_t)$, the least-squares velocity is affine, $v_t(x)=\dot m_t+S_t(x-m_t)$ with $S_t$ symmetric and $S_t\Sigma_t+\Sigma_tS_t=\dot\Sigma_t$ (a Lyapunov equation; OT4ML Prop. 16.22, p. 431 (423)). A non-symmetric field $A_tx+b_t$ moving the same Gaussians differs from it by $(A_t-S_t)(x-m_t)$, which is divergence-free for $\al_t$ and costs extra kinetic energy without changing the path.

§2The Benamou–Brenier formula: $W_2^2$ as least kinetic energy

The problem

§1 measured the energy of a given path. Now only the endpoints are fixed. Which path from $\al_0$ to $\al_1$, and which velocity, cost the least kinetic energy, and what is that cost?

The theorem

Theorem (Benamou–Brenier)OT4ML Def. 14.4 and Thm. 14.5, pp. 330–331 (322–323); compact Thm. 14.5, p. 101; CompOT (7.1)–(7.2), pp. 106–107 (102–103); StatOT Thm. 5.7, p. 150 (144); slide 27

For $\al_0,\al_1\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$, $$\begin{gathered}W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1)=\inf\int_0^1\!\!\int\norm{v_t(x)}^2\dd\al_t(x)\dd t\\\text{over the curves with }\partial_t\al_t+\operatorname{div}(\al_tv_t)=0,\\\al_{t=0}=\al_0,\quad\al_{t=1}=\al_1.\end{gathered}$$ If $\al_0$ has a density and $T$ is its Brenier map to $\al_1$, a minimizer is McCann’s interpolation $\al_t=\push{((1-t)\mathrm{Id}+tT)}\al_0$, with $v_t((1-t)x+tT(x))=T(x)-x$: every particle moves at constant speed along a straight line.

Proof (straight lines for one inequality, Jensen for the other)

Dynamic $\le$ static. Take an optimal coupling $\pi$, and move each pair along the straight segment $\gamma_{x,y}(t)=(1-t)x+ty$. With a Brenier map, the curve $\al_t=\push{((1-t)\mathrm{Id}+tT)}\al_0$ is generated by the velocity $v_t((1-t)x+tT(x))=T(x)-x$, well defined because $x\mapsto(1-t)x+tT(x)$ is injective for $t\lt1$ ($T$ is the gradient of a convex function). Its action is $\int_0^1\!\int\norm{T(x)-x}^2\dd\al_0\dd t=W_2^2$. Without a map, the velocity is the conditional mean of the pair velocities $y-x$ given the position at time $t$; by Jensen it costs no more than $\int\norm{y-x}^2\dd\pi=W_2^2$.

Static $\le$ dynamic. For a smooth velocity, let $T_t$ be its flow, so that $\push{(T_1)}\al_0=\al_1$. By Jensen in time, $\norm{T_1(x)-x}^2=\norm{\int_0^1v_t(T_t(x))\dd t}^2\le\int_0^1\norm{v_t(T_t(x))}^2\dd t$; integrating against $\al_0$ gives $W_2^2\le\int\norm{T_1(x)-x}^2\dd\al_0\le$ action. For a general finite-energy solution of the continuity equation, the flow may not exist; the superposition principle replaces it by a probability measure on paths with the same time marginals, and the same Jensen argument applies path by path (OT4ML p. 331 (323)). $\square$

Watch out: the short proof assumes a flow

StatOT’s proof of Theorem 5.7 starts “let $\dot X_t=v_t(X_t)$ with $X_0\sim\mu_0$”, which needs the ODE to be well posed; its statement restricts to absolutely continuous measures. OT4ML’s proof (and the compact notes’) states the general step: finite-energy solutions of the continuity equation are lifted to measures on paths by the superposition principle, which neither text states as a result.

Two remarks make the theorem concrete. First, the equality case of Jensen in time forces constant speed along each trajectory, and the equality case of the first inequality forces an optimal coupling: the minimizer is unique when $\al_0$ has a density (StatOT Thm. 5.7). Second, the theorem turns $W_2$ into the length of the shortest path, the starting point of the Riemannian (Otto) picture of slides 8–11 and of Lesson 8 §2. The same statement holds over paths: $W_2^2$ is the least value of $\E\int_0^1\norm{\dot\gamma(t)}^2\dd t$ over random curves with endpoint laws $\al_0,\al_1$, and the optimal law of curves charges straight segments only (OT4ML p. 334 (326)).

In one dimension

On the line the Brenier map is $T=F_1^{-1}\circ F_0$ and McCann’s interpolation averages quantile functions, $F_{\al_t}^{-1}=(1-t)F_0^{-1}+tF_1^{-1}$ (CompOT (7.7), p. 108 (104); Lesson 1 §6). Every other path has a larger action. One of them is the linear mixture $\rho_t=(1-t)\rho_0+t\rho_1$, which moves no mass in the Lagrangian sense: it removes mass where $\rho_0\gt\rho_1$ and creates it elsewhere. In dimension one its velocity is unique, because $\operatorname{div}(\rho w)=0$ forces $\rho w$ to be constant, hence zero. From $\partial_x m_t=\rho_0-\rho_1$, the momentum is $m=F_0-F_1$, and $$\begin{aligned}\int_0^1\!\!\int\frac{(F_0-F_1)^2}{(1-t)\rho_0+t\rho_1}\dd x\dd t&=\int\frac{(F_0(x)-F_1(x))^2}{\mathrm{LM}(\rho_0(x),\rho_1(x))}\dd x,\\\mathrm{LM}(a,b)&=\frac{a-b}{\log a-\log b},\end{aligned}$$ since $\int_0^1\frac{\dd t}{(1-t)a+tb}=\frac{\log b-\log a}{b-a}$. This velocity is also the one Dacorogna and Moser’s construction gives in dimension one.

Lab 2The geodesic against the mixture path

§3Convexity and duality

Momenta make the problem convex

The action $\int\norm{v}^2\dd\al$ is not jointly convex in $(\al,v)$, and the continuity equation is bilinear. Both defects disappear with the momentum $m=\rho v$ (slide 29; OT4ML Def. 14.6, p. 331 (323); CompOT (7.3)–(7.5), pp. 107–108 (103–104)): $$\begin{gathered}W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1)=\min\Big\{\int_0^1\!\!\int J(\rho_t,m_t)\dd x\dd t:\ \partial_t\rho_t+\operatorname{div}m_t=0\Big\},\\J(a,m)=\begin{cases}\norm m^2/a&a\gt0,\\0&(a,m)=(0,0),\\+\infty&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\end{gathered}$$ The constraint is now linear, and $J$ is the perspective of $\norm m^2$: it is convex, lower semicontinuous and positively 1-homogeneous. Homogeneity makes $\int J(\rho,m)\dd x$ independent of the reference measure, so the problem makes sense for measures without densities; the value $+\infty$ forbids momentum where there is no mass.

Link: the Beckmann flux of Lesson 4 is a time average of the momentum

Lesson 4 §7 writes $W_1(\al_0,\al_1)=\min\{\norm s_{\TV}:\operatorname{div}s=\al_0-\al_1\}$, a flux without time whose cost is linear in its size. Integrating the continuity equation over $t\in[0,1]$ gives $\operatorname{div}\int_0^1m_t\dd t=\al_0-\al_1$: the time average of any admissible momentum is a Beckmann flux. By Cauchy–Schwarz, $\int_0^1\!\int\norm{m_t}\le\big(\int_0^1\!\int\norm{m_t}^2/\rho_t\big)^{1/2}\big(\int_0^1\!\int\rho_t\big)^{1/2}$, and the last factor is $1$. Minimizing both sides gives $W_1\le W_2$ once more (derived here). Mobilities $a^{s-p}\norm b^p$ interpolate between the two problems (CompOT §7.5, pp. 114–115 (110–111)).

The Hamilton–Jacobi dual

Proposition (dual Benamou–Brenier problem)OT4ML Prop. 14.7, p. 332 (324); compact Prop. 14.7, p. 102; slide 33 (with the halved action)

For smooth compactly supported densities, $$\begin{gathered}W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1)=\sup_\varphi\Big\{\int\varphi_1\dd\al_1-\int\varphi_0\dd\al_0\Big\}\\\text{over the }\varphi\text{ with }\partial_t\varphi_t+\tfrac14\norm{\nabla\varphi_t}^2\le0.\end{gathered}$$ At optimality $m_t=\frac{\rho_t}2\nabla\varphi_t$, so $v_t=\nabla\varphi_t/2$, and $\partial_t\varphi_t+\frac14\norm{\nabla\varphi_t}^2=0$ where $\rho_t\gt0$.

Proof (weak duality by Young’s inequality; contact by the perspective)

Multiply the continuity equation by $\varphi$ and integrate by parts in space and time: $\int\varphi_1\dd\al_1-\int\varphi_0\dd\al_0=\int_0^1\!\!\int(\rho_t\partial_t\varphi_t+\dotp{m_t}{\nabla\varphi_t})\dd x\dd t$. Young’s inequality gives $\dotp m{\nabla\varphi}\le\frac{\norm m^2}\rho+\frac\rho4\norm{\nabla\varphi}^2$, so the integrand is at most $\rho(\partial_t\varphi+\frac14\norm{\nabla\varphi}^2)+J(\rho,m)\le J(\rho,m)$ under the constraint. Every admissible $\varphi$ therefore gives a lower bound on every admissible action. Equality holds in Young’s inequality exactly when $m=\frac\rho2\nabla\varphi$, and in the constraint where $\rho\gt0$; Fenchel–Rockafellar duality shows that the bounds meet (OT4ML’s proof). $\square$

The dual is Kantorovich duality in disguise (Lesson 4 §2). Along any curve $\gamma$ from $x$ to $y$, $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\varphi_t(\gamma(t))=\partial_t\varphi+\dotp{\nabla\varphi}{\dot\gamma}\le\norm{\dot\gamma}^2$ by the same Young inequality; integrating and taking straight lines gives $\varphi_1(y)-\varphi_0(x)\le\norm{x-y}^2$ (OT4ML p. 333 (325)). So $(-\varphi_0,\varphi_1)$ is an admissible Kantorovich pair, and the largest $\varphi_1$ compatible with $\varphi_0$ is the Hopf–Lax formula $\varphi_1(y)=\min_x\varphi_0(x)+\norm{x-y}^2$, a $c$-transform (Lesson 4 §3). Along the optimal characteristics $\varphi$ grows by $\norm{T(x)-x}^2$, which is why the notebook can build $\varphi_t$ from the quantiles.

§4Solving dynamic OT numerically

A staggered grid

On a grid, the convex problem of §3 becomes a finite-dimensional convex program (slide 30; CompOT §7.2, pp. 109–110 (105–106)). Papadakis, Peyré and Oudet store the densities $\rho_{k,i}$ at the times $t_k=k/T$ and cell centres $x_i$, and the momenta $m_{k,i}$ at mid-times and cell faces. Then the discrete continuity equation $(\rho_{k+1,i}-\rho_{k,i})/\Delta t+(m_{k,i+1}-m_{k,i})/\Delta x=0$ is exact. The action is evaluated on centred values obtained by averaging, $\bar\rho_{k,i}=\frac12(\rho_{k,i}+\rho_{k+1,i})$ and $\bar m_{k,i}=\frac12(m_{k,i}+m_{k,i+1})$, so that the discrete problem is $$\min\ \sum_{k,i}J(\bar\rho_{k,i},\bar m_{k,i})\,\Delta t\Delta x$$ over the $(\rho,m)$ that satisfy the discrete continuity equation, the endpoints and no flux at the boundary.

Douglas–Rachford splitting

Write the unknowns as $U=(\rho,m)$ on the staggered grid and $V=(\bar\rho,\bar m)$ on the centred grid. The problem is $\min F+G$ with $F(U,V)=\iota_{\mathcal C}(U)+\sum J(V)\Delta t\Delta x$ and $G(U,V)=\iota_{\{V=I(U)\}}$, where $\mathcal C$ is the affine set of the continuity equation with its endpoint and boundary conditions, and $I$ is the averaging operator. Each proximal map is simple (CompOT §7.3, pp. 110–112 (106–108); OT4ML Alg. 14.1, p. 334 (326)):

  • the prox of $J$ acts point by point: for $(\tilde a,\tilde m)$ it returns the largest root $a$ of the cubic $(a-\tilde a)(a+2\gamma)^2=\gamma\tilde m^2$ and $m=\tilde m\,a/(a+2\gamma)$, or $(0,0)$ when $-4\gamma\tilde a\ge\tilde m^2$;
  • the projection on $\mathcal C$ is a Poisson equation with Neumann conditions on the space-time grid, solved by a discrete cosine transform in both directions;
  • the projection on $\{V=I(U)\}$ needs one tridiagonal solve per row.

Douglas–Rachford iterates $w\leftarrow w+\alpha\big(\mathrm{prox}_{\gamma F}(2x-w)-x\big)$, $x\leftarrow\mathrm{prox}_{\gamma G}(w)$ (CompOT (7.14), with $0\lt\alpha\lt2$); this is the ALG2 scheme of Benamou and Brenier, also ADMM on the dual.

§5Flow matching: learning the velocity of a chosen path

The problem

Benamou–Brenier optimizes the path. A generative model only needs some path from a simple law $\al_0$ (noise) to the data law $\al_1$, provided its velocity can be learned from samples. Flow matching fixes the path by an interpolation, then learns its velocity by a regression that never integrates the learned ODE during training (slides 14–19; OT4ML §16.1, pp. 407–410 (399–402)).

Interpolants

Definition (interpolant)OT4ML Def. 16.1 and Ex. 16.2, pp. 407–408 (399–400); compact Def. 16.1, p. 125; slides 14 and 16

Given a coupling $\pi$ of $(\al_0,\al_1)$, an interpolant is a family $I_t(x,y)$ with $I_0(x,y)=x$ and $I_1(x,y)=y$, differentiable in $t$. It defines $X_t=I_t(X_0,X_1)$ for $(X_0,X_1)\sim\pi$, the curve $\al_t=\mathrm{Law}(X_t)$ and the pathwise velocity $\partial_tI_t(X_0,X_1)$. The basic choice is $I_t(x,y)=(1-t)x+ty$, with velocity $X_1-X_0$.

The coupling matters as much as the path. With the independent coupling $\al_0\otimes\al_1$, paths cross and two empirical measures with $n$ and $m$ points produce $nm$ moving Diracs. With an optimal coupling, the linear interpolant is McCann’s geodesic (OT4ML p. 408 (400)). Rectified flow (Liu, Gong and Liu 2023, cited by OT4ML p. 408 (400)) emphasizes straight couplings and “reflow”: retraining on the pairs $(X_0,\Phi_1(X_0))$ produced by a first flow, which straightens the paths.

The velocity is a conditional expectation

Proposition (flow-matching vector field)OT4ML Prop. 16.3, pp. 408–409 (400–401); compact Prop. 16.2, p. 126; slide 17; sheet (1)

If $\int_0^1\E\norm{\partial_tI_t}^2\dd t\lt\infty$, the minimizer of $\E\norm{v_t(X_t)-\partial_tI_t(X_0,X_1)}^2$ over fields $v_t$ is $v_t(x)=\E[\partial_tI_t(X_0,X_1)\mid X_t=x]$. It belongs to $L^2(\al_t)$ and $(\al_t,v_t)$ solves the continuity equation. For the linear interpolant, $v_t(x)=\E[X_1-X_0\mid X_t=x]=\frac{\E[X_1\mid X_t=x]-x}{1-t}$.

Proof (projection in L², then the weak form)

For fixed $t$, the regression is a least-squares problem in $L^2(\pi)$ over the functions of $X_t$: its solution is the orthogonal projection of $\partial_tI_t$ on that closed subspace, the conditional expectation. By the conditional Jensen inequality, $\int\norm{v_t}^2\dd\al_t\le\E\norm{\partial_tI_t}^2$. For a smooth test function $\varphi$, $$\begin{aligned}\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\E\varphi(X_t)&=\E\dotp{\nabla\varphi(X_t)}{\partial_tI_t}\\&=\E\dotp{\nabla\varphi(X_t)}{\E[\partial_tI_t\mid X_t]}=\int\dotp{\nabla\varphi}{v_t}\dd\al_t:\end{aligned}$$ the weak continuity equation. The middle step conditions on $X_t$, the last step is the definition of $v_t$. For the linear interpolant, $X_1-X_0=(X_1-X_t)/(1-t)$, which gives the last formula. $\square$

Training samples pairs $(x_0,x_1)$ and times $t$, forms $x_t=I_t(x_0,x_1)$, and fits a network $v_\theta(t,x)$ to $x_1-x_0$ by stochastic gradient descent; sampling integrates $\dot x=v_\theta(t,x)$ from noise (slide 19). Intuitively, $v_t(x)$ is the average velocity of all the straight paths passing through $x$ at time $t$ (OT4ML p. 410 (402)).

What the regression guarantees

If the flow $\Phi_t$ of $v_t$ is well defined, it transports $\al_0$ along the same curve, so $\Phi_1$ is a transport map from $\al_0$ to $\al_1$ (OT4ML p. 410 (402)). Combining the Jensen inequality in time of §2 with the conditional Jensen inequality above gives, for every coupling $\pi$, $$\begin{aligned}W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1)&\le\E\norm{\Phi_1(X_0)-X_0}^2\\&\le\int_0^1\!\!\int\norm{v_t}^2\dd\al_t\dd t\le\E_\pi\norm{X_1-X_0}^2.\end{aligned}$$ The flow never costs more than the coupling it was trained on, but in general it costs more than $W_2^2$. In dimension one there is no gap at the first inequality, whatever the coupling: one-dimensional flows preserve the order of the particles, so $\Phi_1$ is the monotone map, which is the optimal map (sheet Exercise 3; Exercise 3 here). The middle inequality can still be strict.

For Gaussian mixtures the conditional expectation is explicit. If $(X_0,X_1)$ is a mixture of jointly Gaussian components $\pi_k\Gauss(m^k,\Sigma^k)$, then $v_t(x)=\sum_k\alpha^k(x)w^k_t(x)$, where $w^k_t$ is the affine velocity of component $k$ and $\alpha^k(x)$ is the posterior probability of component $k$ given $X_t=x$ (sheet Exercise 5). The independent coupling of two mixtures is such a mixture, with one component per pair of components.

Watch out: the flow-matching field is not always the least-squares field of its path

OT4ML p. 407 (399) introduces flow matching as computing the least-squares velocity (14.8) of the interpolation, and slide 20 describes flow matching as fixing the path and minimizing $\int\norm{v_t}^2_{L^2(\al_t)}$ under the continuity equation. The conditional expectation of Prop. 16.3 is that minimizer only when it is a gradient field. That holds when one endpoint is a standard Gaussian and the coupling is independent (OT4ML Prop. 16.7, and the precise statement on p. 411 (403)). It fails in general. For two independent Gaussians with non-commuting covariances the field is linear with a non-symmetric matrix, and in Lab 6 its action is 1.4230 against 1.3400 for the least-squares field of the same path.

§6Diffusion models and the probability-flow ODE

The problem

Diffusion models were developed separately from flow matching. They noise the data with a stochastic process and learn the score $\nabla\log\rho_t$ of the noised densities; sampling integrates a reverse-time process. When one endpoint is Gaussian and the coupling is independent, both constructions describe the same curve of measures (OT4ML p. 411 (403); slides 18 and 20–24). The tool that connects them is Tweedie’s formula.

Tweedie’s formula

Proposition (Tweedie identity)OT4ML Prop. 16.6, p. 411 (403); compact Prop. 16.3, p. 126

Let $Z=W+\sigma\varepsilon$ with $W\sim\be$, $\varepsilon\sim\Gauss(0,\mathrm{Id})$ independent, and let $\rho_\sigma$ be the density of $Z$. Then $\E[W\mid Z=z]=z+\sigma^2\nabla\log\rho_\sigma(z)$.

Proof (differentiate the Gaussian convolution)

With $\varphi_\sigma$ the density of $\Gauss(0,\sigma^2\mathrm{Id})$, $\rho_\sigma(z)=\int\varphi_\sigma(z-w)\dd\be(w)$ and $\nabla\varphi_\sigma(u)=-\sigma^{-2}u\,\varphi_\sigma(u)$. Hence, by Bayes’ rule, $$\begin{aligned}\nabla\rho_\sigma(z)&=-\sigma^{-2}\int(z-w)\varphi_\sigma(z-w)\dd\be(w)\\&=-\sigma^{-2}\big(z-\E[W\mid Z=z]\big)\rho_\sigma(z);\end{aligned}$$ divide by $\rho_\sigma$. $\square$

For the linear interpolant from $X_0\sim\Gauss(0,\mathrm{Id})$ to the data $X_1$, apply it with $W=tX_1$ and $\sigma=1-t$: $\E[X_1\mid X_t=x]=\big(x+(1-t)^2\nabla\log\rho_t(x)\big)/t$, so $$\begin{aligned}v_t(x)&=\frac{\E[X_1\mid X_t=x]-x}{1-t}\\&=\frac xt+\frac{1-t}t\nabla\log\rho_t(x)\qquad(t\gt0),\end{aligned}$$ which is slide 18’s formula and OT4ML’s Prop. 16.7 (p. 412 (404)) in the opposite direction of time. This field is a gradient, $\nabla\big(\frac{\norm x^2}{2t}+\frac{1-t}t\log\rho_t\big)$, so here the flow-matching field is also the least-squares field of §1 (OT4ML p. 411 (403)). Learning the score and learning the velocity are the same task.

Schedules change the paths, not the map

Diffusion models use other noising paths $Z_t=a_tX+b_tY$, with data $X$, noise $Y\sim\Gauss(0,\sigma^2\mathrm{Id})$, and $(a,b)$ going from $(1,0)$ to $(0,1)$. Tweedie’s formula gives the velocity $v_t(z)=\frac{a_t'}{a_t}z+\big(\frac{a'_tb_t^2}{a_t}-b'_tb_t\big)\sigma^2\nabla\log p_t(z)$. The variance-preserving Ornstein–Uhlenbeck choice $a_\tau=e^{-\tau}$, $b_\tau=\sqrt{1-e^{-2\tau}}$ gives $v_\tau(z)=-z-\sigma^2\nabla\log p_\tau(z)$, integrated backward from large $\tau$ to sample. The schedule $(\cos\frac{\pi t}2,\sin\frac{\pi t}2)$ is the same path after a time change, while $a_t=(1-t)(1-2t)$, $b_t=t$ is a genuinely different “overshooting” bridge (OT4ML pp. 414–415 (406–407), Figs. 16.4–16.5; slides 23–24). The trajectories differ. The end points do not, as Lab 5 measures, and the reason is short. OT4ML states it for Gaussians (Remark 16.9); the general argument below is ours.

Proposition (schedule-free form of the probability flow (derived here))the argument follows OT4ML Remark 16.9, which treats Gaussian endpoints

Along the probability flow of $Z_t=a_tX+b_tY$ with $b_t\gt0$, the rescaled position $y=x/b_t$ depends on the schedule only through $\lambda_t=a_t/b_t$: it solves $\frac{\dd y}{\dd\lambda}=\E[X\mid\lambda X+Y=y]$. Hence every schedule started from pure noise ($\lambda=0$) sends a noise sample to the same terminal point $\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}y(\lambda)/\lambda$, while the paths $x_t=b_t\,y(\lambda_t)$ differ through the factor $b_t$.

Proof (change variables in the velocity)

The flow-matching velocity of the interpolant is $\dot x=\E[a_t'X+b_t'Y\mid Z_t=x]$. Since $x=\E[a_tX+b_tY\mid Z_t=x]$, $$\begin{aligned}\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\frac x{b_t}&=\frac{\dot x}{b_t}-\frac{b_t'x}{b_t^2}=\frac1{b_t}\E\Big[\Big(a_t'-\frac{a_tb_t'}{b_t}\Big)X\ \Big|\ Z_t=x\Big]\\&=\lambda_t'\,\E[X\mid Z_t=x].\end{aligned}$$ Conditioning on $Z_t=x$ is conditioning on $\lambda_tX+Y=y$. So $y$ solves an ODE in the variable $\lambda$ alone, and any $C^1$ path $t\mapsto\lambda_t$, even one that leaves $(0,\infty)$ and comes back as for the overshooting bridge, visits the same solution $y(\lambda)$. At the data end, $a_t\to1$ and $x_t=a_t\,y/\lambda_t\to\lim y/\lambda$. $\square$

§7When is the generated map optimal?

The problem

The flow of §5–§6 produces a map $\Phi_1$ with $\push{(\Phi_1)}\al_0=\al_1$. By Brenier’s theorem the optimal map is the gradient of a convex function, and it is unique (Lesson 1 §7). So the question is whether the accumulated flow is such a gradient. Slide 22 answers in words: only the Brenier interpolation is guaranteed to minimize the kinetic action, and a learned sampler can be a valid transport without being an optimal one.

Gaussians: optimal exactly when the covariances commute

Proposition (Gaussian flow matching and optimality)OT4ML Prop. 16.8 and Rem. 16.9, pp. 412–414 (404–406); compact Prop. 16.5, p. 127; sheet Exercise 4

Let $X_0\sim\Gauss(0,\Sigma_0)$ and $X_1\sim\Gauss(0,\Sigma_1)$ be independent, $X_t=(1-t)X_0+tX_1$, $\Sigma_t=(1-t)^2\Sigma_0+t^2\Sigma_1$. The flow-matching velocity is $v_t(x)=(t\Sigma_1-(1-t)\Sigma_0)\Sigma_t^{-1}x$, and with $C=\Sigma_0^{-1/2}\Sigma_1\Sigma_0^{-1/2}$ its flow map at time 1 and the Brenier map are $$\begin{aligned}T_1^{\mathrm{FM}}&=\Sigma_0^{1/2}C^{1/2}\Sigma_0^{-1/2},\\T^{\mathrm{OT}}&=\Sigma_0^{-1/2}\big(\Sigma_0^{1/2}\Sigma_1\Sigma_0^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\Sigma_0^{-1/2}.\end{aligned}$$ They coincide if and only if $\Sigma_0\Sigma_1=\Sigma_1\Sigma_0$. Changing the scalar schedule does not change $T_1^{\mathrm{FM}}$.

Proof (whiten, integrate, compare symmetries)

The variables are jointly Gaussian, so $v_t(x)=\mathrm{Cov}(X_1-X_0,X_t)\,\Sigma_t^{-1}x=(t\Sigma_1-(1-t)\Sigma_0)\Sigma_t^{-1}x$. Whiten the source: $\tilde x=\Sigma_0^{-1/2}x$, in which coordinates $\tilde\Sigma_t=(1-t)^2\mathrm{Id}+t^2C$. Since $\mathrm{Id}$ and $C$ commute, $\tilde T_t=\tilde\Sigma_t^{1/2}$ solves $\dot{\tilde T}_t=(tC-(1-t)\mathrm{Id})\tilde\Sigma_t^{-1}\tilde T_t$, the whitened flow equation, with $\tilde T_0=\mathrm{Id}$. Returning to $x$ gives $T_t^{\mathrm{FM}}=\Sigma_0^{1/2}\tilde\Sigma_t^{1/2}\Sigma_0^{-1/2}$. Both $T_1^{\mathrm{FM}}$ and $T^{\mathrm{OT}}$ push $\Gauss(0,\Sigma_0)$ to $\Gauss(0,\Sigma_1)$, and $T^{\mathrm{OT}}$ is the only symmetric positive definite matrix that does. $T_1^{\mathrm{FM}}$ is similar to $C^{1/2}$, so it has positive eigenvalues, and it is symmetric if and only if $\Sigma_0^{1/2}C^{1/2}=C^{1/2}\Sigma_0^{1/2}$, that is, $\Sigma_0C=C\Sigma_0$, that is, $\Sigma_0\Sigma_1=\Sigma_1\Sigma_0$. With a schedule $(a_t,b_t)$ the whitened covariance is $a_t^2\mathrm{Id}+b_t^2C$ and the flow map is again its square root, ending at the same $C^{1/2}$. $\square$

Non-commuting covariances produce a non-symmetric terminal map, with a rotational or shearing part that no Brenier map has (OT4ML p. 414 (406)). Three further facts complete the picture. In dimension one, flows preserve order and the map is always optimal (Exercise 3). With the optimal coupling instead of the independent one, the linear interpolant is McCann’s path, so flow matching recovers the Brenier map. And Lab 5 shows a non-Gaussian case: Gaussian noise to three atoms, where the map is close to optimal but not optimal. OT4ML also cites a counterexample for Fokker–Planck flows (Lavenant and Santambrogio 2022, p. 414 (406)).

§8Multimarginal transport and barycenters

The problem

The dates list puts “Multimarginal” in this lesson. Several questions need more than two measures at once: averaging $S$ distributions (barycenters), matching several populations, as in matching for teams (Carlier and Ekeland), and many-body costs such as the repulsion of $N$ electrons in density-functional theory. The multimarginal problem couples all the measures by one joint law (OT4ML §12.2, pp. 265–270 (257–262); compact §12.2, p. 80; CompOT §10.1, pp. 163–166 (159–162)).

One joint law, $S$ prescribed marginals

Definition (multimarginal optimal transport)OT4ML Def. 12.16, p. 265 (257); compact Def. 12.12, p. 80; CompOT (10.1) and (10.3), pp. 163–164 (159–160)

For $\al_s\in\Pc(\X_s)$, $s=1,\dots,S$, let $\mathcal U(\al_1,\dots,\al_S)$ be the probability measures on $\X_1\times\dots\times\X_S$ whose $s$-th marginal is $\al_s$. For a cost $c$ bounded from below, $$\mathrm{MMOT}_c(\al_1,\dots,\al_S)=\inf_{\pi\in\mathcal U(\al_1,\dots,\al_S)}\int c\,\dd\pi.$$

For discrete marginals with $n_1,\dots,n_S$ atoms this is again a linear program, now over a nonnegative tensor $\mathbf P_{i_1\dots i_S}$ with $\prod_sn_s$ entries and $\sum_sn_s$ marginal constraints. The tensor size, exponential in $S$, is the computational obstacle. For $S=2$ it is the Kantorovich problem of Lesson 2 §2. The Monge question also generalizes: under a twist condition on “splitting sets”, stronger than the two-marginal twist, the optimal plan is carried by maps $(\Id,T_2,\dots,T_S)$ (Gangbo and Święch, Pass; OT4ML Prop. 12.18, p. 266 (258)).

Barycenters as a multimarginal problem

A Wasserstein barycenter of $\be_1,\dots,\be_S$ with weights $\lambda$ in the simplex minimizes $\sum_s\lambda_sW_2^2(\al,\be_s)$ over $\al$ (Agueh and Carlier; OT4ML Def. 12.1, p. 251 (243)). The latent barycenter point can be eliminated: $\min_x\sum_s\lambda_s\norm{x-x_s}^2$ is attained at the weighted mean $B(x_1,\dots,x_S)=\sum_s\lambda_sx_s$, which gives the cost $$c_{\mathrm{bar}}(x_1,\dots,x_S)=\sum_{s=1}^S\lambda_s\norm{x_s-B(x_1,\dots,x_S)}^2.$$

Proposition (multimarginal formula for quadratic barycenters)OT4ML Prop. 12.21, p. 268 (260); compact Prop. 12.16, p. 80; CompOT Remark 10.2, pp. 164–165 (160–161); StatOT §8.5, Exercise 3, p. 251 (245)

Let $\be_1,\dots,\be_S\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$. If $\pi^\star$ solves the multimarginal problem with cost $c_{\mathrm{bar}}$, then $\al^\star=\push{B}{\pi^\star}$ is a barycenter, and the barycenter value equals $\mathrm{MMOT}_{c_{\mathrm{bar}}}$. Conversely, every barycenter is obtained in this way.

Proof (glue the couplings, then minimize over the hidden point)

Barycenter value $\ge$ MMOT. Take a candidate $\al$ and optimal couplings $\pi_s\in\Pi(\al,\be_s)$. Glue them along their common marginal $\al$ (Lesson 3 §1) into a law of $(X,Y_1,\dots,Y_S)$ with $(X,Y_s)\sim\pi_s$. Then $$\begin{aligned}\sum_s\lambda_sW_2^2(\al,\be_s)&=\E\sum_s\lambda_s\norm{X-Y_s}^2\\&\ge\E\min_x\sum_s\lambda_s\norm{x-Y_s}^2\\&=\E\,c_{\mathrm{bar}}(Y_1,\dots,Y_S),\end{aligned}$$ and the law of $(Y_1,\dots,Y_S)$ lies in $\mathcal U(\be_1,\dots,\be_S)$, so the right side is at least $\mathrm{MMOT}_{c_{\mathrm{bar}}}$.

Equality. From an optimal $\pi^\star$, set $X=B(Y_1,\dots,Y_S)$ with $(Y_s)\sim\pi^\star$. Each $(X,Y_s)$ is a coupling of $\al^\star=\push B{\pi^\star}$ and $\be_s$, so $\sum_s\lambda_sW_2^2(\al^\star,\be_s)\le\E\sum_s\lambda_s\norm{X-Y_s}^2=\mathrm{MMOT}_{c_{\mathrm{bar}}}$. With the first part, $\al^\star$ is a barycenter and the values agree. For the converse, glue optimal couplings of a barycenter $\al^\star$ with each $\be_s$. The values are equal, so the inequality above is an equality, which forces $X=B(Y_1,\dots,Y_S)$ almost surely (the minimizer in $x$ is unique), so $\al^\star=\push B{\pi^\star}$ for the induced optimal $\pi^\star$. $\square$

Corollary (sparse discrete barycenters)OT4ML Cor. 12.22, pp. 268–269 (260–261); CompOT p. 165 (161)

If $\be_s$ has $n_s$ atoms, there is a barycenter supported on at most $\sum_sn_s-S+1$ points, each a weighted average $\sum_s\lambda_sx_{s,i_s}$ of one atom of each input.

Proof (rank of the marginal constraints)

The marginal map $\mathbf P\mapsto$ (its $S$ marginals) has rank $\sum_sn_s-S+1$. A family $(u_s)$ is orthogonal to its image when $\sum_su_{s,i_s}=0$ for every multi-index; varying one index at a time makes each $u_s$ constant, $u_s=c_s\mathbf 1$, with $\sum_sc_s=0$: an orthogonal space of dimension $S-1$. A vertex of the feasible polytope therefore has at most that many positive entries (the argument of Lesson 2 §2), an optimal vertex exists, and $B$ maps each positive entry to one atom. $\square$

On the line, everything is explicit. The barycenter’s quantile function is the weighted average $\sum_s\lambda_sF_{\be_s}^{-1}$ (OT4ML Prop. 12.7, p. 254 (246); Lesson 1 §6), and the optimal multimarginal plan is the comonotone one, the law of $(F_{\be_1}^{-1}(U),\dots,F_{\be_S}^{-1}(U))$ for $U$ uniform on $[0,1]$. Indeed $c_{\mathrm{bar}}=\sum_s\lambda_sx_s^2-\big(\sum_s\lambda_sx_s\big)^2$, so minimizing $\E c_{\mathrm{bar}}$ with fixed marginals means maximizing $\sum_{r,s}\lambda_r\lambda_s\E[X_rX_s]$. Each term is largest for the comonotone coupling of the pair, and the comonotone plan achieves all of them at once. For discrete marginals it is the north-west corner rule run on $S$ cumulative distributions together (Lesson 2 §3). Its support is the number of intervals cut in $[0,1]$ by the levels $F_{\be_s}(x)$, at most $\sum_s(n_s-1)+1$, the bound of the corollary.

Link: a chain cost is a path in discrete time

If the cost only couples consecutive marginals, $c=\sum_{s\lt S}\norm{x_s-x_{s+1}}^2$, the multimarginal value is the sum of the pairwise values $\sum_sW_2^2(\al_s,\al_{s+1})$ (derived here). Any joint law has consecutive pairs that are couplings, which gives $\ge$; gluing optimal pairwise plans along the shared marginals (Lesson 3 §1) gives a joint law that attains the sum. This is a time-discretized version of the path problems of §2, with the intermediate marginals prescribed. For three clouds of 5, 6 and 4 points in the plane, HiGHS on the full tensor and two POT problems both give 9.915443 (independent check). Chains and trees are exactly the interaction graphs of treewidth one, for which entropic multimarginal problems can be solved by message passing instead of full tensor contractions (OT4ML Example 12.24 and (12.33)–(12.34), p. 270 (262)). Prescribing uniform marginals at every intermediate time, and closing the chain by a penalty that sends the last point to a prescribed permutation of the first, CompOT’s Remark 10.3 (pp. 165–166 (161–162)) discretizes Brenier’s relaxation of the incompressible Euler equations; its cost graph is a cycle.

Beyond the course: entropic multimarginal problems and the Coulomb cost

Adding $\eps\KL(\pi\mid\al_1\otimes\dots\otimes\al_S)$ gives a Gibbs-form optimizer and a multimarginal Sinkhorn algorithm, which still stores $\prod_sn_s$ entries unless the cost factors over a sparse graph (OT4ML p. 269 (261); CompOT (10.2), p. 164 (160)); Lesson 7 treats the case $S=2$. For the Coulomb cost $\sum_{i\lt j}\norm{x_i-x_j}^{-1}$ with $N$ equal marginals, deterministic co-motion plans are a common ansatz but not always optimal (OT4ML Prop. 12.20, p. 267 (259)). §9Beyond this lesson, and what the resources get wrong

Schrödinger bridges: Benamou–Brenier with noise

Replace the deterministic paths of §2 by a random motion with a reference law $R^\eps$ on paths, for instance Brownian motion $\dd X_t=\sqrt\eps\,\dd B_t$. The Schrödinger bridge is the path law closest to the reference, in relative entropy, among those with the prescribed endpoints (Schrödinger 1931; Léonard; OT4ML Def. 14.10, p. 336 (328)): $$\mathrm{SB}_\eps(\al,\be)=\inf\big\{\eps\KL(M\mid R^\eps):\ (e_0)_\sharp M=\al,\ (e_1)_\sharp M=\be\big\}.$$

Proposition (endpoint reduction of the Schrödinger problem)OT4ML Prop. 14.11, p. 337 (329); compact Prop. 14.11, p. 104; CompOT (7.19)–(7.20), p. 116 (112)

Write $R^\eps_{01}$ for the law of the endpoints $(X_0,X_1)$ under $R^\eps$, and $R^{\eps,x,y}$ for the reference bridge conditioned on $X_0=x$, $X_1=y$. Then $\mathrm{SB}_\eps(\al,\be)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\al,\be)}\eps\KL(\pi\mid R^\eps_{01})$, and for a fixed endpoint coupling $\pi$ the best path law is the mixture of reference bridges $\int R^{\eps,x,y}\dd\pi(x,y)$.

Proof (chain rule of the relative entropy)

Disintegrate a path law $M$ with respect to its endpoints: $M=\int M^{x,y}\dd\pi(x,y)$ with $\pi=(e_0,e_1)_\sharp M$, and likewise $R^\eps=\int R^{\eps,x,y}\dd R^\eps_{01}$. The chain rule gives $\KL(M\mid R^\eps)=\KL(\pi\mid R^\eps_{01})+\int\KL(M^{x,y}\mid R^{\eps,x,y})\dd\pi(x,y)$. The second term is nonnegative and vanishes exactly when every conditional bridge is the reference bridge. So, once $\pi$ is fixed, the best $M$ is the mixture of reference bridges, and it remains to minimize the first term over the couplings. $\square$

For Brownian motion the endpoint law has density proportional to $e^{-\norm{x-y}^2/(2\eps)}$, so $\eps\KL(\pi\mid R^\eps_{01})=\frac12\int\norm{x-y}^2\dd\pi+\eps\KL(\pi\mid\al\otimes\be)+$ a constant (OT4ML p. 338 (330)). The endpoint problem is entropic transport, solved by Sinkhorn’s algorithm in Lesson 7; the bridge then joins each paired $(x,y)$ by a Brownian bridge, whose position at time $t$ is $\Gauss\big((1-t)x+ty,\ t(1-t)\eps\Id\big)$, instead of the straight segment of McCann’s interpolation. As $\eps\to0$ the bridges collapse onto the segments and the problem returns to Benamou–Brenier. Figure 14.3, the notebook sinkhorn-path-space-bridges and the live demo Schrödinger bridges show the lifted couplings.

There is also a fluid form. With noise $\sqrt\sigma\,\dd B_t$, a controlled density solves $\partial_t\rho_t+\operatorname{div}(\rho_tv_t)=\frac\sigma2\Delta\rho_t$, and the current velocity $u_t=v_t-\frac\sigma2\nabla\log\rho_t$ satisfies the ordinary continuity equation. Expanding $\norm{v_t}^2$ gives, in this page’s convention without $\frac12$, $$\begin{aligned}\int_0^1\!\!\int\norm{v_t}^2\rho_t\dd x\dd t&=\int_0^1\!\!\int\Big(\norm{u_t}^2+\frac{\sigma^2}4\norm{\nabla\log\rho_t}^2\Big)\rho_t\dd x\dd t\\&\quad+\sigma\Big(\int\rho_1\log\rho_1-\int\rho_0\log\rho_0\Big),\end{aligned}$$ since the cross term integrates to the change of entropy. The endpoint entropies are fixed, so the Schrödinger bridge is a Benamou–Brenier problem with a Fisher-information penalty (OT4ML p. 337 (329), with the factor $\frac12$; CompOT (7.21), whose constants are discussed in the table below).

Watch out: which noise gives Sinkhorn’s kernel

Brownian noise of variance $\eps$ per unit time produces the endpoint cost $\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$. The kernel $e^{-\norm{x-y}^2/\eps}$ used by Sinkhorn for the cost $\norm{x-y}^2$ corresponds to noise of variance $\eps/2$ (OT4ML p. 338 (330)). Bridge variances and Fisher weights change accordingly, and the resources do not all use the same convention.

What the resources add that the course does not require

  • Path spaces and other actions. Optimal transport over measures on paths, whose optimal path laws charge the segments (OT4ML §14.2, Prop. 14.9, p. 335 (327); CompOT (7.17)–(7.18), p. 115 (111)). Other mobilities $\theta(a,b)=a^{s-p}\norm b^p$ give $W_p$, the dual Sobolev norms $W^{-1,p}$ and $H^{-1}$ (Dolbeault, Nazaret and Savaré; CompOT §7.5, pp. 114–115 (110–111); OT4ML §14.4). On graphs, a logarithmic mean of the densities makes the heat flow a gradient flow (Maas, Mielke; CompOT Remark 7.2, p. 110 (106)), a theme of Lesson 8 §9.
  • Creating and destroying mass. A source term in the continuity equation, penalized like the momentum, gives the Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao distance (Liero, Mielke and Savaré; Chizat, Peyré, Schmitzer and Vialard; CompOT §7.4, pp. 112–114 (108–110); OT4ML §14.6).
  • Mean-field games. Many agents each solve a control problem whose cost depends on the density of the others; the variational ones are Benamou–Brenier problems with an extra potential energy (Lasry and Lions; Benamou, Carlier and Santambrogio; OT4ML §14.7). Augmented-Lagrangian solvers treat them like §4 (Benamou and Carlier 2015; CompOT p. 112 (108)).
  • Couplings that straighten the paths. Rectified flow retrains on the pairs produced by a first flow (Liu, Gong and Liu 2023). The relation between rectified flows and optimal transport, including the hidden rotation of §7 and mixtures, is analyzed by Hertrich, Chambolle and Delon (2025), co-authored by the lecturer (OT4ML p. 412 (404)).
  • Stochastic interpolants add noise $\gamma(t)Z$ to the path, $X_t=I_t(X_0,X_1)+\gamma(t)Z$, which unifies flows and diffusions (Albergo, Boffi and Vanden-Eijnden; OT4ML Def. 16.4, p. 410 (402); compact Def. 16.7, p. 127).
  • One-step generators. Gradient flows of a discrepancy (MMD, Sinkhorn divergence), Stein variational gradient descent and “drifting” models move particles or a generator without a learned time-dependent field (OT4ML §16.2); they are gradient flows, the subject of Lesson 8 §4.
  • A survey by the author of OT4ML on optimal transport and diffusion in machine learning, for the 2026 International Congress of Mathematicians (Peyré, arXiv:2512.06797).

Exercise 1 — Changing the coupling by an affine map§5 · checked

Starting from a coupling $(X_0,X_1)$, let $X_t=tX_1+(1-t)X_0$; the flow-matching velocity is $$\begin{aligned}v_t(x)&=\E[X_1-X_0\mid X_t=x]\\&=\frac1{1-t}\E[X_1-X_t\mid X_t=x]\\&=\frac1{1-t}\big(\E[X_1\mid X_t=x]-x\big).\qquad(1)\end{aligned}$$ Let $A\in\R^{d\times d}$ be invertible, $b\in\R^d$ and $c\gt0$. Show that: (i) the velocity $v_t^{A,b}$ of the coupling $(AX_0+b,AX_1+b)$ is $v_t^{A,b}(x)=Av_t(A^{-1}(x-b))$; (ii) the velocity $v_t^b$ of $(X_0,X_1+b)$ is $v_t^b(x)=v_t(x-tb)+b$; (iii) the velocity $v_t^c$ of $(X_0,cX_1)$ is $$\begin{aligned}v_t^c(x)&=\frac c{1-t+tc}\,v_r\Big(\frac x{1-t+tc}\Big)+\frac{c-1}{1-t+tc}\,x,\\r&=\frac{tc}{1-t+tc}.\end{aligned}$$

Hint 1
Write each new interpolant through the old one: $AX_t+b$ in (i), $X_t+tb$ in (ii). Conditioning on an invertible function of $X_t$ is conditioning on $X_t$.
Hint 2
In (iii), factor $s=1-t+tc$: $(1-t)X_0+tcX_1=sX_r$ with $r=tc/s$. Then write $cX_1-X_0$ as a combination of $X_r$ and $X_1-X_0$.
Solution

(i) The new interpolant is $(1-t)(AX_0+b)+t(AX_1+b)=AX_t+b$ and its pathwise velocity is $A(X_1-X_0)$. The event $\{AX_t+b=x\}$ is $\{X_t=A^{-1}(x-b)\}$, so $v_t^{A,b}(x)=A\,\E[X_1-X_0\mid X_t=A^{-1}(x-b)]=Av_t(A^{-1}(x-b))$.

(ii) The interpolant is $X_t+tb$ and the velocity $X_1+b-X_0$, so $v_t^b(x)=\E[X_1-X_0\mid X_t=x-tb]+b=v_t(x-tb)+b$.

(iii) With $s=1-t+tc$ and $r=tc/s$, $1-r=(1-t)/s$, so $(1-t)X_0+tcX_1=sX_r$. From $X_0=X_r-r(X_1-X_0)$ and $X_1=X_r+(1-r)(X_1-X_0)$, $$\begin{aligned}cX_1-X_0&=(c-1)X_r+\big(c(1-r)+r\big)(X_1-X_0)\\&=(c-1)X_r+\frac cs(X_1-X_0).\end{aligned}$$ Conditioning on $sX_r=x$ gives $v_t^c(x)=\frac{c-1}s\,x+\frac cs\,v_r(x/s)$. A dilation of the target is a rescaling of space and a change of clock, $t\mapsto r$.

So the construction commutes with every affine change of variables applied to both endpoints (i), and a translation or a dilation of the target changes the field explicitly (ii)–(iii). for a Gaussian coupling, where both sides are explicit, the three identities hold at 200 random $(t,x)$ within 3.5×10⁻¹⁵, 3.1×10⁻¹⁶ and 5.6×10⁻¹⁵.

Exercise 2 — What the optimal map becomes§5, §7 · checked

Assume that $\alpha$ is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure and that $\beta$ is a probability measure on $\R^d$. Write $T^*$ for the optimal transport map from $\alpha$ to $\beta$ for the squared Euclidean cost. What becomes $T^*$ in the cases considered in Exercise 1?

Hint 1
By Brenier’s theorem (Lesson 1 §7), a map that pushes $\alpha$ to the target is optimal if and only if it is the gradient of a convex function. Write $T^*=\nabla\varphi$ and check which transformed maps are still such gradients.
Hint 2
In case (i) the cost becomes $\norm{A(x-y)}^2$. When is it a multiple of $\norm{x-y}^2$?
Solution

(ii) The target is translated by $b$. The map $T^*+b=\nabla\big(\varphi+\dotp b\cdot\big)$ pushes $\alpha$ to it and is the gradient of a convex function: it is the optimal map.

(iii) The target is dilated by $c\gt0$. The map $cT^*=\nabla(c\varphi)$ is again the gradient of a convex function: it is optimal.

(i) Both endpoints are moved by $x\mapsto Ax+b$. The map $S(x)=AT^*(A^{-1}(x-b))+b$ pushes the new source to the new target. If $A=\lambda Q$ with $Q$ orthogonal and $\lambda\gt0$, then $S=\nabla\psi$ with $\psi(x)=\lambda^2\varphi\big(\lambda^{-1}Q^\top(x-b)\big)+\dotp bx$, which is convex: $S$ is optimal. Equivalently, the cost $\norm{Ax-Ay}^2=\lambda^2\norm{x-y}^2$ is proportional to the original one. For a general $A$ the cost changes shape and $S$ is not optimal in general.

This is where flow matching and optimal transport part ways. By Exercise 1(i), the flow-matching map of the transformed coupling is $x\mapsto A\varphi_1(A^{-1}(x-b))+b$ for every invertible $A$. Optimality is preserved only by similarities. A flow-matching map that is optimal for one pair of measures stops being optimal after a shear. centred Gaussians, $\Sigma_0=\begin{pmatrix}1&0.3\\0.3&0.6\end{pmatrix}$, $\Sigma_1=\begin{pmatrix}2&-0.5\\-0.5&0.8\end{pmatrix}$. For the similarity $A=1.8\,R_{0.7}$ the Brenier map of the transformed pair and $AT^*A^{-1}$ differ by below 10⁻¹²; for $A=\begin{pmatrix}1.3&0.4\\-0.2&0.9\end{pmatrix}$ they differ by 0.328 in Frobenius norm. Between $\Gauss(0,\Id)$ and $\Gauss(0,\diag(2,0.5))$ the flow-matching map is the Brenier map (§7). After applying $A$ to both, the flow-matching map is $AT^*A^{-1}$ (difference below 10⁻¹²), and it costs 5.10% more than $W_2^2$.

Exercise 3 — On the line, flow matching is optimal§5 · checked

Assume that $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are probability measures on $\R$. Let $(X_0,X_1)$ be a coupling between $\alpha$ and $\beta$ such that the velocity field $v_t(x)=\E[X_1-X_0\mid X_t=x]$ has a unique flow on $[0,1]$. Show that the flow $\varphi_t$ associated to $v_t$ is such that $\varphi_1$ is an optimal transport map between $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

Hint 1
Two trajectories of a one-dimensional ODE with unique solutions cannot cross. What does that say about $x\mapsto\varphi_1(x)$?
Hint 2
$(\alpha_t,v_t)$ solves the continuity equation (Prop. 16.3, §5), so $\varphi_1$ pushes $\alpha$ to $\beta$. On the line, a nondecreasing map that pushes $\alpha$ to $\beta$ is optimal (Lesson 1 §6).
Solution

$\varphi_1$ is a transport map. The curve $\alpha_t=\mathrm{Law}(X_t)$ solves the continuity equation with the velocity $v_t$ (§5). With a unique flow, this curve is the push-forward $\push{(\varphi_t)}\alpha$ (the superposition principle of §2 represents it by trajectories of the ODE, which are then the trajectories of $\varphi$). At $t=1$: $\push{(\varphi_1)}\alpha=\beta$.

$\varphi_1$ is increasing. Let $x\lt y$. If $\varphi_s(x)=\varphi_s(y)$ at some time $s$, the two trajectories pass through the same point at time $s$, and uniqueness of the solution through that point, backward in time, forces $x=y$. By continuity, $\varphi_t(x)\lt\varphi_t(y)$ for every $t$.

Conclusion. An increasing map that pushes $\alpha$ to $\beta$ coincides $\alpha$-almost everywhere with the monotone rearrangement $F_\beta^{-1}\circ F_\alpha$, which is optimal for $\abs{x-y}^2$ and for every convex cost of $x-y$. The coupling does not matter: even the independent coupling gives the optimal map, while its path is not the geodesic (Lab 4: action 3.0382 against $W_2^2=2.5590$). In dimension 2 the argument breaks down because trajectories can turn around each other, and Lab 6 measures the cost. Lab 4: with the independent coupling of the two mixtures, the terminal map of 400 quantile particles equals the monotone map within 2.6×10⁻⁸.

Exercise 4 — The Gaussian velocity, and when its map is optimal§5, §7 · checked

Hint 1
(i) Apply the recalled formula with $Z=X_1$ and $T=X_t$, then use (1). In the sheet’s block matrix, $\Sigma_{01}=\mathrm{Cov}(X_1,X_0)$ is the block below the diagonal.
Hint 2
(ii) Read “jointly diagonalized” as “diagonal in one orthonormal basis”. In that basis the problem splits into one-dimensional Gaussian problems; Exercise 3 applies to each of them, or integrate the flow explicitly as in §7.

(i) The vector $(X_1,X_t)$ is Gaussian and centred, so $\E[X_1\mid X_t=x]=\mathrm{Cov}(X_1,X_t)\Sigma_t^{-1}x$, with $\mathrm{Cov}(X_1,X_t)=(1-t)\mathrm{Cov}(X_1,X_0)+t\Sigma_1=(1-t)\Sigma_{01}+t\Sigma_1$ and $\Sigma_t=\mathrm{Cov}((1-t)X_0+tX_1)$ as stated. (ii) If $\Sigma_0=PD_0P^\top$ and $\Sigma_1=PD_1P^\top$ with $P$ orthogonal and $D_0,D_1$ diagonal, then $\Sigma_t$ and the velocity matrix are diagonal in the same basis, so each coordinate $y_i=(P^\top x)_i$ follows the one-dimensional Gaussian flow from variance $d_{0,i}$ to $d_{1,i}$: $y_i(t)=y_i(0)\sqrt{(1-t)^2d_{0,i}+t^2d_{1,i}}/\sqrt{d_{0,i}}$. Choice of velocity$v_t+w$ with $\operatorname{div}(\al_tw)=0$ moves the same curve (rotations of $\Gauss(0,\Id)$); the least-squares velocity is a gradient, $-\operatorname{div}(\rho_t\nabla\psi_t)=\partial_t\rho_t$; Gaussian paths: $v=\dot m+S(x-m)$, $S\Sigma+\Sigma S=\dot\Sigma$.
Benamou–Brenier$W_2^2=\min\int_0^1\!\int\norm{v_t}^2\dd\al_t\dd t$ (no $\frac12$); minimizer: McCann’s interpolation, constant speed, $v_t=(T-\Id)\circ T_t^{-1}$; static $\le$ dynamic by Jensen in time.
Convex form, dual$m=\rho v$, $J(a,m)=\norm m^2/a$ (convex, 1-homogeneous); dual: $\sup\int\varphi_1\dd\al_1-\int\varphi_0\dd\al_0$ with $\partial_t\varphi+\frac14\norm{\nabla\varphi}^2\le0$, $v=\nabla\varphi/2$; Hopf–Lax $\varphi_1(y)=\inf_x\varphi_0(x)+\norm{x-y}^2$. §14Self-test

  1. Write the weak form of the continuity equation. Why is it the right form for empirical measures?
    Answer
    $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\int\varphi\dd\al_t=\int\dotp{\nabla\varphi}{v_t}\dd\al_t$ for smooth test functions. It needs no density: for $\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i(t)}$ it reduces to $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\varphi(x_i(t))=\dotp{\nabla\varphi(x_i)}{\dot x_i}$, true when every particle follows $v_t$.
  2. Does a curve of measures determine its velocity? Give an example.
    Answer
    No: adding $w$ with $\operatorname{div}(\al_tw)=0$ moves the same curve. The rotation $v(x)=Jx$ keeps $\Gauss(0,\Id)$ in place while every particle turns, at kinetic energy $2$ per unit angular speed (Exercise 6).
  3. Which velocity has the least kinetic energy along a given curve, and what equation does it solve?
    Answer
    A gradient $\nabla\psi_t$, with $-\operatorname{div}(\rho_t\nabla\psi_t)=\partial_t\rho_t$: it is orthogonal in $L^2(\al_t)$ to the divergence-free fields. For Gaussian curves it is affine with a symmetric matrix solving a Lyapunov equation. In Lab 1 the given field costs 0.6976 and the least-squares field 0.6440 on the same path.
  4. State the Benamou–Brenier formula and describe its minimizer.
    Answer
    $W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1)=\min\int_0^1\!\int\norm{v_t}^2\dd\al_t\dd t$ over curves joining $\al_0$ to $\al_1$. The minimizer is McCann’s interpolation $\push{((1-t)\Id+tT)}{\al_0}$: every particle moves on a straight line at constant speed, and the kinetic energy is $W_2^2$ at every time.
  5. Prove “static $\le$ dynamic” for a smooth velocity.
    Answer
    If $T_t$ is the flow, $\push{(T_1)}\al_0=\al_1$ and, by Jensen in time, $\norm{T_1(x)-x}^2\le\int_0^1\norm{v_t(T_t(x))}^2\dd t$. Integrate against $\al_0$: $W_2^2\le\int\norm{T_1-\Id}^2\dd\al_0\le$ action. Without a flow, the superposition principle replaces trajectories by a measure on paths.
  6. Why does the change of variables $m=\rho v$ help?
    Answer
    The constraint $\partial_t\rho+\operatorname{div}m=0$ becomes linear, and the action $\int\!\!\int\norm m^2/\rho$ is the integral of a convex, 1-homogeneous perspective function. The problem becomes convex, with a dual and proximal solvers.
  7. Write the dual of Benamou–Brenier in this page’s convention, and the optimal velocity.
    Answer
    $W_2^2=\sup\int\varphi_1\dd\al_1-\int\varphi_0\dd\al_0$ over $\varphi$ with $\partial_t\varphi+\frac14\norm{\nabla\varphi}^2\le0$, and $v_t=\nabla\varphi_t/2$ where $\rho_t\gt0$. With the action halved (slides 27, 33) the constraint reads $\partial_t\varphi+\frac12\norm{\nabla\varphi}^2\le0$ and $v=\nabla\varphi$.
  8. How much more than $W_2^2$ does the linear interpolation of densities cost in Lab 2?
    Answer
    Its action is 4.3899, 1.72 times $W_2^2=2.55897$: mixing densities is a valid path, but not a geodesic of $W_2$.
  9. Name the three operations of one Douglas–Rachford iteration on the staggered grid.
    Answer
    A proximal step on the action, one cubic equation per cell; a projection on the continuity equation with the endpoints, one Poisson solve by a discrete cosine transform; a projection on the constraint that the centred variables are the averages of the staggered ones, tridiagonal systems. In Lab 3, 1000 iterations give 2.55776 against 2.55798 for the discretized endpoints.
  10. What is the flow-matching velocity, and why does its flow transport $\al_0$ to $\al_1$?
    Answer
    $v_t(x)=\E[\partial_tI_t(X_0,X_1)\mid X_t=x]$, the minimizer of the regression $\E\norm{v_t(X_t)-\partial_tI_t}^2$. Testing the continuity equation against $\varphi$ and conditioning on $X_t$ shows that $(\al_t,v_t)$ solves it, so the flow pushes $\al_0$ along $\al_t$ to $\al_1$ (OT4ML Prop. 16.3; the compact notes omit this conclusion from the statement).
  11. Order $W_2^2$, the cost of the flow map, the action of the flow-matching path and the cost of the coupling.
    Answer
    $W_2^2\le\E\norm{\Phi_1(X_0)-X_0}^2$, then $\le\int_0^1\!\int\norm{v_t}^2\dd\al_t\dd t$, then $\le\E_\pi\norm{X_1-X_0}^2$: optimality of $W_2$, Jensen in time, conditional Jensen. In Lab 4: 2.5590, 2.558984, 3.0382, 4.2369.
  12. Why is flow matching optimal in dimension 1, whatever the coupling?
    Answer
    Trajectories of a one-dimensional ODE with unique solutions cannot cross, so $\Phi_1$ is increasing; an increasing map that pushes $\al_0$ to $\al_1$ is the monotone rearrangement, which is optimal (Exercise 3). Lab 4 finds a deviation of 2.6×10⁻⁸ from the monotone map.
  13. Is the flow-matching field the least-squares field of its own path?
    Answer
    Only when it is a gradient. It is one for a Gaussian source with the independent coupling (Tweedie). Between two Gaussians with non-commuting covariances it is not: in Lab 6 its action is 1.4230 against 1.3400 for the least-squares field.
  14. State Tweedie’s formula and the velocity it gives from Gaussian noise.
    Answer
    For $Z=W+\sigma\varepsilon$, $\E[W\mid Z=z]=z+\sigma^2\nabla\log\rho_\sigma(z)$. With $X_t=(1-t)X_0+tX_1$, $X_0\sim\Gauss(0,\Id)$ independent of the data: $v_t(x)=\frac xt+\frac{1-t}t\nabla\log\rho_t(x)$ (slide 18). Learning the score and learning the velocity are the same task.
  15. Do different noising schedules give different samplers?
    Answer
    They give different trajectories but the same terminal map: in the variable $y=x/b_t$ the probability flow depends only on $\lambda=a_t/b_t$ (§6). In Lab 5, the OU flow and linear flow matching disagree with the schedule-free flow on 0 and 0 of 4000 samples; the notebook’s Euler scheme on 2.
  16. Is the diffusion map of 6-diffusion the optimal map?
    Answer
    No, but it is close: 0.302% of the mass goes to a different atom than with the Laguerre cells, and the excess cost is 0.0013% of $W_2^2$. With a flat triangle of atoms, 5.71% of the mass changes atom. With two atoms the problem is one-dimensional and the map is optimal.
  17. Why is the notebook’s comparison with a 200-particle OT plan misleading?
    Answer
    A discrete plan with masses $\frac13$ must give each atom a third of the particles, while the flow puts 61, 58, 81 of our 200 draws on the three atoms. Plan and flow disagree on 14 particles; the population OT cells disagree with the flow on 1. The difference is mostly sampling noise.
  18. When is the flow-matching map between two Gaussians optimal? What goes wrong otherwise?
    Answer
    With the independent coupling, exactly when $\Sigma_0\Sigma_1=\Sigma_1\Sigma_0$. Otherwise $T_1^{\mathrm{FM}}=\Sigma_0^{1/2}C^{1/2}\Sigma_0^{-1/2}$ is not symmetric: it contains a rotation, and in Lab 6 it costs 19.2% more than $W_2^2$ at $30°$.
  19. Flow matching commutes with every affine change of both endpoints. Does the optimal map?
    Answer
    Only with similarities $x\mapsto\lambda Qx+b$; under a shear the conjugated Brenier map is no longer optimal (Exercises 1–2). The flow-matching map of a sheared commuting pair costs 5.10% more than $W_2^2$.
  20. How is a quadratic barycenter a multimarginal problem?
    Answer
    Eliminate the barycenter point: $\min_x\sum_s\lambda_s\norm{x-x_s}^2=\sum_s\lambda_s\norm{x_s-B}^2$ with $B=\sum_s\lambda_sx_s$. Solve the multimarginal problem with this cost; the barycenter is the push-forward of the optimal plan by $B$ (OT4ML Prop. 12.21).
  21. How many atoms can a discrete barycenter need, and what happens on the line?
    Answer
    At most $\sum_sn_s-S+1$, the rank of the marginal constraints. On the line the optimal plan is comonotone and its support is the number of intervals cut by the levels of the cumulative distributions: 6 for the example of §8 (bound 7), 10 when all levels differ.
  22. What is a Schrödinger bridge, and how is it related to entropic transport?
    Answer
    The path law closest in relative entropy to a reference diffusion among those with the prescribed endpoints. By the chain rule of the relative entropy it reduces to the endpoint coupling minimizing $\eps\KL(\pi\mid R^\eps_{01})$, which for Brownian motion is entropic transport with cost $\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$; the paths are Brownian bridges between the paired endpoints (Lesson 7).
  23. Which two conventions must you check before comparing formulas across the resources?
    Answer
    Whether the action has a factor $\frac12$ (it changes the Hamilton–Jacobi constant and $v=\nabla\varphi$ or $\nabla\varphi/2$), and the direction of time (noise at $t=0$ on the sheet and slides, data at $t=0$ in OT4ML’s diffusion propositions, which flips the sign of $v_t$ and $t\mapsto1-t$).
