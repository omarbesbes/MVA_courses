Sliced optimal transport

Project both measures on a line, sort, and average over all directions. The result is a distance that costs a sort per direction and is estimated from samples at a rate that does not depend on the dimension. It is also a weaker geometry than Wₚ, and how much weaker can be measured.

§0Where this lesson sits

Optimal transport on the line is a sort (Lesson 1 §3). In higher dimension it is a linear program whose cost grows like $n^3$ (Lesson 2 §5), and its estimation from $n$ samples slows down like $n^{-1/d}$ (Lesson 3 §6). Sliced optimal transport keeps only what is cheap. It projects both measures on a direction, solves the one-dimensional problem by sorting, and averages over all directions of the sphere. The lesson, by Kimia Nadjahi, asks three questions about this average: is it a distance, how does it compare with $W_p$, and what does it buy computationally and statistically.

What it reuses, what it prepares

Reuses.

  • One-dimensional transport by sorting and by quantile functions (Lesson 1 §3, §6). Every slice is such a problem.
  • Push-forwards (Lesson 1 §4): a slice of $\al$ is $\push{(P_\theta)}\al$.
  • The Wasserstein distance, its triangle inequality and the topology it metrizes (Lesson 3 §1, §5); the rate $n^{-1/2}$ on the line (§6).
  • Couplings and their images (Lesson 2 §1): projecting a coupling gives a coupling of the slices (§4, §8).
  • Gaussians and the Bures formula (Lesson 1 §8, Lesson 3 §2): slices of Gaussians are Gaussians (§5).
  • Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality (Lesson 4 §7), used for the statistical rate of §7.

Prepares.

  • Lesson 7 (entropic regularization) is the other way to make transport cheap; its statistics are compared with slicing there.
  • Lesson 8 (gradient flows) §4 studies flows of particles that decrease a discrepancy. The sliced flow of §9 is one, computed by sorting.

Watch out: SW is defined with or without the root, and with or without a normalized sphere

The notes and OT4ML define $SW_p$ with the $p$-th root and the uniform probability $\sigma$, so that $SW_p$ is a distance. CompOT (10.13) writes $SW(\al,\be)^2=\int W_2^2\dd\theta$ over “the $d$-dimensional sphere $\mathbb S^d$” of $\R^d$, with the surface measure. A question that comes back

How much of optimal transport survives if we only compare one-dimensional projections? The distance survives: $SW_p$ separates measures and metrizes the same convergence as $W_p$ (§3). The geometry is weaker: $SW_2^2\le W_2^2/d$, and for smooth deformations the ratio can be even smaller (§4). The cost and the statistics improve: a sort per direction, and a rate $n^{-1/2}$ in every dimension (§6–§7). Transport plans do not survive as such, since each slice has its own matching; lifting one slice gives a feasible plan, not an optimal one (§8). Lesson 7 asks the same question for entropic regularization.

§1Why slice: the cost of transport and the curse of dimension

The problem

Machine learning uses transport distances as losses between a model and data: generative models compare $\push{T_w}\Gauss(0,\mathrm{Id})$ with an empirical measure $\hat\mu_n$ at every training step (slides p. 8, frame 3/12). Two obstacles appear. Computation: $W_2$ between two $n$-point clouds is a linear program with $n^2$ unknowns, solved in $O(n^3)$ operations; for a mini-batch of $n=256$ this is about $10^7$ operations, at every step (frames 5/12–6/12). Statistics: the plug-in estimate $W_p(\hat\al_n,\hat\be_n)$ converges slowly when $d$ is large (frame 7/12).

On the line, everything is easy

For $n$ equal masses on $\R$, the optimal plan sorts both clouds and matches them by rank: $W_p^p(\al,\be)=\frac1n\sum_i\abs{x_{(i)}-y_{(i)}}^p$, computed in $O(n\log n)$ (frame 4/12; Lesson 1 §3). For general measures, $W_p^p=\int_0^1\abs{F_\al^{-1}-F_\be^{-1}}^p$ (Lesson 1 §6), and the empirical rate is $n^{-1/2}$ whenever $\int\sqrt{F(1-F)}\lt\infty$ (Lesson 3 §6). Slicing transports this easy case to $\R^d$.

The curse of dimension, measured

In dimension $d$, the empirical measure needs about $\varepsilon^{-d}$ points to be $\varepsilon$-close to $\al$ in $W_1$: for measures on $[0,1]^d$ with $d\ge3$, $\E W_1(\hat\al_n,\al)$ is of order $n^{-1/d}$, and this cannot be improved (Dudley 1969; StatOT Prop. 2.1, p. 54 (48), and §2.6). For $W_p$ the rate also depends on $p$: $n^{-1/(2p)}$ if $d\lt2p$ and $n^{-1/d}$ if $d\gt2p$ (StatOT (2.21), p. 89 (83)). The slides plot $W_2$ and the sliced distance $SW_2$ of §2 between two independent samples of $\Gauss(0,\mathrm{Id}_d)$ (frame 11/12). Their true value is $0$, so the curves show the estimation error alone. Lab 1 recomputes that figure.

§2The sliced Wasserstein distance

Slices of a measure

Definition (slicing operator)notes Def. 1, p. 1; OT4ML p. 230 (222); StatOT p. 87 (81)

For $\theta\in\R^d$, $P_\theta(x)=\dotp\theta x$. The slice of a measure $\al$ on $\R^d$ in the direction $\theta$ is the measure $\al_\theta=\push{(P_\theta)}\al$ on $\R$: if $X\sim\al$, then $\dotp\theta X\sim\al_\theta$.

The push-forward lifts the projection of points to measures (Lesson 1 §4). The slice of $\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$ is $\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{\dotp\theta{x_i}}$, and for any test function $g$ on $\R$, $\int g\dd\al_\theta=\int g(\dotp\theta x)\dd\al(x)$ (notes (1)). Slices are what a scanner records: for a density $\rho$, the slice has density $s\mapsto\int_{\{\dotp\theta x=s\}}\rho\dd\mathcal H^{d-1}$, the Radon transform of $\rho$ (slides p. 14, frame 8/12; OT4ML Def. 11.25, p. 231 (223)).

Averaging over the sphere

Definition (sliced Wasserstein distance)notes Def. 2 and Remark 3, pp. 1–2; OT4ML Def. 11.23, p. 230 (222); compact Def. 11.19, p. 68; StatOT (2.20), p. 87 (81)

For $p\ge1$ and $\al,\be\in\Pc_p(\R^d)$, $$SW_p(\al,\be)=\Big(\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}W_p^p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)\dd\sigma(\theta)\Big)^{1/p},$$ where $\sigma$ is the uniform probability measure on the unit sphere $\mathbb S^{d-1}$. More generally, $SW_c=\int W_c(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)\dd\sigma$ for a cost $c$ on $\R\times\R$ (notes Def. 2).

The uniform law is defined through polar coordinates, $\int_{\R^d}f\dd x=\int_0^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}f(r\theta)r^{d-1}\dd\tilde\sigma(\theta)\dd r$, by normalizing the surface measure: $\sigma=\tilde\sigma/\abs{\mathbb S^{d-1}}$ with $\abs{\mathbb S^{d-1}}=2\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2)$ (notes Remark 1). In dimension $1$ the sphere is $\{-1,1\}$, both slices have the same transport cost, and $SW_p=W_p$. Each slice is a one-dimensional problem, solved by sorting or by quantiles (§1).

Why directions on the sphere, and not all of $\R^d$

The notes leave the reason as an exercise (Remark 2); here it is. Since $P_{r\theta}=rP_\theta$ and $W_p$ scales with dilations, $W_p\big(\push{(P_{r\theta})}\al,\push{(P_{r\theta})}\be\big)=r\,W_p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)$ for $r\gt0$. A direction $x=r\theta$ of $\R^d$ therefore repeats the information of $\theta$, multiplied by $r^p$. In polar coordinates, $$\int_{\R^d}W_p^p(\al_x,\be_x)\dd x=\abs{\mathbb S^{d-1}}\,SW_p^p(\al,\be)\int_0^{\infty}r^{p+d-1}\dd r=+\infty$$ as soon as $\al\ne\be$. Any rotation-invariant law of directions with a finite $p$-th moment gives a finite average. For instance, if $G\sim\Gauss(0,\mathrm{Id}_d)$, then $G/\norm G$ is uniform and independent of $\norm G$, so $\E\,W_p^p(\al_G,\be_G)=\E\norm G^p\,SW_p^p(\al,\be)$: Gaussian directions give $SW_p^p$ up to the factor $\E\norm G^p$, which is $d$ for $p=2$ (Lab 2). The sphere is the normalization that leaves no factor.

The Radon point of view, and a Hilbert embedding

The family of all slices, $\theta\mapsto\al_\theta$, is the Radon transform of $\al$, and $SW_p$ is the $L^p(\sigma)$ distance between the Radon transforms of $\al$ and $\be$, measured slice by slice with $W_p$ (OT4ML pp. 231–232 (223–224); CompOT p. 171 (167); slides p. 14). For $p=2$ more is true. On the line $W_2$ is the $L^2(0,1)$ distance between quantile functions, so $$SW_2^2(\al,\be)=\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}\int_0^1\big(F_{\al_\theta}^{-1}(u)-F_{\be_\theta}^{-1}(u)\big)^2\dd u\dd\sigma(\theta):$$ $SW_2$ is a Hilbert distance between the fields of quantile functions (OT4ML Remark 11.26, p. 232 (224)). Consequently $e^{-\gamma SW_2^2}$ is a positive-definite kernel on probability measures for every $\gamma\gt0$, which makes kernel methods on distributions available.

§3$SW_p$ is a distance

The metric axioms

Theorem (SWₚ is a distance)notes Thm. 1, pp. 2–3 (after Bonnotte 2013); OT4ML Prop. 11.24, p. 230 (222); compact Prop. 11.20, p. 68; StatOT Thm. 2.25, p. 87 (81)

For $p\ge1$, $SW_p$ is a distance on $\Pc_p(\R^d)$. Moreover $SW_p(\al_n,\al)\to0$ if and only if $\al_n\rightharpoonup\al$ and the $p$-th moments converge, the topology of $W_p$ (Lesson 3 §5).

Proof (slice by slice, Minkowski, and Cramér–Wold)

Symmetry comes from that of $W_p$ in each slice. Triangle inequality. For every $\theta$, $W_p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)\le W_p(\al_\theta,\xi_\theta)+W_p(\xi_\theta,\be_\theta)$. Taking the $L^p(\sigma)$ norm and using Minkowski’s inequality, $\norm{a+b}_{L^p(\sigma)}\le\norm a_{L^p(\sigma)}+\norm b_{L^p(\sigma)}$, gives $SW_p(\al,\be)\le SW_p(\al,\xi)+SW_p(\xi,\be)$.

Separation. If $SW_p(\al,\be)=0$, then $\al_\theta=\be_\theta$ for $\sigma$-almost every $\theta$. The map $\theta\mapsto\al_\theta$ is continuous: coupling $\dotp\theta X$ with $\dotp{\theta'}X$ gives $W_p(\al_\theta,\al_{\theta'})\le(\E\norm X^p)^{1/p}\norm{\theta-\theta'}$ (StatOT p. 87 (81)). So equality holds for every $\theta$, by density of a set of full measure. Then for all $s\in\R$, $\hat\al(s\theta)=\int e^{-2i\pi s\dotp\theta x}\dd\al(x)=\widehat{\al_\theta}(s)=\widehat{\be_\theta}(s)=\hat\be(s\theta)$: the Fourier transforms of $\al$ and $\be$ agree everywhere, and $\al=\be$. This is the Cramér–Wold theorem: a law on $\R^d$ is determined by its one-dimensional projections.

Topology (OT4ML’s proof). If $W_p(\al_n,\al)\to0$, then $SW_p(\al_n,\al)\to0$ by the comparison of §4. Conversely, $\int\!\!\int\abs{\dotp\theta x}^p\dd\al_n\dd\sigma=\kappa_{d,p}\int\norm x^p\dd\al_n$ shows that the moments are bounded, so the sequence is tight; every weak limit has the same slices as $\al$, hence equals $\al$; and the convergence of $SW_p(\al_n,\delta_0)$ gives that of the moments. $\square$

Watch out: three slips in the notes’ proof (pp. 2–3)

In (b), the second line is written “$=$” where the triangle inequality of $W_p$ only gives “$\le$”. In (9), the integral of $e^{-2i\pi t}\dd[\push{(p_\theta)}\mu](t)$ is over $\R^d$; the slice lives on $\R$. And the step from “for almost every $\theta$” (7) to the Fourier identity for every $\theta$ needs a continuity argument, given above and in StatOT p. 87 (81) (the characteristic functions are continuous); OT4ML’s proof states it.

The theorem says that $SW_p$ and $W_p$ have the same convergent sequences. It says nothing about how fast they go to zero together: §4 shows that, quantitatively, the two distances can be very different.

§4$SW_p$ against $W_p$

Slicing never increases the cost

Proposition (comparison with Wₚ)notes Prop. 1, p. 3; OT4ML Prop. 11.24, p. 230 (222); compact Prop. 11.20, p. 68

For $\al,\be\in\Pc_p(\R^d)$, $SW_p^p(\al,\be)\le\kappa_{d,p}\,W_p^p(\al,\be)$, where $$\begin{gathered}\kappa_{d,p}=\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}\abs{\theta_1}^p\dd\sigma(\theta)=\frac{\Gamma(d/2)\,\Gamma((p+1)/2)}{\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma((d+p)/2)}\le1,\\ \kappa_{d,2}=\frac1d.\end{gathered}$$

Proof (project an optimal coupling)

Let $\pi$ be an optimal coupling of $\al$ and $\be$. Its image $\push{(P_\theta\times P_\theta)}\pi$ is a coupling of $\al_\theta$ and $\be_\theta$ (the sheet’s Exercise 2(a)), so $$W_p^p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)\le\int\abs{\dotp\theta{x-y}}^p\dd\pi(x,y).$$ Integrate over $\theta$ and exchange the integrals. By rotation invariance of $\sigma$, $\int\abs{\dotp\theta z}^p\dd\sigma(\theta)=\norm z^p\int\abs{\theta_1}^p\dd\sigma=\kappa_{d,p}\norm z^p$ for every $z$. Hence $SW_p^p\le\kappa_{d,p}\int\norm{x-y}^p\dd\pi=\kappa_{d,p}W_p^p$. The value of $\kappa_{d,p}$ is a beta integral: the first coordinate of a uniform point of $\mathbb S^{d-1}$ has density proportional to $(1-t^2)^{(d-3)/2}$ on $[-1,1]$. For $p=2$, $\sum_i\theta_i^2=1$ and symmetry give $\E\theta_1^2=1/d$. $\square$

The notes write the constant as $c_{d,p}=\frac1d\int\norm\theta_p^p\dd\sigma$, which is the same number, since $\norm\theta_p^p=\sum_i\abs{\theta_i}^p$ and all coordinates have the law of $\theta_1$. They also write the spherical identity as an inequality (their (15)); it is an equality. For $p\ge2$, $\abs{\theta_1}^p\le\theta_1^2$ gives $\kappa_{d,p}\le1/d$ (the notes’ Remark 4, left as an exercise). For large $d$, Stirling’s formula gives $\kappa_{d,1}\sim\sqrt{2/(\pi d)}$.

Equality cases. If $\be$ is a translate of $\al$ by $z$, every slice is translated by $\dotp\theta z$, and $W_p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)=\abs{\dotp\theta z}$. Then $SW_p^p=\kappa_{d,p}\norm z^p=\kappa_{d,p}W_p^p$: the bound is attained. In high dimension this is a strong statement. A translation by a unit vector in $\R^{100}$ has $W_2=1$ but $SW_2=0.1$, because only directions nearly aligned with the translation see it.

No reverse inequality with the same exponent

On a ball $B_R$ the two distances are comparable, but with a degraded exponent. Bonnotte’s estimate is $$W_p^p(\al,\be)\le C_{d,p}\,R^{p-\frac1{d+1}}\,SW_p(\al,\be)^{\frac1{d+1}}$$ (notes Remark 4, (21); OT4ML Prop. 11.24, after Bonnotte’s thesis, Lemma 5.1.4). For $p=1$ the optimal exponent is $1/d$ (Carlier, Figalli, Mérigot and Wang, cited by OT4ML p. 231 (223)), and it cannot be improved. A small $SW_p$ therefore forces a small $W_p$, but only at a rate that degrades with the dimension: the two distances define the same convergence (§3) without being equivalent up to constants.

Small deformations

The comparison is clearest for infinitesimal displacements. Let $T_t=\mathrm{Id}+t\nabla\varphi$ with $\nabla^2\varphi$ bounded, and $\al_t=\push{(T_t)}\al$. For small $t$, $T_t$ is the gradient of a convex function, hence Brenier’s map, and $W_2^2(\al,\al_t)=t^2\int\norm{\nabla\varphi}^2\dd\al$. Projecting the coupling $(\mathrm{Id},T_t)$ gives $SW_2^2(\al,\al_t)\le\frac{t^2}d\int\norm{\nabla\varphi}^2\dd\al$ (OT4ML Prop. 11.27, pp. 232–233 (224–225); compact Prop. 11.22). Two cases show that the ratio depends on the measure, not only on $d$.

  • Atoms with fixed weights. If $\al=\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i}$ and the atoms move with velocities $v_i$, then $SW_2^2/W_2^2\to1/d$. For almost every $\theta$ the projected order of the atoms does not change for small $t$, so each slice keeps the projected diagonal coupling (OT4ML Remark 11.28).
  • Gaussians. If $\al=\Gauss(0,\mathrm{Id})$ and $\varphi=\frac12x^\top Ax$, the slice of $\al_t$ is $\Gauss(0,\norm{(\mathrm{Id}+tA)\theta}^2)$, and $W_2^2(\al_\theta,\al_{t,\theta})/t^2\to(\theta^\top A\theta)^2$. The fourth moments of the sphere, $\E\theta_i^4=\frac3{d(d+2)}$ and $\E\theta_i^2\theta_j^2=\frac1{d(d+2)}$, give $$\begin{gathered}\lim_{t\to0}\frac{SW_2^2(\al,\al_t)}{t^2}=\frac{(\operatorname{tr}A)^2+2\operatorname{tr}(A^2)}{d(d+2)}\\ \lt\ \frac{\operatorname{tr}(A^2)}d=\lim_{t\to0}\frac{W_2^2(\al,\al_t)}{d\,t^2}\end{gathered}$$ unless $A$ is a multiple of the identity, by Cauchy–Schwarz on the eigenvalues (OT4ML Remark 11.29, pp. 233–234 (225–226)).

A smooth measure can be deformed inside each slice in ways that the slice does not see, for instance by a shear. The same mechanism makes $SW_2$ a metric that is not a length metric: its intrinsic length satisfies $SW_2\le\ell_{SW_2}\le d^{-1/2}W_2$, with the second inequality strict along Gaussian deformations (OT4ML p. 234 (226), after Park and Slepčev).

§5Gaussians

Slices of Gaussians are Gaussians

Proposition (sliced Wasserstein between Gaussians)OT4ML Prop. 11.30, p. 234 (226); compact Prop. 11.23, p. 69

For $\al=\Gauss(m_\al,\Sigma_\al)$ and $\be=\Gauss(m_\be,\Sigma_\be)$, $$\begin{aligned}SW_2^2(\al,\be)={}&\frac{\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2}d\\&+\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}\Big(\sqrt{\theta^\top\Sigma_\al\theta}-\sqrt{\theta^\top\Sigma_\be\theta}\Big)^2\dd\sigma(\theta).\end{aligned}$$

The slice of $\Gauss(m,\Sigma)$ is $\Gauss(\theta^\top m,\theta^\top\Sigma\theta)$, and on the line $W_2^2\big(\Gauss(a,s^2),\Gauss(b,t^2)\big)=(a-b)^2+(s-t)^2$ (Lesson 1 §8). The mean term integrates to $\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2/d$ because $\int\theta\theta^\top\dd\sigma=\mathrm{Id}/d$. For general covariances the angular integral has no closed form; it is computed by quadrature or Monte Carlo. Two cases do have one (OT4ML Remark 11.31, p. 235 (227)).

  • Proportional covariances, $\Sigma_\al=s^2\Sigma$ and $\Sigma_\be=t^2\Sigma$: $SW_2^2=\big(\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+(s-t)^2\operatorname{tr}\Sigma\big)/d$. With $\Sigma=\mathrm{Id}$ this is the sheet’s Exercise 1: $SW_2\big(\Gauss(0,s^2\mathrm{Id}),\Gauss(0,t^2\mathrm{Id})\big)=\abs{s-t}$, while $W_2=\sqrt d\,\abs{s-t}$.
  • Rank one, $\Sigma_\al=a^2uu^\top$ and $\Sigma_\be=b^2vv^\top$ with $\chi=\abs{\dotp uv}$: $$\begin{aligned}SW_2^2&=\frac1d\Big(\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+a^2+b^2\\&\qquad-\frac{4ab}\pi\big(\sqrt{1-\chi^2}+\chi\arcsin\chi\big)\Big)\\&\le\frac1d\Big(\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+a^2+b^2-2ab\chi\Big)=\frac{W_2^2}d.\end{aligned}$$ For orthogonal directions and equal scales, $SW_2^2=2a^2(1-2/\pi)/d$.

The constant $\frac4\pi(\sqrt{1-\chi^2}+\chi\arcsin\chi)$ comes from $\E\abs{\dotp uG\dotp vG}=\frac2\pi(\sqrt{1-\chi^2}+\chi\arcsin\chi)$ for a standard Gaussian vector $G$, divided by $\E\norm G^2=d$ to pass from Gaussian to unit directions (§2).

Means separate from shapes. The mean term is not special to Gaussians. For any $\al,\be\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$ with means $u,v$ and centered versions $\al_0,\be_0$, $SW_2^2(\al,\be)=\frac1d\norm{u-v}^2+SW_2^2(\al_0,\be_0)$ (the sheet’s Exercise 3, §11). On the clouds of Lab 5, the total 0.7030 splits into 0.6855 for the means and 0.0175 for the shapes; the difference between the two sides is below 10⁻¹².

angular quadrature on 4000 directions gives 1.064009 for the rank-one formula with $a=1$, $b=2$, $\chi=\frac12$ in $\R^2$, against the closed form 1.064009 and the bound $W_2^2/d=1.5000$. In $\R^3$, a spherical quadrature on $700\times1400$ nodes gives 0.70934 against 0.70934. The orthogonal case gives 0.3634 against $2(1-2/\pi)/2=0.3634$. For $\Gauss(0,0.25\,\mathrm{Id}_3)$ against $\Gauss(0,2.25\,\mathrm{Id}_3)$, the quadrature gives $SW_2=1.000000=\abs{0.5-1.5}$, while $W_2=\sqrt3=1.7321$.

§6Computing $SW_p$: sort, and average over random directions

The algorithm

For two clouds of $n$ points, each slice is solved exactly by sorting. The average over the sphere is replaced by an average over $K$ random directions (notes §2, (22)–(24); slides p. 18, frame 12/12): $$\widehat{SW}{}_p^p(\al,\be)=\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^KW_p^p(\al_{\theta_k},\be_{\theta_k}),\qquad\theta_1,\dots,\theta_K\ \text{i.i.d.}\sim\sigma.$$ The three steps are: sample the directions, project both clouds on each, sort and average. They cost $O(Kdn)$ for the projections and $O(Kn\log n)$ for the sorts, against $O(n^3)$ for $W_p$ in dimension $d\gt1$ (notes Remark 5; StatOT Exercise 5 of §2.10).

Proposition (uniform directions from Gaussian vectors)notes Prop. 2, p. 4

If $X\sim\Gauss(0,\mathrm{Id}_d)$, then $X/\norm X$ is uniformly distributed on $\mathbb S^{d-1}$, and it is independent of $\norm X$.

Proof (polar coordinates, or rotation invariance)

For a bounded continuous $f$ on the sphere, polar coordinates give $$\begin{aligned}\E f\big(X/\norm X\big)&=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\int_0^{\infty}e^{-r^2/2}r^{d-1}\dd r\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}f\dd\tilde\sigma\\&=c_d\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}f\dd\tilde\sigma,\end{aligned}$$ with $c_d=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\,2^{d/2-1}\Gamma(d/2)=\Gamma(d/2)/(2\pi^{d/2})=1/\abs{\mathbb S^{d-1}}$. So the law of $X/\norm X$ is $\sigma$. The factorization of the integral in $r$ and $\theta$ is the independence. Alternatively, $\Gauss(0,\mathrm{Id})$ is invariant under rotations, hence so is the law of $X/\norm X$, and $\sigma$ is the only rotation-invariant probability on the sphere. $\square$

How many directions?

$\widehat{SW}{}_p^p$ is an unbiased estimate of $SW_p^p$, and its variance is $\frac1K\operatorname{Var}_\theta\big[W_p^p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)\big]$. By Cauchy–Schwarz, $$\E\big|\widehat{SW}{}_p^p-SW_p^p\big|\le\Big(\frac{\operatorname{Var}_\theta[W_p^p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)]}K\Big)^{1/2},$$ an error of order $K^{-1/2}$ whatever the dimension. For $p=1$ and supports in $B_R$, the slice values lie in $[0,2R]$, so the variance is at most $R^2$ and the error at most $R/\sqrt K$ (OT4ML Remark 10.17, p. 207 (199)). For large $K$ the central limit theorem gives the constant: the mean absolute error is about $\sqrt{2/\pi}\approx0.80$ times the bound.

Watch out: the slides’ Monte Carlo bound has the wrong power (p. 18, frame 12/12)

The slides state $\E\big|\widehat{SW}{}_p^p-SW_p^p\big|\le\frac1k\operatorname{Var}_\theta\big[W_p^p(\mu^\theta,\nu^\theta)\big]$. The correct bound is the square root, $(\operatorname{Var}_\theta/k)^{1/2}$; the printed one is the bound on the mean squared error. A scaling check shows the problem: multiplying both clouds by $\lambda$ multiplies the error by $\lambda^p$ and the variance by $\lambda^{2p}$. Lab 4 measures the failure: with one direction, the error is 5.2×10⁻² where $\operatorname{Var}/k$ is 3.5×10⁻³, and with 256 directions 2.9×10⁻³ against 1.4×10⁻⁵.

Beyond the course: fewer directions for the same accuracy

Independent directions are the simplest choice, not the best one. In $\R^2$, equispaced angles with a random rotation turn the average into a quadrature rule, and Lab 4 shows its error falling much faster than $K^{-1/2}$. In higher dimension the slides’ references propose orthogonal directions (Rowland et al. 2019), quasi-Monte Carlo point sets on the sphere (Nguyen et al. 2024), control variates built from spherical harmonics (Leluc et al. 2024), and a comparison of these sampling strategies (Sisouk, Delon and Tierny 2025).

§7Statistics: a rate that does not depend on the dimension

The empirical rate of $SW_1$

Theorem (dimension-free empirical rate)OT4ML Thm. 10.16, p. 207 (199); compact Thm. 10.13, p. 59; StatOT Prop. 2.26, p. 88 (82); slides p. 17, frame 11/12

If $\al,\be$ are supported in the ball $B(0,R)$ and $\hat\al_n$, $\hat\be_m$ are empirical measures of $n$ and $m$ independent samples, then $$\E\big|SW_1(\hat\al_n,\hat\be_m)-SW_1(\al,\be)\big|\le R\Big(\frac1{\sqrt n}+\frac1{\sqrt m}\Big).$$

Proof (the one-dimensional rate, slice by slice)

By the triangle inequality for $SW_1$, $\abs{SW_1(\hat\al_n,\hat\be_m)-SW_1(\al,\be)}\le SW_1(\hat\al_n,\al)+SW_1(\hat\be_m,\be)$. Fix $\theta$. The slice of $\hat\al_n$ is the empirical measure of the $n$ projected samples, supported in $[-R,R]$, and on the line $W_1=\int\abs{\hat F-F}$ (Lesson 1 §6). Since $n\hat F(s)$ is binomial with parameter $F(s)$, Cauchy–Schwarz gives $$\begin{aligned}\E\,W_1\big((\hat\al_n)_\theta,\al_\theta\big)&=\int_{-R}^R\E\abs{\hat F(s)-F(s)}\dd s\\&\le\int_{-R}^R\sqrt{\frac{F(s)(1-F(s))}n}\dd s\le\frac R{\sqrt n},\end{aligned}$$ because $F(1-F)\le\frac14$. Averaging over $\theta$ gives $\E\,SW_1(\hat\al_n,\al)\le R/\sqrt n$; the same holds for $\be$. $\square$

The slides state the corresponding rate $n^{-1/(2p)}$ for $SW_p$ (frame 11/12), and Lab 1 measures a slope close to $-1/2$ for $SW_2$ in dimensions 2, 10 and 50. In comparison, the plug-in $W_p$ converges at the rate $n^{-1/d}$ when $d\gt2p$ (StatOT (2.21), p. 89 (83)). Directions add their own budget: with $K$ directions, OT4ML Remark 10.17 gives $\E\big|\widehat{SW}_1-SW_1(\al,\be)\big|\le R\big(K^{-1/2}+n^{-1/2}+m^{-1/2}\big)$, so $K$ of the order of $\min(n,m)$ directions keeps the rate (OT4ML (10.7)).

Watch out: the condition on d in the slides’ rate for Wₚ (p. 12, frame 7/12)

The slides state, after Dudley (1969), that for measures with bounded support in $\R^d$ and $d\gt2$, $\E\abs{W_p(\mu_n,\nu_n)-W_p(\mu,\nu)}=O(n^{-1/d})$. Dudley’s result concerns $W_1$. For $W_p$ the rate $n^{-1/d}$ requires $d\gt2p$, and for $2\lt d\lt2p$ it is $n^{-1/(2p)}$ (StatOT (2.21), p. 89 (83)). For $p=2$ and $d=3$ the rate is $n^{-1/4}$, not $n^{-1/3}$. The conclusion of the frame, that $W_p$ suffers from the dimension, is unchanged.

The dimension comes back through the signal

A dimension-free rate for the estimation error does not make the distance itself independent of the dimension. A unit translation in $\R^d$ has $SW_2=1/\sqrt d$ (§4), while the estimation noise of $SW_2$ at sample size $n$ is of order $n^{-1/2}$. To see the translation one needs roughly $n\gtrsim d$ samples. With $n=500$ samples of $\Gauss(0,\mathrm{Id}_d)$ and of the same law translated by $e_1$ (20 pairs, 200 directions), $SW_2$ is 0.729, 0.332 and 0.184 for $d=2,10,50$, against the true values 0.707, 0.316 and 0.141 and a noise level of about 0.109 (Lab 1). In dimension 50 the signal is barely above the noise. The plug-in $W_2$ is 1.043, 2.429 and 7.855 for a true value of $1$: there the noise dominates as soon as $d\ge10$.

§8From a number to a plan: projected plans, lifted plans, Min-SW and max-sliced

The problem

$SW_p$ is a number. Each slice has its own monotone matching, and the matchings of different slices are not consistent with one another, so no transport plan between $\al$ and $\be$ comes out of the definition. Two natural attempts to get one are to project an optimal plan, or to lift the matching of one slice.

Projecting an optimal plan

The image of an optimal coupling $\pi$ of $\al,\be$ by $P_\theta\times P_\theta$ is a coupling of the slices: this was the first step of the proof of §4, and it is the sheet’s Exercise 2(a). It is not optimal for the slices in general (Exercise 2(b)). Two pairs $x_1=(0,0)\mapsto y_1=(1,0)$ and $x_2=(1,10)\mapsto y_2=(0,10)$ are optimal in the plane, since crossing would cost $100$ per pair instead of $1$. Their projections on the first axis are matched in decreasing order, at cost $1$, while the sorted matching costs $0$. Averaged over directions, the projected plan costs exactly $W_2^2/d$, by the identity $\int\theta\theta^\top\dd\sigma=\mathrm{Id}/d$; the slice optima average to $SW_2^2$, which is smaller.

Lifting one slice: Min-SW

Conversely, sorting both clouds along one direction $\theta$ and matching them by rank gives a plan in the plane, $$\pi_\theta=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{(x_{\sigma_\theta(i)},\,y_{\tau_\theta(i)})},$$ where $\sigma_\theta,\tau_\theta$ sort the projected points (OT4ML (11.36), p. 237 (229), after Mahey, Chapel, Gasso, Bonet and Courty). It is a feasible coupling, so its cost bounds $W_2^2$ from above, and the best direction gives the Min-SW discrepancy, $\mathrm{Min}\text{-}SW_2^2=\min_\theta\int\norm{x-y}^2\dd\pi_\theta$. For general measures ties must be handled by optimizing over all lifts compatible with the projected monotone coupling (OT4ML Def. 11.34, p. 237 (229); compact Def. 11.26, p. 71).

Proposition (Min-SW: an upper bound, not a distance)OT4ML Props. 11.35–11.36, pp. 238–239 (230–231); compact Props. 11.27–11.28, p. 71

$W_2\le\mathrm{Min}\text{-}SW_2$, with equality between nondegenerate Gaussians. $\mathrm{Min}\text{-}SW_2$ is symmetric and separates measures, but it does not satisfy the triangle inequality, and it does not metrize $W_2$ convergence: there are $\al_n\to\Gauss(0,\mathrm{Id})$ in $W_2$ with $\inf_n\mathrm{Min}\text{-}SW_2(\al_n,\Gauss(0,\mathrm{Id}))\gt0$.

For Gaussians, sorting along an eigenvector of the Brenier map $x\mapsto m_\be+A(x-m_\al)$ of Lesson 1 §8 reproduces that map, since the projections are related by the increasing affine map $s\mapsto\lambda s$. The failure of the triangle inequality is shown by four points; Lab 5 recomputes the book’s example. The topological failure comes from the transverse directions: one slice of an $n$-point cloud cannot resolve the $d-1$ coordinates orthogonal to $\theta$ of a continuous law.

The best single direction: max-sliced and subspace distances

Instead of averaging the slices, one can keep the most informative one: $\mathrm{MaxSW}_p(\al,\be)=\max_{\theta}W_p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)$ (Deshpande et al. 2019, in the slides’ references; OT4ML Def. 11.32, p. 235 (227)). It is a distance with the topology of $W_p$, $SW_p\le\mathrm{MaxSW}_p\le W_p$ (OT4ML Prop. 11.33, pp. 235–237 (227–229)), and it keeps the full translation part, which averaging dilutes by $\kappa_{d,p}$. For $p=1$ it can be estimated from samples at the parametric rate (StatOT Exercise 9 of §2.10, p. 93 (87)). Projecting on $k$-dimensional subspaces instead of lines interpolates between $SW_p$ ($k=1$) and $W_p$ ($k=d$). For $p=2$ the averaged version satisfies $SW_{2,2,k}^2\le\frac kd W_2^2$ (Prop. 11.33), and the max over subspaces is the projection-robust distance $\mathrm{PRW}_{2,k}$, whose min–max relaxation is the subspace-robust distance of Paty and Cuturi (2019) (OT4ML Def. 11.48, p. 247 (239); Lin et al. 2020 in the slides’ references).

§9Sliced gradient flows, and what slicing is used for

Moving particles by sorting

Fix a target $\be=\frac1n\sum_j\delta_{y_j}$ and move the points of $\al_x=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$ to decrease $E(x)=\frac12SW_2^2(\al_x,\be)$. In each slice, the point of rank $r$ in the projection of $x$ is matched with the point of rank $r$ in the projection of $y$, so $$\nabla_{x_i}E(x)=\frac1n\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}\dotp{x_i-y_{\tau_\theta\circ\sigma_\theta^{-1}(i)}}{\theta}\,\theta\dd\sigma(\theta),$$ and gradient descent with a few random directions per step is a stochastic method (CompOT (10.15), pp. 170–171 (166–167)). Each step only needs sorts. CompOT reports that numerically this flow has no local minimizer and converges to $\be$, and that it was used for color transfer and texture synthesis (Rabin et al. 2011). It is related to the iterative distribution transfer of Pitié, Kokaram and Dahyot, which matches one-dimensional projections one after the other (CompOT p. 171 (167); slides p. 15, frame 9/12). OT4ML presents it as the Wasserstein gradient flow of $\frac12SW_2^2(\cdot,\be)$, whose velocity in each slice is the one-dimensional displacement $T_\theta(s)-s$ lifted along $\theta$ (pp. 417–418 (409–410)). Cozzi and Santambrogio prove long-time convergence under their hypotheses when the target is Gaussian, and show that the limit map is in general not the optimal transport map (OT4ML p. 418 (410)). Since $\int\theta\theta^\top\dd\sigma=\mathrm{Id}/d$, a pure translation is corrected at the rate $1/d$ per unit of time, $d$ times slower than by the $W_2$ flow.

The $W_2$ flow of $\frac12W_2^2(\cdot,\be)$ is simpler: it moves each point in a straight line to its optimal partner, $x_i(t)=y_{\sigma(i)}+e^{-t}(x_i(0)-y_{\sigma(i)})$ (Lesson 8, Exercise 7). The sliced flow does not know $\sigma$; its trajectories bend as the slices reorder the points (OT4ML Fig. 16.6, p. 418 (410)).

Question: does the sliced flow reach the target, along which paths, and how far from optimal is the matching it finds?

Setting: 300 source points in two Gaussian blobs and 300 target points on a noisy ring (generated, seed 21). The sliced flow takes 600 steps $x_i\leftarrow x_i-\frac1K\sum_k\dotp{x_i-y_{r(i,k)}}{\theta_k}\theta_k$, where $r(i,k)$ is the target point with the same rank as $x_i$ along $\theta_k$, using $K=64$ equispaced directions with a random rotation at each step (seed 3); $SW_2^2$ is measured with the directions of each step. The $W_2$ flow moves along the exact assignment (Hungarian method). $W_2^2$ to the target is computed exactly every 10 steps.

What we found (measured)

  • At the start $W_2^2=2.16402$. The sliced flow reaches $SW_2^2$ of 3.7×10⁻⁵ after 50 steps, 2.0×10⁻⁵ after 100, 6.1×10⁻⁶ after 200 and 9.4×10⁻¹⁰ after 400. $W_2^2$ to the target is 2.0×10⁻⁴ after 200 steps and below 10⁻¹⁴ after 600: every particle lands on a target point.
  • At step 200, $SW_2^2$ is 6.1×10⁻⁶ while $W_2^2$ is 2.0×10⁻⁴, a ratio far below the $1/d=\frac12$ of small rigid deformations (§4). In most slices the sorted matching is much cheaper than the projection of the optimal plan (Exercise 2): the slices barely see the remaining displacement.
  • The matching found by the flow costs 2.16613, 0.10% above the optimal assignment, and agrees with it for only 71 of the 300 particles. Many matchings nearly as cheap as the optimal one exist, and the flow lands on one of them along curved paths.
  • A NumPy replay of the 600 steps with the page’s random generator, followed by POT’s exact matching of the end points, gives the same cost and the same count (independent check).

Where slicing is used

  • Color transfer and texture synthesis: iterated one-dimensional matchings (Pitié et al. 2007), then sliced flows and barycenters (Rabin et al. 2011), as above.
  • Generative models (slides p. 16, frame 10/12): sliced-Wasserstein GANs and auto-encoders (Deshpande et al. 2018; Kolouri et al. 2019; Wu et al. 2019) train a generator by minimizing $\widehat{SW}_2$ between generated and real mini-batches. Each loss evaluation is a few sorts.
  • Barycenters: minimizing $\sum_s\lambda_sSW_2^2(\al,\be_s)$ over the positions of particles uses the gradient above (CompOT (10.16)–(10.17), p. 171 (167)). Alternatively, since $SW$ compares Radon transforms, one can average the quantile functions slice by slice and return to a measure by filtered back-projection (CompOT (10.18)–(10.20), pp. 171–172 (167–168); OT4ML Fig. 12.3, p. 265 (257), notebook sliced-radon-barycenter). The averaged slices need not be the Radon transform of any measure, so the result is only an approximate barycenter; CompOT notes that in practice it nearly is one.

§10Beyond this lesson, and what the resources get wrong

What the resources add that the course does not require

  • Other slicings, all in the slides’ references: generalized sliced distances with nonlinear projections (Kolouri et al. 2019), distributional and energy-based weightings of the directions (Nguyen et al. 2021; Nguyen and Ho 2023), and spherical and manifold versions (Bonet et al. 2023, 2025; Quellmalz et al. 2024).
  • Sliced transport plans: averaging the lifted plans of all directions, or choosing them more cleverly (Liu et al. 2024; Tanguy, Chapel and Delon 2025; Chapel, Tavenard and Vaiter 2025), and the minimization of OT4ML Def. 11.34 over compatible lifts.
  • Statistics: robustness and computational guarantees (Nietert et al. 2022), and fast approximations using the concentration of random projections in high dimension (Nadjahi et al. 2021; Diaconis and Freedman 1984).
  • The sliced gradient flow of Gaussians stays Gaussian and reduces to an ODE on means and covariances (OT4ML Prop. 16.23, p. 432 (424)), a case of the Gaussian closures of Lesson 8 §5.
  • The intrinsic geometry of $SW_2$, its geodesics and length metric (Park and Slepčev; OT4ML p. 234 (226)).

Exercise 1 — Comparing isotropic centered Gaussians§5 · checked

Let $\al=\Gauss(0,s^2\Id_d)$ and $\be=\Gauss(0,t^2\Id_d)$ with $s,t\ge0$ (the sheet’s $\sigma,\tau$; here $\sigma$ is the uniform law on the sphere). (a) Compute $SW_2(\al,\be)$. (b) Compare it with $W_2(\al,\be)$.

Hint 1
The slice of $\Gauss(m,\Sigma)$ along $\theta$ is $\Gauss(\dotp\theta m,\theta^\top\Sigma\theta)$. Here $\theta^\top(s^2\Id)\theta=s^2$ for every unit $\theta$.
Hint 2
On the line, $W_2^2(\Gauss(a,s^2),\Gauss(b,t^2))=(a-b)^2+(s-t)^2$ (Lesson 1 §8). For $W_2$ in $\R^d$, use the Bures formula with commuting covariances.
Solution

(a) Every slice compares $\Gauss(0,s^2)$ with $\Gauss(0,t^2)$, so $W_2^2(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)=(s-t)^2$ for all $\theta$, and the average over the sphere is the same: $SW_2(\al,\be)=\abs{s-t}$, whatever the dimension.

(b) The covariances commute, so $\Bures^2(s^2\Id,t^2\Id)=\tr(s^2\Id+t^2\Id-2st\Id)=d(s-t)^2$ and $W_2(\al,\be)=\sqrt d\,\abs{s-t}$. Hence $SW_2=W_2/\sqrt d$: the bound $SW_2^2\le W_2^2/d$ of §4 is an equality. The reason is general. In the proof of §4, $W_2^2(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)\le\int\dotp\theta{x-y}^2\dd\pi$ is an equality exactly when the projected optimal plan is optimal for the slice (Exercise 2). Here the optimal map $x\mapsto\frac tsx$ projects to $u\mapsto\frac tsu$, which is increasing, hence optimal on every slice. The same holds for every map $x\mapsto\lambda x+u$ with $\lambda\gt0$, translations included. This is the proportional case of OT4ML Remark 11.31, $SW_2^2=(\norm{\Delta m}^2+(s-t)^2\tr\Sigma)/d$ with $\Sigma=\Id$. In high dimension, the sliced distance between two isotropic Gaussians is $\sqrt d$ times smaller than $W_2$. in $d=3$ with $s=0.5$ and $t=1.5$, angular quadrature of the slice formula gives $SW_2=1.000000$, and the Bures formula gives $W_2=1.7321=\sqrt3$; SciPy’s matrix square root agrees.

Exercise 2 — Projected optimal transport plans§4, §8 · checked

Let $\al,\be\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$, let $\pi$ be an optimal plan between them, and for $\theta\in\mathbb S^{d-1}$ let $\pi_\theta=\push{(P_\theta\otimes P_\theta)}\pi$, the law of $(\dotp\theta X,\dotp\theta Y)$ when $(X,Y)\sim\pi$. (a) Show that $\pi_\theta$ is a transport plan between $\al_\theta$ and $\be_\theta$. (b) Is $\pi_\theta$ an optimal transport plan between $\al_\theta$ and $\be_\theta$?

Hint 1
(a) Test the first marginal of $\pi_\theta$ against a bounded function $g$ of one variable and use the change of variables formula (notes (1)). (b) Look for an optimal plan in the plane whose pairs cross after projection.
Hint 2
(b) Two pairs are enough: $x_1=(0,0)\mapsto y_1=(1,0)$ and $x_2=(1,10)\mapsto y_2=(0,10)$, projected on the first axis.
Solution

(a) For bounded measurable $g$, $$\begin{aligned}\int g(u)\dd\pi_\theta(u,v)&=\int g(\dotp\theta x)\dd\pi(x,y)\\&=\int g(\dotp\theta x)\dd\al(x)=\int g\dd\al_\theta:\end{aligned}$$ the first equality is the definition of the push-forward, the second uses that the first marginal of $\pi$ is $\al$, the third is notes (1). The second marginal is $\be_\theta$ in the same way, and $\pi_\theta$ has finite second moments since $\abs{\dotp\theta x}\le\norm x$. In one line: the first marginal of $\pi_\theta$ is $\push{(P_\theta\circ\mathrm{pr}_1)}\pi=\push{(P_\theta)}\al$.

(b) Not in general. Put mass $\frac12$ on each point of the hint. In the plane, the matching $x_1\mapsto y_1$, $x_2\mapsto y_2$ costs 1 and the crossing one 100, so the first is optimal. On the first axis, $x_1,x_2$ project to $0,1$ and $y_1,y_2$ to $1,0$: the projected plan matches $0\mapsto1$ and $1\mapsto0$, a decreasing matching of cost 1, while the sorted matching costs 0. On the line the optimal plans for $\abs{u-v}^2$ are the monotone ones (Lesson 2 §3), so $\pi_\theta$ is optimal exactly when it is monotone. That holds in dimension 1, and in every direction for maps $x\mapsto\lambda x+u$ (Exercise 1). For two nondegenerate Gaussians it holds along the eigenvectors of the Brenier matrix, which is how OT4ML proves that Min-SW is exact on Gaussians (Prop. 11.36, §8).

What remains true is the inequality used in §4: $W_2^2(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)\le\int\dotp\theta{x-y}^2\dd\pi$ for every $\theta$, and the right-hand side averages to $W_2^2/d$. Equality in $SW_2^2\le W_2^2/d$ holds exactly when $\pi_\theta$ is optimal for almost every $\theta$. POT’s emd2 confirms the two-pair example. On the clouds of Lab 5, the projection of the optimal plan is optimal on 0.0% of 3600 directions, costs up to 2.91 times the slice optimum, and averages 0.7240 $=W_2^2/2$.

Exercise 3 — Translation decomposition for $SW_2$§5 · checked

Let $\al,\be\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$ and let $\al_0,\be_0$ be their centered versions. (a) Prove that for every $\theta\in\mathbb S^{d-1}$, $\E_{u\sim\al_\theta}[u]=\dotp\theta{\E_{x\sim\al}[x]}$, and the same for $\be$. (b) Prove that $(\al_\theta)_0=(\al_0)_\theta$ and $(\be_\theta)_0=(\be_0)_\theta$, where $\al_0=\push{(T_{-u})}\al$ and $\be_0=\push{(T_{-v})}\be$ for a unique pair $(u,v)\in\R^d\times\R^d$, to be given ($T_w(x)=x+w$). (c) Show that $SW_2(\al,\be)^2=\frac1d\norm{u-v}^2+SW_2(\al_0,\be_0)^2$. The sheet’s hint: $\E_\theta[\theta\theta^\top]=\frac1d\Id_d$.

Hint 1
(a) is the change of variables with $g(u)=u$. (b) The mean of $\push{(T_{-w})}\al$ is $m_\al-w$; and $P_\theta\circ T_{-w}=T_{-\dotp\theta w}\circ P_\theta$.
Hint 2
(c) On the line, if $\mu$ has mean $a$, its quantile function is $a$ plus that of $\mu_0$, and the quantile function of $\mu_0$ integrates to $0$ over $(0,1)$. Expand $W_2^2(\mu,\nu)=\int_0^1(F_\mu^{-1}-F_\nu^{-1})^2$.
Solution

(a) $\int u\dd\al_\theta(u)=\int\dotp\theta x\dd\al(x)=\dotp\theta{m_\al}$, by linearity, with $m_\al=\E_\al[x]$ (finite since $\al\in\Pc_2$).

(b) The mean of $\push{(T_{-w})}\al$ is $m_\al-w$, so the only centered translate of $\al$ is obtained with $u=m_\al$, and likewise $v=m_\be$. Then $(\al_0)_\theta=\push{(P_\theta\circ T_{-u})}\al=\push{(T_{-\dotp\theta u}\circ P_\theta)}\al=\push{(T_{-\dotp\theta u})}\al_\theta$, which is the centered version of $\al_\theta$ by (a).

(c) On the line, let $\mu,\nu$ have means $a,b$. Then $F_\mu^{-1}=a+F_{\mu_0}^{-1}$ and $F_\nu^{-1}=b+F_{\nu_0}^{-1}$, so $$\begin{aligned}W_2^2(\mu,\nu)&=(a-b)^2+W_2^2(\mu_0,\nu_0)\\&\quad+2(a-b)\int_0^1\big(F_{\mu_0}^{-1}-F_{\nu_0}^{-1}\big)\\&=(a-b)^2+W_2^2(\mu_0,\nu_0),\end{aligned}$$ because $\int_0^1F_{\mu_0}^{-1}$ is the mean of $\mu_0$, which is $0$. Apply this to $\mu=\al_\theta$ and $\nu=\be_\theta$, whose means are $\dotp\theta u$ and $\dotp\theta v$ by (a), and whose centered versions are $(\al_0)_\theta$ and $(\be_0)_\theta$ by (b): $W_2^2(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)=\dotp\theta{u-v}^2+W_2^2((\al_0)_\theta,(\be_0)_\theta)$. Integrate over $\theta$: $\int\dotp\theta{u-v}^2\dd\sigma=(u-v)^\top\E[\theta\theta^\top](u-v)=\frac1d\norm{u-v}^2$.

The same argument in $\R^d$, with any coupling $(X,Y)$ and $\E\norm{X-Y}^2=\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+\E\norm{(X-m_\al)-(Y-m_\be)}^2$, gives $W_2^2(\al,\be)=\norm{u-v}^2+W_2^2(\al_0,\be_0)$. So slicing divides the mean part by $d$, which is why the signal of a translation in Lab 1 decays like $1/\sqrt d$. The Gaussian formula of §5 is the case $\al,\be$ Gaussian. on the clouds of Lab 5 (20,000 directions), the total 0.7030 equals the mean part 0.6855 plus the centered part 0.0175, and the difference is below 10⁻¹²; an independent NumPy quadrature agrees.

Exercise 4 — The constant $c_{d,p}$ (notes, Remark 4)§4 · checked

The notes define $c_{d,p}=\frac1d\int\norm\theta_p^p\dd\sigma(\theta)$, with $\norm\theta_p^p=\sum_i\abs{\theta_i}^p$, and prove $SW_p^p\le c_{d,p}W_p^p$ (Proposition 1). (a) Show that $c_{d,p}=\kappa_{d,p}=\int\abs{\dotp e\theta}^p\dd\sigma(\theta)$ for any unit vector $e$. (b) Show that $c_{d,p}\le\frac1d$ when $p\ge2$, the claim the notes leave as an exercise. (c) Does it hold for $1\le p\lt2$?

Hint 1
(a) The uniform law on the sphere is invariant under permutations of the coordinates and under rotations. (b) On the sphere, $\abs{\theta_i}\le1$.
Hint 2
(b) For $p\ge2$, $\abs{\theta_i}^p\le\theta_i^2$. (c) Compare $\norm\theta_1$ with $\norm\theta_2=1$, or use the Gamma formula of §4.
Solution

(a) Each coordinate $\theta_i$ has the law of $\theta_1$, so $\frac1d\E\norm\theta_p^p=\frac1d\sum_i\E\abs{\theta_i}^p=\E\abs{\theta_1}^p$, and by rotation invariance $\dotp e\theta$ has the law of $\theta_1$ for every unit $e$. So the notes’ $c_{d,p}$ is OT4ML’s $\kappa_{d,p}=\frac{\Gamma(d/2)\Gamma((p+1)/2)}{\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma((d+p)/2)}$ (§4). This also explains why (15) in the notes is an equality.

(b) For $p\ge2$ and $\abs{\theta_i}\le1$, $\abs{\theta_i}^p\le\theta_i^2$, so $\norm\theta_p^p\le\norm\theta_2^2=1$ and $c_{d,p}\le\frac1d$. Equality holds for $p=2$, since $\E\theta_1^2=\frac1d\E\norm\theta^2=\frac1d$, and the inequality is strict for $p\gt2$. For every $p\ge1$, $\abs{\theta_1}\le1$ gives $c_{d,p}\le1$, the bound printed in Proposition 1, which yields $SW_p\le W_p$ (Exercise 6). For $p\ge2$ the sharper conclusion is $SW_p^p\le W_p^p/d$.

(c) No. For $p=1$, $\norm\theta_1\ge\norm\theta_2=1$, so $c_{d,1}\ge\frac1d$, with strict inequality for $d\ge2$; in fact $\kappa_{d,1}\approx\sqrt{2/(\pi d)}$ for large $d$, much larger than $1/d$. For instance $10\,\kappa_{10,1}=2.587$ and $10\,\kappa_{10,1.5}=1.558$. over $2\le d\le50$ and $p\in\{2,2.25,\dots,8\}$, the largest value of $d\,\kappa_{d,p}$ is 1.0000, at $p=2.00$. The notes’ definition, evaluated by Monte Carlo on $\mathbb S^9$ with 200,000 directions, gives $c_{10,3}\approx0.0470$, against $\kappa_{10,3}=0.0470$ from the Gamma formula; SciPy and NumPy agree.

Exercise 5 — Why average over the sphere? (notes, Remark 2)§2 · checked

The notes say that integrating $\theta\mapsto W_p^p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)$ over $\R^d$ instead of $\mathbb S^{d-1}$ “repeats directional information and gives a divergent radial factor”, and leave the justification as an exercise. Here $P_x(y)=\dotp xy$ for any $x\in\R^d$. (a) Show that $W_p(\al_{r\theta},\be_{r\theta})=r\,W_p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)$ for $r\gt0$. (b) Deduce that $\int_{\R^d}W_p^p(\al_x,\be_x)\dd x=+\infty$ unless $\al=\be$. (c) If the direction is $x=R\theta$ with $\theta\sim\sigma$ and $R\ge0$ independent of $\theta$, show that $\E\,W_p^p(\al_x,\be_x)=\E[R^p]\,SW_p^p(\al,\be)$. What does a standard Gaussian direction give for $p=2$?

Hint 1
(a) $P_{r\theta}=rP_\theta$, and dilating two measures on the line by $r$ multiplies the cost of every coupling by $r^p$.
Hint 2
(b) Polar coordinates, notes (4). (c) For $G\sim\Gauss(0,\Id_d)$, $G/\norm G$ is uniform on the sphere and independent of $\norm G$ (§6).
Solution

(a) $\al_{r\theta}=\push{(D_r)}\al_\theta$ with $D_r(u)=ru$. The map $D_r\times D_r$ is a bijection between the couplings of $\al_\theta,\be_\theta$ and those of the dilated slices, and it multiplies every cost $\int\abs{u-v}^p$ by $r^p$. Taking the infimum gives the claim.

(b) In polar coordinates, with $\tilde\sigma$ the surface measure, $$\begin{aligned}\int_{\R^d}W_p^p(\al_x,\be_x)\dd x&=\int_0^\infty\!\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}r^pW_p^p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)\,r^{d-1}\dd\tilde\sigma(\theta)\dd r\\&=\abs{\mathbb S^{d-1}}\,SW_p^p(\al,\be)\int_0^\infty r^{p+d-1}\dd r.\end{aligned}$$ The last integral diverges, so the total is $+\infty$ as soon as $SW_p(\al,\be)\gt0$, that is, $\al\ne\be$ (§3). Every point of a ray carries the same information, rescaled by $r^p$.

(c) By (a), $W_p^p(\al_{R\theta},\be_{R\theta})=R^pW_p^p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)$; take the expectation and use independence. For $G\sim\Gauss(0,\Id_d)$, $R=\norm G$ and $\E\norm G^2=d$, so Gaussian directions give $d\,SW_2^2$. Any rotation-invariant law of directions with a finite $p$-th moment gives $SW_p^p$ up to a constant factor; the uniform law on the sphere is the normalization with factor $1$. This is the argument of §2, written out in steps. in Lab 2 ($d=2$), the average of $W_2^2$ over 200,000 Gaussian directions is 0.9640 ± 0.0030, against $d\,SW_2^2=0.9636$; a NumPy Monte Carlo agrees.

Exercise 6 — $SW_p\le W_p$, and a smoothed analogue (StatOT §2.10, Exercise 4)§3, §4 · checked

(a) Prove that $SW_p\le W_p$ for every $p\ge1$. Is this inequality tight? (b) For $s\gt0$, the smoothed distance $W_1^{(s)}(\al,\be)=W_1(\al*\Gauss(0,s^2\Id),\be*\Gauss(0,s^2\Id))$ compares the two measures after adding Gaussian noise (StatOT §2.8, beyond this course). Show that $W_1^{(s)}\le W_1$. Is this inequality tight?

Hint 1
(a) Project an optimal plan (Exercise 2(a)); $P_\theta$ is 1-Lipschitz. (b) If $(X,Y)$ is an optimal coupling and $Z\sim\Gauss(0,s^2\Id)$ is independent of it, what are the laws of $X+Z$ and $Y+Z$?
Hint 2
For tightness, compare with the sharper constant $\kappa_{d,p}$ of §4 and try translations. For (b) on the line, use $W_1=\int\abs{F_\al-F_\be}$; compare $\delta_0$ with $\frac12(\delta_{-1}+\delta_1)$.
Solution

(a) For each $\theta$, $\pi_\theta$ is a plan between the slices (Exercise 2) and $\abs{\dotp\theta{x-y}}\le\norm{x-y}$, so $W_p^p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)\le\int\abs{\dotp\theta{x-y}}^p\dd\pi\le W_p^p(\al,\be)$. Averaging over $\theta$ gives $SW_p\le W_p$. Integrating over $\theta$ before bounding gives more: $\int\abs{\dotp\theta z}^p\dd\sigma=\kappa_{d,p}\norm z^p$, so $SW_p^p\le\kappa_{d,p}W_p^p$ (§4), with $\kappa_{d,p}\lt1$ for $d\ge2$. Hence $SW_p\le W_p$ is an equality only in dimension 1. For $d\ge2$ it is strict unless $\al=\be$, and the sharp constant is $\kappa_{d,p}^{1/p}$. That constant is attained by translations: if $\be=\push{(T_u)}\al$, then $W_p=\norm u$ (the translation costs $\norm u^p$, and $W_p\ge W_1\ge\norm{m_\al-m_\be}=\norm u$ by Jensen), and each slice is a translation by $\dotp\theta u$, so $SW_p^p=\int\abs{\dotp\theta u}^p\dd\sigma=\kappa_{d,p}\norm u^p$.

(b) $(X+Z,Y+Z)$ is a coupling of $\al*\Gauss(0,s^2\Id)$ and $\be*\Gauss(0,s^2\Id)$, with cost $\E\norm{X-Y}=W_1(\al,\be)$. Translations give equality again: both smoothed measures are translates by $u$, so both distances equal $\norm u$. The inequality is strict in general. On the line, for $\al=\delta_0$ and $\be=\frac12(\delta_{-1}+\delta_1)$, $W_1=1$, and $W_1^{(s)}=\int\abs{\Phi(\frac xs)-\frac12\Phi(\frac{x+1}s)-\frac12\Phi(\frac{x-1}s)}\dd x$, which equals 0.8005, 0.6095 and 0.3687 for $s=0.25$, $0.5$ and $1$: after smoothing, part of the mass no longer needs to move. on 300 random pairs of 6-point clouds in $\R^3$, $3\,SW_2^2/W_2^2$ ranges from 0.381 to 0.948, and it equals 1.0000 for a translated copy. For a translation by 0.7 on the line, the smoothed $W_1$ is 0.7000. The same pairs, a Fibonacci-lattice quadrature with POT, and SciPy quadrature for the smoothed values give the same numbers.

Exercise 7 — Computing sliced distances (StatOT §2.10, Exercise 5)§1, §6 · checked

(a) If $\al,\be\in\Pc_p(\R)$ are each uniform on $n$ points, given as unordered lists, show that $W_p(\al,\be)$ can be computed in $O(n\log n)$ time. (b) If $\al,\be\in\Pc_p(\R^d)$ are each uniform on $n$ points, show that $W_p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)$ can be computed in $O(dn+n\log n)$ time. (c) If $\al,\be$ are supported in the unit ball $B_1$ and $\theta_1,\dots,\theta_m$ are i.i.d. uniform on $\mathbb S^{d-1}$, show that $\widehat{SW}_1=\frac1m\sum_iW_1(\al_{\theta_i},\be_{\theta_i})$ approximates $SW_1(\al,\be)$ with an additive error of order $m^{-1/2}$.

Hint 1
(a) Lesson 1 §3: on the line, the monotone matching is optimal. (b) Each projection is an inner product of length $d$. (c) The terms are i.i.d. with mean $SW_1$; in which interval do they lie?
Hint 2
(c) $\E\abs{\bar V-\E\bar V}\le(\operatorname{Var}\bar V)^{1/2}$, and a random variable with values in an interval of length $L$ has variance at most $L^2/4$.
Solution

(a) Sort both lists, in $O(n\log n)$, and match by rank: $W_p^p=\frac1n\sum_i\abs{x_{(i)}-y_{(i)}}^p$ costs $O(n)$ more. (b) The $n+n$ projections cost $O(dn)$, then (a) applies. With $K$ directions the cost is $O(Kdn+Kn\log n)$ (notes Remark 5), against $O(n^3)$ for $W_p$ in dimension $d\ge2$ by the Hungarian method (Lesson 1 §2).

(c) The variables $V_i=W_1(\al_{\theta_i},\be_{\theta_i})$ are i.i.d. with mean $SW_1(\al,\be)$ (§2 with $p=1$). The slices are supported in $[-1,1]$, so any coupling moves mass by at most $2$ and $0\le V_i\le2$; hence $\operatorname{Var}V_i\le1$. Then $\E\abs{\widehat{SW}_1-SW_1}\le(\operatorname{Var}V/m)^{1/2}\le m^{-1/2}$. Hoeffding’s inequality gives the tail bound $\mathbb P(\abs{\widehat{SW}_1-SW_1}\ge\varepsilon)\le2e^{-m\varepsilon^2/2}$. This is OT4ML Remark 10.17, p. 207 (199), with $R=1$: the square root is the one missing on the slides (§6). for two clouds of 100 points in the unit ball of $\R^3$ (largest norm 0.987), $SW_1=0.21432$ and $\operatorname{Var}V=0.00549$. The mean absolute error is 6.4×10⁻², 1.5×10⁻² and 3.8×10⁻³ for $m=1$, $16$ and $256$, against $(\operatorname{Var}V/m)^{1/2}=$ 7.4×10⁻², 1.9×10⁻² and 4.6×10⁻³, and the crude bound $m^{-1/2}=$ 1.0000, 0.2500 and 0.0625. A Fibonacci lattice of $2\times10^6$ directions gives the same $SW_1$, and a NumPy Monte Carlo agrees statistically.

§14Cheat sheet

Slices$\al_\theta=\push{(P_\theta)}\al$, $P_\theta(x)=\dotp\theta x$; $\int g\dd\al_\theta=\int g(\dotp\theta x)\dd\al$; the slice of $\frac1n\sum\delta_{x_i}$ is $\frac1n\sum\delta_{\dotp\theta{x_i}}$; slices of a density are its Radon transform.
Definition$SW_p^p(\al,\be)=\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}W_p^p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)\dd\sigma(\theta)$, $\sigma$ the uniform probability on the sphere; $d=1$: $SW_p=W_p$; integrating over $\R^d$ diverges; Gaussian directions give $\E\norm G^p\,SW_p^p$.
Computationsort and match: $W_p^p=\frac1n\sum\abs{s_{(i)}-t_{(i)}}^p$; $K$ directions $\theta=G/\norm G$: $O(Kdn+Kn\log n)$, against $O(n^3)$ for $W_p$.
Metrictriangle: slice by slice, then Minkowski in $L^p(\sigma)$; separation: continuity in $\theta$ and Cramér–Wold ($\hat\al(s\theta)=\widehat{\al_\theta}(s)$); same convergent sequences as $W_p$; $SW_2$ is Hilbertian, $e^{-\gamma SW_2^2}$ is a positive-definite kernel.
Comparison$SW_p^p\le\kappa_{d,p}W_p^p$, $\kappa_{d,p}=\frac{\Gamma(d/2)\Gamma((p+1)/2)}{\sqrt\pi\Gamma((d+p)/2)}$, $\kappa_{d,2}=\frac1d$, $\kappa_{d,p}\le\frac1d$ iff $p\ge2$; equality iff projected optimal plans are optimal (translations, $x\mapsto\lambda x+u$). Reverse on $B_R$: $W_p^p\le C_{d,p}R^{p-\frac1{d+1}}SW_p^{\frac1{d+1}}$.
Small deformationsatoms moved by $tv_i$: $SW_2^2/W_2^2\to\frac1d$; $\Gauss(0,\Id)$ deformed by $\Id+tA$: $\frac{(\tr A)^2+2\tr A^2}{d(d+2)\tr A^2}\lt\frac1d$ for non-scalar $A$.
Gaussians$SW_2^2=\frac{\norm{\Delta m}^2}d+\int(\sqrt{\theta^\top\Sigma_\al\theta}-\sqrt{\theta^\top\Sigma_\be\theta})^2\dd\sigma$; isotropic: $SW_2=\abs{s-t}=W_2/\sqrt d$; any pair: $SW_2^2=\frac1d\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+SW_2^2(\al_0,\be_0)$.
Monte Carlo$\E\abs{\widehat{SW}{}_p^p-SW_p^p}\le\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}_\theta/K}$ (the slides print $\operatorname{Var}/K$); bounded slices on $B_R$: $\le R/\sqrt K$ for $SW_1$; equispaced directions in 2D converge far faster.
Statistics$\E\abs{SW_1(\hat\al_n,\hat\be_m)-SW_1(\al,\be)}\le R(n^{-1/2}+m^{-1/2})$: dimension-free; $W_p$: $n^{-1/d}$ if $d\gt2p$; but the signal of a translation shrinks like $1/\sqrt d$.
Plansprojected optimal plans are plans, not optimal; lifted plans (sort along $\theta$, match ranks): cost $\ge W_2^2$; Min-SW: symmetric, separating, no triangle inequality, does not metrize $W_2$, exact on Gaussians; $SW_p\le\mathrm{MaxSW}_p\le W_p$.
Sliced flow$x_i\leftarrow x_i-\frac\tau K\sum_k\dotp{x_i-y_{r(i,k)}}{\theta_k}\theta_k$; converges numerically; its matching is not the optimal one; translations relax at rate $\frac1d$.
BarycentersLagrangian: gradient steps on $\sum_s\lambda_sSW_2^2(\al,\be_s)$; Radon: average quantile functions slice by slice, then filtered back-projection (approximate).

§15Self-test

  1. What is the slice of $\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$ along $\theta$, and how do you compute $W_p$ between two such slices?
    Answer
    $\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{\dotp\theta{x_i}}$. Sort both lists of projections and match them by rank: $W_p^p=\frac1n\sum_i\abs{s_{(i)}-t_{(i)}}^p$, in $O(n\log n)$.
  2. Why is $SW_p$ an average over the unit sphere, and not over all of $\R^d$?
    Answer
    Because $W_p(\al_{r\theta},\be_{r\theta})=rW_p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)$: a ray repeats one direction with weight $r^p$, and the integral over $\R^d$ diverges. Gaussian directions give $\E\norm G^p\,SW_p^p$, which is $d\,SW_2^2$ for $p=2$ (0.9640 against 0.9636 in Lab 2).
  3. Which axiom of a distance is the delicate one for $SW_p$, and how is it proved?
    Answer
    Separation. $SW_p=0$ gives equal slices for almost every $\theta$, hence for every $\theta$ by continuity of $\theta\mapsto\al_\theta$; then $\hat\al(s\theta)=\widehat{\al_\theta}(s)=\hat\be(s\theta)$ and $\al=\be$ (Cramér–Wold).
  4. State the comparison between $SW_p$ and $W_p$. When is it an equality?
    Answer
    $SW_p^p\le\kappa_{d,p}W_p^p$, with $\kappa_{d,2}=1/d$. It is an equality exactly when the projections of an optimal plan are optimal for almost every slice, for instance when $\be$ is the image of $\al$ by $x\mapsto\lambda x+u$, $\lambda\gt0$.
  5. Can $W_p$ be bounded by a function of $SW_p$?
    Answer
    On a ball $B_R$, yes: $W_p^p\le C_{d,p}R^{p-1/(d+1)}SW_p^{1/(d+1)}$ (Bonnotte), and for $p=1$ the exponent $1/d$ is sharp. The exponent degrades with the dimension, so a small $SW_p$ guarantees little about $W_p$ in high dimension.
  6. For a small deformation of a discrete measure, what is the limit of $SW_2^2/W_2^2$? And for a Gaussian deformed by a non-scalar linear map?
    Answer
    $1/d$ for atoms (Lab 3: 0.4981 at $t=0.001$ in $d=2$). For $\Gauss(0,\Id)$ deformed by $\Id+tA$, the limit is $\frac{(\tr A)^2+2\tr A^2}{d(d+2)\tr A^2}$, strictly below $1/d$ (0.2500 for $A=\diag(1,-1)$ in $d=2$).
  7. Give $SW_2$ between two Gaussians.
    Answer
    $SW_2^2=\frac1d\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+\int\big(\sqrt{\theta^\top\Sigma_\al\theta}-\sqrt{\theta^\top\Sigma_\be\theta}\big)^2\dd\sigma(\theta)$: each slice is a pair of Gaussians on the line. It has closed forms when the covariances are proportional or of rank one (OT4ML Remark 11.31).
  8. Compare $SW_2$ and $W_2$ between $\Gauss(0,s^2\Id_d)$ and $\Gauss(0,t^2\Id_d)$.
    Answer
    $SW_2=\abs{s-t}$ and $W_2=\sqrt d\abs{s-t}$ (Exercise 1): the bound $SW_2^2\le W_2^2/d$ is an equality.
  9. How does $SW_2^2$ split into a mean part and a centered part, and how does this differ from $W_2^2$?
    Answer
    $SW_2^2=\frac1d\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+SW_2^2(\al_0,\be_0)$ and $W_2^2=\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+W_2^2(\al_0,\be_0)$ (Exercise 3): slicing divides the translation part by $d$.
  10. How do you draw a uniform direction on $\mathbb S^{d-1}$?
    Answer
    Normalize a standard Gaussian vector, $G/\norm G$ (notes Proposition 2): the Gaussian density depends only on $\norm x$. Uniform angles do not give uniform directions for $d\ge3$.
  11. What is the Monte Carlo error of $\widehat{SW}{}_p^p$ with $K$ random directions, and what do the slides print?
    Answer
    $\E\abs{\widehat{SW}{}_p^p-SW_p^p}\le\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}_\theta/K}$. The slides print $\operatorname{Var}_\theta/K$, which bounds the mean squared error. In Lab 4 at $K=256$ the measured error is 2.9×10⁻³, against 1.4×10⁻⁵ for $\operatorname{Var}/K$.
  12. Why are equispaced directions in the plane much better than random ones?
    Answer
    The slice cost is a periodic function of the angle, and the midpoint rule integrates smooth periodic functions extremely fast, while random directions give $K^{-1/2}$. For the Gaussians of Lab 4, 16 equispaced directions already give an error of 1.9×10⁻¹⁰.
  13. What is the cost of $\widehat{SW}_p$ with $K$ directions, compared with $W_p$?
    Answer
    $O(Kdn+Kn\log n)$ for the projections and the sorts, against $O(n^3)$ for an exact assignment (notes Remark 5).
  14. Why is the sample complexity of $SW_1$ free of the dimension?
    Answer
    Each slice is a one-dimensional problem, where empirical measures converge at the rate $n^{-1/2}$; averaging over directions keeps that rate: $\E\abs{SW_1(\hat\al_n,\hat\be_m)-SW_1(\al,\be)}\le R(n^{-1/2}+m^{-1/2})$ (OT4ML Thm. 10.16). $W_p$ converges like $n^{-1/d}$ when $d\gt2p$.
  15. In Lab 1, $SW_2$ between two samples of the same Gaussian is almost the same in $d=2$, $10$ and $50$. Does that make $SW_2$ equally useful in every dimension?
    Answer
    No. The noise level is dimension-free (0.106, 0.108, 0.109 at $n=500$), but the signal also shrinks: a unit translation gives $SW_2=1/\sqrt d$, 0.141 for $d=50$.
  16. Is the projection of an optimal plan optimal for the slices?
    Answer
    Only when it is monotone (Exercise 2). On the clouds of Lab 5 it is optimal on 0.0% of the directions; on average it costs exactly $W_2^2/d$.
  17. What is a lifted plan, and is Min-SW a distance?
    Answer
    Sort both clouds along $\theta$, match the ranks and pay the cost in $\R^d$: a feasible plan, so its cost is at least $W_2^2$. Min-SW minimizes it over $\theta$. It is symmetric and separating, but the triangle inequality fails (3.9686 $\gt$ 3.5707 on the book’s four points), it does not metrize $W_2$ convergence, and it equals $W_2$ between nondegenerate Gaussians.
  18. Define the max-sliced distance and place it between $SW_p$ and $W_p$.
    Answer
    $\mathrm{MaxSW}_p=\max_\theta W_p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)$, and $SW_p\le\mathrm{MaxSW}_p\le W_p$. In Lab 2 the best direction carries 0.990 of $W_2^2$.
  19. Describe one step of the sliced gradient flow. Does it reach the target, and with the optimal matching?
    Answer
    Draw directions, sort, and move each particle along each direction toward the target point of the same rank: $x_i\leftarrow x_i-\frac\tau K\sum_k\dotp{x_i-y_{r(i,k)}}{\theta_k}\theta_k$. In Lab 6 it reaches the target ($W_2^2$ below 10⁻¹⁴ after 600 steps), but its matching is 0.10% above the optimal one and agrees with it for only 71 of 300 particles.
  20. Why does the sliced flow correct a translation $d$ times more slowly than the $W_2$ flow?
    Answer
    For a translation by $u$, all slices keep their order and the velocity is $-\int\dotp u\theta\theta\dd\sigma=-u/d$, since $\int\theta\theta^\top\dd\sigma=\Id/d$. The $W_2$ flow has velocity $-u$.
  21. What does the Hilbert structure of $SW_2$ give?
    Answer
    $SW_2$ is the $L^2$ distance between the fields of quantile functions $(\theta,u)\mapsto F_{\al_\theta}^{-1}(u)$ (OT4ML Remark 11.26), so $e^{-\gamma SW_2^2}$ is a positive-definite kernel on probability measures.
  22. Name two ways of computing sliced barycenters.
    Answer
    Lagrangian: gradient steps on the positions of particles for $\sum_s\lambda_sSW_2^2(\al,\be_s)$. Eulerian: average the quantile functions slice by slice and return to a measure by filtered back-projection, which is only approximate because the averaged slices need not be a Radon transform.
  23. For which $p$ is the notes’ constant $c_{d,p}$ at most $1/d$?
    Answer
    For $p\ge2$ (Exercise 4), with equality at $p=2$. For $p=1$, $c_{d,1}=\kappa_{d,1}\approx\sqrt{2/(\pi d)}$ is much larger than $1/d$.
