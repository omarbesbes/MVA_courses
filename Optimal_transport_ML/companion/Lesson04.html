§0Where this lesson sits

The Kantorovich problem is a linear program, and every linear program has a dual. Here the dual variables are two functions, one on each space, read as prices. A pair of prices that never exceeds the transport cost gives a lower bound on every plan, and the best pair gives the optimal value (Kantorovich duality). The dual is also a tool. Its partial maximization, the $c$-transform, regularises the potentials and proves Brenier’s theorem. When one measure is discrete it becomes a finite-dimensional concave problem over cell weights: semi-discrete transport, and with free weights, quantization. When the cost is a distance, the potentials are the 1-Lipschitz functions, $W_1$ becomes a norm, and generative adversarial networks learn it. The lesson ends where the outline starts: comparing transport with likelihood-based losses (the Kullback–Leibler divergence) for mixture estimation, variational inference and Gaussian mixtures.

A question that comes back

What certifies that a transport plan is optimal, and what do the certificates compute? Here: a pair of potentials below the cost whose total value equals the cost of the plan. The certificate is checked entry by entry (complementary slackness), its gradient is a transport map (Brenier), its weights are found by mass balancing (semi-discrete), and its Lipschitz form is the critic of a GAN. In Lesson 7 the certificate becomes smooth and is computed by Sinkhorn’s iterations; in Lesson 8 §2 it is the first variation that drives a gradient flow.

§1The discrete dual: prices that certify a plan

The problem

Lesson 2 solved the transport linear program and found sparse optimal plans. How do we know a plan is optimal without comparing it with all others? For the assignment problem, Lesson 1 used a certificate: numbers $u_i,v_j$ with $u_i+v_j\le C_{i,j}$ everywhere and equality on the chosen pairs. The general version is the dual linear program.

Prices below the cost

Picture a carrier who buys each unit of mass at $x_i$ for $f_i$ and sells it at $y_j$ for $g_j$ (CompOT Remark 2.21, p. 28 (24)). The carrier’s offer is competitive if no route earns more than it costs, $f_i+g_j\le C_{i,j}$: otherwise the shipper would run that route without the carrier. Whatever plan $P$ is used, the carrier’s revenue $\sum_{i,j}P_{i,j}(f_i+g_j)=\dotp fa+\dotp gb$ is then at most the transport cost $\dotp CP$.

Definition (admissible potentials)OT4ML Def. 5.1, p. 95 (87)

$\mathrm R(C)=\{(\mathbf f,\mathbf g)\in\R^n\times\R^m:\ \mathbf f_i+\mathbf g_j\le C_{i,j}\ \text{for all}\ i,j\}$. It depends on the cost only, not on the marginals.

Proposition (discrete Kantorovich duality)OT4ML Prop. 5.2, p. 95 (87); CompOT Prop. 2.4, p. 27 (23); notes Prop. 12, p. 23

$$\mathrm L_C(a,b)=\min_{P\in\Couplings(a,b)}\dotp CP=\max_{(\mathbf f,\mathbf g)\in\mathrm R(C)}\dotp{\mathbf f}a+\dotp{\mathbf g}b .$$

Proof (the Lagrangian, then linear-programming duality)

Introduce multipliers $\mathbf f,\mathbf g$ for the two marginal constraints: $\mathcal L(P,\mathbf f,\mathbf g)=\dotp CP+\dotp{a-P\mathbb 1}{\mathbf f}+\dotp{b-P^\top\mathbb 1}{\mathbf g}$. For fixed potentials, $$\inf_{P\ge0}\mathcal L=\dotp a{\mathbf f}+\dotp b{\mathbf g}+\inf_{P\ge0}\dotp{C-\mathbf f\oplus\mathbf g}P,$$ and the last infimum is $0$ if $C-\mathbf f\oplus\mathbf g\ge0$ and $-\infty$ otherwise. So the dual function is finite exactly on $\mathrm R(C)$, where it equals $\dotp a{\mathbf f}+\dotp b{\mathbf g}$; this gives weak duality. The primal polytope is nonempty (it contains $a\otimes b$) and compact, so finite-dimensional linear-programming duality gives equality and dual attainment. $\square$

Proposition (complementary slackness)OT4ML Prop. 5.3, pp. 95–96 (87–88); CompOT Prop. 3.2, p. 45 (41)

For $P\in\Couplings(a,b)$ and $(\mathbf f,\mathbf g)\in\mathrm R(C)$, the gap is $\dotp CP-\dotp{\mathbf f}a-\dotp{\mathbf g}b=\sum_{i,j}P_{i,j}(C_{i,j}-\mathbf f_i-\mathbf g_j)\ge0$. Both are optimal if and only if $P$ charges only contacts: $P_{i,j}\gt0\Rightarrow\mathbf f_i+\mathbf g_j=C_{i,j}$.

This is the certificate: exhibit a feasible plan and an admissible pair that touch the cost wherever the plan puts mass. The network simplex of Lesson 2 §5 carries such a pair at every vertex, and stops when the pair becomes admissible. Two consequences follow from the dual formula. $(a,b)\mapsto\mathrm L_C(a,b)$ is convex, as a maximum of linear functions; and $C\mapsto\mathrm L_C(a,b)$ is concave, as a minimum of linear functions (OT4ML p. 96 (88)).

§2Kantorovich duality for general measures

Continuous potentials

For arbitrary measures the price vectors become continuous functions, paired with measures by integration.

Theorem (Kantorovich duality)OT4ML Prop. 5.5, p. 96 (88), proof p. 97 (89); CompOT Prop. 2.5, p. 30 (26); notes Prop. 13, p. 23; StatOT Thm. 1.20, p. 46 (40)

Let $\X,\Y$ be compact metric spaces and $c$ continuous on $\X\times\Y$. Then $$\mathcal L_c(\al,\be)=\max_{f\oplus g\le c}\int_\X f\,\dd\al+\int_\Y g\,\dd\be,$$ the maximum being over continuous $f,g$ with $f(x)+g(y)\le c(x,y)$ for all $x,y$. Optimal pairs are called Kantorovich potentials.

Proof sketch (weak duality, separation from a cone, compactness by c-transforms)

Weak duality. If $f\oplus g\le c$ and $\pi\in\Couplings(\al,\be)$, then $\int f\dd\al+\int g\dd\be=\int(f(x)+g(y))\dd\pi\le\int c\dd\pi$.

No gap. Let $V=\mathcal L_c(\al,\be)$ and consider the convex cone $\mathcal K=\{(\pi_1,\pi_2,\int c\dd\pi+r):\pi\ge0,\ r\ge0\}$ of marginal pairs and costs. It is closed (compactness of $\X\times\Y$). For $t\lt V$ the point $(\al,\be,t)$ is not in $\mathcal K$, so a continuous linear functional, given by functions $F,G$ and a number $\lambda$, separates them. Testing with $\pi=\delta_{(x,y)}$ gives $F(x)+G(y)+\lambda c(x,y)\ge0$, and $\lambda\gt0$; then $f=-F/\lambda$, $g=-G/\lambda$ are admissible with dual value above $t$.

Attainment. Replace a maximizing sequence $(f_k,g_k)$ by $\tilde g_k=f_k^c$ and then $\tilde f_k=\tilde g_k^{\bar c}$ (§3). These replacements keep admissibility, do not decrease the objective, and give functions with the modulus of continuity of $c$. After fixing the additive gauge, Arzelà–Ascoli gives a uniformly convergent subsequence, whose limit is an optimal pair. $\square$

On noncompact spaces the statement needs integrability assumptions, and value and attainment become separate questions. OT4ML records the one case needed below, the quadratic cost on $\R^d$ (Prop. 5.12, §4).

Complementary slackness, and the slack

Proposition (continuous complementary slackness)OT4ML Prop. 5.7, p. 97 (89); CompOT (2.26), p. 30 (26)

For $\pi\in\Couplings(\al,\be)$ and admissible $(f,g)$, the gap $\int c\dd\pi-\int f\dd\al-\int g\dd\be=\int(c-f-g)\dd\pi$ is nonnegative; both are optimal exactly when it vanishes, and then $\supp\pi\subset\{f(x)+g(y)=c(x,y)\}$.

The slack $s(x,y)=c(x,y)-f(x)-g(y)\ge0$ is a landscape over $\X\times\Y$; optimal plans live in its zero valley (OT4ML Fig. 5.3). Lab 1 draws it. Discrete potentials are samples of the continuous ones: $(\mathbf f_i,\mathbf g_j)=(f(x_i),g(y_j))$ (OT4ML p. 97 (89)).

The 1D quadratic case

Let $c(x,y)=\abs{x-y}^2$ on $\R$, with $\al$ atomless and $T=F_\be^{-1}\circ F_\al$ the monotone map of Lesson 1 §6. Then $f'(x)=2(x-T(x))$ for $\al$-almost every $x$, and $\varphi(x)=\frac{x^2}2-\frac{f(x)}2$ is convex with $\varphi'=T$ (OT4ML pp. 97–98 (89–90)). The potential is obtained by integrating $2(x-T(x))$, and $g=f^c$. This is Brenier’s theorem in 1D, read off the dual: the convex potential of the map is, up to the quadratic term, the dual potential.

§3$c$-transforms

The best response to a potential

Fix $f$ and maximise the dual over $g$ alone. The constraint $g(y)\le c(x,y)-f(x)$ must hold for every $x$, so the best choice is the largest admissible value.

Definition (c-transform)OT4ML Def. 5.8, p. 99 (91); CompOT (5.1)–(5.2), p. 89 (85); notes (48), p. 24

$f^c(y)=\inf_{x\in\X}c(x,y)-f(x)$ and, for a function $g$ on $\Y$, $g^{\bar c}(x)=\inf_{y\in\Y}c(x,y)-g(y)$.

  • Best response. For fixed $f$, the maximizers over admissible $g$ are exactly the $g$ equal to $f^c$ $\be$-almost everywhere (OT4ML Prop. 5.9, p. 99 (91)). So $\mathcal L_c(\al,\be)=\max_f\int f\dd\al+\int f^c\dd\be$: one potential is enough.
  • Regularity for free. If $\abs{c(x,y)-c(x,y')}\le\omega(d(y,y'))$, every finite $f^c$ has modulus $\omega$; an $L$-Lipschitz cost gives $L$-Lipschitz transforms (OT4ML Prop. 5.10, p. 99 (91)). This is the compactness used in the attainment proof of §2.
  • Discrete transforms are lower envelopes. For a finite source, $f^c(y)=\min_i c(x_i,y)-\mathbf f_i$: the lower envelope of the shifted cost sections $y\mapsto c(x_i,y)-\mathbf f_i$ (OT4ML p. 100 (92), Fig. 5.4). For matrices, $(\mathbf f^C)_j=\min_iC_{i,j}-\mathbf f_i$ are column minima.

The algebra of $c$-transforms, and why alternating them stalls

Proposition (algebra of c-transforms)OT4ML Prop. 5.14, p. 102 (94); CompOT Prop. 3.1, p. 44 (40); notes Prop. 15, p. 25; sheet Ex. 3

(i) $f\le f'\Rightarrow f^c\ge f'^c$; (ii) $f^{c\bar c}\ge f$; (iii) $g^{\bar cc}\ge g$; (iv) $f^{c\bar cc}=f^c$.

Proof (each line uses one inequality)

(i) is the minus sign in the definition. (ii): for every $x,y$, $f^c(y)\le c(x,y)-f(x)$, that is $c(x,y)-f^c(y)\ge f(x)$; take the infimum over $y$. (iii) is (ii) with the roles exchanged. (iv): (ii) and (i) give $f^c\ge(f^{c\bar c})^c=f^{c\bar cc}$, and (iii) applied to $g=f^c$ gives $f^{c\bar cc}\ge f^c$. $\square$

Alternating exact best responses $(f,g)\mapsto(f,f^c)\mapsto(f^{c\bar c},f^c)$ never decreases the dual value, and by (iv) it stops after one full cycle (OT4ML (5.9), p. 102 (94)). The fixed point is admissible but need not be optimal: its value still depends on the starting $f$. The hard minimum is idempotent before the joint problem is solved. Replacing it by a soft minimum (log-sum-exp) removes this idempotence; alternating soft transforms are Sinkhorn’s algorithm (Lesson 7).

The ranges of the transforms are the $c$-concave functions (OT4ML Defs. 5.15–5.16, pp. 102–103 (94–95)). For $c=-\dotp xy$ they are the concave functions; for $c=\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$, the $u$ with $u-\frac12\norm y^2$ concave; for $c=d$, exactly the 1-Lipschitz functions (§7). For the bilinear cost on bounded intervals, $f^{c\bar c}$ is the smallest concave majorant of $f$ built from affine functions with slopes in $-\Y$, which can lie above the ordinary concave envelope (OT4ML p. 102 (94), Fig. 5.5).

§4Brenier’s theorem, proved

The quadratic cost turns $c$-transforms into convex conjugates

Take $c(x,y)=\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$ on $\R^d$. For every coupling, $\int c\dd\pi=\frac12\int\norm x^2\dd\al+\frac12\int\norm y^2\dd\be-\int\dotp xy\dd\pi$: only the bilinear term depends on the plan. Write $\varphi(x)=\frac12\norm x^2-f(x)$. Then $$f^c(y)=\tfrac12\norm y^2-\varphi^*(y),\qquad f^{c\bar c}(x)=\tfrac12\norm x^2-\varphi^{**}(x),$$ with the Legendre transform $\varphi^*(y)=\sup_x\dotp xy-\varphi(x)$ (OT4ML Def. 5.11 and p. 101 (93)). The double transform replaces $\varphi$ by its convex lower-semicontinuous envelope $\varphi^{**}$. So after one $c$-transform pair, the potential is $\frac12\norm x^2$ minus a convex function.

Proposition (quadratic dual attainment on R^d)OT4ML Prop. 5.12, p. 101 (93); compact Prop. 5.11, p. 23

Let $\al,\be\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$ and $c=\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$. The dual is attained by $f=\frac12\norm x^2-\varphi$, $g=\frac12\norm y^2-\varphi^*$ with $\varphi$ proper, lower-semicontinuous and convex, and every optimal coupling satisfies $\varphi(x)+\varphi^*(y)=\dotp xy$, that is $y\in\partial\varphi(x)$, $\pi$-almost everywhere.

The book’s proof starts from an integrable dual optimum provided by Villani’s Theorem 5.10 (cited, not reproved) and convexifies it with Fenchel–Moreau; the contact relation is complementary slackness, since the slack $c-f-g=\varphi(x)+\varphi^*(y)-\dotp xy\ge0$ (Fenchel’s inequality) integrates to zero against an optimal plan.

Theorem (Brenier)OT4ML Remark 5.13, p. 102 (94), proving Thm. 2.23; CompOT Thm. 2.1, pp. 31–33 (27–29)

If moreover $\al$ has a density, every optimal coupling is $(\Id,\nabla\varphi)_\sharp\al$: the optimal plan is unique and induced by the gradient of a convex function.

Proof (convex functions are differentiable almost everywhere)

By Proposition 5.12, every optimal coupling lives on the graph of the subdifferential $\partial\varphi$. The function $\varphi$ is finite $\al$-almost everywhere, so its convex domain has nonempty interior and a Lebesgue-null boundary, and a convex function is differentiable Lebesgue-almost everywhere in the interior of its domain. Since $\al$ has a density, $\partial\varphi(x)=\{\nabla\varphi(x)\}$ for $\al$-almost every $x$. Every optimal coupling is therefore concentrated on the graph of $T=\nabla\varphi$, which gives both the map and its uniqueness. Rescaling the cost to $\norm{x-y}^2$ doubles the potentials and leaves the map unchanged. $\square$

Key idea: the map is the gradient of a price

The optimal map is not found by searching over maps. It falls out of the dual: the price $f$, corrected by the quadratic term, is a convex function whose gradient sends each point to its destination. In 1D this is $\varphi'=T$, measured in Lab 1. In the semi-discrete case of §5 the convex function is a maximum of finitely many affine functions, and its gradient is constant on each Laguerre cell.

Other routes, and what each source proves

  • Cyclical monotonicity. StatOT proves Brenier’s theorem without duality: the support of an optimal plan is cyclically monotone (Lesson 2 §8), Rockafellar’s theorem puts it inside the subdifferential of a convex function, and differentiability does the rest (StatOT Thm. 1.8, p. 29 (23), proof p. 35 (29)). OT4ML’s route hides the same ingredient inside the cited dual-attainment theorem.
  • CompOT’s sketch (pp. 31–33 (27–29)) follows the duality route but mixes the two normalizations of the cost (§10, errata).
  • The compact notes and the 2019 notes announce the proof (“detailed in Section 5.3”) but §5.3 of the 2019 notes contains only the $c$-transform calculus, and the compact notes keep Proposition 5.11 without the remark that draws the conclusion.

§5Semi-discrete transport: balancing cell masses

The problem

One measure is continuous and the other has finitely many atoms: a density of customers against $m$ warehouses, an image against a palette, data against a codebook. For the quadratic cost and a continuous side with a density, the optimal plan is a map, constant on each of $m$ cells (OT4ML p. 112 (104)). How are the cells computed? The dual answer is: through $m$ numbers.

The semi-dual is a finite-dimensional concave problem

Eliminate the source potential by its $\bar c$-transform. For any measures this gives the unconstrained, concave semi-dual $\mathcal E_0(g)=\int g^{\bar c}\dd\al+\int g\dd\be$, with $\mathcal L_c(\al,\be)=\sup_g\mathcal E_0(g)$ and invariance under $g\mapsto g+s$ (OT4ML Def. 6.1, p. 105 (97)). When $\be=\sum_jb_j\delta_{y_j}$ is discrete, $g$ is a vector $\mathbf g\in\R^m$ and $$\begin{gathered}\mathbf g^{\bar c}(x)=\min_jc(x,y_j)-\mathbf g_j,\\ \mathcal L_c(\al,\be)=\max_{\mathbf g\in\R^m}\ \int\mathbf g^{\bar c}\dd\al+\sum_j\mathbf g_jb_j\end{gathered}$$ (OT4ML (6.12)–(6.13), p. 110 (102); CompOT (5.6)–(5.7), p. 91 (87)). The continuous potential has disappeared: what remains is a concave function of $m$ variables.

Definition (Laguerre cells)OT4ML Def. 6.7, p. 110 (102); compact Def. 6.6, p. 26; CompOT §5.2, p. 91 (87)

$\mathrm L_j(\mathbf g)=\{x:\ c(x,y_j)-\mathbf g_j\le c(x,y_{j'})-\mathbf g_{j'}\ \text{for all}\ j'\}$. For $c=\norm{x-y}^2$ they form a power diagram: its walls are straight and move parallel to themselves when the weights change. For constant $\mathbf g$ the Laguerre cells are the Voronoi cells.

Proposition (gradient of the semi-discrete dual)OT4ML Prop. 6.9, p. 111 (103); compact Prop. 6.8, p. 27; CompOT p. 91 (87); 2019 notes Prop. 16, p. 26

If the minimizing index in $\mathbf g^{\bar c}(x)$ is unique for $\al$-almost every $x$, then $\mathcal E_0$ is differentiable and $\nabla\mathcal E_0(\mathbf g)_j=b_j-\al(\mathrm L_j(\mathbf g))$: prescribed mass minus captured mass.

Proof (differentiate the minimum where it is attained once)

On the cell where $j(x)$ is the unique minimizer, $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\min_j\big(c(x,y_j)-\mathbf g_j-th_j\big)\big|_{t=0}=-h_{j(x)}$. The difference quotients are bounded by $\norm h_\infty$, so dominated convergence gives $\dd\mathcal E_0(\mathbf g)[h]=-\sum_jh_j\al(\mathrm L_j(\mathbf g))+\sum_jh_jb_j$. $\square$

At a maximizer every cell captures exactly its prescribed mass. Conversely, if $\al(\mathrm L_j)=b_j$ for all $j$, the map $T(x)=y_j$ on $\mathrm L_j$ pushes $\al$ to $\be$, and its graph lies in the contact set, so it is optimal by complementary slackness (OT4ML p. 112 (104)). For the quadratic cost, $T=\nabla\varphi$ with $\varphi(x)=\max_j\dotp x{y_j}-\frac12(\norm{y_j}^2-\mathbf g_j)$, a convex, piecewise affine function: this is Brenier’s map of §4. Raising $\mathbf g_j$ lowers the power distance to $y_j$ and enlarges its cell (OT4ML Fig. 6.3). Gradient ascent $\mathbf g\leftarrow\mathbf g+\tau(b-\al(\mathrm L(\mathbf g)))$ is therefore a mass-balancing procedure (Alg. 6.3, p. 112 (104)).

In 1D the cells are intervals ordered like the sites. The optimal ones are the quantile intervals $[F_\al^{-1}(B_{j-1}),F_\al^{-1}(B_j)]$, with $B_j=b_1+\dots+b_j$: the Laguerre realization of the monotone rearrangement (OT4ML (6.15), p. 111 (103); closed form for equal weights in Prop. 6.8).

Stochastic ascent

The semi-dual is an expectation, $\mathcal E_0(\mathbf g)=\E_{X\sim\al}\big[\mathbf g^{\bar c}(X)+\dotp{\mathbf g}b\big]$. The gradient of the integrand at one sample is $b-e_{j(X)}$, where $j(X)$ is the index of the cell containing $X$. Stochastic ascent therefore needs only samples of $\al$, no grid (OT4ML (6.18)–(6.20) and Alg. 6.4, pp. 113–114 (105–106); CompOT §5.4, pp. 96–97 (92–93)). Steps with $\sum\tau_\ell=\infty$ and $\sum\tau_\ell^2\lt\infty$ give almost-sure convergence. A constant step $R/(G\sqrt L)$ with averaging gives $\mathcal E_0(\mathbf g^\star)-\E\,\mathcal E_0(\bar{\mathbf g}_L)\le RG/\sqrt L$, with $G\le2$ (OT4ML Prop. 6.10, p. 113 (105); compact Prop. 6.9, p. 27).

Watch out: the sign of the stochastic gradient in CompOT §5.4 and the 2019 notes

CompOT p. 96 (92) and the 2019 notes p. 26 write the integrand as $\mathbf g^{\bar c}(x)-\dotp{\mathbf g}b$ and its gradient as $(\mathbf 1_{\mathrm L_j(\mathbf g)}(x)-b_j)_j$. Both signs are flipped with respect to their own semi-dual (CompOT (5.12)–(5.13), with $+\dotp{\mathbf g}b$) and to OT4ML (6.18). Ascending along the printed vector enlarges the cells that already captured the sample. On the instance of Lab 3, with the notebook’s steps, the dual value falls from 0.117516 to −0.907863 after 30 steps and to −2.897873 after 300. The correct update is $\mathbf g_j\leftarrow\mathbf g_j+\tau_\ell(b_j-\mathbf 1_{j=j(x_\ell)})$.

§6Optimal quantization

Free masses give Voronoi cells

Quantization asks for the best $m$-atom approximation of a measure, optimizing both the positions and the weights: $\mathcal Q_m(\al)=\min_{Y,b}W_p(\al,\sum_jb_j\delta_{y_j})$ (OT4ML Def. 6.11, p. 114 (106); compact Def. 6.10, p. 28). This is vector quantization, the problem behind compression and $k$-means. For a bounded density on a bounded set, the optimal error decays like $m^{-1/d}$, with bounds of that order from above and from below. This is the curse of dimension of approximation (Prop. 6.12, p. 114 (106)).

Proposition (free masses give Voronoi cells)OT4ML Prop. 6.13, p. 115 (107); compact Prop. 6.12, p. 28; 2019 notes p. 27

For fixed distinct codepoints and $c=d^p$, $\min_bW_p^p(\al,\sum_jb_j\delta_{y_j})=\int\min_jc(x,y_j)\dd\al(x)$. The minimum is attained by sending each point to a nearest codepoint, with $b_j$ the mass of its Voronoi cell.

Each direction of the proof takes one line. Every coupling with a measure on $Y$ costs at least $\min_jc(x,y_j)$ at each $x$, and the nearest-codepoint map attains this bound. Free weights therefore mean zero dual weights: the semi-discrete cells of §5 become Voronoi cells. What remains is a non-convex problem in the positions, $\min_Y\int\min_j\norm{x-y_j}^2\dd\al$. At a minimizer each $y_j$ is the centroid of its cell.

Lloyd’s algorithm alternates the two conditions: compute the Voronoi cells, then move each site to the centroid of its cell (Alg. 6.5, p. 117 (109)). Each half-step minimizes the energy over one block of variables, so the energy never increases. The limit is a stationary configuration, not necessarily a global minimum (p. 115 (107)).

Watch out: the 2019 notes on quantization (p. 27)

The notes define the Voronoi cell as $\{x:\forall j',\ c(x,y_{j'})\le c(x,y_j)\}$, which is the set where $y_j$ is the farthest codepoint; the inequality must be reversed. They also write $\mathcal Q_m(\al)=\min_Y\int\min_jc(x,y_j)\dd\al$ where the left side should be $\mathcal Q_m(\al)^p$. Finally, they state that in 1D “the optimal sampling is simply $y_j=\mathcal C_\al^{-1}(j/m)$” (with a “ToDo: see where this is proved”). That formula is wrong for both versions of the problem, as Lab 4 measures. For equal weights it places each atom at the right end of its quantile bin, and the error is 4 times the optimum. For free weights the optimal codepoints are not quantiles.

Equal weights on the line

Prescribing equal weights $\frac1m$ gives a different problem, which the quantile formula solves in 1D. With the atoms sorted, $W_2^2(\al,\frac1m\sum\delta_{y_i})=\sum_i\int_{I_i}\abs{Q(u)-y_i}^2\dd u$, where $I_i=((i-1)/m,i/m]$ and $Q=F_\al^{-1}$; the problem decouples over the bins.

Proposition (equal-weight quantization on the line)OT4ML Props. 6.14–6.15, pp. 118–119 (110–111); compact Props. 6.13–6.14, p. 29

The optimal atoms are the bin averages $y_i^\star=m\int_{I_i}Q$, and $m^2\,\mathcal Q_m^{\mathrm{eq}}(\al)^2\to\frac1{12}\int_0^1\abs{Q'}^2$. For $m$ i.i.d. samples instead, $m\,\E W_2^2(\al,\hat\al_m)\to\int_0^1u(1-u)\abs{Q'(u)}^2\dd u$.

So well-placed atoms have a $W_2$ error of order $m^{-1}$, and random ones of order $m^{-1/2}$. Midpoint quantiles $Q((i-\frac12)/m)$ are asymptotically as good as bin averages but not optimal. The book makes this point (p. 118 (110)); deck 3, slide 53, calls the midpoint quantiles the explicit optimal placement. Since $\int_0^1Q'(u)^2\dd u=\int\frac{\dd x}{\rho(x)}$, the deterministic constant is $\frac1{12}\int\frac{\dd x}\rho$ and the random one is $\int\frac{F(1-F)}\rho\dd x$.

Beyond the course: where free codepoints go (Zador, Panter–Dite)

With free weights, optimal codepoints are asymptotically distributed with density $\sigma^\star\propto\rho^{d/(d+p)}$ (OT4ML p. 116 (108), citing Zador’s theorem). In 1D, for $p=2$, this is $\rho^{1/3}$, flatter than $\rho$: low-density regions get relatively more codepoints than quantiles would give them. The energy then behaves like $\frac1{12}\big(\int\rho^{1/3}\big)^3m^{-2}$, the Panter–Dite formula. Placing free codepoints at quantiles gives point density $\rho$ and the constant $\frac1{12}\int\frac{\dd x}\rho$, which is larger by Hölder’s inequality, with equality only for the uniform law.

§7$W_1$: Lipschitz prices, a norm, and flows

The problem

When the cost is the distance itself, $c=d$, mass travels in straight lines and the price system becomes special. The certificate of §2 is then a single function, with the same function as the price on both sides, up to sign. This function is the critic of a Wasserstein GAN (§8).

Kantorovich–Rubinstein: one potential, slope at most one

Proposition (c-transforms for c = d)OT4ML Prop. 6.17, p. 120 (112); compact Prop. 6.16, p. 29; CompOT Prop. 6.1, p. 101 (97); StatOT Lemma 1.22, pp. 46–47 (40–41); 2019 notes Prop. 17, p. 28

For $c=d$ on a metric space, $f=g^c$ for some $g$ if and only if $\mathrm{Lip}(f)\le1$. In that case $f^c=-f$.

Proof (the reverse triangle inequality, both ways)

If $f=g^c$, then $$\begin{aligned}\abs{f(x)-f(y)}&=\Big|\inf_z[d(x,z)-g(z)]-\inf_z[d(y,z)-g(z)]\Big|\\&\le\sup_z\abs{d(x,z)-d(y,z)}\le d(x,y).\end{aligned}$$ Conversely, if $\mathrm{Lip}(f)\le1$, then $d(x,y)-f(x)\ge-f(y)$ for all $x$, so $f^c(y)\ge-f(y)$, and taking $x=y$ gives $f^c(y)\le-f(y)$. Hence $f^c=-f$, and applying this to $-f$ gives $(-f)^c=f$, so $f$ is itself a $c$-transform. $\square$

Since the dual can be restricted to closed pairs $(f,f^c)=(f,-f)$ (§3), the dual of $W_1$ has one unknown:

Theorem (Kantorovich–Rubinstein formula)OT4ML (6.24), p. 121 (113); CompOT (6.1), p. 101 (97); StatOT Thm. 1.21, pp. 46–47 (40–41)

$$W_1(\al,\be)=\max_{\mathrm{Lip}(f)\le1}\int f\dd(\al-\be)\eqdef\norm{\al-\be}_{W_1}.$$

The right side depends only on the signed measure $\xi=\al-\be$. On a compact space it defines a norm on signed measures of zero total mass, the Kantorovich–Rubinstein norm; mass zero is needed, because constants are 1-Lipschitz (OT4ML p. 121 (113)). For discrete measures it is a linear program with $O(N^2)$ constraints $\abs{f_k-f_\ell}\le d(z_k,z_\ell)$ ((6.25)). On the line, constraints between neighbours suffice. Integrating by parts, $\int f\dd(\al-\be)=-\int f'(x)\,(F_\al-F_\be)(x)\dd x$, so the optimal slope is $f'=-\operatorname{sign}(F_\al-F_\be)$ and $W_1=\int\abs{F_\al-F_\be}$, the formula of Lesson 1 §6.

Beckmann: transport as a flux

In $\R^d$, $\mathrm{Lip}(f)\le1$ reads $\norm{\nabla f}_\infty\le1$ ((6.26)). Dualizing this pointwise constraint introduces a vector field, the flux, and turns $W_1$ into a divergence-constrained problem.

Proposition (Euclidean Beckmann formula)OT4ML Def. 6.20 and Prop. 6.21, p. 122 (114); compact Prop. 6.19, p. 30; CompOT (6.4), p. 102 (98)

For $\al,\be$ with finite first moments, $W_1(\al,\be)=\min\{\norm s_{\TV}:\ \operatorname{div}s=\al-\be\}$, the minimum over vector-valued measures $s$, with $\dotp{\operatorname{div}s}\varphi=-\int\dotp{\nabla\varphi}{\dd s}$. An optimal flux is $\int\dotp{\zeta}{\dd s}=\iint\int_0^1\dotp{\zeta((1-t)x+ty)}{y-x}\dd t\dd\pi(x,y)$ for an optimal plan $\pi$.

The flux records the oriented mass crossing each region; away from $\al$ and $\be$ it is divergence-free, the local conservation law. It must be allowed to be a measure: moving one Dirac mass to another gives a flux concentrated on a segment (OT4ML p. 121 (113)). On a graph with edge lengths $\ell_e$ the same duality gives a min-cost flow, $W_{1,G}(a,b)=\min\{\sum_e\ell_e\abs{m_e}:\operatorname{div}_Gm=a-b\}$, with constraints only on edges (Prop. 6.23, p. 123 (115)). This is a sparse linear program, solved by the network simplex of Lesson 2 on $O(N)$ variables for planar graphs instead of $N^2$ (Remark 6.24, p. 124 (116)).

Link: Beckmann plus time is Benamou–Brenier

The flux $s$ has no time variable: it says how much mass crosses each place, not when. Lesson 6 §3 adds time and replaces $\norm s$ by the kinetic energy $\norm{v}^2\rho$, which gives $W_2$ and the dynamic formulation behind flow matching.

Watch out: the W₁ dual is a linear program, and the flux is a measure

CompOT p. 102 (98) and the 2019 notes p. 29 call the discrete dual (6.2) “a finite-dimensional convex program with quadratic-cone constraints”. Its constraints $\abs{f_k-f_\ell}\le d(z_k,z_\ell)$ are linear, so it is a linear program; a quadratic cone appears only for $\norm{\nabla f(x)}_2\le1$ on a grid. Deck 3, slide 29, writes the Beckmann problem as $\min\int\norm{m(x)}\dd x$, a flux with a density. For two Dirac masses no such minimizer exists; the minimum is attained only among vector measures (OT4ML Def. 6.18). CompOT Remark 6.1 says that “$W_p$ is the dual of $p$-Hölder functions”; with the cost $d^p$ this dual gives $W_p^p$, not $W_p$.

§8Divergences, dual norms and GANs

The problem

A generative model pushes a latent law $\zeta$ through a network: $\al_\theta=\push{(g_\theta)}\zeta$. It is fitted to data $\be=\frac1m\sum_j\delta_{y_j}$ by minimizing a discrepancy $D(\al_\theta,\be)$. When $\al_\theta$ has a density, it and the data are mutually singular, so the discrepancy must be able to compare singular measures. Which ones can, and how do they behave when the model is far from the data?

Dual norms (integral probability metrics)

A dual norm tests the signed measure against a class $B$ of functions: $\norm{\xi}_B=\sup_{f\in B}\int f\dd\xi$ (OT4ML Def. 7.1, p. 125 (117); compact Def. 7.1, p. 31). The choice of $B$ sets the topology. $\norm f_\infty\le1$ gives total variation (Ex. 7.2), $\mathrm{Lip}(f)\le1$ gives $W_1$ (Ex. 7.3), and both constraints together give the flat norm, close to the bounded-Lipschitz (Dudley) metric (Ex. 7.4). The unit ball of a reproducing-kernel Hilbert space gives the maximum mean discrepancy, $\mathrm{MMD}_k(\al,\be)^2=\iint k\dd(\al-\be)\dd(\al-\be)$ (Def. 7.9 and Prop. 7.10, p. 128 (120)), studied in Lesson 7.

Proposition (metrization by dual norms)OT4ML Prop. 7.5, p. 126 (118), and Cor. 7.6, p. 127 (119); compact Prop. 7.2 and Cor. 7.3, p. 31; 2019 notes Prop. 18, p. 30

On a compact $\X$, with $B=-B$: if $\mathrm{Span}(B)$ is dense in $\mathcal C(\X)$, then $\norm{\al_n-\al}_B\to0$ implies $\al_n\rightharpoonup\al$; if $B$ is compact for $\norm\cdot_\infty$, then $\al_n\rightharpoonup\al$ implies $\norm{\al_n-\al}_B\to0$. The Lipschitz ball, normalized at a point, satisfies both conditions (Arzelà–Ascoli), so $W_1$ metrizes weak convergence.

This is the dual proof of the result of Lesson 3 §5. The unit ball of $\norm\cdot_\infty$ is not compact, which is why total variation is a strong norm: $\norm{\delta_{x_n}-\delta_x}_{\TV}=2$ for every $x_n\neq x$, while $W_1(\delta_{x_n},\delta_x)\to0$. In the other direction, $W_1\le\frac{\mathrm{diam}(\X)}2\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}$ (OT4ML Remark 7.22, p. 132 (124)).

$\varphi$-divergences compare densities

A $\varphi$-divergence compares the two measures through their density ratio: $\mathcal D_\varphi(\al|\be)=\int\varphi\big(\frac{\dd\al}{\dd\be}\big)\dd\be+\varphi'_\infty\,\al^\perp(\X)$, with $\varphi$ convex, $\varphi(1)=0$, and $\al^\perp$ the part of $\al$ singular to $\be$ (OT4ML Def. 7.16, p. 130 (122); compact Def. 7.10, p. 32). Examples:

  • $\varphi(s)=s\log s-s+1$ gives KL (Ex. 7.19). It has superlinear growth, so $\KL(\al|\be)=+\infty$ as soon as $\al$ has mass where $\be$ has none.
  • $\varphi(s)=\abs{s-1}$ gives total variation (Ex. 7.20). It is the only classical example that is both a $\varphi$-divergence and a dual norm.
  • The Jensen–Shannon divergence $\mathrm{JS}^2=\frac12\KL(\al|m)+\frac12\KL(\be|m)$, with $m=\frac{\al+\be}2$, is finite for any pair and at most $\log2$, a value attained exactly on disjoint supports (p. 132 (124)).

Theorem (Pinsker inequality)OT4ML Thm. 7.21, p. 131 (123); compact Thm. 7.13, p. 33

For probability measures, with the full-variation convention, $\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}^2\le2\KL(\al|\be)$.

Proof (reduce to two points, then a second derivative)

Let $A$ be a set where $\al-\be$ is positive, $a=\al(A)$ and $b=\be(A)$; then $a-b=\frac12\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}$. Coarse-graining onto $(A,A^c)$ cannot increase KL (Jensen on each part), so $\KL(\al|\be)\ge a\log\frac ab+(1-a)\log\frac{1-a}{1-b}$. For fixed $b$, the difference between this binary relative entropy and $2(a-b)^2$ vanishes with its first derivative at $a=b$, and its second derivative is $\frac1a+\frac1{1-a}-4\ge0$. So it is nonnegative, which gives $\KL\ge2(a-b)^2=\frac12\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}^2$. $\square$

Every $\varphi$-divergence has a dual form, $\mathcal D_\varphi(\al|\be)=\sup_{f\le\varphi'_\infty}\int f\dd\al-\int\varphi^*(f)\dd\be$ (OT4ML Prop. 7.24, p. 133 (125); compact Prop. 7.14, p. 33). The supremum is over all continuous $f$. Restricted to a neural network $f_\xi$, the dual form gives the adversarial losses of $f$-GANs.

GANs: the critic is a dual potential

The original GAN corresponds, up to a constant and a change of variables of the discriminator, to the generator $2\varphi_{\mathrm{JS}}$, that is, to minimizing $2\,\mathrm{JS}^2(\al_\theta,\be)$ (OT4ML p. 134 (126)). A Wasserstein GAN minimizes the Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual instead, $$\min_\theta\ \sup_{\mathrm{Lip}(f)\le1}\ \E f(g_\theta(Z))-\frac1m\sum_jf(y_j),$$ with $f$ a 1-Lipschitz network, the critic (CompOT §9.4, p. 160 (156); deck 3, slide 34). MMD-GANs use an RKHS ball. The difference shows when the model and the data are mutually singular. $\mathrm{JS}^2$ then saturates at $\log2$, whatever the distance, so its gradient in $\theta$ carries no information. $W_1$ and MMD are continuous for weak convergence and still decrease as the model moves towards the data.

Watch out: weight clipping does not make a critic 1-Lipschitz

The first WGAN clipped each parameter of the critic to $[-c,c]$. This bounds the Lipschitz constant by some constant that depends on the architecture and on $c$, not by 1. The image of the parameter box is neither the Lipschitz ball nor a convex set of functions (OT4ML Remark 7.26, p. 135 (127)). The 2019 notes (p. 33) say that clipping $\norm\xi_\infty\le1$ is done “in order to ensure $f_\xi\in B=\{f;\mathrm{Lip}(f)\le1\}$”, which it does not ensure. The compact notes drop Remark 7.26.

Beyond the course: gradient penalties and spectral normalization

Later WGANs replace clipping by a penalty on $(\norm{\nabla f}-1)^2$ at points between data and samples (Gulrajani et al., 2017), or divide each layer by its spectral norm (Miyato et al., 2018). Neither is in the course resources. Both approximate the Lipschitz constraint; neither computes $W_1$ exactly.

§9Mixtures: maximum likelihood, variational inference, and OT between Gaussian mixtures

The problem

The outline of this lesson starts with “variational inference, comparison with KL, mixture estimation, OT on mixture of Gaussians”. Two questions are involved. Fitting a model to data or to a target density needs a loss, and the classical losses are KL in one direction or the other: how do they compare with transport? Comparing two mixtures can be done on their densities, or on their components: which geometry does each choice give?

Maximum likelihood is a KL projection

For i.i.d. samples $x_i$ of a law $\bar\be$ with density, the normalized negative log-likelihood of a model $\rho_\theta$ converges: $$-\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\log\rho_\theta(x_i)\ \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\ -\int\log\rho_\theta\dd\bar\be=\KL(\bar\be\,|\,\al_\theta)+h(\bar\be),$$ where $h(\bar\be)=-\int\rho_{\bar\be}\log\rho_{\bar\be}$ does not depend on $\theta$. Maximum likelihood therefore minimizes $\KL(\text{data}\,|\,\text{model})$, the “forward” KL. This loss needs a model with a density that is positive wherever the data are. A generative model $\push{(h_\theta)}\zeta$ supported on a low-dimensional set has no density, so its likelihood is $-\infty$. This is the reason for the weak losses of §8, and for minimum Kantorovich estimators, $\min_\theta\mathcal L_c(\al_\theta,\be)$ (2019 notes §9.1, pp. 40–41; CompOT §9.4, p. 159 (155)).

Watch out: the limit of the likelihood in the 2019 notes (p. 40)

The notes write $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{MLE}}(\al,\be)=-\sum_i\log\rho_\theta(x_i)\to\KL(\al|\bar\be)$. Without the factor $\frac1n$ the sum diverges. With it, the limit is $\KL(\bar\be|\al)+h(\bar\be)$: the arguments are in the other order, and there is an additive constant. The order matters, as the next subsection shows.

Variational inference uses the other KL

Variational inference approximates a target $\pi$, known up to a constant, by the best member of a simple class: $q^\star\in\argmin_{q\in Q}\KL(q\,|\,\pi)$ (StatOT (VI), p. 173 (167)). The unknown normalizing constant only adds a constant to $\KL(q|\pi)$, which is why this direction is used. The two directions behave very differently on a multimodal target.

  • Forward KL, $\KL(\pi|q)$, covers the mass. Wherever $\pi$ has mass, $q$ must have some too, otherwise the integrand $\pi\log(\pi/q)$ is large. For the Gaussian family, $\KL(\pi|q)=-\int\pi\log q+\mathrm{const}$, and $-\int\pi\log q=\frac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2)+\frac{\E_\pi(X-m)^2}{2\sigma^2}$. It is minimized by the mean and the variance of $\pi$: moment matching.
  • Reverse KL, $\KL(q|\pi)$, seeks a mode. Wherever $q$ has mass, $\pi$ must have some, so $q$ avoids the valleys of $\pi$ and settles in one mode. Each mode gives a local minimum. A Gaussian that fits a component of weight $w$ almost exactly has $\KL(q|\pi)\approx\log(1/w)$.
  • $W_2$ compares positions. In 1D, $W_2^2(\pi,\Gauss(m,\sigma^2))=\int_0^1\big(Q_\pi(u)-m-\sigma\Phi^{-1}(u)\big)^2\dd u$, a least-squares fit of the quantile function by an affine function of $\Phi^{-1}$. The optimum is $m=\E_\pi X$ and $\sigma=\int_0^1(Q_\pi-m)\Phi^{-1}$. By Cauchy–Schwarz this $\sigma$ is at most the standard deviation, with equality only for Gaussian $\pi$.

OT between Gaussian mixtures

A mixture $\sum_ia_i\Gauss(m_i,\Sigma_i)$ can be seen as a measure on $\R^d$ (the collapsed mixture) or as a discrete measure $\mathfrak A=\sum_ia_i\delta_{\Gauss(m_i,\Sigma_i)}$ on the space of Gaussians with the Bures–Wasserstein metric. Transporting the components as atoms gives a $3\times3$ problem for three components, instead of a problem on $\R^d$: $$\mathrm{MW}_2^2(\mathfrak A,\mathfrak B)=\min_{P\in\Couplings(a,b)}\sum_{i,j}P_{i,j}\Big(\norm{m_i-n_j}^2+\mathcal B(\Sigma_i,\Lambda_j)^2\Big)$$ (OT4ML Def. 13.9 and (13.8), p. 293 (285); compact (13.7), p. 87; in 1D, $\mathcal B(\sigma^2,\tau^2)^2=(\sigma-\tau)^2$). It interpolates by moving each active pair of components along its Gaussian geodesic, $\bar\al_t=\sum_{i,j}P^\star_{i,j}\Gauss(m_{ij,t},\Sigma_{ij,t})$.

Proposition (collapsing is non-expansive)OT4ML Prop. 13.10, p. 294 (286); compact Prop. 13.9, p. 87

$W_p(\bar\al_{\mathfrak A},\bar\be_{\mathfrak B})\le\mathrm W_p(\mathfrak A,\mathfrak B)$: transport between the mixtures as densities costs at most transport between their components.

The proof is gluing, as in Lesson 3 §1. Choose an optimal plan between each pair of components, mix these plans with the weights of a component coupling $\Pi$, and the result is a coupling of the two collapsed mixtures with cost $\int W_p^p\dd\Pi$. The inequality is strict as soon as the true optimal plan splits a component. The component distance is cheap and keeps the components identifiable, which is what one wants when they are meaningful objects (StatOT §5.6, pp. 158–160 (152–154), after Chen, Georgiou and Tannenbaum, and Delon and Desolneux). It is not a distance between the densities: the same density can be written as a mixture in many ways. StatOT p. 159 (153) gives the example $\Gauss(0,\mathrm{I})=\int\Gauss(x,\tau\mathrm I)\dd\Gauss(0,(1-\tau)\mathrm I)(x)$ for every $\tau\in[0,1]$.

Exercise 1 — Total variation and 0/1 cost§2, §7 · checked

Let $d(x,y)=\mathbb 1_{x\neq y}$ be the discrete $0/1$ distance. Show that for any $p\ge1$ and probability measures $\al,\be$, $W_p^p(\al,\be)=\frac12\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}$. Start with $\al=\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i}$ and $\be=\sum_ib_i\delta_{x_i}$ on the same finite set.

Hint 1
$d^p=d$ for every $p$, so $W_p^p$ is the optimal cost for $c(x,y)=\mathbb 1_{x\ne y}$: the cost of a plan is the mass it moves. How much mass can stay in place at $x_i$?
Hint 2
For the lower bound, use a price. With the $0/1$ distance, which functions are 1-Lipschitz? Try $f=\mathbb 1_A$ with $A=\{i:\ a_i\gt b_i\}$.
Solution

Upper bound (a plan). A coupling $P$ leaves the mass $P_{i,i}\le\min(a_i,b_i)$ at $x_i$ and pays $1$ for every unit it moves, so its cost is $1-\sum_iP_{i,i}$. The plan $P=\diag(\min(a,b))+\frac1s(a-b)_+(b-a)_+^\top$, with $s=\sum_i(a_i-b_i)_+$, is a coupling: its row sums are $\min(a_i,b_i)+(a_i-b_i)_+=a_i$, and its column sums are $b_j$ for the same reason. It costs $1-\sum_i\min(a_i,b_i)=\frac12\sum_i\abs{a_i-b_i}$, since $\min(a,b)=\frac{a+b-\abs{a-b}}2$.

Lower bound (a price). For the $0/1$ distance, $f$ is 1-Lipschitz exactly when $\abs{f(x)-f(y)}\le1$ for $x\ne y$, that is, when its values lie in an interval of length 1. Take $f=\mathbb 1_A$ with $A=\{a_i\gt b_i\}$. By weak duality with the pair $(f,-f)$ (§7), $W_1\ge\int f\dd(\al-\be)=\sum_{i\in A}(a_i-b_i)=\frac12\norm{a-b}_1$, because $a-b$ has total mass zero.

The two bounds agree, so both are optimal: $W_p^p=W_1=\frac12\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}$, with OT4ML’s convention $\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}=\sum_i\abs{a_i-b_i}$. For general measures the same two objects work. The plan keeps $\al\wedge\be$ on the diagonal and couples $(\al-\be)_+$ with $(\al-\be)_-$ by their normalized product. The price is $\mathbb 1_A$ for a set $A$ where $\al-\be\ge0$ (a Hahn set). This is OT4ML Prop. 4.21, p. 81 (73), and CompOT Remark 2.26 with the factor $\frac12$ that it misses (Lesson 3 §8). on 500 random pairs of histograms with 3 to 12 points, the network simplex with the $0/1$ cost and $\frac12\norm{a-b}_1$ differ by below 10⁻¹⁴; POT’s ot.emd2 agrees.

Exercise 2 — Equivalence of topologies on a finite metric space§7 · checked

Let $(X,d)$ be a finite metric space, $d_{\min}=\min_{x\ne y}d(x,y)$ and $d_{\max}=\max_{x,y}d(x,y)$. Show that for all probability measures $\al,\be$ on $X$, $\frac{d_{\min}}2\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}\le W_1(\al,\be)\le\frac{d_{\max}}2\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}$. Conclude that the $W_1$ topology and the total-variation topology coincide on $\Pc(X)$.

Hint 1
Compare the cost $d$ with the $0/1$ cost of Exercise 1: $d_{\min}\mathbb 1_{x\ne y}\le d(x,y)\le d_{\max}\mathbb 1_{x\ne y}$.
Hint 2
Dual proof of the lower bound: is $d_{\min}\mathbb 1_A$ 1-Lipschitz for $d$? Dual proof of the upper bound: a 1-Lipschitz $f$ takes values in an interval of length at most $d_{\max}$. Center it.
Solution

Primal proof. The cost of any plan is monotone in the cost function, so $d_{\min}\mathcal L_{0/1}\le\mathcal L_d\le d_{\max}\mathcal L_{0/1}$, and Exercise 1 gives $\mathcal L_{0/1}=\frac12\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}$.

Dual proof. Lower bound: $f=d_{\min}\mathbb 1_A$ with $A=\{\al\gt\be\}$ satisfies $\abs{f(x)-f(y)}\le d_{\min}\le d(x,y)$ for $x\ne y$, so it is 1-Lipschitz, and $W_1\ge\int f\dd(\al-\be)=\frac{d_{\min}}2\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}$. Upper bound: if $\mathrm{Lip}(f)\le1$, then $\max f-\min f\le d_{\max}$. Since $\al-\be$ has mass zero, $f$ can be shifted so that $\abs f\le\frac{d_{\max}}2$, and then $\int f\dd(\al-\be)\le\frac{d_{\max}}2\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}$. Taking the supremum over $f$ (Kantorovich–Rubinstein, §7) gives the bound. This is OT4ML Remark 7.22 with $\mathrm{diam}=d_{\max}$.

Topologies. The two inequalities say that $W_1$ and $\norm\cdot_{\TV}$ are equivalent metrics on $\Pc(X)$, so they have the same convergent sequences and the same open sets. Both coincide with the Euclidean topology of the simplex. On an infinite space no such $d_{\min}\gt0$ exists, and the comparison fails: $\delta_{x_n}\to\delta_x$ in $W_1$ but not in total variation (§8). Both constants are sharp: on a two-point space both inequalities are equalities. on 500 random spaces of 3 to 12 points in the unit square, the smallest value of $W_1/(\frac{d_{\min}}2\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV})$ is 1.0041 and the largest value of $W_1/(\frac{d_{\max}}2\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV})$ is 0.9995. The witness $d_{\min}\mathbb 1_A$ never exceeds $W_1$ (yes), and POT confirms both inequalities on 500 other random spaces.

Exercise 3 — The algebra of $c$-transforms§3 · checked

Show that $f\le f'\Rightarrow f^c\ge f'^c$; $f^{c\bar c}\ge f$; $g^{\bar cc}\ge g$; $f^{c\bar cc}=f^c$.

Hint 1
$f^c(y)=\inf_x c(x,y)-f(x)$ is an infimum of functions that decrease when $f$ increases. For the second identity, write $f^{c\bar c}(x)=\inf_y c(x,y)-f^c(y)$ and bound $f^c(y)$ from above by one term of its infimum.
Hint 2
For the last identity, apply the first one to the inequality of the second, and the third one to $g=f^c$.
Solution

(i) If $f\le f'$, then $c(x,y)-f(x)\ge c(x,y)-f'(x)$ for every $x$; taking the infimum over $x$ gives $f^c(y)\ge f'^c(y)$. (ii) For all $x,y$, $f^c(y)\le c(x,y)-f(x)$, so $c(x,y)-f^c(y)\ge f(x)$, and the infimum over $y$ gives $f^{c\bar c}(x)\ge f(x)$. (iii) The same argument with the roles of $\X$ and $\Y$ exchanged. (iv) Apply (i) to $f\le f^{c\bar c}$: $(f^{c\bar c})^c\le f^c$. Apply (iii) to $g=f^c$: $(f^c)^{\bar cc}\ge f^c$. The two left sides are both $f^{c\bar cc}$, so $f^{c\bar cc}=f^c$.

This is OT4ML Prop. 5.14, p. 102 (94) (compact Prop. 5.12; 2019 notes Prop. 15). The last identity explains the stall of Lab 2: after one pair of transforms, $(f^{c\bar c},f^c)$ is a fixed point of the alternation, whether or not it is optimal. on 500 random cost matrices from $3\times3$ to $10\times10$, the four statements hold to rounding: (i) yes, (ii) yes, (iii) yes, and $\max\abs{f^{c\bar cc}-f^c}=$0 (exactly): the minima select the same entries.

Exercise 4 — Two Gaussian mixtures (StatOT §1.8, Exercise 6)§7, §9 · checked

Let $m\gt0$, $\mu=\frac12\Gauss(-m,1)+\frac12\Gauss(m,1)$ and $\nu=\frac14\Gauss(-m,1)+\frac34\Gauss(m,1)$. Prove that $W_2(\mu,\nu)\asymp m$, that is, $c\,m\le W_2(\mu,\nu)\le C\,m$ for constants independent of $m$. The optimal map is painful to compute; bounds are not.

Hint 1
Lower bound: $W_1\le W_2$ (Jensen), and every 1-Lipschitz $f$ gives $\E_\nu f-\E_\mu f\le W_1$. Which $f$ sees the difference between $\mu$ and $\nu$?
Hint 2
Upper bound: $\nu$ is $\mu$ with a quarter of the mass moved from the left component to the right one. Build a coupling that translates exactly that quarter by $2m$.
Solution

Lower bound. With $f(x)=x$, $W_2\ge W_1\ge\E_\nu X-\E_\mu X=\frac34m-\frac14m-0=\frac m2$. In fact $W_1=\frac m2$ exactly. Indeed $F_\mu-F_\nu=\frac14\big(\Phi(x+m)-\Phi(x-m)\big)\ge0$, so the CDFs do not cross and $W_1=\int\abs{F_\mu-F_\nu}=\frac14\cdot2m$.

Upper bound. Draw $X\sim\mu$. If $X$ comes from the left component, set $Y=X+2m$ with probability $\frac12$ and $Y=X$ otherwise; if $X$ comes from the right component, set $Y=X$. Then $Y\sim\frac14\Gauss(-m,1)+\frac34\Gauss(m,1)=\nu$, and $\E\abs{X-Y}^2=\frac14(2m)^2=m^2$. Hence $\frac m2\le W_2(\mu,\nu)\le m$.

Both constants are sharp. As $m\to0$, linearizing the 1D quantile formula gives $W_2^2\approx\int\frac{(F_\mu-F_\nu)^2}{\rho_\mu}\approx\int\frac{(m\varphi/2)^2}{\varphi}=\frac{m^2}4$, so $W_2/m\to\frac12$. As $m\to\infty$, a quarter of the mass has to cross the gap of width $2m$ between the modes, so $W_2/m\to1$. The lower bound by a price and the upper bound by a plan are the two halves of the duality of §2. the quantile formula gives $W_2/m=$ 0.5002, 0.5049, 0.5262, 0.6255, 0.7823 and 0.8860 for $m=0.1,0.5,1,2,4,8$, and $W_1/m=$0.5000 at every $m$; SciPy quadrature agrees.

§14Cheat sheet

Discrete duality$\mathrm L_C(a,b)=\max_{\mathbf f\oplus\mathbf g\le C}\dotp{\mathbf f}a+\dotp{\mathbf g}b$; gap $=\sum P_{i,j}(C_{i,j}-\mathbf f_i-\mathbf g_j)$; optimal iff $P$ lives on contacts; a vertex has at most $n+m-1$ entries.
Continuous dualitycompact spaces, continuous $c$: $\mathcal L_c=\max_{f\oplus g\le c}\int f\dd\al+\int g\dd\be$, attained by a closed pair $(g^{\bar c},f^c)$; $\supp\pi\subset\{f\oplus g=c\}$.
$c$-transforms$f^c(y)=\inf_xc(x,y)-f(x)$; $f\le f'\Rightarrow f^c\ge f'^c$; $f^{c\bar c}\ge f$; $f^{c\bar cc}=f^c$: alternation stalls after one cycle. Lipschitz costs give Lipschitz transforms.
Quadratic cost$c=\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$: $f^c=\frac12\norm y^2-\varphi^*$ with $\varphi=\frac12\norm x^2-f$ convex; contacts $y\in\partial\varphi(x)$; density $\Rightarrow$ $T=\nabla\varphi$ (Brenier). For $c=\norm{x-y}^2$: $\varphi=\frac12\norm x^2-\frac f2$, in 1D $f'=2(x-T(x))$.
Semi-dual$\mathcal E_0(\mathbf g)=\int\min_j(c(x,y_j)-\mathbf g_j)\dd\al+\dotp{\mathbf g}b$, concave; $\nabla\mathcal E_0=b-\al(\mathrm L(\mathbf g))$; stochastic gradient $b-e_{j(x)}$; averaged rate $RG/\sqrt L$.
Laguerre cells$\mathrm L_j=\{c(x,y_j)-\mathbf g_j\le c(x,y_{j'})-\mathbf g_{j'}\}$; power diagram for $\norm{x-y}^2$; Voronoi for constant $\mathbf g$; raising $\mathbf g_j$ enlarges $\mathrm L_j$.
Quantizationfree weights $\Rightarrow$ Voronoi, energy $\int\min_j\norm{x-y_j}^2\dd\al$; Lloyd: cells then centroids, energy never increases; rate $m^{-1/d}$.
Quantization in 1Dequal weights: bin averages, $m^2\mathcal Q^2\to\frac1{12}\int\frac{\dd x}\rho$; midpoints asymptotically equal; right ends 4×; i.i.d.: $m\E W_2^2\to\int\frac{F(1-F)}\rho$; free weights: density $\propto\rho^{1/3}$, $\frac1{12}(\int\rho^{1/3})^3$.
$W_1$$c=d$: $c$-concave $=$ 1-Lipschitz, $f^c=-f$; $W_1=\max_{\mathrm{Lip}f\le1}\int f\dd(\al-\be)$; 1D: $\int\abs{F_\al-F_\be}$, witness slope $-\operatorname{sign}(F_\al-F_\be)$; Beckmann $\min\{\norm s_{\TV}:\operatorname{div}s=\al-\be\}$; graphs: min-cost flow on edges.
Dual norms$\norm\xi_B=\sup_{f\in B}\int f\dd\xi$: $\norm f_\infty\le1$ (TV), Lipschitz (W₁), both (flat), RKHS ball (MMD); compact $B$ with dense span $\Rightarrow$ metrizes weak convergence.
Divergences$\mathcal D_\varphi=\int\varphi(\frac{\dd\al}{\dd\be})\dd\be+\varphi'_\infty\al^\perp(\X)$; KL $=+\infty$ without overlap; $\mathrm{JS}^2\le\log2$, equality iff disjoint; Pinsker $\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}^2\le2\KL$; $W_1\le\frac{\mathrm{diam}}2\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}$.
GANsvanilla GAN $\approx\min_\theta2\,\mathrm{JS}^2$ (saturates on disjoint supports); WGAN: $\min_\theta\sup_{\mathrm{Lip}f\le1}\E f(g_\theta(Z))-\E f(Y)$; clipping $\ne$ 1-Lipschitz.
KL directionsMLE: $\KL(\text{data}|\text{model})$ + const (mass-covering, moment matching for Gaussians); VI: $\KL(q|\pi)$ (mode-seeking, $\approx\log\frac1w$ on a mode of weight $w$); best Gaussian in $W_2$: $\sigma=\int(Q-m)\Phi^{-1}$.
Gaussian mixtures$\mathrm{MW}_2^2=\min_P\sum P_{i,j}(\norm{m_i-n_j}^2+\mathcal B(\Sigma_i,\Lambda_j)^2)$; $W_2(\text{densities})\le\mathrm{MW}_2$; Lab 7: 1.8784 against 0.5839.

§15Self-test

  1. Why does every admissible pair $(f,g)$ give a lower bound on the cost of every plan?
    Answer
    For a coupling $\pi$, $\int f\dd\al+\int g\dd\be=\int(f(x)+g(y))\dd\pi\le\int c\dd\pi$, because the marginals of $\pi$ are $\al$ and $\be$ and $f\oplus g\le c$.
  2. State complementary slackness, and explain how a plan and a pair of potentials certify each other.
    Answer
    The gap is $\int(c-f\oplus g)\dd\pi\ge0$. Both are optimal if and only if it is zero, i.e. $\pi$ lives on the contact set $\{f\oplus g=c\}$. Exhibiting both is a certificate: a feasible plan and an admissible pair with equal values.
  3. In Lab 1, the shifted target has 61 contacts but only 60 plan entries. What does that mean?
    Answer
    The optimal vertex is degenerate: one contact carries no mass. A non-degenerate vertex of the transport polytope has $n+m-1=61$ positive entries.
  4. For $c=-xy$ on a bounded interval, $f^{c\bar c}$ is a concave closure of $f$. Why is it not the ordinary concave envelope?
    Answer
    $f^{c\bar c}$ is an infimum of affine functions $x\mapsto-xy-f^c(y)$ whose slopes $-y$ lie in $-\Y$. On a bounded $\Y$ the slopes are restricted, so the closure can lie above the concave envelope (by 0.271 in Lab 2).
  5. Why does alternating $f\mapsto f^c\mapsto f^{c\bar c}$ stop improving after one cycle, and is the result optimal?
    Answer
    Because $f^{c\bar cc}=f^c$: the pair $(f^{c\bar c},f^c)$ is a fixed point. It need not be optimal; in Lab 2 the alternation from a sine stays at −0.099127 while the optimum is 0.522569. Lesson 7 replaces the hard minimum by a soft one, which keeps improving.
  6. For $c=\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$, express $f^c$ with a convex conjugate. Where does the convexity of Brenier’s potential come from?
    Answer
    $f^c(y)=\frac12\norm y^2-\varphi^*(y)$ with $\varphi=\frac12\norm x^2-f$. If $f=g^{\bar c}$, then $\varphi(x)=\sup_y\dotp xy-(\frac12\norm y^2-g(y))$ is a supremum of affine functions, hence convex.
  7. Why does a source with a density turn the contact set into the graph of a map?
    Answer
    Contacts satisfy $y\in\partial\varphi(x)$. A convex function is differentiable except on a Lebesgue-null set, so for $\al$-almost every $x$ the subdifferential is $\{\nabla\varphi(x)\}$, and $\pi$ is carried by the graph of $\nabla\varphi$.
  8. In semi-discrete transport, what is the gradient of the semi-dual, and what does gradient ascent do to the cells?
    Answer
    $\nabla\mathcal E_0(\mathbf g)_j=b_j-\al(\mathrm L_j(\mathbf g))$. Ascent raises the weight of cells with too little mass, which enlarges them, and lowers the others: it balances the cell masses.
  9. Why did the notebook’s stochastic ascent stall 2.9% below the optimum it plots?
    Answer
    It samples the two Gaussians with probability ½ each, while the grid measure on which the dual is evaluated gives them 0.392 and 0.608. The ascent converges to the potentials of another measure.
  10. What do the Laguerre cells become when the weights $b$ are free, and why?
    Answer
    Voronoi cells. For fixed codepoints, $\min_bW_p^p=\int\min_jc(x,y_j)\dd\al$, attained by sending each point to a nearest codepoint (OT4ML Prop. 6.13); this corresponds to zero dual weights.
  11. Why does Lloyd’s energy never increase, and does Lloyd find the optimal quantizer?
    Answer
    Each half-step minimizes the energy over one block: the Voronoi assignment for fixed sites, the centroids for fixed cells. The problem is non-convex in the sites, so Lloyd stops at a stationary configuration, not necessarily the global minimum.
  12. On the line, with equal weights $\frac1m$, which atoms are optimal and at which rate does the error decay? What about i.i.d. samples?
    Answer
    The bin averages $m\int_{I_i}Q$, with $m^2W_2^2\to\frac1{12}\int_0^1Q'^2=\frac1{12}\int\frac{\dd x}\rho$. Midpoint quantiles have the same limit. I.i.d. samples: $m\,\E W_2^2\to\int_0^1u(1-u)Q'^2$, an $m^{-1/2}$ rate for $W_2$.
  13. For $c=d$, which functions are $c$-concave, and what is $f^c$?
    Answer
    The 1-Lipschitz functions, and $f^c=-f$ (OT4ML Prop. 6.17). Closed pairs are $(f,-f)$, which gives the Kantorovich–Rubinstein formula.
  14. On the line, what is the optimal 1-Lipschitz witness of $W_1(\al,\be)$?
    Answer
    $f'=-\operatorname{sign}(F_\al-F_\be)$, since $\int f\dd(\al-\be)=-\int f'(F_\al-F_\be)\dd x$. Its value is $\int\abs{F_\al-F_\be}$ (0.762479 in Lab 5).
  15. What is an optimal Beckmann flux between $\delta_x$ and $\delta_y$, and why must fluxes be measures?
    Answer
    The unit-speed flow along the segment: $\int\dotp\zeta{\dd s}=\int_0^1\dotp{\zeta((1-t)x+ty)}{y-x}\dd t$, of total variation $\norm{y-x}=W_1$. It is concentrated on a segment, so it has no density with respect to Lebesgue measure.
  16. Why is total variation a strong norm? Give the inequality between $W_1$ and total variation on a bounded space.
    Answer
    $\norm{\delta_{x_n}-\delta_x}_{\TV}=2$ for all $x_n\ne x$, even when $x_n\to x$. On the other hand $W_1\le\frac{\mathrm{diam}}2\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}$, so convergence in total variation implies convergence in $W_1$.
  17. State Pinsker’s inequality with OT4ML’s convention. What does it give for Gaussian translates?
    Answer
    $\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}^2\le2\KL(\al|\be)$. For $\Gauss(0,1)$ and $\Gauss(t,1)$, $\sqrt{2\KL}=\abs t$, which happens to be $W_1$; TV is smaller (0.7658 at $t=1$).
  18. Why can a GAN trained with the Jensen–Shannon loss stall when model and data have disjoint supports?
    Answer
    $\mathrm{JS}^2$ equals $\log2$ for every pair of mutually singular measures, so it does not change when the model moves and its gradient vanishes. $W_1$ keeps growing with the distance (Lab 6).
  19. Does clipping the critic’s weights to $[-c,c]$ make it 1-Lipschitz?
    Answer
    No. It bounds the Lipschitz constant by an unknown constant that depends on the architecture and on $c$, and the set of reachable critics is not the Lipschitz ball (OT4ML Remark 7.26).
  20. Which KL does maximum likelihood minimize, and which does variational inference minimize?
    Answer
    Maximum likelihood minimizes $\KL(\text{data}|\text{model})$, up to the entropy of the data. Variational inference minimizes $\KL(q|\pi)$, which needs $\pi$ only up to a constant.
  21. Fitting one Gaussian to the trimodal mixture of Lab 7: what do forward KL, reverse KL and $W_2$ choose?
    Answer
    Forward KL: moment matching, $\sigma=1.670$. Reverse KL: a local minimum on one mode, the best being a side mode with $\sigma=0.180$ and value $\log(1/0.45)$. $W_2$: the mean and $\sigma=\int(Q-m)\Phi^{-1}=1.477$.
  22. Which is larger, the component distance $\mathrm{MW}_2$ between two Gaussian mixtures or $W_2$ between their densities? Why?
    Answer
    $\mathrm{MW}_2\ge W_2$: gluing the component plans gives a coupling of the densities (OT4ML Prop. 13.10). The true plan may split components, which the component distance does not allow (1.8784 against 0.5839 in Lab 7).
  23. On a finite metric space, why do $W_1$ and total variation define the same topology?
    Answer
    $\frac{d_{\min}}2\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}\le W_1\le\frac{d_{\max}}2\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}$ with $d_{\min}\gt0$ (Exercise 2): the two metrics are equivalent.
  24. Which sign error would make a stochastic semi-discrete ascent diverge, and where is it printed?
    Answer
    Using $\mathbf 1_{\mathrm L_j}(x)-b_j$ instead of $b_j-\mathbf 1_{\mathrm L_j}(x)$, as in CompOT §5.4 and the 2019 notes p. 26. On the instance of Lab 3 the dual value falls to −2.897873 after 300 steps.
