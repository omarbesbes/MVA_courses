§0Where this lesson sits

Lesson 2 made the Kantorovich problem a well-posed minimisation over couplings, first between histograms, then between arbitrary measures. This lesson asks what the optimal value is. When the cost is a power $d^p$ of a distance, its $p$-th root is a distance on probability measures with finite $p$-th moment: the Wasserstein distance $W_p$. Its triangle inequality comes from one construction, the gluing of two couplings that share a marginal. The lesson then studies the geometry and the topology of this distance. Its geodesics move mass instead of fading it. It metrizes weak convergence once moments are controlled. It compares with total variation in both directions on bounded spaces. Finally it measures the two classical limit theorems: empirical measures converge in $W_p$ (law of large numbers), and normalised sums converge to the Gaussian in $W_1$ at explicit rates (central limit theorem).

§1From a transport cost to a distance

The problem

Lesson 2 attached a number $\mathcal L_c(\al,\be)=\min_{\pi\in\Couplings(\al,\be)}\int c\,\dd\pi$ to two probability measures. Many applications need more than a number: a distance, symmetric and zero only on equal measures, which satisfies the triangle inequality. A distance lets one speak of convergence, of balls of measures and of geodesics. Euclidean distances between densities, such as $\norm{f-g}_{L^2}$, do not do this job. They are not defined for discrete measures, and they ignore how far apart the masses are: two Diracs at distinct points are at the same distance from each other whether they are $10^{-6}$ or $10^6$ apart. The question is for which costs $c$ the transport value is a distance.

The tool: gluing two couplings

The triangle inequality $\mathcal L(\al,\gamma)\le\mathcal L(\al,\be)+\mathcal L(\be,\gamma)$ asks for a coupling of $\al$ and $\gamma$ built from a coupling $\pi$ of $(\al,\be)$ and a coupling $\xi$ of $(\be,\gamma)$. For maps this is composition: if $T_\sharp\al=\be$ and $S_\sharp\be=\gamma$, then $(S\circ T)_\sharp\al=\gamma$. For couplings the analogue is to send the mass at $x$ to $y$ with $\pi$, then to $z$ with $\xi$, choosing $z$ knowing only $y$.

Lemma (discrete gluing)OT4ML Lemma 4.1, p. 74 (66); compact Lemma 4.1, p. 15

Let $a,b,c$ be histograms, $P\in\Couplings(a,b)$ and $Q\in\Couplings(b,c)$. The three-way array $S_{i,j,k}=P_{i,j}Q_{j,k}/b_j$ (and $0$ when $b_j=0$) is non-negative, with $\sum_kS_{i,j,k}=P_{i,j}$ and $\sum_iS_{i,j,k}=Q_{j,k}$. Consequently its $(i,k)$-marginal $$R=P\diag(1/b)\,Q,\qquad R_{i,k}=\sum_{j:\,b_j\gt0}\frac{P_{i,j}Q_{j,k}}{b_j},$$ belongs to $\Couplings(a,c)$.

Proof (two lines of bookkeeping)

If $b_j\gt0$, then $\sum_kS_{i,j,k}=\frac{P_{i,j}}{b_j}\sum_kQ_{j,k}=\frac{P_{i,j}}{b_j}b_j=P_{i,j}$. If $b_j=0$, column $j$ of $P$ is zero because its sum is $b_j$, so both sides vanish. The same computation gives $\sum_iS_{i,j,k}=Q_{j,k}$. Then $\sum_kR_{i,k}=\sum_jP_{i,j}=a_i$ and $\sum_iR_{i,k}=\sum_jQ_{j,k}=c_k$. $\square$

In probabilistic terms, $S$ is the law of a triple $(X,Y,Z)$ where $(X,Y)\sim P$, $(Y,Z)\sim Q$, and $X$ and $Z$ are conditionally independent given $Y$. The same construction works for arbitrary measures once conditional laws exist.

Lemma (gluing lemma)OT4ML Lemma 4.4, p. 75 (67); StatOT Lemma B.5, p. 263 (257); compact Lemma 4.4, p. 15

Let $\X,\Y,\mathcal Z$ be Polish spaces, $\pi\in\Couplings(\al,\be)$ and $\xi\in\Couplings(\be,\gamma)$. There is a probability measure $\sigma$ on $\X\times\Y\times\mathcal Z$ whose $(x,y)$-marginal is $\pi$ and whose $(y,z)$-marginal is $\xi$.

The proof disintegrates both couplings along their common marginal, $\pi(\dd x,\dd y)=\pi_y(\dd x)\be(\dd y)$ and $\xi(\dd y,\dd z)=\be(\dd y)\xi_y(\dd z)$, and sets $\sigma(\dd x,\dd y,\dd z)=\pi_y(\dd x)\,\xi_y(\dd z)\,\be(\dd y)$. The Polish hypothesis is what guarantees that the kernels $y\mapsto\pi_y$ and $y\mapsto\xi_y$ exist. On histograms, $\pi_{y_j}=\sum_i(P_{i,j}/b_j)\delta_{x_i}$, and one recovers $S$.

The definition, and the metric theorem

Fix a Polish metric space $(\X,d)$ and $p\ge1$. The transport cost $d^p$ is finite between two measures as soon as both have a finite $p$-th moment, because $d(x,y)^p\le2^{p-1}\big(d(x,x_0)^p+d(x_0,y)^p\big)$ makes the product coupling admissible with finite cost.

Definition (Wasserstein space and distance)OT4ML Defs. 4.5–4.6, p. 76 (68); StatOT Def. 1.2, p. 22 (16); CompOT (2.18), p. 25 (21)

$\Pc_p(\X)=\big\{\al\ \text{probability on}\ \X:\ \int d(x,x_0)^p\,\dd\al(x)\lt\infty\big\}$, which does not depend on $x_0$, and for $\al,\be\in\Pc_p(\X)$ $$W_p(\al,\be)=\Big(\min_{\pi\in\Couplings(\al,\be)}\int_{\X\times\X}d(x,y)^p\,\dd\pi(x,y)\Big)^{1/p}.$$ The minimum is attained (Lesson 2 §6). For histograms on a finite space with distance matrix $D$, $W_p(a,b)=\mathrm L_{D^p}(a,b)^{1/p}$ (OT4ML Def. 4.2).

Proposition (Wₚ is a distance)OT4ML Props. 4.3 and 4.7, pp. 75–76 (67–68); StatOT Prop. 1.3, p. 22 (16); CompOT Props. 2.2–2.3, pp. 23–25 (19–21); notes Props. 6–7, pp. 15–16

$W_p$ is a distance on $\Pc_p(\X)$: $W_p(\al,\be)=W_p(\be,\al)\ge0$, $W_p(\al,\be)=0$ if and only if $\al=\be$, and $W_p(\al,\gamma)\le W_p(\al,\be)+W_p(\be,\gamma)$.

Proof (gluing, then the triangle inequality of d, then Minkowski)

Symmetry. The flip $(x,y)\mapsto(y,x)$ maps $\Couplings(\al,\be)$ onto $\Couplings(\be,\al)$ and preserves the cost, because $d$ is symmetric.

Definiteness. The diagonal coupling $(\Id,\Id)_\sharp\al$ has cost $0$. Conversely, an optimal coupling of cost $0$ is concentrated on $\{x=y\}$, so for every bounded $f$, $\int f\dd\al=\int f(x)\dd\pi=\int f(y)\dd\pi=\int f\dd\be$.

Triangle inequality. Take optimal $\pi\in\Couplings(\al,\be)$ and $\xi\in\Couplings(\be,\gamma)$, glue them into $\sigma$, and let $(X,Y,Z)\sim\sigma$. The law of $(X,Z)$ is a coupling of $\al$ and $\gamma$, so $$\begin{aligned}W_p(\al,\gamma)&\le\E[d(X,Z)^p]^{1/p}\le\E\big[(d(X,Y)+d(Y,Z))^p\big]^{1/p}\\&\le\E[d(X,Y)^p]^{1/p}+\E[d(Y,Z)^p]^{1/p}.\end{aligned}$$ The second step is the triangle inequality of $d$, the third is Minkowski’s inequality in $L^p(\sigma)$. The right-hand side is $W_p(\al,\be)+W_p(\be,\gamma)$, because the laws of $(X,Y)$ and $(Y,Z)$ are the optimal $\pi$ and $\xi$. $\square$

Key idea: the root is not decoration

The $p$-th root is what makes Minkowski’s inequality apply. Without it the triangle inequality fails already for Diracs: $W_2^2(\delta_0,\delta_2)=4\gt W_2^2(\delta_0,\delta_1)+W_2^2(\delta_1,\delta_2)=2$. It also fails on the histograms of Fig. 4.1: Lab 1 measures $W_2^2(a,c)=3.8888$, against $W_2^2(a,b)+W_2^2(b,c)=2.0278$. For $0\lt p\lt1$ the situation reverses: $d^p$ is itself a distance, and it is the value $\mathcal L_{d^p}$ without root that is a distance (CompOT Remark 2.15, p. 24 (20)).

Key idea: gluing is composition of plans

When $\pi$ and $\xi$ are induced by maps $T$ and $S$, the glued coupling is induced by $S\circ T$ (OT4ML p. 76 (68)). In general, gluing composes plans through conditional independence. OT4ML Remark 4.8 (p. 77 (69)) calls it “the metric engine”. The same argument proves the triangle inequality for Gromov–Wasserstein and several other transport distances.

First computations

  • Diracs. $\Couplings(\delta_x,\delta_y)=\{\delta_{(x,y)}\}$, so $W_p(\delta_x,\delta_y)=d(x,y)$ for every $p$. The map $x\mapsto\delta_x$ embeds $(\X,d)$ isometrically into $(\Pc_p(\X),W_p)$ (StatOT Example 1.4, p. 23 (17)). Two distinct Diracs are at total variation $2$ however close they are; in $W_p$ they converge when the points do (§4).
  • Two atoms with moving weights. $W_p\big(\lambda\delta_x+(1-\lambda)\delta_y,\ \tau\delta_x+(1-\tau)\delta_y\big)=\abs{\lambda-\tau}^{1/p}\,d(x,y)$: the excess mass $\abs{\lambda-\tau}$ must travel from one atom to the other, and everything else stays (StatOT Example 1.4). The exponent $1/p$ is the first sign that $W_p$ and $W_q$ are not equivalent distances (§5).
  • Ordering in $p$. For $p\le q$, Jensen’s inequality applied to any coupling gives $W_p\le W_q$ (StatOT p. 23 (17); OT4ML Prop. 4.17). The smallest of these distances is $W_1$.

Proposition (mean bound and quadratic decomposition)OT4ML Prop. 4.9, p. 77 (69); compact Prop. 4.8, p. 16; CompOT Remark 2.19, p. 26 (22)

For $\al,\be\in\Pc_p(\R^d)$ with means $m_\al,m_\be$: $\norm{m_\al-m_\be}\le W_p(\al,\be)$. For $p=2$, with $\al_0,\be_0$ the centred translates, $$W_2^2(\al,\be)=\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+W_2^2(\al_0,\be_0).$$

Proof (one cross term vanishes)

For every coupling, $m_\al-m_\be=\int(x-y)\dd\pi$, and Jensen gives $\norm{m_\al-m_\be}\le(\int\norm{x-y}^p\dd\pi)^{1/p}$. For $p=2$, write $x=m_\al+u$, $y=m_\be+v$. Then $\norm{x-y}^2=\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+2\dotp{m_\al-m_\be}{u-v}+\norm{u-v}^2$, and the middle term integrates to $0$ because $u$ and $v$ have mean zero under every coupling. Translation is a bijection between the couplings of $(\al,\be)$ and those of $(\al_0,\be_0)$. $\square$

This decomposition is why Gaussian computations separate a mean term from a covariance term (the Bures term of Lesson 1 §8), and why the Gaussian couplings of §2 may assume zero means.

§2Couplings of general measures, case by case

The problem

Between histograms, a coupling is a matrix and the Kantorovich problem is a linear program. Between measures with densities, or between a density and a discrete measure, a coupling is a measure on a product space. To see what optimal couplings look like, and when they are maps, one has to compute some. The exercise sheet of this lesson does exactly this, in four kinds of cases: trivial marginals, Gaussians, a density against atoms, and a density concentrated on a segment. This section follows it; the full solutions are in §9.

Couplings that are forced, and couplings that are free

  • Different masses. If $\al(\X)\ne\be(\Y)$, then $\Couplings(\al,\be)=\emptyset$: both marginals of a coupling have its total mass $\pi(\X\times\Y)$ (sheet Ex. 1(a)). Transport between measures of different masses needs an unbalanced formulation, beyond the course.
  • A Dirac against anything. A coupling of $\delta_x$ and $\be$ is concentrated on $\{x\}\times\Y$, and its second marginal is $\be$, so it is $\delta_x\otimes\be$. There is exactly one coupling, the product (sheet Ex. 1(b)(i)).
  • Two Diracs against a density. A coupling of $\frac12(\delta_x+\delta_y)$ and $U[0,1]$ splits the uniform law into two halves: $\pi=\frac12\delta_x\otimes\mu_x+\frac12\delta_y\otimes\mu_y$ with $\frac12\mu_x+\frac12\mu_y=U[0,1]$. Writing $\mu_x=2g\,\dd t$, any measurable $g:[0,1]\to[0,1]$ with $\int g=\frac12$ gives one. There are infinitely many couplings, from the monotone one ($g=\mathbf 1_{[0,1/2]}$) to the product ($g\equiv\frac12$) (sheet Ex. 1(b)(ii)).

Gaussian couplings

For two centred Gaussians $\al=\Gauss(0,\Sigma_\al)$ and $\be=\Gauss(0,\Sigma_\be)$, restrict the Kantorovich problem to couplings that are themselves Gaussian, $\pi=\Gauss\big(0,\begin{psmallmatrix}\Sigma_\al&K\\K^\top&\Sigma_\be\end{psmallmatrix}\big)$. The unknown is the cross-covariance $K=\E[XY^\top]$, a $d\times d$ matrix. The family is the multivariate version of StatOT’s correlation family (1.1) (p. 19 (13), Fig. 1.2), where $\Sigma_\al=\Sigma_\be=1$ and $K=\rho\in[-1,1]$.

  • Cost. $\E\norm{X-Y}^2=\tr\Sigma_\al+\tr\Sigma_\be-2\tr K$. It is linear in $K$, so the problem is to maximise $\tr K$.
  • Constraint. The block matrix must be positive semi-definite. With $\Sigma_\al\succ0$, a Schur complement shows that this holds exactly when $\Sigma_\be-K^\top\Sigma_\al^{-1}K\succeq0$, that is when $K=\Sigma_\al^{1/2}M\Sigma_\be^{1/2}$ with $\norm M_{\mathrm{op}}\le1$.
  • In 1D (sheet Ex. 2), $K=c$ with $c^2\le\sigma_\al^2\sigma_\be^2$. The cost $\sigma_\al^2+\sigma_\be^2-2c$ is minimal at $c=\sigma_\al\sigma_\be$, with value $(\sigma_\al-\sigma_\be)^2$. The covariance then has rank 1, and $Y=(\sigma_\be/\sigma_\al)X$: the coupling sits on the graph of the increasing linear map, which is the Brenier map of Lesson 1 §8.

Proposition (optimal Gaussian coupling)sheet “Continuous Kantorovich”, Ex. 5, pp. 2–3, with two corrections (see the warning below); OT4ML Prop. 2.56, p. 46 (38), for the value

Write $S=\Sigma_\be^{1/2}\Sigma_\al^{1/2}=U\diag(s)V^\top$ (singular value decomposition). Over Gaussian couplings, $\tr K$ is maximal for $M=VU^\top$, that is $$\begin{gathered}K^\star=\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\big(\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}=\Sigma_\al A,\\ A=\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}\big(\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}.\end{gathered}$$ The optimal cost is $\tr\Sigma_\al+\tr\Sigma_\be-2\tr\big(\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}=\Bures(\Sigma_\al,\Sigma_\be)^2$. The optimal joint covariance has rank $d$, and $Y=AX$ almost surely.

Proof (von Neumann’s trace inequality, and the right order of U and V)

With $K=\Sigma_\al^{1/2}M\Sigma_\be^{1/2}$, $\tr K=\tr(M\Sigma_\be^{1/2}\Sigma_\al^{1/2})=\tr(MS)$. For $\norm M_{\mathrm{op}}\le1$, von Neumann’s trace inequality gives $\tr(MS)\le\sum_is_i$, the sum of the singular values of $S$. Equality holds for $M=VU^\top$: then $\tr(VU^\top U\diag(s)V^\top)=\tr(V\diag(s)V^\top)=\sum_is_i$. (The other order fails in general: $\tr(UV^\top U\diag(s)V^\top)=\tr\big((V^\top U)^2\diag(s)\big)$.) Since $S^\top S=\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al^{1/2}=V\diag(s^2)V^\top$, we have $\sum_is_i=\tr(S^\top S)^{1/2}$.

To identify $K^\star$, use $VU^\top=V\diag(s)V^\top\,V\diag(1/s)U^\top=(S^\top S)^{1/2}S^{-1}$, with $S^{-1}=\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}\Sigma_\be^{-1/2}$. Then $K^\star=\Sigma_\al^{1/2}VU^\top\Sigma_\be^{1/2}=\Sigma_\al^{1/2}(S^\top S)^{1/2}\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}$. The matrix $A$ is symmetric positive definite and satisfies $A\Sigma_\al A=\Sigma_\be$ (it is the Gaussian Brenier map of Lesson 1). The coupling of $X$ and $AX$ has cross-covariance $\E[X(AX)^\top]=\Sigma_\al A=K^\star$, so it is the optimum. Its Schur complement $\Sigma_\be-K^{\star\top}\Sigma_\al^{-1}K^\star=\Sigma_\be-A\Sigma_\al A$ vanishes, hence rank $d$. $\square$

Restricting to Gaussian couplings loses nothing. By Brenier’s theorem the optimal coupling over all couplings is induced by the gradient of a convex function, and the symmetric positive matrix $A$ is such a gradient (of $\frac12x^\top Ax$). The value is therefore $W_2^2(\al,\be)$, the Bures formula of Lesson 1 §8.

A density against two atoms, and back

Let $\al=U[0,1]$ and $\be=\frac12\delta_x+\frac12\delta_y$ with $x\lt y$ (sheet Ex. 3). The map $T=x$ on $[0,\frac12)$ and $T=y$ on $[\frac12,1]$ pushes $\al$ to $\be$. Monge against Kantorovich, for general measures

Every admissible map $T$ defines the graph coupling $(\Id,T)_\sharp\al$, so $W_p\le\Wt_p$ always. The two can differ (Lesson 2 found $1.5$ against $0.5$ when the source has atoms). When the source has no atoms, they have the same infimum, even when no map attains it.

Proposition (Kantorovich is the relaxation of Monge)OT4ML Prop. 4.15, p. 79 (71); compact Prop. 4.11, p. 16

Let $(\X,d)$ be compact, $p\ge1$, and $\al,\be\in\Pc(\X)$ with $\al$ atomless. Then $\Wt_p(\al,\be)=W_p(\al,\be)$, as an equality of infima: the Kantorovich minimum is attained, the Monge infimum need not be.

The proof shows that graph couplings are dense in $\Couplings(\al,\be)$ for weak convergence. Take any coupling $\pi$ and partitions $(A_i)$, $(B_j)$ of $\X$ into cells of diameter at most $\varepsilon$, and set $m_{i,j}=\pi(A_i\times B_j)$. Because $\al$ has no atoms, each cell $A_i$ can be cut into pieces $A_{i,j}$ of masses $m_{i,j}$, and each piece can be mapped onto the normalised restriction of $\be$ to $B_j$ (OT4ML Prop. 2.16, p. 26 (18)). The resulting map $T$ gives the same mass as $\pi$ to every rectangle $A_i\times B_j$, so its cost is within the oscillation of $d^p$ on $\varepsilon$-rectangles of the cost of $\pi$. Corollary 4.16 (p. 80 (72)) then identifies $W_p^p$ as the lower-semicontinuous envelope of the Monge cost $\Wt_p^p$.

The sheet’s Exercise 6 is the standard example where the infimum is not attained. $\al$ is uniform on the segment $\{0\}\times[0,1]$, $\be$ puts half of its mass uniformly on each of the segments $\{\pm1\}\times[0,1]$, and the cost is $\norm{x-y}^2$. Every coupling costs at least $1$ (each unit of mass moves horizontally by $1$). The coupling that splits every point equally between its two horizontal neighbours costs exactly $1$. No map does: a map that sends each point straight across must use only part of the segment for each side, so the heights cannot be uniform. Lesson 1’s Lab 5 measured maps built on $n$ alternating cells: they cost $1+\frac1{12n^2}$, which tends to $1$ and never reaches it (Lesson 1, Lab 5). Brenier’s theorem does not apply because $\al$ has no density in $\R^2$: it lives on a segment.

§3Geodesics: moving mass or mixing mass

The problem

Once measures form a metric space, one can ask for the shortest path between two of them. There are two natural candidates. The mixture $(1-t)\al_0+t\al_1$ keeps every atom where it is and changes its weight: mass fades out at one place and appears at another. The displacement interpolation moves every unit of mass along a straight segment from its origin to its destination, as McCann’s interpolation did for maps in Lesson 1 §9. For $W_2$, only the second is a geodesic.

Displacement interpolation is a constant-speed geodesic

Definition (geodesic induced by an optimal plan)OT4ML Def. 4.12, p. 78 (70); compact Def. 4.9, p. 16; StatOT Thm. 7.6, p. 215 (209)

Let $\al_0,\al_1\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$ and $\pi^\star$ an optimal plan for $W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1)$. With $e_t(x,y)=(1-t)x+ty$, set $\al_t=(e_t)_\sharp\pi^\star$ for $t\in[0,1]$.

Proposition (constant speed)OT4ML Prop. 4.13, p. 78 (70); compact Prop. 4.10, p. 16; StatOT Thm. 7.6 and Cor. 7.7, pp. 215–217 (209–211)

For $0\le s\le t\le1$, $W_2(\al_s,\al_t)=(t-s)\,W_2(\al_0,\al_1)$. Moreover, the plan $(e_s,e_t)_\sharp\pi^\star$ is optimal between $\al_s$ and $\al_t$.

Proof (an upper bound, then the triangle inequality forces equality)

The plan $(e_s,e_t)_\sharp\pi^\star$ couples $\al_s$ and $\al_t$, and it costs $\int\norm{e_t-e_s}^2\dd\pi^\star=(t-s)^2\int\norm{x-y}^2\dd\pi^\star=(t-s)^2W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1)$. So $W_2(\al_s,\al_t)\le(t-s)W_2(\al_0,\al_1)$ for all $s\le t$. Apply this to the three pieces $[0,s]$, $[s,t]$, $[t,1]$ and use the triangle inequality: $$\begin{aligned}W_2(\al_0,\al_1)&\le W_2(\al_0,\al_s)+W_2(\al_s,\al_t)+W_2(\al_t,\al_1)\\&\le\big(s+(t-s)+(1-t)\big)W_2(\al_0,\al_1).\end{aligned}$$ All inequalities are equalities, so each piece has exactly its upper bound, and the plan $(e_s,e_t)_\sharp\pi^\star$, which reaches it, is optimal. $\square$

When the optimal plan splits mass, an atom of $\al_0$ becomes several moving atoms (Fig. 4.2). When $\al_0$ has a density, Brenier’s theorem gives a map $T$ and the geodesic is unique: $\al_t=\big((1-t)\Id+tT\big)_\sharp\al_0$, McCann’s interpolation (StatOT Cor. 7.8, p. 217 (211)). On a general geodesic metric space, segments are replaced by geodesics between points. When these are not unique, one chooses a measure on paths (a dynamical optimal plan; OT4ML Remark 4.14, p. 78 (70), beyond the course).

Why the mixture is not a $W_2$ geodesic

Between $m_s=(1-s)\al_0+s\al_1$ and $m_t$ with $s\lt t$, the measures share the part $(1-t)\al_0+s\al_1$. Leave it in place and transport the remaining $(t-s)\al_0$ optimally onto $(t-s)\al_1$. This gives $$W_p(m_s,m_t)\le(t-s)^{1/p}\,W_p(\al_0,\al_1).$$ For two Diracs at distance $d$ this is an equality, $W_2(m_s,m_t)=\sqrt{t-s}\,d$. A polygonal path through $N+1$ equally spaced times then has length $N\cdot\sqrt{1/N}\,d=\sqrt N\,d$, which tends to infinity: the mixture path has infinite length in $W_2$. Lab 3 measures the same growth on the clouds of Fig. 4.2. For $p=1$ the exponent is $1$, and the argument of the proof above shows that the mixture is a $W_1$ geodesic. Geodesics of $W_1$ are far from unique.

§4Weak convergence, and total variation

The problem

Which notion of convergence should a distance between measures capture? The strongest one, total variation, compares the masses of every measurable set. It is too strong for most of what we do: a Dirac at $1/k$ never converges to $\delta_0$, and an empirical measure never converges to a density, since the two are mutually singular. The useful notion tests measures against continuous functions only.

Weak convergence

Definition (weak, or narrow, convergence)OT4ML Def. 4.18, p. 81 (73); compact Def. 4.14, p. 17; CompOT Def. 2.2, p. 26 (22); notes Def. 2, p. 17

$\al_k\rightharpoonup\al$ if $\int f\dd\al_k\to\int f\dd\al$ for every bounded continuous function $f$. On a compact space, bounded continuous functions are just continuous functions, and this is weak$^\ast$ convergence in the duality between measures and continuous functions.

  • In probabilistic terms, if $X_k\sim\al_k$ and $X\sim\al$, weak convergence is convergence in law of $X_k$ to $X$. The variables need not live on the same probability space. Almost-sure convergence implies convergence in probability, which implies convergence in law (OT4ML p. 82 (74)).
  • Diracs. $\delta_{x_k}\rightharpoonup\delta_x$ if and only if $x_k\to x$: test with a bounded continuous $f$ that peaks at $x$ (Remark 4.20, p. 81 (73)).
  • Riemann sums. $\frac1k\sum_{i=1}^k\delta_{i/k}\rightharpoonup U[0,1]$, because $\frac1k\sum_if(i/k)\to\int_0^1f$. Yet the two measures are mutually singular, so their total variation stays $2$ (Remark 4.19, p. 81 (73)).

Total variation is a transport cost

The total variation norm is $\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}=\sup_{\norm f_\infty\le1}\int f\dd(\al-\be)=\abs{\al-\be}(\X)$ (OT4ML Def. 2.7 and Prop. 2.8, p. 22 (14)). For densities it is $\norm{f-g}_{L^1}$; for histograms, $\norm{a-b}_1$. It is itself a transport cost, for the cost that only asks whether a point moves.

Proposition (total variation as a 0/1 transport cost)OT4ML Prop. 4.21, p. 81 (73); compact Prop. 4.15, p. 17; StatOT p. 21 (15); CompOT Remarks 2.26–2.27, p. 34 (30)

Let $d_0(x,y)=\mathbf 1_{x\ne y}$. Then $\mathcal L_{d_0}(\al,\be)=\min_{\pi\in\Couplings(\al,\be)}\pi(\{x\ne y\})=\frac12\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}$. On a countable space with the discrete metric, this is $W_p(\al,\be)^p$ for every $p$.

Proof (keep the common part, move the rest anyhow)

Let $\eta=\al\wedge\be$ be the common part (density $\min(a,b)$ with respect to $\al+\be$), and $\al'=\al-\eta$, $\be'=\be-\eta$. These two residuals are mutually singular and have the same mass $r=1-\eta(\X)=\frac12\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}$.

Lower bound. For any coupling $\pi$, the mass it leaves on the diagonal is a measure $\zeta$ with $\zeta\le\al$ and $\zeta\le\be$, hence $\zeta\le\eta$. So $\pi(\{x\ne y\})=1-\zeta(\X)\ge1-\eta(\X)=r$.

Attained. If $r\gt0$, the coupling $\pi^\star=(\Id,\Id)_\sharp\eta+r^{-1}\al'\otimes\be'$ has the right marginals. Its product part gives no mass to the diagonal because $\al'\perp\be'$, so it costs exactly $r$. $\square$

Watch out: the factor 1/2

CompOT Remark 2.26 (p. 34 (30)) states that, for the cost matrix $\mathbb 1_{n\times n}-\Id_n$, “$\mathrm L_C(a,b)=\norm{a-b}_1$”. The correct value is $\frac12\norm{a-b}_1$: for $a=(1,0)$ and $b=(0,1)$ the only coupling moves mass $1$, at cost $1$, while $\norm{a-b}_1=2$. Lab 4 confirms $\mathrm L_{0/1}=\frac12\norm{a-b}_{\TV}$ on 400 random pairs. StatOT’s statement, $\inf_\gamma\gamma(X\ne Y)=d_{\TV}$, is correct in its own convention, where $d_{\TV}$ is already halved.

Comparing the two topologies

Proposition (comparison on finite spaces)OT4ML Prop. 4.26, p. 84 (76); compact Prop. 4.18, p. 18

On a finite metric space with at least two points, let $d_{\min}$ and $d_{\max}$ be the smallest and largest distances between distinct points. Then $$\frac{d_{\min}}2\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}\le W_1(\al,\be)\le\frac{d_{\max}}2\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV},$$ with equality for pairs of Diracs at distance $d_{\min}$, respectively $d_{\max}$.

The proof is one line: $d_{\min}d_0\le d\le d_{\max}d_0$, integrate against any coupling, minimise, and apply Proposition 4.21. On a finite space, the two topologies therefore coincide, and the comparison degrades as $d_{\max}/d_{\min}$ grows. On spaces where distinct points can be arbitrarily close, no lower bound survives: that is the moving Dirac. In the other direction, the coupling that keeps the common part also bounds $W_p$ on any bounded set.

Theorem (Wₚ is dominated by total variation on bounded sets)StatOT Thm. 1.6, p. 25 (19)

For measures with densities $f,g$ on $\R^d$ and $p\ge1$, $W_p^p(\mu,\nu)\le2^{p-1}\inf_{x_0}\int\norm{x-x_0}^p\abs{f(x)-g(x)}\dd x$. If both supports lie in a ball of diameter $D$, then $W_p^p(\mu,\nu)\le D^p\,d_{\TV}(\mu,\nu)=\frac{D^p}2\norm{\mu-\nu}_{\TV}$.

StatOT notes that densities are not needed; the same proof works for any measures. On bounded spaces, convergence in total variation therefore implies convergence in $W_p$. On unbounded spaces it does not: §5 builds a sequence whose total variation to its limit tends to $0$ while $W_1$ stays equal to $1$.

A weighted total variation: the bottleneck cost

The sheet’s Exercise 4 takes $c(x,y)=m(x)+m(y)$ for $x\ne y$ and $c(x,x)=0$, with $m\gt0$. This is a metric: picture every point joined to a central hub by an edge of length $m(x)$, and measure distances through the hub. Every unit of mass that moves pays its departure edge and its arrival edge, so only the mass that must move counts: $$\begin{gathered}\mathcal L_c(\al,\be)=\int m\,\dd\abs{\al-\be},\\ \pi^\star=(\Id,\Id)_\sharp\mu+\frac{(\al-\mu)\otimes(\be-\mu)}{1-\mu(\X)},\quad\mu=\al\wedge\be.\end{gathered}$$ With $m\equiv\frac12$ this is Proposition 4.21. On a finite space the hub picture is a tree with one internal node, and the formula is the case of a star of OT4ML’s cumulative formula on trees, $W_1=\sum_e\ell_e\abs{\al(V_e)-\be(V_e)}$ (Prop. 2.50, p. 41 (33)), which Lesson 1 left beyond the course. The solution in §9 proves both inequalities.

§5Convergence in $W_p$ is weak convergence plus moments

The problem

If $W_p(\al_k,\al)\to0$, then $\int h\dd\al_k\to\int h\dd\al$ for every bounded 1-Lipschitz $h$, because $\abs{\int h\dd(\al_k-\al)}\le W_1(\al_k,\al)\le W_p(\al_k,\al)$. So $W_p$ convergence implies weak convergence. The converse fails on unbounded spaces. A vanishing amount of mass can escape to infinity without disturbing weak convergence, while it costs a lot to transport. The question is what must be added to weak convergence.

The characterisation

Proposition (Wasserstein convergence)OT4ML Prop. 4.25, p. 83 (75); compact Prop. 4.17, p. 18; StatOT p. 23 (17); CompOT p. 26 (22)

Let $(\X,d)$ be Polish, $1\le p\lt\infty$, and $\al_k,\al\in\Pc_p(\X)$. For any $x_0$, the following are equivalent:

  1. $W_p(\al_k,\al)\to0$;
  2. $\al_k\rightharpoonup\al$ and $\int d(x,x_0)^p\dd\al_k\to\int d(x,x_0)^p\dd\al$;
  3. $\al_k\rightharpoonup\al$ and $\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_k\int_{\{d(x,x_0)\gt R\}}d(x,x_0)^p\dd\al_k=0$ (uniformly integrable $p$-th moments).

On a compact space the moment condition is automatic, and $W_p$ metrizes weak convergence.

Proof (Skorokhod and Vitali for the converse)

(1) ⇒ (2). Weak convergence was shown above. For the moments, take any coupling $\pi$ of $\al_k$ and $\al$; the triangle inequality in $L^p(\pi)$ gives $\big\lvert\norm{d(\cdot,x_0)}_{L^p(\al_k)}-\norm{d(\cdot,x_0)}_{L^p(\al)}\big\rvert\le\norm{d(x,y)}_{L^p(\pi)}$. Minimise over $\pi$.

(2) ⇒ (1). By Skorokhod’s representation theorem there are $X_k\sim\al_k$ and $X\sim\al$ on one probability space with $X_k\to X$ almost surely. The variables $d(X_k,x_0)^p$ converge almost surely and in expectation, so by Scheffé’s lemma they converge in $L^1$ and are uniformly integrable. Since $d(X_k,X)^p\le2^{p-1}\big(d(X_k,x_0)^p+d(X,x_0)^p\big)$, the variables $d(X_k,X)^p$ are uniformly integrable too and tend to $0$ almost surely. Vitali’s theorem gives $\E\,d(X_k,X)^p\to0$, and the law of $(X_k,X)$ is a coupling, so $W_p^p(\al_k,\al)\le\E\,d(X_k,X)^p\to0$.

(2) ⇔ (3) is the usual link between convergence of expectations and uniform integrability, applied with the truncations $\min(d(x,x_0)^p,R^p)$, which are bounded and continuous. $\square$

The book numbers the three conditions 1–3 and its proof calls them (i)–(iii); they are the same conditions.

Different $p$, same topology on bounded spaces

Proposition (comparison of the Wₚ)OT4ML Prop. 4.17, p. 80 (72); compact Prop. 4.13, p. 17; notes Prop. 8, p. 16 (with an exponent to correct)

On a bounded space, for $1\le p\le q\lt\infty$, $$W_p\le W_q\le\operatorname{diam}(\X)^{\frac{q-p}q}\,W_p^{\,p/q}.$$

The left inequality is Jensen’s inequality; the right one follows from $d^q\le\operatorname{diam}^{q-p}d^p$ under any coupling. The exponent $p/q\lt1$ is sharp. On $[0,1]$, take $\al=\delta_0$ and $\be=(1-\varepsilon)\delta_0+\varepsilon\delta_1$: then $W_1=\varepsilon$ and $W_2=\sqrt\varepsilon=W_1^{1/2}$, equality in the bound. So all the $W_p$ define the same convergent sequences on a bounded space, but they are not equivalent distances. The 2019 notes print the exponent as $q/p$, which would give $W_2\le\varepsilon^2$ in this example, while $W_2=\sqrt\varepsilon$.

The value is continuous

Because $W_p$ is a distance, $\abs{W_p(\al,\be)-W_p(\al',\be')}\le W_p(\al,\al')+W_p(\be,\be')$: the transport value is 1-Lipschitz in each argument for $W_p$ itself. With Proposition 4.25, $W_p(\al_k,\be_k)\to W_p(\al,\be)$ whenever $\al_k\rightharpoonup\al$ and $\be_k\rightharpoonup\be$ with converging $p$-th moments; on a compact space, weak convergence alone suffices. The Monge value has no such property: Lesson 2, Lab 7 shows it jumping between $1.5$ and $0.4$ along a sequence of measures that converges weakly.

Three sequences that separate the notions

Each sequence below converges weakly to its limit. They differ in what else converges.

  • Moving Dirac, $\delta_{1/k}\to\delta_0$: total variation $2$ for every $k$; $W_1=W_2=1/k$.
  • Riemann sums, $\frac1k\sum_{i\le k}\delta_{i/k}\to U[0,1]$: total variation $2$; $W_1=\frac1{2k}$ and $W_2=\frac1{\sqrt3\,k}$ (each atom collects the interval of length $1/k$ to its left).
  • Escaping mass, $\al_k=(1-\frac1k)\delta_0+\frac1k\delta_{k^\gamma}\to\delta_0$: total variation $\frac2k\to0$, so this sequence even converges strongly. But the mass $\frac1k$ has to travel $k^\gamma$, so $W_1=k^{\gamma-1}$ and $W_2=k^{\gamma-1/2}$. For $\gamma=\frac12$, $W_1\to0$ while $W_2=1$. For $\gamma=1$, $W_1=1$: strong convergence without $W_1$ convergence. The $p$-th moment of $\al_k$ is $k^{\gamma p-1}$, and it converges to the moment $0$ of $\delta_0$ exactly when $W_p$ does, as Proposition 4.25 says.

Beyond the course: what the metrization does and does not say

StatOT (p. 23 (17)) warns that metrizing weak convergence is “hardly a discriminating feature”: the Lévy–Prokhorov and Fortet–Mourier (bounded-Lipschitz) distances, and the kernel norms of the 2019 notes (§6.3), do it too. What is specific to $W_p$ is quantitative: a small $W_p$ controls the moments (StatOT Prop. 1.5, p. 24 (18), for sub-exponential laws), and §6–§7 measure the rates of the two limit theorems with it.

§6Empirical measures: the law of large numbers in $W_p$

The problem

In practice one rarely sees a measure $\al$, only samples $X_1,\dots,X_n$ from it, summarised by the empirical measure $\hat\al_n=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{X_i}$ (OT4ML (10.1), p. 195 (187)). Transport between data sets is transport between empirical measures. Two questions follow: does $\hat\al_n$ converge to $\al$ in $W_p$, and how fast?

Consistency

Proposition (empirical law of large numbers in Wₚ)OT4ML Prop. 10.1, p. 195 (187); compact Prop. 10.1, p. 55

Let $(\X,d)$ be Polish, $p\ge1$, $\al\in\Pc_p(\X)$ and $X_1,X_2,\dots$ i.i.d. with law $\al$. Almost surely, $\hat\al_n\rightharpoonup\al$ and $W_p(\hat\al_n,\al)\to0$. Moreover $\E\,W_p^p(\hat\al_n,\al)\to0$.

Proof (two laws of large numbers, then Proposition 4.25)

On a Polish space, weak convergence is tested by a countable family $(\varphi_k)$ of bounded continuous functions. For each $k$, the strong law of large numbers gives $\int\varphi_k\dd\hat\al_n=\frac1n\sum_i\varphi_k(X_i)\to\int\varphi_k\dd\al$ almost surely. A countable intersection of almost sure events is almost sure, so $\hat\al_n\rightharpoonup\al$ almost surely. The strong law applied to the integrable variable $d(X_1,x_0)^p$ gives the convergence of the $p$-th moments. Proposition 4.25 then gives $W_p(\hat\al_n,\al)\to0$ almost surely. For the expectation, the bound $W_p^p(\hat\al_n,\al)\le2^{p-1}\big(\int d(x,x_0)^p\dd\hat\al_n+\int d(x,x_0)^p\dd\al\big)$ and the de la Vallée-Poussin criterion give uniform integrability. $\square$

The proposition says nothing about speed. In one dimension the speed can be computed exactly.

The rate on the line

On $\R$, $W_1(\hat\al_n,\al)=\int\abs{F_n-F}$, where $F_n$ is the empirical CDF (Vallender’s identity, the CDF formula of Lesson 1 §6). At each $x$, $nF_n(x)$ is binomial with parameters $n$ and $F(x)$, so $\E\abs{F_n(x)-F(x)}\le\sqrt{F(x)(1-F(x))/n}$. Integrating, $$\E\,W_1(\hat\al_n,\al)\le\frac1{\sqrt n}\int_{\R}\sqrt{F(1-F)}\,\dd x .$$ This is StatOT’s suggested proof of the 1D case of its Proposition 2.1 (§2.10, Exercise 6, p. 92 (86); Prop. 2.1 is on p. 54 (48)). The bound is useful when the integral is finite, which requires slightly more than a finite second moment.

Proposition (limit of the 1D rate)derived here from the binomial CLT; checked in Lab 6

If $J=\int\sqrt{F(1-F)}\,\dd x\lt\infty$, then $\sqrt n\,\E\,W_1(\hat\al_n,\al)\to\sqrt{2/\pi}\;J$.

Proof (pointwise CLT and dominated convergence)

Fix $x$ with $0\lt F(x)\lt1$. By the central limit theorem, $\sqrt n(F_n(x)-F(x))$ converges in law to $\Gauss\big(0,F(x)(1-F(x))\big)$. Its second moment is $F(x)(1-F(x))$ for every $n$, so it is uniformly integrable, and $\E\abs{\sqrt n(F_n(x)-F(x))}\to\sqrt{2/\pi}\sqrt{F(x)(1-F(x))}$, the mean absolute value of that Gaussian. The left-hand side is bounded by $\sqrt{F(x)(1-F(x))}$ for every $n$, which is integrable by assumption. Dominated convergence in $x$ gives the claim. $\square$

For $\Gauss(0,1)$, $J=1.61474$ and the limit is 1.28838. The expectation is also computable exactly at every $n$, without sampling: $\E\abs{nF_n(x)-nF(x)}$ is the mean absolute deviation of a binomial variable, which has de Moivre’s closed form $2\nu\binom n\nu u^\nu(1-u)^{n-\nu+1}$, with $u=F(x)$ and $\nu=\lfloor nu\rfloor+1$. Lab 6 integrates it.

Watch out: finite mean is not enough for the 1/√n rate

Student’s law with 2 degrees of freedom has a finite mean but an infinite variance. Its CDF has tails $F(x)\approx\frac1{2x^2}$, so $\sqrt{F(1-F)}\approx\frac1{\sqrt2\abs x}$ is not integrable: $J=\infty$. Proposition 10.1 still applies with $p=1$, and $W_1(\hat\al_n,\al)\to0$, but Lab 6 measures $\sqrt n\,\E W_1$ growing without bound, by about $\frac1{\sqrt\pi}\ln10$ per decade. (Heuristically, the bound holds up to $\abs x\approx\sqrt n$, where the tail mass $\frac1{2x^2}$ is of order $\frac1n$, and $\int_{\abs x\lt\sqrt n}\frac{\dd x}{\sqrt2\abs x}=\frac1{\sqrt2}\ln n+O(1)$. Multiplied by $\sqrt{2/\pi}$, this gives the slope $\frac1{\sqrt\pi}$.) Sample averages are also unreliable here: $W_1\ge\abs{\bar X_n}$ (the mean bound of §1) has infinite variance, so their standard error means nothing.

§7The central limit theorem, measured in $W_1$

The problem

The central limit theorem is a statement about weak convergence: the law of a normalised sum approaches a Gaussian. A metric for weak convergence turns it into a number at each $n$, and the question becomes quantitative: how far is the law of the sum from the Gaussian after $n$ steps, and what decides the rate?

Sums are convolutions

Definition (convolution)OT4ML Def. 4.22, p. 82 (74); compact Def. 4.16, p. 18

$\al*\be=\mathrm{add}_\sharp(\al\otimes\be)$ with $\mathrm{add}(x,y)=x+y$: the law of $X+Y$ for independent $X\sim\al$, $Y\sim\be$. For densities, $\rho_{\al*\be}=\rho_\al*\rho_\be$.

If $\al$ has mean $0$ and identity covariance, the law of $Z_n=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i\le n}X_i$ is $\al_n=(D_{1/\sqrt n})_\sharp\al^{*n}$, where $D_s(x)=sx$, and the CLT states $\al_n\rightharpoonup\Gauss(0,\Id)$ (OT4ML Remark 4.23, p. 82 (74)). Fig. 4.3 shows it for $\al=\frac12(\delta_{-1}+\delta_1)$: $\al_n$ stays discrete for every $n$, on a lattice of spacing $2/\sqrt n$, while the atom masses divided by the spacing approach the Gaussian density.

A universal rate

Proposition (Berry–Esseen bound in W₁)OT4ML Prop. 10.2, p. 196 (188); compact Prop. 10.2, p. 55; slide 64

If the $X_i$ are i.i.d. real, $\E X_i=0$, $\E X_i^2=1$ and $\E\abs{X_i}^3\lt\infty$, then $W_1\big(\al_n,\Gauss(0,1)\big)\le C\,\E\abs{X_1}^3/\sqrt n$ for a universal constant $C$.

The proof uses the Kantorovich–Rubinstein formula of Lesson 4 §7, $W_1(\al_n,\Gauss(0,1))=\sup_{\mathrm{Lip}(h)\le1}\abs{\E h(Z_n)-\E h(G)}$, so the bound is a uniform statement about all 1-Lipschitz test functions. For each $h$, Stein’s method solves $f'(x)-xf(x)=h(x)-\E h(G)$, whose solution has $\norm{f'}_\infty+\norm{f''}_\infty\le C$, and then compares $\E[f'(Z_n)]$ with $\E[Z_nf(Z_n)]$ by removing one summand at a time and expanding to second order (OT4ML p. 196 (188)). The rate $n^{-1/2}$ cannot be improved in general, but a given law can do better or have a different constant.

What decides the constant

In 1D, Vallender’s identity $W_1(\al_n,\Gauss(0,1))=\int\abs{F_n-\Phi}$ reduces everything to the CDF error, and Edgeworth expansions describe that error term by term.

Proposition (symmetric lattice laws)OT4ML Prop. 10.3, p. 196 (188); compact Prop. 10.3, p. 55

If $X$ is centred, symmetric, of unit variance and supported on a finite subset of $a+h\mathbb Z$, with $h$ the maximal span, then $\sqrt n\,W_1(\al_n,\Gauss(0,1))\to h/4$.

The lattice Edgeworth expansion gives $F_n(x)-\Phi(x)=\frac h{\sqrt n}\psi\big(\frac{\sqrt nx-na}h\big)\varphi(x)+r_n(x)$ with the sawtooth $\psi(u)=\frac12-\{u\}$ and $\norm{r_n}_{L^1}=o(n^{-1/2})$. The sawtooth oscillates faster and faster, so its absolute value averages to $\int_0^1\abs{\psi}=\frac14$ against the slowly varying $\varphi$. For the Rademacher law, $h=2$ and the limit is $\frac12$.

Proposition (symmetric laws with a density)OT4ML Prop. 10.4 and eq. (10.2), p. 197 (189); compact Prop. 10.4, p. 55

If $X$ is centred, symmetric, of unit variance, with a density and all moments, then $W_1(\al_n,\Gauss(0,1))=\frac{\abs{\kappa_4}}{24n}\int\abs{H_3}\varphi+o(n^{-1})$, where $\kappa_4=\E X^4-3$ and $H_3(x)=x^3-3x$. For $X\sim U[-\sqrt3,\sqrt3]$, $\kappa_4=-\frac65$ and $nW_1\to\frac{1+4e^{-3/2}}{10\sqrt{2\pi}}=0.07550065$.

A density removes the lattice sawtooth, and symmetry removes the skewness term of order $n^{-1/2}$. The first surviving term is of order $n^{-1}$: the rate is faster than Berry–Esseen’s.

Two cases the book does not treat (derived here by the same argument, and checked in Lab 7). Without symmetry, the skewness term $-\frac{\kappa_3}{6\sqrt n}(x^2-1)\varphi(x)$ of the non-lattice expansion is the leading one, where $\kappa_3=\E X^3$. Since $\int\abs{x^2-1}\varphi=4\varphi(1)$ (because $(1-x^2)\varphi=(x\varphi)'$), this gives $$\sqrt n\,W_1(\al_n,\Gauss(0,1))\to\frac{2\abs{\kappa_3}}3\varphi(1).$$ For $X=E-1$ with $E$ exponential, $\kappa_3=2$ and the limit is $\frac43\varphi(1)=0.3226276$. For a skewed lattice law, both the sawtooth and the skewness term are of order $n^{-1/2}$. They combine inside the absolute value, and periodic averaging gives $$\sqrt n\,W_1\to\int\varphi(x)\,\E_U\Big\lvert h\psi(U)-\frac{\kappa_3}6(x^2-1)\Big\rvert\,\dd x,\qquad U\sim U[0,1].$$ For a standardised Bernoulli($0.3$), $h=2.1822$ and $\kappa_3=0.8729$, and the limit is 0.564705. It is neither the sum nor the larger of the two separate effects, $h/4=0.5455$ and $\frac{2\abs{\kappa_3}}3\varphi(1)=0.1408$.

Exercise 1 — Couplings in basic cases§2 · checked

(a) Let $\al,\be$ be finite positive measures with different total masses. What is $\Couplings(\al,\be)$? (b)(i) What are the couplings between $\delta_x$ and $U[0,1]$? (ii) What are the couplings between $\frac12(\delta_x+\delta_y)$ and $U[0,1]$?

Hint 1
Write the marginal constraints on sets: $\pi(A\times\Y)=\al(A)$ and $\pi(\X\times B)=\be(B)$. What do they give for $A=\X$, and for $A=\X\setminus\{x\}$?
Hint 2
In (b)(ii), cut $\pi$ along the two atoms: $\pi=\frac12\delta_x\otimes\mu_x+\frac12\delta_y\otimes\mu_y$. Which conditions must $\mu_x,\mu_y$ satisfy, and can they have atoms?
Solution

(a) $\pi(\X\times\Y)$ equals both $\al(\X)$ and $\be(\Y)$, so $\Couplings(\al,\be)=\emptyset$ when the masses differ.

(b)(i) $\pi((\R\setminus\{x\})\times[0,1])=\delta_x(\R\setminus\{x\})=0$, so $\pi$ lives on $\{x\}\times[0,1]$, where $\pi(\{x\}\times B)=\pi(\R\times B)=\abs B$. Hence $\Couplings=\{\delta_x\otimes U[0,1]\}$: only the product.

(b)(ii) If $x=y$ this is (i). If $x\ne y$, $\pi$ lives on $\{x,y\}\times[0,1]$. Set $\mu_x(B)=2\pi(\{x\}\times B)$ and $\mu_y(B)=2\pi(\{y\}\times B)$: these are probability measures with $\frac12\mu_x+\frac12\mu_y=U[0,1]$. Hence $\mu_x\le2\,U[0,1]$ has a density $2g$ with $0\le g\le1$ and $\int_0^1g=\frac12$, and $\mu_y$ has density $2(1-g)$. Conversely, every such $g$ gives a coupling, $\pi=\delta_x\otimes(g\,\dd t)+\delta_y\otimes((1-g)\dd t)$. There are infinitely many couplings, from the monotone $g=\mathbf 1_{[0,1/2]}$ (for $x\lt y$) to the product $g\equiv\frac12$. For a convex cost $\abs{u-v}^p$ with $x\lt y$, the monotone one is optimal (Exercise 3). For $x=0$, $y=1$ and $p=2$ it costs $\frac1{12}$, the product $\frac13$.

a linear program between $U[0,1]$ on 2000 midpoints and $\frac12(\delta_0+\delta_1)$ gives 0.083333 (the midpoint rule gives exactly $\frac1{12}-\frac1{12M^2}=0.083333$); the product costs 0.333333.

Exercise 2 — Gaussian couplings in $\R$ (zero means)§2 · checked

$\al=\Gauss(0,\sigma_\al^2)$, $\be=\Gauss(0,\sigma_\be^2)$ with $\sigma_\al,\sigma_\be\ge0$, and Gaussian couplings $\pi=\Gauss(0,\Sigma)$, $\Sigma=\begin{psmallmatrix}\sigma_\al^2&c\\c&\sigma_\be^2\end{psmallmatrix}\succeq0$. (a) Express $\E(X-Y)^2$ in terms of $c$. (b) Which constraint on $c$ does $\Sigma\succeq0$ impose? (c) Solve the problem restricted to Gaussian couplings. (d) What is the rank of $\Sigma$, and the support of $\pi$, in each case? Compare with Brenier’s theorem.

Hint 1
$\E(X-Y)^2=\E X^2+\E Y^2-2\E XY$. A symmetric $2\times2$ matrix with non-negative diagonal is positive semi-definite if and only if its determinant is non-negative.
Hint 2
At $\abs c=\sigma_\al\sigma_\be$ the determinant vanishes: which linear relation between $X$ and $Y$ holds almost surely? Treat separately the cases where $\sigma_\al$ or $\sigma_\be$ is $0$.
Solution

(a) $\E(X-Y)^2=\sigma_\al^2+\sigma_\be^2-2c$. (b) $\det\Sigma=\sigma_\al^2\sigma_\be^2-c^2\ge0$, that is $\abs c\le\sigma_\al\sigma_\be$. (c) The cost decreases in $c$, so $c^\star=\sigma_\al\sigma_\be$ and the value is $(\sigma_\al-\sigma_\be)^2$. This is $W_2^2(\al,\be)$ of Lesson 1, so nothing is lost by restricting to Gaussian couplings.

(d) If $\abs c\lt\sigma_\al\sigma_\be$, $\Sigma$ has rank 2 and $\pi$ has a density on $\R^2$: the support is the whole plane. If $\sigma_\al,\sigma_\be\gt0$ and $c=\sigma_\al\sigma_\be$, $\Sigma$ has rank 1 and $Y=\frac{\sigma_\be}{\sigma_\al}X$ almost surely: the support is the graph of the increasing linear map $T(x)=\frac{\sigma_\be}{\sigma_\al}x=\varphi'(x)$, with $\varphi(x)=\frac{\sigma_\be}{2\sigma_\al}x^2$ convex. This is Brenier’s map, as predicted, since $\al$ has a density. At $c=-\sigma_\al\sigma_\be$ the support is the graph of a decreasing map: also deterministic, but the worst Gaussian coupling, of cost $(\sigma_\al+\sigma_\be)^2$. If $\sigma_\al=0\lt\sigma_\be$, then $c=0$, rank 1, and $\pi=\delta_0\otimes\be$ lives on the vertical axis: no map from $\al=\delta_0$ can split it, consistent with Brenier, whose hypothesis fails. If $\sigma_\be=0$, the support is the horizontal axis, the graph of $T=0$. If both vanish, rank 0 and $\pi=\delta_{(0,0)}$.

for $(\sigma_\al,\sigma_\be)=(2,1),(0.5,1.7),(1.3,1.3),(3,0.2)$, the 1D quantile formula with $2\times10^5$ levels gives $(\sigma_\al-\sigma_\be)^2$ to within 5.2e−5 (the error of the midpoint rule).

Exercise 3 — Uniform → two Diracs; two Diracs → uniform§2 · checked

$x\ne y$ in $\R$, $\al=U[0,1]$, $\be=\frac12\delta_x+\frac12\delta_y$, cost $\abs{u-v}^2$ (the answers are the same for $\abs{u-v}^p$, $p\ge1$). (a) Find the optimal Monge map $T$ pushing $\al$ to $\be$. (b) Is there a Monge map $S$ with $S_\sharp\be=\al$? (c) Describe an optimal Kantorovich coupling.

Hint 1
In 1D, the optimal map for a convex cost is $F_\be^{-1}\circ F_\al$ (Lesson 1 §6). What are $F_\al$ and $F_\be^{-1}$ here?
Hint 2
For (b): how many atoms can $S_\sharp\be$ have?
Solution

Take $x\lt y$. (a) $F_\al(u)=u$ and $F_\be^{-1}(s)=x$ for $s\le\frac12$, $y$ for $s\gt\frac12$, so $T=x$ on $[0,\frac12]$ and $T=y$ on $(\frac12,1]$ (the value at $\frac12$ does not matter). It is optimal for every $\abs{u-v}^p$, $p\ge1$ (OT4ML Thm. 2.41; Lesson 1 §6), and $W_p^p=\int_0^{1/2}\abs{u-x}^p\dd u+\int_{1/2}^1\abs{u-y}^p\dd u$. (b) No: $S_\sharp\be=\frac12\delta_{S(x)}+\frac12\delta_{S(y)}$ has atoms, and $U[0,1]$ has none. (c) $\pi=(\Id,T)_\sharp\al$, that is $\pi(A\times\{x\})=\abs{A\cap[0,\frac12]}$ and $\pi(A\times\{y\})=\abs{A\cap(\frac12,1]}$. It is the quantile coupling, optimal. Its flip is an optimal coupling of $\be$ and $\al$ with the same cost: the Kantorovich problem is symmetric, the Monge problem is not. For $x=0.2$, $y=0.9$, $p=2$: $W_2^2=\frac{0.3^3+0.2^3}3+\frac{0.1^3+0.4^3}3=\frac1{30}$.

the linear program on 2000 midpoints of $[0,1]$ gives 0.033333 against $\frac1{30}=0.033333$.

Exercise 4 — Bottleneck cost: metric property and closed-form value§4 · checked

$m:\X\to(0,\infty)$ measurable, $c(x,y)=0$ if $x=y$ and $m(x)+m(y)$ otherwise. (a) Show that $c$ is a metric. (b) For $\al,\be$ with $\int m\dd\al+\int m\dd\be\lt\infty$, compute $W_c(\al,\be)=\inf_\pi\int c\,\dd\pi$. (c) Give an optimal plan in terms of $\mu=\al\wedge\be$.

Hint 1
For the triangle inequality $c(x,z)\le c(x,y)+c(y,z)$, distinguish $y\in\{x,z\}$ and $y\notin\{x,z\}$. For (b), split a coupling into its diagonal part and the rest, as in the proof of Proposition 4.21.
Hint 2
If the diagonal part of $\pi$ is $(\Id,\Id)_\sharp\zeta$, the off-diagonal part has marginals $\al-\zeta$ and $\be-\zeta$. Its cost is $\int m\dd(\al-\zeta)+\int m\dd(\be-\zeta)$. Which $\zeta$ is best?
Solution

(a) Symmetry is clear, and $c(x,y)=0$ only if $x=y$ because $m\gt0$. For $x\ne z$: if $y=x$ or $y=z$, the right-hand side is $c(x,z)$ itself; otherwise it is $m(x)+2m(y)+m(z)\ge m(x)+m(z)$. If $x=z$ the left side is $0$.

(b) For any coupling, write $\pi=(\Id,\Id)_\sharp\zeta+\pi'$ with $\pi'$ off the diagonal. Then $\zeta\le\al$, $\zeta\le\be$, hence $\zeta\le\mu$, and the marginals of $\pi'$ are $\al-\zeta$ and $\be-\zeta$. So $$\begin{aligned}\int c\,\dd\pi&=\int(m(x)+m(y))\dd\pi'\\&=\int m\dd\al+\int m\dd\be-2\int m\dd\zeta\\&\ge\int m\dd(\al-\mu)+\int m\dd(\be-\mu)=\int m\,\dd\abs{\al-\be},\end{aligned}$$ since $\al-\mu=(\al-\be)_+$ and $\be-\mu=(\al-\be)_-$. (c) If $\al=\be$, take $(\Id,\Id)_\sharp\al$. Otherwise $r=1-\mu(\X)\gt0$ and $\pi^\star=(\Id,\Id)_\sharp\mu+r^{-1}(\al-\mu)\otimes(\be-\mu)$ is a coupling. Since $\al-\mu\perp\be-\mu$, its product part charges no point of the diagonal, so it costs $r^{-1}\big(r\int m\dd(\al-\mu)+r\int m\dd(\be-\mu)\big)=\int m\,\dd\abs{\al-\be}$: equality in (b). With $m\equiv\frac12$ one recovers $\frac12\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}$ (Proposition 4.21).

on the 400 pairs of Lab 4, the linear program with this cost equals $\sum m\abs{a-b}$ (largest deviation below 10⁻¹⁴), and the plan $\pi^\star$ costs the same (below 10⁻¹⁴).

Exercise 5 — Gaussian couplings in $\R^d$ (zero means)optional · §2 · checked

$\al=\Gauss(0,\Sigma_\al)$, $\be=\Gauss(0,\Sigma_\be)$ with $\Sigma_\al,\Sigma_\be\succ0$, couplings with joint covariance $\begin{psmallmatrix}\Sigma_\al&K\\K^\top&\Sigma_\be\end{psmallmatrix}\succeq0$. (a) Express $\E\norm{X-Y}^2$ in terms of $K$. (b) Characterise the admissible $K$. (c) Minimise the cost over admissible $K$. (d) Rank of the joint covariance and support of $\pi$, for strictly admissible $K$ and at the optimum; compare with Brenier’s theorem.

Hint 1
For (b), use the Schur complement with respect to the invertible block $\Sigma_\al$, then substitute $K=\Sigma_\al^{1/2}M\Sigma_\be^{1/2}$.
Hint 2
For (c), $\tr K=\tr(MS)$ with $S=\Sigma_\be^{1/2}\Sigma_\al^{1/2}$. Write $S=U\diag(s)V^\top$ and look for the contraction $M$ that turns $MS$ into a positive semi-definite matrix.
Solution (the sheet’s, corrected)

(a) $\tr\Sigma_\al+\tr\Sigma_\be-2\tr K$. (b) The joint covariance is positive semi-definite if and only if $\Sigma_\be-K^\top\Sigma_\al^{-1}K\succeq0$. With $M=\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}K\Sigma_\be^{-1/2}$ this reads $\Id-M^\top M\succeq0$, that is $\norm M_{\mathrm{op}}\le1$. Equivalently $K\Sigma_\be^{-1}K^\top\preceq\Sigma_\al$.

(c) $\tr K=\tr(MS)\le\sum_is_i$ by von Neumann’s trace inequality. The bound is reached at $M=VU^\top$, since $VU^\top S=V\diag(s)V^\top$; the sheet’s $UV^\top$ is a slip. Hence $$K^\star=\Sigma_\al^{1/2}VU^\top\Sigma_\be^{1/2}=\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\big(\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\Sigma_\al^{-1/2},$$ whose last factor is $\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}$ and not the $\Sigma_\al^{1/2}$ of the sheet’s box (proof of the identity in §2). The value is $\tr\Sigma_\al+\tr\Sigma_\be-2\tr(\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al^{1/2})^{1/2}$, the squared Bures distance. It is attained by $T(x)=Ax$ with $A=\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}(\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al^{1/2})^{1/2}\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}$, for which $\mathrm{Cov}(X,AX)=\Sigma_\al A=K^\star$.

(d) If $\norm M_{\mathrm{op}}\lt1$, the Schur complement is positive definite, the joint covariance has rank $2d$ and $\pi$ has a density on $\R^{2d}$. At the optimum the Schur complement $\Sigma_\be-A\Sigma_\al A$ vanishes: rank $d$, $Y=AX$ almost surely, and $\supp\pi=\{(x,Ax)\}$, a $d$-dimensional subspace. Since $\al$ has a density, Brenier’s theorem predicts an optimal plan induced by the gradient of a convex function; $A$ is symmetric positive definite, the gradient of $\frac12x^\top Ax$. The Gaussian optimum is the global optimum.

Lab 2: the corrected $K^\star$ reaches the optimum of an SDP over all Gaussian couplings (0.69634 and 0.74293 on the two pairs); the boxed formula gives a joint covariance with smallest eigenvalue −0.0014 and −0.821; $M=UV^\top$ costs 1.35263 on the needle pair.

Exercise 6 — A non-Monge optimal plan in $\R^2$optional · §2

$\al$ uniform on $S_0=\{0\}\times[0,1]$, $\be=\frac12U(S_-)+\frac12U(S_+)$ with $S_\pm=\{\pm1\}\times[0,1]$, cost $\norm{x-y}^2$. (a) Show that every coupling costs at least $1$. (b) Check that $\pi^\star(\cdot\mid X=(0,s))=\frac12\delta_{(-1,s)}+\frac12\delta_{(1,s)}$ defines an optimal coupling of cost $1$. (c) Show that every map $T$ with $T_\sharp\al=\be$ costs more than $1$. (d) Describe $\supp\pi^\star$ and explain why this does not contradict Brenier’s theorem.

Hint 1
With $x=(0,s)$ and $y=(\pm1,t)$, $\norm{x-y}^2=1+(t-s)^2$.
Hint 2
For (c), write $T(0,s)=(\xi(s),u(s))$. Cost $1$ forces $u(s)=s$; what does that imply for the distribution of the heights on $S_-$?
Solution

(a) $\norm{x-y}^2=1+(t-s)^2\ge1$ on the supports, so every coupling costs at least $1$. (b) Each half of the mass at height $s$ goes to height $s$ on each side, so the $y$-marginal gives each segment mass $\frac12$ with uniform heights: $\pi^\star\in\Couplings(\al,\be)$. It costs $1$ because $t=s$ almost surely: it is optimal.

(c) A map sends the set $A_-=\{s:\xi(s)=-1\}$ to $S_-$, so $\abs{A_-}=\frac12$, and the heights $u(s)$, $s\in A_-$, must be uniform on $[0,1]$. Its cost is $1+\int_0^1(u(s)-s)^2\dd s$. Cost $1$ would force $u(s)=s$ almost everywhere, and then the heights on $S_-$ would be uniform on $A_-$, a set of measure $\frac12$, not on $[0,1]$. So every map costs more than $1$. The infimum over maps is still $1$: $\al$ is atomless on a compact set, so Proposition 4.15 applies, and Lesson 1’s Lab 5 measured maps on $n$ alternating cells costing $1+\frac1{12n^2}$. The Monge infimum is not attained.

(d) $\supp\pi^\star=\{((0,s),(-1,s))\}\cup\{((0,s),(1,s))\}$: two graphs. Every source point sends mass to two points, so the support is not the graph of a map. Brenier’s theorem needs $\al$ to have a density on $\R^2$, and $\al$ lives on a segment.

§12Cheat sheet

Gluing$R=P\diag(1/b)Q\in\Couplings(a,c)$; in general, $X$ and $Z$ conditionally independent given $Y$; composition of plans.
Wasserstein$W_p=\big(\min_\pi\int d^p\dd\pi\big)^{1/p}$ on $\Pc_p$; a distance (gluing + Minkowski); the root is needed for $p\gt1$.
Simple values$W_p(\delta_x,\delta_y)=d(x,y)$; two atoms: $\abs{\lambda-\tau}^{1/p}d(x,y)$; $W_p\le W_q$ for $p\le q$; $\norm{m_\al-m_\be}\le W_p$; $W_2^2=\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+W_2^2(\text{centred})$.
Gaussian couplingscost $\tr\Sigma_\al+\tr\Sigma_\be-2\tr K$; $K=\Sigma_\al^{1/2}M\Sigma_\be^{1/2}$, $\norm M_{\mathrm{op}}\le1$; optimum $M=VU^\top$, $K^\star=\Sigma_\al A$, $Y=AX$; value $=$ Bures$^2$.
Monge vs Kantorovich$W_p\le\Wt_p$; equal infima if $\al$ is atomless on a compact space (OT4ML Prop. 4.15); attained or not (segment to two segments: not).
Geodesics$\al_t=((1-t)x+ty)_\sharp\pi^\star$; $W_2(\al_s,\al_t)=(t-s)W_2(\al_0,\al_1)$; mixtures: $W_p(m_s,m_t)\le(t-s)^{1/p}W_p$, infinite $W_2$-length, but $W_1$ geodesics.
Weak convergence$\int f\dd\al_k\to\int f\dd\al$ for bounded continuous $f$ = convergence in law; Diracs: $\delta_{x_k}\rightharpoonup\delta_x\iff x_k\to x$.
Total variation$\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}=\abs{\al-\be}(\X)$ (OT4ML) $=2d_{\TV}$ (StatOT); $\min_\pi\pi(x\ne y)=\frac12\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}$; finite spaces: $\frac{d_{\min}}2\TV\le W_1\le\frac{d_{\max}}2\TV$; bounded sets: $W_p^p\le D^pd_{\TV}$.
Metrization$W_p(\al_k,\al)\to0\iff\al_k\rightharpoonup\al$ and $p$-th moments converge; on bounded spaces $W_p\le W_q\le\operatorname{diam}^{(q-p)/q}W_p^{p/q}$.
Escaping mass$(1-\frac1k)\delta_0+\frac1k\delta_{k^\gamma}$: TV $=\frac2k$, $W_1=k^{\gamma-1}$, $W_2=k^{\gamma-1/2}$.
LLN$W_p(\hat\al_n,\al)\to0$ a.s. for $\al\in\Pc_p$; in 1D $\E W_1\le n^{-1/2}\int\sqrt{F(1-F)}$ and $\sqrt n\,\E W_1\to\sqrt{2/\pi}\int\sqrt{F(1-F)}$ ($1.28838$ for $\Gauss(0,1)$); in dimension $d\ge3$, $n^{-1/d}$.
CLT in $W_1$Berry–Esseen $W_1\le C\E\abs X^3/\sqrt n$; symmetric lattice: $\sqrt nW_1\to h/4$; symmetric density: $nW_1\to\frac{\abs{\kappa_4}}{24}\int\abs{H_3}\varphi$; skewed density: $\sqrt nW_1\to\frac{2\abs{\kappa_3}}3\varphi(1)$.

§13Self-test

  1. Why is $\mathcal L_{d^2}=W_2^2$ not a distance? Give a counterexample.
    Answer
    The triangle inequality fails: $W_2^2(\delta_0,\delta_2)=4\gt W_2^2(\delta_0,\delta_1)+W_2^2(\delta_1,\delta_2)=2$. The square root restores it (Minkowski).
  2. In the glued plan $R=P\diag(1/b)Q$, what is the probabilistic meaning of the division by $b_j$?
    Answer
    $P_{i,j}/b_j$ is the conditional probability of $x_i$ given $y_j$; $R$ draws $X$ and $Z$ independently given $Y$.
  3. Why is $W_p$ finite on $\Pc_p(\X)$?
    Answer
    The product coupling costs $\int\!\!\int d(x,y)^p\le2^{p-1}\big(\int d(x,x_0)^p\dd\al+\int d(x_0,y)^p\dd\be\big)\lt\infty$.
  4. Compute $W_p\big(\frac12\delta_0+\frac12\delta_1,\frac14\delta_0+\frac34\delta_1\big)$.
    Answer
    $\abs{\frac12-\frac14}^{1/p}\cdot1=4^{-1/p}$: mass $\frac14$ moves by $1$.
  5. Show that $W_1(\al,\be)\ge\abs{m_\al-m_\be}$ on $\R$.
    Answer
    For every coupling, $\abs{m_\al-m_\be}=\abs{\int(x-y)\dd\pi}\le\int\abs{x-y}\dd\pi$; minimise.
  6. Couplings of $\delta_x$ and a measure $\be$?
    Answer
    Only $\delta_x\otimes\be$.
  7. In the Gaussian problem, why is it enough to maximise $\tr K$, and what is the constraint on $K$?
    Answer
    The cost is $\tr\Sigma_\al+\tr\Sigma_\be-2\tr K$. The constraint is that the block covariance be positive semi-definite, that is $K=\Sigma_\al^{1/2}M\Sigma_\be^{1/2}$ with $\norm M_{\mathrm{op}}\le1$.
  8. How can you see that the sheet’s boxed $K^\ast$ is wrong without any matrix computation?
    Answer
    In 1D it gives $c=\sigma_\al^3\sigma_\be$, which violates $\abs c\le\sigma_\al\sigma_\be$ as soon as $\sigma_\al\gt1$, and contradicts Exercise 2’s $c^\star=\sigma_\al\sigma_\be$.
  9. Why does Proposition 4.15 need an atomless source?
    Answer
    Its proof cuts each cell of the source into pieces of prescribed masses; an atom cannot be cut. With atoms the infima can differ ($1.5$ against $0.5$ in Lesson 2).
  10. In Exercise 6, is the Monge infimum $1$? Is it attained?
    Answer
    Yes, by Proposition 4.15 (atomless source on a compact set); maps on $n$ cells cost $1+\frac1{12n^2}$. Not attained.
  11. Prove that $W_2(\al_s,\al_t)\le(t-s)W_2(\al_0,\al_1)$ along a displacement interpolation.
    Answer
    The plan $(e_s,e_t)_\sharp\pi^\star$ couples $\al_s,\al_t$ and costs $(t-s)^2\int\norm{x-y}^2\dd\pi^\star$.
  12. What is the $W_2$-length of the mixture path between $\delta_0$ and $\delta_1$?
    Answer
    Infinite: $W_2(m_s,m_t)=\sqrt{t-s}$, so $N$ equal steps have length $\sqrt N$.
  13. Is the mixture path a $W_1$ geodesic?
    Answer
    Yes: $W_1(m_s,m_t)\le(t-s)W_1(\al_0,\al_1)$, and the triangle inequality forces equality.
  14. Does $\delta_{1/k}$ converge to $\delta_0$ in total variation? Weakly? In $W_p$?
    Answer
    Not in total variation ($2$ for all $k$); weakly and in $W_p$ ($=1/k$), yes.
  15. State the 0/1-cost identity with OT4ML’s and with StatOT’s conventions.
    Answer
    $\min_\pi\pi(x\ne y)=\frac12\norm{\al-\be}_{\TV}$ (OT4ML) $=d_{\TV}(\al,\be)$ (StatOT).
  16. On a finite space, why do total variation and $W_1$ define the same convergence?
    Answer
    $d_{\min}d_0\le d\le d_{\max}d_0$ gives $\frac{d_{\min}}2\TV\le W_1\le\frac{d_{\max}}2\TV$ with $d_{\min}\gt0$.
  17. Give a sequence that converges in total variation but not in $W_1$.
    Answer
    $(1-\frac1k)\delta_0+\frac1k\delta_k$: TV $=\frac2k\to0$, $W_1=1$.
  18. Give a sequence that converges in $W_1$ but not in $W_2$.
    Answer
    $(1-\frac1k)\delta_0+\frac1k\delta_{\sqrt k}$: $W_1=k^{-1/2}\to0$, $W_2=1$.
  19. Why is the exponent in $W_q\le\operatorname{diam}^{(q-p)/q}W_p^{p/q}$ sharp?
    Answer
    $\al=\delta_0$, $\be=(1-\varepsilon)\delta_0+\varepsilon\delta_1$ on $[0,1]$: $W_1=\varepsilon$, $W_2=\sqrt\varepsilon=W_1^{1/2}$.
  20. Which two laws of large numbers prove Proposition 10.1?
    Answer
    The strong law for a countable convergence-determining family of test functions (weak convergence), and for $d(X,x_0)^p$ (moments); then Proposition 4.25.
  21. Why does $\sqrt n\,\E W_1(\hat\al_n,\al)$ diverge for Student’s $t(2)$?
    Answer
    $\int\sqrt{F(1-F)}=\infty$ because $F(x)\approx\frac1{2x^2}$; the measured growth is $\frac1{\sqrt\pi}\ln n$.
  22. Rademacher steps: limit of $\sqrt n\,W_1(\al_n,\Gauss(0,1))$? Uniform steps on $[-\sqrt3,\sqrt3]$: rate?
    Answer
    $h/4=\frac12$ (lattice of span $2$); for the uniform, order $1/n$ with constant $\frac{1+4e^{-3/2}}{10\sqrt{2\pi}}\approx0.0755$.
  23. What replaces the $n^{-1}$ rate of the uniform for exponential steps, and why?
    Answer
    $n^{-1/2}$, with $\sqrt nW_1\to\frac43\varphi(1)\approx0.3226$: the skewness $\kappa_3=2$ gives an Edgeworth term of order $n^{-1/2}$, which symmetry kills for the uniform.
