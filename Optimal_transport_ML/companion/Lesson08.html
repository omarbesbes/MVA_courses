Wasserstein gradient flows

A gradient flow on measures moves mass, not values: each step trades a decrease of the energy against the Wasserstein cost of moving. The limit is a continuity equation whose velocity is minus the gradient of the first variation. The same equation describes heat and diffusion, Langevin sampling, particles attracted by a target, neurons of a wide network and tokens of a transformer; convexity along Wasserstein geodesics decides how fast, and whether, it reaches the minimum.

§0Where this lesson sits

Lesson 6 described how a curve of measures moves: a continuity equation with a velocity field, and $W_2$ as the least kinetic energy of such a motion. This lesson asks the converse question. Given an energy $f(\al)$ on probability measures (an entropy, a divergence to a target, the risk of a neural network written in terms of its neurons), how does one descend it? The answer is an implicit Euler scheme in which the step length is measured by $W_2$ (the JKO scheme), whose small-step limit is a continuity equation with velocity $-\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$. The lesson computes these velocities for the energies of machine learning, measures how far the scheme is from its limit, proves the rates that convexity along geodesics gives, and runs the flow on three objects the outline names: neurons of a two-layer network, tokens of a transformer, and populations of cells.

Watch out: factors of 2 that change the clock, not the path

Three conventions coexist and each rescales time. (1) The interaction energy $\iint k\,\dd\al\,\dd\al$ has first variation $2\int k(\cdot,y)\,\dd\al(y)$; with the factor $\frac12$ of StatOT the 2 disappears. (2) The step $\argmin W_2^2+\tau F$ (CompOT (9.26)–(9.27)) is the JKO step of size $\tau/2$, so its iterates follow the limit PDE at time $t=\tau\ell/2$, not $\tau\ell$ (§9). (3) For $n$ particles of mass $\frac1n$, $\nabla_Wf(\al)(x_i)=n\nabla_{x_i}F(X)$ with $F(X)=f(\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i})$: plain gradient descent on the positions, as in notebook 7, runs the Wasserstein flow $n$ times slower. The trajectories are the same; only the speed differs.

A question that comes back

What does it mean to run gradient descent on a measure, and when does the descent reach the minimum? A step must move mass, so its length is a transport cost and its direction is a velocity field (§1–§2). The limit is a PDE when the measure has a density and an ODE system when it is a cloud of particles (§2, §4). Convexity along $W_2$ geodesics, not ordinary convexity, gives rates (§3); without it the flow can stall at a stationary measure that is not a minimizer (§6) or linger for very long near one (§7). The labs measure each of these behaviours.

§1The JKO scheme: implicit gradient steps in $W_2$

The problem

Let $f:\Pc_2(\R^d)\to(-\infty,+\infty]$ be an energy on probability measures with finite second moment. Gradient descent needs a step: a rule that takes $\al$ to a nearby measure with a lower energy. Measures do not form a vector space in which one subtracts a gradient: $\al-\tau g$ is a signed measure in general, and its mass can be negative. Mixtures $(1-t)\al+t\be$ stay probability measures, but they move mass vertically (they lower one bump and raise another), while many energies of interest are naturally decreased by moving mass horizontally (spreading a density, pulling particles towards a target). The step should therefore be measured by a transport distance.

Two Euclidean steps

For a smooth $h:\R^d\to\R$, the explicit and implicit Euler steps of $\dot x=-\nabla h(x)$ with step $\tau$ are (OT4ML p. 356 (348); CompOT (9.24)–(9.25), p. 153 (149)) $$x_{k+1}=x_k-\tau\nabla h(x_k),\qquad x_{k+1}\in\argmin_x\ \frac1{2\tau}\norm{x-x_k}^2+h(x).$$ The second one is the proximal point step. When the minimizer is unique and $h$ is differentiable, its optimality condition is $x_{k+1}=x_k-\tau\nabla h(x_{k+1})$: the gradient is taken at the end of the step. For convex $h$ the implicit step decreases $h$ for every $\tau>0$ and the iterates converge; the explicit step needs $\tau$ below $2/L$ when $\nabla h$ is $L$-Lipschitz. The implicit step also makes sense for non-smooth $h$, where no gradient exists.

The JKO step

Definition (JKO minimizing movement)OT4ML Def. 15.1, p. 356 (348); compact Def. 15.1, p. 111; slides 36–38; StatOT (6.14), p. 189 (183); CompOT (9.27), p. 153 (149)

Given $\tau>0$ and $\al^{(0)}\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$, define recursively $$\al^{(k+1)}\in\argmin_{\al\in\Pc_2(\R^d)}\ \frac1{2\tau}W_2^2\big(\al^{(k)},\al\big)+f(\al).$$ The piecewise-constant curve $\al_\tau(t)=\al^{(k)}$ for $t\in[k\tau,(k+1)\tau)$ is the minimizing movement of step $\tau$.

For a single Dirac mass, $W_2^2(\delta_x,\delta_y)=\norm{x-y}^2$, and the JKO step restricted to Diracs is the implicit Euler step of $h(x)=f(\delta_x)$ (slide 37). The scheme is named after Jordan, Kinderlehrer and Otto (1998), who used it to show that the Fokker–Planck equation is the gradient flow of the free energy for $W_2$; StatOT recalls this (p. 189 (183)), OT4ML does not cite the paper. For $f(\al)=\int V\,\dd\al+\int\rho\log\rho$, Jordan, Kinderlehrer and Otto proved that $\al_\tau$ converges as $\tau\to0$ to the solution of $\partial_t\rho=\operatorname{div}(\rho\nabla V)+\Delta\rho$; the general theory of such limits (curves of maximal slope in metric spaces) is due to Ambrosio, Gigli and Savaré (OT4ML Defs. 15.8–15.9, pp. 359–360 (351–352)).

What one JKO step does to each particle

The optimality condition of the Euclidean implicit step has a transport analogue. It uses the first variation of $\frac12W_2^2(\cdot,\be)$, which is a Kantorovich potential (Exercise 6, and Lesson 4 §2).

Proposition (Euler–Lagrange equation of a JKO step (formal, derived here))the argument follows OT4ML p. 359 (351) and sheet Ex. 6 (1)–(2)

Assume that $\al^{(k+1)}=\rho\,\dd x$ minimizes the JKO step, has a positive density, and that $f$ has a differentiable first variation $\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$ at $\al^{(k+1)}$. Let $T$ be the optimal (Brenier) map from $\al^{(k+1)}$ to $\al^{(k)}$. Then $$\frac{T(x)-x}{\tau}=\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}\big(\al^{(k+1)}\big)(x)\qquad\text{for }\al^{(k+1)}\text{-a.e. }x.$$ Read backwards, a particle that ends the step at $x$ started at $T(x)$ and moved by $-\tau\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al^{(k+1)})(x)$: an implicit Euler step of the velocity $v=-\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$, evaluated at the new measure.

Derivation (first variations of both terms)

Let $\varphi$ be a Kantorovich potential from $\al^{(k+1)}$ to $\al^{(k)}$ for the cost $\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$. By the envelope argument of Exercise 6 (1), the first variation of $\al\mapsto\frac12W_2^2(\al,\al^{(k)})$ at $\al^{(k+1)}$ is $\varphi$, up to a constant. For any probability $\be$ with a bounded density, the mixture $(1-\vartheta)\al^{(k+1)}+\vartheta\be$ is admissible, and minimality gives, at first order in $\vartheta\downarrow0$, $$\int\Big(\frac\varphi\tau+\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}\big(\al^{(k+1)}\big)\Big)\dd\big(\be-\al^{(k+1)}\big)\ \ge\ 0.$$ Since $\be$ is arbitrary, $\frac\varphi\tau+\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$ equals its minimum on the support of $\al^{(k+1)}$, which is the whole space because the density is positive: it is constant. Taking gradients, $\nabla\varphi/\tau=-\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$. For the quadratic cost, $\frac12\norm x^2-\varphi$ is convex and its gradient is the Brenier map, $T(x)=x-\nabla\varphi(x)$ (Lesson 4 §4). Substituting $\nabla\varphi=x-T(x)$ gives the claim. $\square$ The argument is formal: it needs the existence of the first variations and of the potential’s gradient; Jordan, Kinderlehrer and Otto made it rigorous for the Fokker–Planck energy.

Letting $\tau\to0$, the displacement per unit time becomes a velocity, and the curve satisfies the continuity equation with $v_t=-\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al_t)$ (§2). The formal derivation through $(\Id+\tau v)_\sharp\al$ in OT4ML (p. 359 (351)) reaches the same velocity by minimizing a first-order expansion of the step over $v$.

On the line: a convex problem in the quantile function

In one dimension, $W_2^2(\al,\be)=\int_0^1\abs{Q_\al(s)-Q_\be(s)}^2\dd s$ with the quantile functions $Q=F^{-1}$ (Lesson 1 §6). For $\al=\rho\,\dd x$, the relation $\rho(Q(s))\,Q'(s)=1$ turns the entropy into a function of $Q$: $$\int\rho\log\rho\,\dd x=\int_0^1\log\rho\big(Q(s)\big)\,\dd s=-\int_0^1\log Q'(s)\,\dd s.$$ The JKO step of the entropy is therefore $\min_Q\int_0^1\frac{(Q-Q_k)^2}{2\tau}-\log Q'$, a strictly convex problem in $Q$ ($-\log$ is convex and $Q'$ is linear in $Q$), with the constraint $Q'>0$ enforced by the logarithm. With $N$ particles $x_1\lt\dots\lt x_N$ of mass $\frac1N$ at the levels $s_j=(j-\frac12)/N$, and the density $\frac1N/(x_{i+1}-x_i)$ on each gap, the step minimizes $$G(x)=\sum_{i=1}^N\frac{(x_i-x_i^{(k)})^2}{2\tau}-\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\log\big(x_{i+1}-x_i\big),$$ whose Hessian is tridiagonal. Newton’s method solves it in a few iterations, for every $\tau$. Its optimality condition, $\frac{x_i-x_i^{(k)}}\tau=\frac1{x_i-x_{i-1}}-\frac1{x_{i+1}-x_i}$, is the implicit Euler step of the particle system $\dot x_i=\frac1{x_i-x_{i-1}}-\frac1{x_{i+1}-x_i}$, a discretization of the heat equation in quantile form, $\partial_tQ=-\partial_s(1/\partial_sQ)$. The explicit Euler step of the same system is stable only if $\tau$ is below about $\frac12\min_i(x_{i+1}-x_i)^2$.

Lab 1JKO steps for the heat equation, against the exact flow

Question: how far is the minimizing movement from the heat flow for a given step, and what does the implicit step buy over the explicit one?

Setting: the initial law of Fig. 15.1 (figure notebook gradflow-jko-entropy-steps), $\rho_0=0.58\,\Gauss(-1.12,0.055)+0.42\,\Gauss(0.88,0.085)$ (variances), whose heat flow $\partial_t\rho=\Delta\rho$ adds $2t$ to each variance. 200 particles at the levels $(j-\frac12)/N$, JKO steps of the entropy as above (Newton with the tridiagonal Hessian and a backtracking line search, stopped when the Newton decrement $g^\top H^{-1}g$ falls below $10^{-22}$), up to $t=0.42$. Errors are $W_2$ distances between the particle cloud and the exact quantiles at the same levels. The $\tau\to0$ limit of the particles is computed by explicit steps of $5\times10^{-6}$.

What we found (measured)

  • The error at $t=0.42$ halves with the step: 7.01×10⁻², 3.99×10⁻², 2.15×10⁻², 1.10×10⁻² and 5.47×10⁻³ for $\tau$ from 0.21 down to 0.013125 (2 to 32 steps). Against the $\tau\to0$ limit of the same particles (pure time error) the ratio between the last two steps is 1.94: the scheme is first order, with an error of about 0.45$\,\tau$ here. For comparison, the flow moves the measure by $W_2(\rho_0,\rho_{0.42})=0.547$.
  • The particle discretization itself costs 1.69×10⁻³ at $N=200$ (2.68×10⁻³ at 100 particles, 1.14×10⁻³ at 400): far below the time error at these steps.
  • Newton needs 8.0 iterations per step at $\tau=0.21$ and 4.4 at $\tau=0.013125$; every step is solved, whatever $\tau$.
  • The explicit Euler step of the same particles keeps them ordered at $\tau=1.2×10⁻⁵$ and loses the order at $\tau=1.5×10⁻⁵$ (here at step 164; the exact step depends on rounding, since the unstable mode grows from $10^{-16}$). The estimate $\frac12\min_i(x_{i+1}-x_i)^2$ gives 1.3×10⁻⁵. Reaching $t=0.42$ explicitly takes 35000 steps; the JKO scheme takes 4 for an error of 4%.
  • Fig. 15.1 shows the exact heat flow at $t=0.045$, $0.11$, $0.22$, $0.42$, not JKO iterates (the caption says “successive implicit-Euler minimizers”). JKO steps with those step lengths would differ from the plotted curves by 1.98×10⁻², 2.90×10⁻², 3.56×10⁻² and 4.11×10⁻² in $W_2$, that is 7.5% of the distance travelled at the last time.

Key idea: an implicit step, with transport as the step length

The JKO step is a proximal step in which the distance is $W_2$. Its optimality condition moves every particle by $-\tau$ times the gradient of the first variation at the new measure, so the scheme is an implicit Euler method for a continuity equation. Like every implicit method it is stable for every step and first-order accurate; each step is a convex problem when the energy is convex along $W_2$ geodesics (§3), and on the line it is convex in the quantile function.

Link: the entropic JKO step is a Sinkhorn iteration

Replacing $W_2^2$ by its entropic version $L^\eps$ (Lesson 7) turns the JKO step for energies of the form $\int\varphi(\rho)$ into a generalized Sinkhorn iteration, in which the second marginal is replaced by a proximal map of the energy (OT4ML §8.6, Prop. 8.26; CompOT §4.6). This is how JKO steps are computed on grids in two and three dimensions; it is beyond the course.

§2First variations, Wasserstein gradients and the limit PDE

The problem

To pass from JKO steps to an equation one needs a derivative of $f$ with respect to the measure. There are two natural ways to perturb a measure. Mixing in another measure, $(1-\vartheta)\al+\vartheta\be$, changes how much mass sits where (a vertical perturbation). Pushing it forward by $\Id+\tau v$ moves the mass (a horizontal perturbation). The first gives the first variation, a function on space; the second gives the Wasserstein gradient, a vector field, and the second is the gradient of the first.

The first variation

Definition (first variation)OT4ML Def. 15.2, p. 356 (348); compact Def. 15.2, p. 111; StatOT Def. 5.9, p. 151 (145); CompOT Rem. 9.10, p. 153 (149); slide 39

Let $f(\al)\lt+\infty$. The Wasserstein gradient

Definition (Wasserstein gradient and gradient flow)OT4ML Def. 15.5 and (15.4), p. 358 (350); compact Def. 15.4, p. 111; StatOT Prop. 5.10 and Def. 5.14, pp. 152–153 (146–147); slides 39–40

If $f$ has a differentiable first variation, its Wasserstein gradient at $\al$ is the vector field $\nabla_Wf(\al)=\nabla_x\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al)$, and the Wasserstein gradient flow of $f$ is the continuity equation $$\partial_t\al_t+\operatorname{div}(\al_tv_t)=0,\qquad v_t=-\nabla_Wf(\al_t).$$

Proposition (the Wasserstein gradient is the Riesz representative of the derivative)OT4ML Prop. 15.7, p. 358 (350); compact Prop. 15.5, p. 111; StatOT Prop. 5.10, p. 152 (146)

Let $\al$ have a smooth positive density and $v$ be a smooth, compactly supported velocity field. Then $$\frac{\dd}{\dd\tau}\Big|_{\tau=0}f\big((\Id+\tau v)_\sharp\al\big)=\int\dotp{\nabla\tfrac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al)(x)}{v(x)}\,\dd\al(x).$$ For the metric $\norm v_{L^2(\al)}^2=\int\norm v^2\dd\al$ on velocities, $\nabla_Wf(\al)$ is the gradient of $f$.

Proof (push-forward expansion and one integration by parts)

For a test function $\psi$, $$\begin{aligned}\int\psi\,\dd(\Id+\tau v)_\sharp\al&=\int\psi(x+\tau v(x))\,\dd\al\\&=\int\psi\,\dd\al+\tau\int\dotp{\nabla\psi}v\,\dd\al+o(\tau),\end{aligned}$$ so $(\Id+\tau v)_\sharp\al=\al-\tau\operatorname{div}(\al v)+o(\tau)$ in the sense of distributions. The perturbation $-\tau\operatorname{div}(\al v)$ has zero mass, and the definition of the first variation gives $$\begin{aligned}f\big((\Id+\tau v)_\sharp\al\big)&=f(\al)-\tau\int\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}\operatorname{div}(\al v)\,\dd x+o(\tau)\\&=f(\al)+\tau\int\dotp{\nabla\tfrac{\delta f}{\delta\al}}{v}\,\dd\al+o(\tau),\end{aligned}$$ the last step by integration by parts (no boundary term, $v$ has compact support). The derivative is the $L^2(\al)$ inner product with $\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$, which is therefore the Riesz representative. $\square$

Two facts make this the right object. First, velocities that move $\al$ the same way differ by a field $w$ with $\operatorname{div}(\al w)=0$, and the gradient $\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$ is the one of least $L^2(\al)$ norm: it is the least-squares velocity of Lesson 6 §1 (slides 43–44; StatOT Def. 5.5, p. 149 (143)). Second, along the flow the energy decreases at the rate of the squared norm of the gradient.

Proposition (energy dissipation)StatOT (5.10), p. 153 (147); OT4ML Prop. 15.26, first identity, p. 374 (366)

Along a smooth Wasserstein gradient flow, $$\frac{\dd}{\dd t}f(\al_t)=-\int\norm{\nabla_Wf(\al_t)}^2\dd\al_t\ \le\ 0.$$

Proof (chain rule)

By the definition of the first variation and the continuity equation, $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}f(\al_t)=\int\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}\,\partial_t\al_t=-\int\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}\operatorname{div}(\al_tv_t)=\int\dotp{\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}}{v_t}\dd\al_t$, and $v_t=-\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$. $\square$

Watch out: the same PDE, two different gradient flows

The heat equation is the $W_2$ gradient flow of the entropy $\int\rho\log\rho$. It is also the $L^2(\dd x)$ gradient flow of the Dirichlet energy $\frac12\int\norm{\nabla\rho}^2$, whose first variation is $-\Delta\rho$ (OT4ML Rem. 15.14, p. 361 (353); CompOT p. 154 (150); sheet Ex. 2). A PDE does not determine its energy or its metric: one has to state both. The two readings give different convergence results, and only the transport reading extends to particles. (The sheet asks for “$\partial_t\rho_t=\delta f(\al_t)$”: the descent is $-\delta f$, see §9.)

Fokker–Planck and Langevin: three representations of one flow

For $\be=Z^{-1}e^{-V}\dd x$, the gradient flow of $\KL(\cdot\mid\be)$ is the Fokker–Planck equation $\partial_t\rho=\operatorname{div}(\rho\nabla V)+\Delta\rho$, because $\operatorname{div}(\rho\nabla\log\rho)=\Delta\rho$. The same curve of measures is the law of the Langevin diffusion.

Theorem (Langevin dynamics follow the Wasserstein gradient flow of KL)StatOT Thm. 6.11 and Cors. 6.12–6.14, p. 186 (180); OT4ML Ex. 15.15 and (15.10), pp. 363–364 (355–356); slides 47–51

Let $\nabla V$ be Lipschitz and $X_t$ solve $\dd X_t=-\nabla V(X_t)\,\dd t+\sqrt2\,\dd B_t$. Then the law $\mu_t$ of $X_t$ solves $\partial_t\mu_t=\Delta\mu_t+\operatorname{div}(\mu_t\nabla V)$: it is the Wasserstein gradient flow of $\KL(\cdot\mid\be)$. For $V=0$, the law of $\sqrt2B_t$ is the heat flow, the gradient flow of the entropy. If $\nabla^2V\succeq\lambda\Id$ with $\lambda\gt0$, then $\KL(\mu_t\mid\be)\le e^{-2\lambda t}\KL(\mu_0\mid\be)$ (proved in §3).

Proof sketch (Itô's formula and duality)

For a smooth test function $\psi$, Itô's formula gives $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\E\psi(X_t)=\E\,\mathcal L\psi(X_t)$ with the generator $\mathcal L\psi=\Delta\psi-\dotp{\nabla V}{\nabla\psi}$ (StatOT derives it from a second-order Taylor expansion, (6.10), p. 185 (179)). In terms of the law, $\int\psi\,\partial_t\mu_t=\int(\Delta\psi-\dotp{\nabla V}{\nabla\psi})\dd\mu_t=\int\psi\,\big(\Delta\mu_t+\operatorname{div}(\mu_t\nabla V)\big)$ after two integrations by parts. $\square$

So one flow has three representations (OT4ML (15.10) and Fig. 15.7; slide 51): (1) independent noisy particles, the Langevin diffusion, simulated by the Euler–Maruyama scheme $X_{k+1}=X_k-h\nabla V(X_k)+\sqrt{2h}\,\xi_k$ (slide 50); (2) the Fokker–Planck PDE on a grid; (3) deterministic particles moved by the velocity $-\nabla V-\nabla\log\rho_t=-\nabla\log(\rho_t/\rho_\be)$. The third one needs the score $\nabla\log\rho_t$ of the current law, which an empirical measure does not have: it must be estimated, for instance from a kernel density estimate (KDE) of the particles (StatOT §6.1.4, pp. 181–183 (175–177), which calls this fix biased). The three representations agree as curves of measures, not as particles: Langevin particles cross each other, the deterministic particles of a one-dimensional flow keep their order.

A Euler–Maruyama step of size $h$ for the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process ($V=\frac12x^2$) is $X\leftarrow(1-h)X+\sqrt{2h}\,\xi$. A Gaussian law stays Gaussian, with variance $v\leftarrow(1-h)^2v+2h$, whose fixed point is $\frac{2h}{1-(1-h)^2}=\frac1{1-h/2}$: the chain is biased by about $\frac h2$ in variance, for every number of particles. This is the asymptotic bias that StatOT mentions for the discretized Langevin algorithm (§6.2.2, p. 188 (182)).

Lab 2One flow, three particle systems

Question: the Langevin diffusion, the deterministic score flow and the kernel-density score flow all represent the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck flow; what error does each carry?

Setting: the flow of $\KL(\cdot\mid\Gauss(0,1))$, $\partial_t\rho=\partial_x(x\rho)+\partial_x^2\rho$, from the mixture of Fig. 15.11 (figure notebook gradflow-hwi-entropy-decay), $0.60\,\Gauss(-1.62,0.46^2)+0.40\,\Gauss(1.08,0.64^2)$, whose exact law at time $t$ is again a mixture ($X_t=e^{-t}X_0+(1-e^{-2t})^{1/2}Z$). 400 particles start at the quantile levels $(i-\frac12)/n$ of $\rho_0$ and run to $t=3$. Langevin: Euler–Maruyama with step $h$ and the page’s seeded generator. Exact score: RK4 with step 0.01 on $\dot x=-x-\partial_x\log\rho_t(x)$. KDE score: explicit steps of 0.02 on $\dot x=-x-\partial_x\log\hat\rho(x)$, $\hat\rho$ the Gaussian kernel estimate of the particles with bandwidth $b$. $W_2$ is computed between the particles and the exact law by quadrature on quantiles.

What we found (measured)

  • The exact law at $t=3$ has variance 1.0026 (from 2.040 at $t=0$). The Euler–Maruyama chain, as a law, has variance 1.1123, 1.0544 and 1.0278 for $h=0.20$, 0.10, 0.05: already its fixed point $1/(1-h/2)$ (1.1111, 1.0526, 1.0256). Its $W_2$ bias, 5.39×10⁻², 2.59×10⁻², 1.27×10⁻², halves with $h$.
  • With 400 particles that bias is hidden by sampling: the particles are 0.113, 0.066 and 0.079 away from the exact law, and their variance is 1.153, 1.091, 1.101. With 4000 particles at $h=0.05$ the distance falls to 0.027.
  • The deterministic particles with the exact score stay on the exact quantiles (largest gap 1.3×10⁻⁷); their distance 0.0218 to the law is the quantization error of 400 atoms, and their variance is 0.9993.
  • With a kernel-density score the particles settle near $\Gauss(0,1-b^2)$, too narrow: variance 0.904 against $1-b^2=0.91$ for $b=0.3$, 0.752 against 0.75 for $b=0.5$. A stationary law must satisfy $\nabla\log(\rho*\Gauss(0,b^2))=-x$, which forces $\rho*\Gauss(0,b^2)=\Gauss(0,1)$. For $b=0.1$ the variance is 0.959 instead of 0.99: each particle’s own kernel carries on average a fraction 0.056 (one in 18) of the weight in its density estimate, which weakens the estimated score.

Key idea: one curve of measures, several particle systems

The Fokker–Planck PDE, the Langevin SDE and the deterministic score flow describe the same curve of measures. Noisy particles pay a Monte Carlo error of order $n^{-1/2}$ and, once discretized, a bias of order $h$. Deterministic particles have neither, but need the score of their own law, which a cloud of particles does not have; estimating it with a kernel of width $b$ shrinks the stationary law by $b^2$. Energies that are well defined on clouds (potentials, interactions, MMD, transport costs) avoid the issue: their particle flows are exact (§4).

Beyond the course: McKean–Vlasov limits and sampling algorithms

When the drift depends on the law of all particles, $\dd X^i=b(X^i,\al^n_t)\dd t+\sqrt2\sigma\dd B^i$, propagation of chaos gives, as $n\to\infty$, the McKean–Vlasov equation, which is the gradient flow of $\mathcal E(\rho)+\sigma^2\int\rho\log\rho$ when $b=-\nabla\frac{\delta\mathcal E}{\delta\rho}$ (OT4ML p. 364 (356); StatOT §6.3.1, pp. 194–195 (188–189)). Stein variational gradient descent replaces the estimated score by a kernel-smoothed velocity that is exact for particles (StatOT §6.1.4; OT4ML §16.2). Sampling algorithms built on the proximal view of §1 (proximal sampler, underdamped Langevin) are surveyed in StatOT §6.2.3.

§3Convexity along geodesics, and how fast the flow converges

The problem

Gradient descent in $\R^d$ converges to the minimizer at a rate when the function is convex, and exponentially when it is strongly convex or satisfies a Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality. For flows on measures, ordinary convexity (along mixtures $(1-t)\al_0+t\al_1$) is the wrong notion: the flow does not travel along mixtures but along transport paths. The right notion is convexity along $W_2$ geodesics, the McCann interpolations of Lesson 1 §9. This section states which energies have it, which do not, and what rates follow.

Geodesic convexity

Definition (geodesic convexity)OT4ML Def. 15.16, p. 367 (359); compact Def. 15.9, p. 113; StatOT §5.4, p. 154 (148)

A constant-speed $W_2$ geodesic between $\al_0$ and $\al_1$ is $\al_t=((1-t)P_0+tP_1)_\sharp\pi^\star$, with $\pi^\star$ an optimal coupling and $P_0(x,y)=x$, $P_1(x,y)=y$; it is $((1-t)\Id+tT)_\sharp\al_0$ when an optimal map $T$ exists. A functional $f$ is $\lambda$-geodesically convex ($\lambda\in\R$) if along every such geodesic $$f(\al_t)\le(1-t)f(\al_0)+tf(\al_1)-\frac\lambda2t(1-t)W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1).$$ It is geodesically convex for $\lambda=0$.

Proposition (potential and interaction energies)OT4ML Prop. 15.17, pp. 367–368 (359–360); compact Prop. 15.10, p. 113

If $h$ is $\lambda$-convex on $\R^d$, then $\al\mapsto\int h\,\dd\al$ is $\lambda$-geodesically convex; conversely, convexity along geodesics between Diracs forces $h$ to be convex. If $W$ is $\lambda$-convex on $\R^d\times\R^d$, then $\al\mapsto\frac12\iint W\,\dd\al\,\dd\al$ is $\lambda$-geodesically convex.

Proof (convexity along each transport ray, integrated)

Let $(X_0,X_1)\sim\pi^\star$ and $X_t=(1-t)X_0+tX_1\sim\al_t$. Then $h(X_t)\le(1-t)h(X_0)+th(X_1)-\frac\lambda2t(1-t)\norm{X_0-X_1}^2$; taking expectations and using $\E\norm{X_0-X_1}^2=W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1)$ gives the first claim. The geodesic between $\delta_x$ and $\delta_y$ is $\delta_{(1-t)x+ty}$, which gives the converse. For the interaction energy take two independent copies $(X_0,X_1)$, $(X'_0,X'_1)$ of the optimal coupling: $W(X_t,X'_t)$ is bounded by the convex combination minus $\frac\lambda2t(1-t)(\norm{X_0-X_1}^2+\norm{X'_0-X'_1}^2)$, whose expectation is $\lambda\,t(1-t)W_2^2$; the factor $\frac12$ in front of the energy turns it into $\frac\lambda2t(1-t)W_2^2$. $\square$

Internal energies need another mechanism, because the density along the geodesic is not an average of the end densities: mass is compressed or stretched by the Jacobian of the map.

Theorem (McCann’s displacement convexity)OT4ML Thm. 15.19, pp. 369–370 (361–362); compact Thm. 15.12, p. 114; StatOT Thm. 6.3, p. 175 (169) for the entropy

Let $g:[0,\infty)\to\R$ be convex with $g(0)=0$, and suppose that $r\mapsto\psi(r)=r^dg(r^{-d})$ is convex and non-increasing on $(0,\infty)$. Then $\mathcal U_g(\al)=\int g(\rho)\,\dd x$ (and $+\infty$ if $\al$ has no density) is geodesically convex. This covers the entropy $g=s\log s$, for which $\psi(r)=-d\log r$, and the powers $g=\frac{s^m}{m-1}$ for $m\ge1-\frac1d$.

Proof (the Jacobian along the McCann interpolation)

For smooth positive densities, let $T=\nabla\phi$ be the Brenier map from $\al_0$ to $\al_1$, $T_t=(1-t)\Id+tT$ and $J_t(x)=\det((1-t)\Id+t\nabla T(x))$. The Monge–Ampère equation along the interpolation reads $\rho_t(T_t(x))J_t(x)=\rho_0(x)$ (Lesson 1 §9). Since $\nabla T$ is symmetric positive semidefinite and $\det^{1/d}$ is concave on such matrices, $J_t^{1/d}\ge(1-t)+t\det(\nabla T)^{1/d}$. With $s_t(x)=(J_t(x)/\rho_0(x))^{1/d}$ and $\rho_1(T(x))\det\nabla T(x)=\rho_0(x)$ this reads $s_t\ge(1-t)\rho_0^{-1/d}+t\rho_1(T(x))^{-1/d}$. The change of variables $y=T_t(x)$ gives $\int g(\rho_t(y))\,\dd y=\int\rho_0(x)\psi(s_t(x))\,\dd x$, and since $\psi$ is non-increasing and convex, $$\begin{aligned}\psi(s_t)&\le\psi\big((1-t)\rho_0^{-1/d}+t\rho_1(T)^{-1/d}\big)\\&\le(1-t)\psi(\rho_0^{-1/d})+t\psi(\rho_1(T)^{-1/d}).\end{aligned}$$ Multiplying by $\rho_0$ and integrating gives $\mathcal U_g(\al_t)\le(1-t)\mathcal U_g(\al_0)+t\mathcal U_g(\al_1)$. $\square$

Proposition (relative entropy to a log-concave target)OT4ML Prop. 15.20 (3), p. 370 (362); compact Prop. 15.13, p. 114; StatOT Cor. 6.4, p. 176 (170)

If $\be=Z^{-1}e^{-V}\dd x$ with $\nabla^2V\succeq\lambda\Id$, then $\KL(\cdot\mid\be)$ is $\lambda$-geodesically convex.

Indeed $\KL(\al\mid\be)=\int\rho\log\rho+\int V\dd\al+\log Z$: the entropy is $0$-convex by McCann’s theorem and the potential is $\lambda$-convex by the proposition above. For the standard Gaussian, $\lambda=1$.

What is not geodesically convex

The losses that compare a model to data, the ones machine learning descends, are rarely geodesically convex (OT4ML Rem. 15.24, p. 372 (364)). Three examples.

  • $W_2^2(\cdot,\be)$ itself, in dimension $d\ge2$ (OT4ML Ex. 15.23, p. 372 (364); Exercise 8): a geodesic between two pairs of Diracs passes through a measure farther from $\be$ than both ends. It is instead 2-semiconcave: $$\begin{aligned}W_2^2(\al_t,\be)\ge{}&(1-t)W_2^2(\al_0,\be)+tW_2^2(\al_1,\be)\\&-t(1-t)W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1).\end{aligned}$$
  • $\mathrm{MMD}^2_k(\cdot,\be)$ for a Gaussian or Laplace kernel: these kernels are positive definite but not convex functions of $(x,y)$, and the loss is not geodesically convex in general (OT4ML p. 368 (360)). On the line the energy-distance kernel $k=-\abs{x-y}$ is an exception (OT4ML Prop. 15.18, p. 368 (360)): along quantile geodesics the self-interaction is affine and the cross term convex.
  • The risk of a two-layer network as a function of its neurons (§6): the kernel of (15.29) is not convex in the neuron variable.

Rates

Proposition (convex flows: an O(1/t) rate)OT4ML Prop. 15.26, p. 374 (366); compact Prop. 15.16, p. 115; StatOT Ex. 5.10-4, p. 170 (164)

Let $f$ be geodesically convex with a minimizer $\al^\star$, and $\al_t$ a smooth Wasserstein gradient flow of $f$. Then $f(\al_t)-f(\al^\star)\le\frac{W_2^2(\al_0,\al^\star)}{2t}$.

Proof (the above-tangent inequality and the derivative of the distance)

Let $T_t$ be the optimal map from $\al_t$ to $\al^\star$. Convexity along the geodesic $((1-s)\Id+sT_t)_\sharp\al_t$, differentiated at $s=0$, gives the above-tangent inequality $f(\al^\star)-f(\al_t)\ge\int\dotp{\nabla_Wf(\al_t)}{T_t-\Id}\dd\al_t$. The first variation of $\frac12W_2^2(\cdot,\al^\star)$ is a potential whose gradient is $\Id-T_t$, so $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\frac12W_2^2(\al_t,\al^\star)=\int\dotp{x-T_t(x)}{v_t}\dd\al_t$ with $v_t=-\nabla_Wf(\al_t)$. Together: $$f(\al_t)-f(\al^\star)\le\int\dotp{v_t}{T_t-\Id}\dd\al_t=-\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\frac12W_2^2(\al_t,\al^\star).$$ Integrate from 0 to $t$ and use that $s\mapsto f(\al_s)$ is non-increasing: $t\,(f(\al_t)-f^\star)\le\int_0^t(f(\al_s)-f^\star)\dd s\le\frac12W_2^2(\al_0,\al^\star)$. $\square$

Definition (Wasserstein–Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality)OT4ML Def. 15.27, pp. 374–375 (366–367); compact Def. 15.17, p. 116; StatOT Def. 5.15, p. 154 (148)

With $f^\star=\inf f\gt-\infty$, $f$ satisfies the Wasserstein–PL inequality with constant $\kappa\gt0$ if $\int\norm{\nabla_Wf(\al)}^2\dd\al\ge2\kappa\big(f(\al)-f^\star\big)$ for every $\al$ in its domain.

Theorem (PL gives exponential convergence)OT4ML Thm. 15.28, p. 375 (367); compact Thm. 15.18, p. 116; StatOT Lemma 5.16 and Cor. 5.17, p. 154 (148)

Under the Wasserstein–PL inequality, along a gradient flow satisfying the energy-dissipation identity, $f(\al_t)-f^\star\le e^{-2\kappa t}(f(\al_0)-f^\star)$. If $f$ is lower semicontinuous, $\al_t$ converges in $W_2$ to a minimizer $\al_\infty$ with $W_2(\al_t,\al_\infty)\le\sqrt{2/\kappa}\,(f(\al_0)-f^\star)^{1/2}e^{-\kappa t}$.

Proof of the energy decay (Grönwall)

With $E(t)=f(\al_t)-f^\star$, dissipation and PL give $E'(t)=-\int\norm{\nabla_Wf}^2\dd\al_t\le-2\kappa E(t)$, hence $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}(e^{2\kappa t}E)\le0$ and $E(t)\le e^{-2\kappa t}E(0)$. The distance bound comes from the length of the remaining path: $\int_t^\infty\norm{v_s}_{L^2(\al_s)}\dd s=\int_t^\infty\frac{-E'(s)}{\norm{v_s}}\dd s\le\int_t^\infty\frac{-E'(s)}{\sqrt{2\kappa E(s)}}\dd s=\sqrt{2/\kappa}\,\sqrt{E(t)}$ (OT4ML p. 375 (367)). $\square$

Proposition (strong geodesic convexity implies PL)OT4ML Prop. 15.29, p. 376 (368); compact Prop. 15.19, p. 116; StatOT Lemma A.11 via Cor. 5.17

If $f$ is $\lambda$-geodesically convex with $\lambda\gt0$ and attains its minimum, it satisfies the Wasserstein–PL inequality with $\kappa=\lambda$.

Proof (slope along the geodesic to the minimizer)

Let $g=f(\al)-f^\star$, $r=W_2(\al,\al^\star)$ and $a=\norm{\nabla_Wf(\al)}_{L^2(\al)}$. Along the geodesic from $\al$ to $\al^\star$, $f(\al)-f(\al_s)\ge sg+\frac\lambda2s(1-s)r^2$; dividing by $W_2(\al,\al_s)=sr$ and letting $s\to0$, the slope satisfies $a\ge\frac gr+\frac\lambda2r$. So $g\le ar-\frac\lambda2r^2\le\max_{r\ge0}(ar-\frac\lambda2r^2)=\frac{a^2}{2\lambda}$. $\square$

Entropy, Fisher information and $W_2$: the functional inequalities

For $f=\KL(\cdot\mid\be)$ the squared Wasserstein slope is the relative Fisher information $\mathcal I(\al\mid\be)=\int\norm{\nabla\log\frac{\dd\al}{\dd\be}}^2\dd\al$ (OT4ML Rem. 15.41, p. 381 (373)). The PL inequality for KL is therefore an inequality between entropy and Fisher information: the logarithmic Sobolev inequality. Three classical inequalities relate the entropy $H=\KL(\al\mid\be)$, the distance $W=W_2(\al,\be)$ and the Fisher information $I=\mathcal I(\al\mid\be)$.

Theorem (logarithmic Sobolev, Talagrand and HWI inequalities)OT4ML Def. 15.39, Props. 15.40, 15.42, 15.49 and 15.50, pp. 380–385 (372–377); compact Props. 15.27, 15.28, 15.32, 15.33, pp. 118–119

Let $\be=Z^{-1}e^{-V}\dd x$ with $\nabla^2V\succeq\lambda\Id$, $\lambda\gt0$. Then for every $\al\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$:

  1. (HWI, Otto–Villani) $H\le W\sqrt I-\frac\lambda2W^2$.
  2. (log-Sobolev, Bakry–Émery) $H\le\frac1{2\lambda}I$; along the gradient flow of $\KL(\cdot\mid\be)$, $H(\al_t)\le e^{-2\lambda t}H(\al_0)$.
  3. (Talagrand, for the standard Gaussian, $\lambda=1$) $\frac12W^2\le H$.

For $\be=\Gauss(0,\Id)$ the three are equalities when $\al=\Gauss(a,\Id)$ is a translate.

Proof of HWI and of log-Sobolev from it

Let $T$ be the Brenier map from $\al$ to $\be$ and $\al_t=((1-t)\Id+tT)_\sharp\al$. By the proposition above $e(t)=\KL(\al_t\mid\be)$ is $\lambda W^2$-convex in $t$, so $0=e(1)\ge e(0)+e'(0)+\frac\lambda2W^2$. The velocity of the geodesic at $t=0$ is $T-\Id$, so by Proposition 15.7 $e'(0)=\int\dotp{\nabla\log\frac{\dd\al}{\dd\be}}{T-\Id}\dd\al$, and Cauchy–Schwarz gives $-e'(0)\le\sqrt I\,\norm{T-\Id}_{L^2(\al)}=\sqrt I\,W$. Hence $H\le W\sqrt I-\frac\lambda2W^2$. Maximizing the right-hand side over $W\ge0$ gives $H\le\frac I{2\lambda}$, and Grönwall along $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}H(\al_t)=-I(\al_t)$ gives the decay. $\square$ Talagrand’s inequality for the Gaussian has a short proof by the Monge–Ampère equation of the Brenier map from $\gamma_d$ to $\al$ and $\log\det(\Id+D\xi)\le\operatorname{div}\xi$ (OT4ML Prop. 15.49, p. 385 (377)).

For the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck flow towards $\gamma=\Gauss(0,1)$ there is also a direct coupling bound: if $(X_0,Y_0)$ is an optimal coupling of $\rho_0$ and $\gamma$, then $X_t=e^{-t}X_0+(1-e^{-2t})^{1/2}Z$ and $Y_t=e^{-t}Y_0+(1-e^{-2t})^{1/2}Z$, driven by the same $Z$, have laws $\rho_t$ and $\gamma$, so $W_2(\rho_t,\gamma)\le e^{-t}W_2(\rho_0,\gamma)$ (derived here; it is the contraction that $1$-convexity gives).

Key idea: curvature of the energy along geodesics sets the rate

Convexity along $W_2$ geodesics plays the role that convexity plays for gradient descent: $\lambda$-convexity gives the PL inequality, hence $e^{-2\lambda t}$ decay of the energy and $e^{-\lambda t}$ convergence in $W_2$; plain convexity gives $O(1/t)$. For the relative entropy, PL is the logarithmic Sobolev inequality, and the rate is attained by the slowest mode (for the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck flow, the mean). Losses that compare a model to data are usually not geodesically convex, which is why the analysis of §6 needs other arguments.

Beyond the course: Łojasiewicz exponents, curvature, isoperimetry

On a Riemannian manifold, $\lambda$-convexity of the entropy is equivalent to a lower bound $\lambda$ on the Ricci curvature, which is how Lott, Sturm and Villani define curvature for metric measure spaces (OT4ML Thm. 15.36, p. 379 (371)). The same Jacobian argument as McCann’s proves the Brunn–Minkowski and isoperimetric inequalities, and linearizing log-Sobolev near $\be$ gives the Poincaré inequality (OT4ML §15.3).

§4Particle flows of discrepancies: MMD, energy distance, Sinkhorn

The problem

To fit a cloud of particles $\al=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$ to a target $\be$ (samples of a data distribution), one descends a discrepancy $f(\al)=D(\al,\be)$. The entropy-based energies of §2 are not defined on clouds; discrepancies built from kernels and transport costs are, and their Wasserstein gradient flows are exact particle systems.

MMD flows

For a symmetric kernel $k$ that is positive definite, or conditionally positive definite on zero-mass measures like the energy-distance kernel $k(x,y)=-\norm{x-y}$ (Lesson 4 §8), $$\mathrm{MMD}_k^2(\al,\be)=\iint k\,\dd\al\,\dd\al-2\iint k\,\dd\al\,\dd\be+\iint k\,\dd\be\,\dd\be$$ is an interaction energy plus a linear potential (OT4ML p. 362 (354)). With the table of §2, the Wasserstein gradient flow of the $n$ particles is $$\dot x_i=-\frac2n\sum_j\nabla_xk(x_i,x_j)+2\int\nabla_xk(x_i,y)\,\dd\be(y),$$ a self-interaction plus an attraction by the target’s kernel mean. For $k=-\norm{x-y}$, $\nabla_xk(x,y)=-\frac{x-y}{\norm{x-y}}$: the particles repel each other and are attracted by every target point with a force of unit size, whatever the distance. A Gaussian kernel $e^{-\norm{x-y}^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ gives forces that vanish beyond a few $\sigma$, so a target far away is invisible. In one dimension, $\mathrm{ED}^2=2\int\abs{F_\al-F_\be}^2\dd x$ (slide 52), and the energy distance is geodesically convex (OT4ML Prop. 15.18, p. 368 (360)).

§5Gaussian closures: flows that stay in a finite-dimensional family

The problem

A gradient flow on measures is a PDE, infinite-dimensional. For some energies the family of Gaussians is invariant: a Gaussian start stays Gaussian, and the PDE reduces to an ordinary differential equation for the mean and the covariance. These closed forms are the test cases of the theory (sheet Exercises 3–5) and they show how different energies bend the path towards the same target.

Affine velocities preserve Gaussians

Proposition (affine velocities and Gaussian curves)OT4ML Prop. 16.22, p. 431 (423); compact Prop. 16.13, p. 131; sheet Ex. 3; StatOT Thm. 5.22, p. 158 (152)

Let $\al_0=\Gauss(m_0,\Sigma_0)$ with $\Sigma_0\succ0$, and let $(m_t,\Sigma_t)$ solve $\dot m_t=b_t$, $\dot\Sigma_t=A_t\Sigma_t+\Sigma_tA_t^\top$. Then $\al_t=\Gauss(m_t,\Sigma_t)$ solves the continuity equation with the affine velocity $v_t(x)=b_t+A_t(x-m_t)$. If the Wasserstein gradient of $f$ is affine on Gaussians, $\nabla_Wf(\Gauss(m,\Sigma))(x)=b_f+A_f(x-m)$ with $A_f$ symmetric, the gradient flow of $f$ from a Gaussian stays Gaussian with $\dot m=-b_f$, $\dot\Sigma=-(A_f\Sigma+\Sigma A_f)$.

Proof (a linear ODE moves a Gaussian to a Gaussian)

The characteristics solve $\dot X_t=b_t+A_t(X_t-m_t)$, so $\tilde X_t=X_t-m_t$ solves $\dot{\tilde X}_t=A_t\tilde X_t$ and $\tilde X_t=\Phi_t\tilde X_0$ with the fundamental matrix $\dot\Phi_t=A_t\Phi_t$, $\Phi_0=\Id$. A linear image of a Gaussian is Gaussian, with covariance $\Sigma_t=\Phi_t\Sigma_0\Phi_t^\top$, and $\dot\Sigma_t=A_t\Sigma_t+\Sigma_tA_t^\top$. In discrete time (sheet Ex. 3) the same argument gives $\Sigma_{k+1}=(\Id-\tau A_k)\Sigma_k(\Id-\tau A_k)^\top$, whose first-order term is the ODE. $\square$

§6Training a two-layer network: a gradient flow of its neurons

The problem

A two-layer network with $n$ hidden neurons is trained by gradient descent on its parameters. The loss is not convex in the parameters, and permuting the neurons does not change the network. Writing the network as an average over its neurons removes the permutation and makes the network linear in the empirical measure of the neurons. Training becomes a Wasserstein gradient flow of a convex functional (convex for mixtures, not along geodesics), and the question of global convergence becomes a question about the support of that measure.

Neurons as particles

A neuron is a point $x=(u,v)\in\R\times\R^d$, with output weight $u$ and input weights $v$; its feature is $\psi(x,z)=u\,\sigma(\dotp vz)$ for an activation $\sigma$ (here ReLU, $\sigma(s)=\max(s,0)$). The network of width $n$ and its mean-field version are $$\begin{gathered}G_X(z)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\psi(x_i,z),\\G_\al(z)=\int\psi(x,z)\,\dd\al(x),\qquad\al=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i},\end{gathered}$$ and the risk for data $(z,y)\sim\zeta$ is $f(\al)=\frac12\E_\zeta\abs{G_\al(z)-y}^2$ (OT4ML §15.4, p. 387 (379); slides 62–63; StatOT (6.23)–(6.26), pp. 201–202 (195–196)). OT4ML writes the outer weight as a vector $v$ and the inner one as $u$; the slides write $a\,\sigma(w^\top z)$; the names here follow the figure notebook. The first variation and the Wasserstein gradient are (OT4ML (15.28), p. 387 (379)) $$\begin{gathered}\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al)(x)=\E_\zeta\big[(G_\al(z)-y)\,\psi(x,z)\big],\\\nabla_Wf(\al)(x)=\E_\zeta\big[(G_\al(z)-y)\,\nabla_x\psi(x,z)\big].\end{gathered}$$ Expanding the square, $f$ is a quadratic interaction plus a potential, $f(\al)=\frac12\iint\kappa\,\dd\al\,\dd\al+\int g\,\dd\al+\frac12\E y^2$ with $\kappa(x,x')=\E\,\psi(x,z)\psi(x',z)$ and $g(x)=-\E\,y\,\psi(x,z)$ ((15.29)–(15.30)). The kernel $\kappa$ is positive semidefinite, so $f$ is convex along mixtures, but $\kappa$ is not a convex function of the neurons, so $f$ is not geodesically convex.

Proposition (gradient descent on the neurons is the Wasserstein flow)StatOT Prop. 6.16, p. 202 (196); OT4ML p. 387 (379)

If $\al_0=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i(0)}$, the Wasserstein gradient flow of $f$ stays empirical, $\al_t=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i(t)}$, and the neurons follow $\dot x_i=-\nabla_Wf(\al_t)(x_i)=-n\nabla_{x_i}F(X)$, the Euclidean gradient flow of $F(X)=f(\al_X)$ with time multiplied by $n$.

The proof is the particle case of (15.5): $\nabla_{x_i}F=\frac1n\nabla_x\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(x_i)$, and a measure moved by a velocity field stays a sum of the moved Diracs. The interest of the measure view is that $\al_0$ may also be a density, the limit of infinitely many neurons, and that statements about $\al$ do not depend on $n$.

When is a stationary measure a global minimizer?

Proposition (stationary positive densities are global minimizers)OT4ML Prop. 15.51, pp. 388–389 (380–381); compact Prop. 15.34, p. 120

Let $f=Q+\int V\,\dd\al$ with $Q$ convex along mixtures, on a connected domain. If a gradient flow converges to $\al_\infty=\rho_\infty\dd x$ with $\rho_\infty\gt0$ almost everywhere, $\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al_\infty)$ is $C^1$, the energy stays bounded below and the squared slope is lower semicontinuous along the convergence, then $\al_\infty$ is a global minimizer.

Proof (zero slope on a full support, then convexity along mixtures)

Integrating the dissipation identity gives $\int_0^\infty\int\norm{\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al_t)}^2\dd\al_t\,\dd t\lt\infty$, so the slope tends to 0 along a sequence of times and, by lower semicontinuity, $\int\norm{\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al_\infty)}^2\dd\al_\infty=0$. With $\rho_\infty\gt0$ everywhere and a continuous gradient, $\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al_\infty)=0$ on the connected domain, so the first variation is a constant $c$. For any $\be$, convexity along mixtures gives $f(\be)\ge f(\al_\infty)+\int\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al_\infty)\,\dd(\be-\al_\infty)=f(\al_\infty)+c-c$. $\square$

The positivity assumption is essential: on its support a stationary measure only learns that the first variation is locally constant there. For ReLU features, which are 2-homogeneous in the neuron ($\psi(\lambda x,z)=\lambda^2\psi(x,z)$ for $\lambda\gt0$), Chizat and Bach replaced positivity by a condition on directions.

Proposition (2-homogeneous features: full directional support suffices (formal))OT4ML Prop. 15.52, pp. 389–390 (381–382); compact Prop. 15.35, p. 120; slide 66

Let $\psi$ be 2-homogeneous in $x$ and $f(\al)=J(G_\al)$ with $J$ convex and differentiable. Let $\al$ be stationary ($\nabla_x\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al)=0$ on $\supp\al$) and assume that for every unit direction $\omega$ the support of $\al$ meets the ray $\{\lambda\omega:\lambda\gt0\}$. Then $\al$ is a global minimizer of $f$.

Proof (a negative direction would show up as a radial slope)

Let $h=\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al)=DJ(G_\al)[\psi(\cdot,\cdot)]$; it is 2-homogeneous, $h(\lambda x)=\lambda^2h(x)$, so $h(0)=0$. At a support point $x=r\omega$, stationarity gives $0=\dotp{\nabla h(r\omega)}\omega=\frac{\dd}{\dd s}h(s\omega)|_{s=r}=2r\,h(\omega)$, so $h(\omega)=0$ and $h=0$ on $\supp\al$; in particular $\int h\,\dd\al=0$. If some $\be$ had $f(\be)\lt f(\al)$, convexity of $J$ would give $\int h\,\dd(\be-\al)\le f(\be)-f(\al)\lt0$, so $h(x)\lt0$ for some $x=r\omega\ne0$, hence $h(\omega)\lt0$. The support meets the ray of $\omega$ at some $r_\omega\omega$, where $\dotp{\nabla h(r_\omega\omega)}\omega=2r_\omega h(\omega)\lt0$, contradicting stationarity. $\square$

The proof gives a certificate. For a stationary $\al$, the 2-homogeneous function $h$ vanishes on the support; $\al$ is a global minimizer exactly when $h\ge0$ everywhere, that is, when $\min_{\norm\omega=1}h(\omega)\ge0$. A negative minimum names a direction in which new neurons would lower the risk. The rigorous Chizat–Bach theorem replaces the support assumption on the limit by an assumption on the initialization and its propagation (OT4ML p. 390 (382); StatOT p. 203 (197)).

With the linear activation $\sigma(s)=s$ the feature is $u\dotp vz$, the first variation is quadratic in $x$, and a Gaussian law of neurons stays Gaussian: Exercise 5 and OT4ML Ex. 16.24 (p. 435 (427)) reduce the training to an ODE for the cross-covariance $\E[uv]$, as in §5.

Key idea: convex in the measure, not along geodesics

The risk of a two-layer network is a convex function of the measure of its neurons, but its Wasserstein gradient flow only moves the mass that is already there. A stationary measure is optimal when its support sees every direction (Proposition 15.52); otherwise the first variation can stay negative in a direction that no neuron explores, and the flow stops. The function $h=\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$ certifies optimality: its minimum over the sphere is 0 at a global minimizer and negative at the stall, in the direction of the missing feature.

Beyond the course: noise, other geometries, depth

Adding entropy to the risk gives the mean-field Langevin dynamics, whose noise keeps a positive density and makes Proposition 15.51 applicable (StatOT p. 203 (197); OT4ML p. 389 (381)). Changing the metric changes the training path: Muon-type normalized updates are gradient flows for spectral transport metrics (OT4ML §15.5, pp. 390–394 (382–386); slides 68–69). Deep residual networks have a mean-field limit as a conditional Wasserstein flow over layers (OT4ML §15.8).

§7Tokens in a transformer: particles moved by attention

The problem

A transformer maps a set of tokens $x_1,\dots,x_n\in\R^d$ to a set of tokens, layer after layer. Each attention layer replaces every token by a weighted average of the others. Seen across depth, the tokens are particles and the layers are time steps; the question is what the dynamics does to the cloud of tokens, and whether it is a gradient flow.

Residual attention is an Euler step of a continuity equation

With query, key and value matrices $\theta=(Q,K,V)$, a residual attention block with step $\frac1T$ acts as (OT4ML (16.34)–(16.35), p. 425 (417); StatOT (6.27)–(6.28), pp. 204–205 (198–199); slides 75–76) $$\begin{gathered}x_i\ \mapsto\ x_i+\frac1T\sum_j\frac{e^{\dotp{Qx_i}{Kx_j}}}{\sum_\ell e^{\dotp{Qx_i}{Kx_\ell}}}\,Vx_j,\\\text{that is}\quad\al^{(\ell+1)}=\big(\Id+\tfrac1T\Gamma_\theta[\al^{(\ell)}]\big)_\sharp\al^{(\ell)},\end{gathered}$$ with $\Gamma_\theta[\al](x)=V\int y\,e^{\dotp{Qx}{Ky}}\dd\al(y)/\int e^{\dotp{Qx}{Ky}}\dd\al(y)$. As the depth grows with a fixed total time, the token measure follows $\partial_t\al_t+\operatorname{div}(\al_t\Gamma_{\theta_t}[\al_t])=0$. The velocity depends on the whole cloud, as in the mean-field flows of §4 and §6.

It is not a Wasserstein gradient flow in general. For $V=Q^\top K$, $\Gamma_\theta[\al]=\nabla_xU_\al$ with $U_\al(x)=\log\int e^{\dotp{Qx}{Ky}}\dd\al(y)$, a gradient in $x$; but the natural energy $\int U_\al\,\dd\al$ has a first variation with an extra term, and the attention field misses it (OT4ML p. 430 (422); StatOT p. 205 (199), after [SABP22]). Without the softmax normalization, and with tokens on the sphere (layer normalization), the dynamics is the gradient flow of an interaction energy $-\iint e^{\dotp xy}$, whose minimizers are Diracs: all tokens collapse to one point (StatOT p. 206 (200); two factor-2 and wording slips there, §9).

$L^2$ attention is mean shift

Replace the score $\dotp{Qx}{Ky}$ by $-\norm{x-y}^2/(2\eps)$ and take $V=\Id$. The attention output is the local barycenter $m_\eps[\al](x)=\int y\,K_\eps(x,y)\dd\al(y)/\int K_\eps(x,y)\dd\al(y)$ with $K_\eps=e^{-\norm{x-y}^2/(2\eps)}$, and the residual displacement is the mean-shift vector (OT4ML (16.36), p. 425 (417); slide 77, with $\eps$ in place of $2\eps$) $$\begin{gathered}M_\eps[\al](x)=m_\eps[\al](x)-x=\eps\,\nabla\log\rho_\eps[\al](x),\\\rho_\eps[\al]=\int K_\eps(\cdot,y)\,\dd\al(y).\end{gathered}$$ Each token climbs the log of the smoothed token density. When all tokens move and the density is recomputed, this is blurring mean shift, $X\leftarrow X+\tau(P_X-\Id)X$ with the row-stochastic matrix $P_X=\diag(K_Xa)^{-1}K_X\diag(a)$ ((16.37)–(16.39), p. 426 (418)): a Markov averaging whose matrix changes with the cloud.

Theorem (consensus for positive mean shift (Dobrushin contraction))OT4ML Prop. 16.19 and Thm. 16.20, pp. 426–428 (418–420), (16.56)–(16.57), p. 429 (421); dropped by the compact notes

For a row-stochastic matrix $P$, the Dobrushin coefficient $\delta(P)=1-\min_{i,\ell}\sum_j\min(P_{ij},P_{\ell j})$ is the exact contraction factor of $P$ on vectors modulo constants, and $\delta(P)\le\lambda(P)$, the Birkhoff coefficient of Lesson 7 §5. For a positive kernel and a compactly supported start, the diameter $D(t)$ of the support of the mean-shift flow satisfies $$D(t)\le D(s)\exp\Big(-\int_s^t\big(1-\delta(P_{\al_r})\big)\dd r\Big),$$ and the tokens converge to a single point. For the Gaussian kernel $K_\eps$, $\delta\le\lambda=\tanh\big(\frac{D^2}{4\eps}\big)$ on a set of diameter $D$.

Proof idea (widths along every direction contract)

For two rows $p,p'$ of $P$, remove their common mass $\sum_j\min(p_j,p'_j)$: the two remaining measures have mass at most $\delta(P)$, and the averages of a vector $z$ under $p$ and $p'$ differ by at most that mass times $\max z-\min z$. Hence the width $\max_iz_i-\min_iz_i$ of $Pz$ is at most $\delta(P)$ times that of $z$. Apply this to the coordinates $z=X\theta$ of the tokens in every direction $\theta$: the width of the cloud in direction $\theta$ satisfies $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}w_\theta\le-(1-\delta)w_\theta$, and the diameter is the largest width. For the Gaussian kernel the cross-ratio $\frac{K(x,y)K(x',y')}{K(x',y)K(x,y')}=e^{\dotp{x-x'}{y-y'}/\eps}$ is at most $e^{D^2/\eps}$, which gives $\lambda=\tanh(D^2/4\eps)$ through Birkhoff’s formula. $\square$

The Birkhoff bound is useless while the cloud is wide: $1-\tanh(D^2/4\eps)\approx2e^{-D^2/(2\eps)}$. Consensus is then guaranteed but can take extremely long, and in between the tokens form clusters, which is the clustering of tokens observed in deep transformers (OT4ML Rem. 16.21 and Fig. 16.10, pp. 429–430 (421–422); slides 78–80). The same mechanism slows Sinkhorn at small temperature: a kernel with a large projective diameter contracts slowly (OT4ML Rem. 16.21).

A Gaussian cloud stays Gaussian: tilting $\Gauss(m,\Sigma)$ by $K_\eps(x,\cdot)$ gives $M_\eps[\Gauss(m,\Sigma)](x)=-\eps(\Sigma+\eps\Id)^{-1}(x-m)$, an affine field, so $\dot\Sigma=-2\eps\Sigma(\Sigma+\eps\Id)^{-1}$ and, on the line, $\dot v=-\frac{2\eps v}{v+\eps}$ (Exercise 9; OT4ML Exs. 16.27–16.28, p. 438 (430), for drifting and for dot-product attention).

Key idea: attention averages, and averaging contracts

Each attention layer applies a row-stochastic matrix to the tokens; positivity of the kernel makes it a strict contraction of the cloud’s width, with the Dobrushin coefficient as exact factor. The guaranteed rate is exponentially small when the cloud is wide compared with the bandwidth, so the dynamics first forms clusters and only much later merges them. This is the same projective mechanism that makes Sinkhorn slow at small temperature.

Beyond the course: what attention dynamics can compute

Composing residual attention blocks generates rich families of permutation-equivariant maps; universality results for transformers rest on this (slides 81–82). Doubly stochastic attention (Sinkformers) replaces the softmax by a Sinkhorn normalization and gives Wasserstein-type gradient flows (OT4ML p. 430 (422)). Training, which optimizes the parameters $\theta_t$ of the flow, is the open part of this picture.

§8Populations of cells: transport between snapshots

The problem

Single-cell RNA sequencing measures the expression of thousands of genes in each cell, and destroys the cell. A time course is therefore a sequence of populations, one per collection day, never the same cells twice: one observes empirical measures $\al_{t_1},\al_{t_2},\dots$, not trajectories. Developmental biology asks where the cells of day $t_k$ go, which ancestors produce which fates, and when the fates separate. Transport answers with couplings between consecutive snapshots, and gradient flows answer with a dynamical model whose velocity is fitted to the snapshots.

Couplings between snapshots

OT4ML Example 3.24 (pp. 61–62 (53–54)) writes each day as an empirical measure $\al_k=\sum_ia_i^{[k]}\delta_{z_i}$ over its cells and couples consecutive days by a transport plan $P^{[k]}$ with marginals $a^{[k]}$ and $a^{[k+1]}$. An entry $P^{[k]}_{ij}$ is a soft ancestor–descendant mass, not evidence that the same cell was observed twice; normalizing its rows gives transition probabilities, and multiplying them across days gives lineages. Waddington-OT (Schiebinger et al., Cell 2019) computes its plans in a space of gene expression and relaxes the marginal constraints, with growth estimates, by unbalanced transport, because cells divide and die (OT4ML Ex. 11.4; Lesson 7 §10). Fig. 3.7 shows three snapshots of their reprogramming time course in the force-layout embedding (FLE) that their tutorial uses for display; “this visualization need not coincide with the ground geometry used to compute $P^{[k]}$” (p. 62 (54)).

A gradient-flow model goes one step further (OT4ML Ex. 15.10, p. 360 (352); slides 70–73): it posits $\partial_t\al_t+\operatorname{div}(\al_tv_t)=0$ with $v_t=-\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al_t)$, for instance $f(\al)=\int V\,\dd\al+\sigma^2\int\rho\log\rho$, a drift down a potential landscape $V$ (Waddington’s epigenetic landscape) plus diffusion. Fitting $V$, interaction terms, or a neural velocity field to unpaired snapshots links the picture to the Fokker–Planck equations of §2, to dynamic transport (Lesson 6) and to flow matching.

A standard check of such reconstructions holds out a collection day and predicts it from its neighbours. The displacement interpolation between days $t_{k-1}$ and $t_{k+1}$, at the time fraction of $t_k$, is the transport prediction; the mixture of the two neighbouring populations, which says that cells jump rather than move, is the baseline.

Key idea: snapshots give measures, transport gives motion

When individuals cannot be followed, a time course is a curve of measures, and transport supplies the missing correspondence: couplings between consecutive days, interpolations between them, and, with a gradient-flow model, a velocity field and a landscape. A held-out day tests the reconstruction; the mixture of the neighbours is the baseline to beat.

Pointers

  • Unbalanced flows. The Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao metric adds a growth term to the continuity equation, $\partial_t\rho+\operatorname{div}(\rho v)=\rho r$; its gradient flows let mass appear and disappear (OT4ML §15.7, p. 398 (390); StatOT §5.7; slide 60; Lesson 7 §10 for the static version).

Multi-species flows share one domain (pp. 366–367 (358–359)).
  • Other geometries. Changing the action that measures speed changes the flow: mobility and spectral (Muon-type) flows (§15.5), nonlocal flows that give fractional heat equations and Markov chains (§15.6), and second-order, inertial flows (§15.9), all in OT4ML pp. 390–406 (382–398).
  • One-step generators. Particle flows of MMD, Sinkhorn divergences and drifting fields, used to train generators in one step, are in OT4ML §16.2; Lesson 6 §9 points to them.

(2) For $f(\al)=D_\varphi(\al\mid\nu)$, with smooth positive densities: (a) compute $\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$ and $\nabla_{\mathsf W}f$; (b) write the gradient-flow PDE; (c) specialize to $\varphi(s)=s\log s$.

Hint 1
(1) is Jensen’s inequality for the probability $\nu$. For (2a), perturb $\al$ into $\al+\vartheta\chi$ with $\chi$ of zero mass: $\rho$ becomes $\rho+\vartheta\frac{\dd\chi}{\dd\nu}$.
Hint 2
For (2c), $\varphi'(s)=\log s+1$ and $\log\frac{\dd\al}{\dd\nu}=\log\rho_\al-\log\rho_\nu$; the result is the Fokker–Planck equation of §2 with $V=-\log\rho_\nu$.
Solution

(1) $\int\varphi(\rho)\dd\nu\ge\varphi(\int\rho\,\dd\nu)=\varphi(1)=0$.

(2a) $\frac{\dd}{\dd\vartheta}\big|_0\int\varphi(\rho+\vartheta\frac{\dd\chi}{\dd\nu})\dd\nu=\int\varphi'(\rho)\frac{\dd\chi}{\dd\nu}\dd\nu=\int\varphi'(\rho)\,\dd\chi$, so $\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}=\varphi'\big(\frac{\dd\al}{\dd\nu}\big)$ and $\nabla_{\mathsf W}f(\al)=\nabla\varphi'\big(\frac{\dd\al}{\dd\nu}\big)=\varphi''\big(\frac{\dd\al}{\dd\nu}\big)\nabla\frac{\dd\al}{\dd\nu}$.

(2b) $\partial_t\al_t=\operatorname{div}\big(\al_t\nabla\varphi'(\frac{\dd\al_t}{\dd\nu})\big)$, a nonlinear diffusion towards $\nu$.

(2c) $\nabla_{\mathsf W}f=\nabla\log\frac{\rho_\al}{\rho_\nu}$ and $\partial_t\rho=\operatorname{div}(\rho\nabla\log\rho)-\operatorname{div}(\rho\nabla\log\rho_\nu)=\Delta\rho+\operatorname{div}(\rho\nabla V)$ with $V=-\log\rho_\nu$: the Fokker–Planck equation, the law of $\dd X=-\nabla V\dd t+\sqrt2\dd B$. first variation against central differences of $D_\varphi$ on a periodic grid of 512 points: relative error 1.2×10⁻¹⁰ for $s\log s$ and 5.6×10⁻¹¹ for $(s-1)^2$.

Exercise 2 — Dirichlet energy and Fisher information§2 · checked

Let $\al_t=\rho_t\,\dd x$ with smooth positive densities. (1) For the Dirichlet energy $\mathcal E(\al)=\int\abs{\nabla\rho}^2\dd x$: (a) compute $\frac{\delta\mathcal E}{\delta\rho}$ and the steepest descent for the $L^2(\dd x)$ metric; (b) compute the $W_2$-gradient of $\mathcal E$ and its gradient-flow PDE. (2) Same questions for the Fisher information $\mathcal I(\al)=\int\abs{\nabla\log\rho}^2\rho\,\dd x$. (The sheet prints the $L^2$ flow as $\partial_t\rho_t=\delta f(\al_t)$ and $\mathcal I$ with $\dd x$ instead of $\rho\,\dd x$; both are corrected here, §9.)

Hint 1
Integrate by parts: $\int\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla\eta=-\int\eta\,\Delta\rho$. For $\mathcal I=\int\abs{\nabla\rho}^2/\rho$, differentiate the numerator and the denominator separately.
Hint 2
The steepest descent for $L^2(\dd x)$ is $\partial_t\rho=-\frac{\delta\mathcal E}{\delta\rho}$; for $W_2$ it is $\partial_t\rho=\operatorname{div}(\rho\nabla\frac{\delta\mathcal E}{\delta\rho})$. For the Fisher information, $2\frac{\Delta\sqrt\rho}{\sqrt\rho}=\Delta\log\rho+\frac12\abs{\nabla\log\rho}^2$.
Solution

(1a) $\frac{\dd}{\dd\vartheta}\int\abs{\nabla(\rho+\vartheta\eta)}^2=2\int\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla\eta=-2\int\eta\Delta\rho$, so $\frac{\delta\mathcal E}{\delta\rho}=-2\Delta\rho$ and the $L^2$ descent is $\partial_t\rho=2\Delta\rho$: the heat equation run twice as fast (with the factor $\frac12$ in $\mathcal E$ it is exactly the heat equation, OT4ML Rem. 15.14).

(1b) $\nabla_{\mathsf W}\mathcal E=-2\nabla\Delta\rho$ and $\partial_t\rho=-2\operatorname{div}(\rho\nabla\Delta\rho)$, a fourth-order equation of thin-film type.

(2a) At $\vartheta=0$, $$\begin{aligned}\frac{\dd}{\dd\vartheta}\int\frac{\abs{\nabla(\rho+\vartheta\eta)}^2}{\rho+\vartheta\eta}&=\int\Big(\frac{2\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla\eta}\rho-\frac{\abs{\nabla\rho}^2}{\rho^2}\eta\Big)\\&=\int\eta\Big(-2\operatorname{div}\frac{\nabla\rho}\rho-\abs{\nabla\log\rho}^2\Big),\end{aligned}$$ so $$\frac{\delta\mathcal I}{\delta\rho}=-2\Delta\log\rho-\abs{\nabla\log\rho}^2=-4\frac{\Delta\sqrt\rho}{\sqrt\rho},$$ and the $L^2$ descent is $\partial_t\rho=2\Delta\log\rho+\abs{\nabla\log\rho}^2$.

(2b) $\partial_t\rho=-4\operatorname{div}\big(\rho\nabla\frac{\Delta\sqrt\rho}{\sqrt\rho}\big)$, the Derrida–Lebowitz–Speer–Spohn (quantum drift–diffusion) equation. For a Gaussian $\Gauss(m,s^2)$ on the line, $\frac{\delta\mathcal I}{\delta\rho}=\frac2{s^2}-\frac{(x-m)^2}{s^4}$ is quadratic, the velocity $2(x-m)/s^4$ is affine, the Gaussian is preserved (§5) and $\dot s=2/s^3$: $s^4$ grows like $8t$. both first variations against central differences on a periodic grid of 512 points: relative errors 3.8×10⁻⁵ (Dirichlet) and 4.2×10⁻⁵ (Fisher), the size of the second-order grid error.

Exercise 3 — Gaussians under linear dynamics§5 · checked

Let $X_{t+1}=X_t-\tau A_tX_t$ with deterministic matrices $A_t$ and $X_0\sim\Gauss(0,\Sigma_0)$. (1) Show that every $X_t$ is Gaussian and give a recursion for $\Sigma_t$. (2) For $\dot X_t=-A_tX_t$, explain why $X_t\sim\Gauss(0,\Sigma_t)$ and find the matrix ODE for $\Sigma_t$ as the limit $\tau\to0$ of (1).

Hint 1
A linear image $MX$ of a centered Gaussian vector is centered Gaussian with covariance $M\Sigma M^\top$.
Hint 2
Expand $(\Id-\tau A)\Sigma(\Id-\tau A)^\top$ to first order in $\tau$, divide by $\tau$.
Solution

(1) $X_{t+1}=(\Id-\tau A_t)X_t$ is a linear image of a Gaussian, so $X_t\sim\Gauss(0,\Sigma_t)$ with $\Sigma_{t+1}=(\Id-\tau A_t)\Sigma_t(\Id-\tau A_t)^\top$.

(2) The ODE is linear: $X_t=\Phi_tX_0$ with $\dot\Phi_t=-A_t\Phi_t$, $\Phi_0=\Id$, so $X_t$ is Gaussian with $\Sigma_t=\Phi_t\Sigma_0\Phi_t^\top$. From (1), $\frac{\Sigma_{t+\tau}-\Sigma_t}\tau=-(A_t\Sigma_t+\Sigma_tA_t^\top)+\tau A_t\Sigma_tA_t^\top$, and as $\tau\to0$ $$\dot\Sigma_t=-\big(A_t\Sigma_t+\Sigma_tA_t^\top\big).$$ This is Proposition 16.22 with $b=0$: the velocity $-A_tx$ is affine. for $A_t=A_0+tA_1$ and $2\times2$ matrices, the recursion differs from SciPy’s solution of the ODE at $t=1$ by 1.59×10⁻³ ($\tau=0.01$) and 7.94×10⁻⁴ ($\tau=0.005$): ratio 2.00, first order.

Exercise 4 — Quadratic kernels§2, §5 · checked

For $f(\al)=\iint k(x,y)\,\dd\al(x)\dd\al(y)$, with mean $m(\al)$ and covariance $\Sigma(\al)$: (1) for $k(x,y)=\dotp{Ax}y$: (a) express $f$ with $m(\al)$; (b) compute $\nabla_{\mathsf W}f$; (c) write the PDE and solve it from any $\al_0$. (2) For $k(x,y)=\dotp{Ax}y\dotp{Bx}y$: (a) compute $\nabla_{\mathsf W}f$; (b) show that $\al_0=\Gauss(0,\Sigma_0)$ gives $\al_t=\Gauss(0,\Sigma_t)$, and find the ODE of $\Sigma_t$.

Hint 1
For a non-symmetric $k$, $\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(x)=\int\big(k(x,y)+k(y,x)\big)\dd\al(y)$.
Hint 2
$\int\dotp{Ax}y\dotp{Bx}y\,\dd\al(y)=x^\top A^\top SBx$ with the raw second moment $S=\int yy^\top\dd\al(y)$; for centered $\al$, $S=\Sigma$.
Solution

(1a) $f(\al)=\dotp{Am}m$. (1b) $\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(x)=\dotp{Ax}m+\dotp{Am}x=\dotp x{(A+A^\top)m}$, so $\nabla_{\mathsf W}f(\al)=(A+A^\top)m(\al)$, the same vector at every point. (1c) $\partial_t\al_t=\operatorname{div}(\al_t(A+A^\top)m_t)$: all the mass translates with velocity $-(A+A^\top)m_t$, so $\dot m_t=-(A+A^\top)m_t$, $m_t=e^{-(A+A^\top)t}m_0$, and $\al_t=(x\mapsto x+m_t-m_0)_\sharp\al_0$.

(2a) With $S=\int yy^\top\dd\al$, $\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(x)=x^\top(A^\top SB+ASB^\top)x$, so $$\begin{gathered}\nabla_{\mathsf W}f(\al)(x)=L(S)\,x,\\L(S)=A^\top SB+B^\top SA+ASB^\top+BSA^\top.\end{gathered}$$ (2b) The velocity $-L(S_t)x$ is linear, so by Exercise 3 a centered Gaussian stays a centered Gaussian, and with $S_t=\Sigma_t$: $$\dot\Sigma_t=-\big(L(\Sigma_t)\Sigma_t+\Sigma_tL(\Sigma_t)\big),$$ a cubic matrix ODE. The same ODE holds for the raw second moment of any $\al_0$, Gaussian or not. torch autograd: $n\nabla_{x_i}F$ equals $L(S)x_i$ on 50 random points; 400 particles moved by the gradient of the first variation, itself computed by central differences of the sum over particles, follow the ODE to $t=0.5$ within 1.26×10⁻² (step 0.01) and 6.25×10⁻³ (step 0.005), ratio 2.01; the trace of $\Sigma$ shrinks by a factor 0.318.

Exercise 5 — A two-layer linear network (optional, with solution)§6 · checked

Neurons $x=(u,v)\in\R\times\R^d$, network $g_\al(z)=\int u\dotp zv\,\dd\al(u,v)$, loss $f(\al)=\int(g_\al(z)-h(z))^2\dd\rho(z)$. (1) Write $f=\iint k\,\dd\al\,\dd\al+\int b\,\dd\al+c$. (2) Show that the Wasserstein flow from $\Gauss(0,\Sigma_0)$ stays Gaussian.

Hint 1
Expand the square; $\int\dotp zv\dotp z{v'}\dd\rho=v^\top Kv'$ with $K=\int zz^\top\dd\rho$.
Hint 2
The first variation is quadratic in $x=(u,v)$, so its gradient is linear in $x$: Exercise 3 applies.
Solution

(1) $k(x,y)=uu'v^\top Kv'$ and $b(x)=-2u\dotp v{c_h}$ with $c_h=\int z\,h(z)\dd\rho(z)$, $c=\int h^2\dd\rho$ (as in the sheet). (2) With $m=\int uv\,\dd\al\in\R^d$, $\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(x)=2uv^\top Km-2u\dotp v{c_h}=2u\dotp ve$ where $e=Km-c_h$, and $$\nabla_{\mathsf W}f(\al)(u,v)=\big(2\dotp ve,\ 2ue\big),$$ linear in $(u,v)$ (no constant term). The flow $\dot u=-2\dotp ve$, $\dot v=-2ue$ is a linear ODE with coefficients that depend on $\al_t$ only through $m_t$: a centered Gaussian stays centered Gaussian (Exercise 3), as the sheet’s solution says. (The sheet calls $k$ “bilinear in $x$ and $y$”; it has degree two in each, because $x=(u,v)$ enters through the product $uv$.) The stationary points have $e=0$, i.e. $Km=c_h$: the network reaches the least-squares linear predictor. This is OT4ML Ex. 16.24 with the outer and inner weights exchanged. torch autograd confirms the gradient formula on 40 random neurons; 4000 Gaussian neurons trained to $t=3$ with $K=\diag(1,0.5)$ and $h(z)=\dotp wz$, $w=(1.0,−0.5)$, reach $m=(1.000,−0.497)$, and the three marginals keep a kurtosis of 3.00, 2.96, 2.90 (3 for a Gaussian).

Exercise 6 — The flow of $W_2^2$ (optional, with solution)§1, §5 · checked

Fix $\be\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$, $f(\al)=W_2(\al,\be)^2$, and the cost $c(x,y)=\frac12\abs{x-y}^2$. (1) With the Kantorovich dual and the envelope theorem, compute $\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$. (2) With Brenier’s theorem, compute $\nabla_{\mathsf W}f$. (3) Relate the flow to the McCann interpolation between $\al_0$ and $\be$.

Hint 1
With $c=\frac12\abs{x-y}^2$, the dual gives $F=\frac12W_2^2=\sup\{\int\varphi\dd\al+\int\psi\dd\be\}$, and $f=2F$. The derivative of a supremum is the derivative of the objective at the optimizer.
Hint 2
$\varphi_\al=\frac12\abs x^2-u_\al$ with $\nabla u_\al=T_\al$ the Brenier map from $\al$ to $\be$. For (3), look for $\al_t=\mu_{s(t)}$ on the geodesic and compare the velocities.
Solution

(1) $\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}=2\varphi_\al$, up to a constant, with $\varphi_\al$ an optimal potential (the sheet’s argument).

(2) $\nabla\varphi_\al=x-T_\al(x)$, so $\nabla_{\mathsf W}f(\al)=2(x-T_\al(x))$ and the flow has velocity $v_t=2(T_t-\Id)$, with $T_t$ the Brenier map from $\al_t$ to $\be$.

(3) On the geodesic $\mu_s=((1-s)\Id+sT)_\sharp\al_0$, a particle at $X_s=(1-s)x+sT(x)$ has speed $T(x)-x$, and the optimal map from $\mu_s$ to $\be$ sends $X_s$ to $T(x)$, so $T_s(X_s)-X_s=(1-s)(T(x)-x)$: the geodesic velocity at $\mu_s$ is $\frac{T_s-\Id}{1-s}$ (the solution writes $T_s-\Id$, which holds only at $s=0$). The flow’s velocity $2(T_s-\Id)$ is $2(1-s)$ times the geodesic velocity, so $\al_t=\mu_{s(t)}$ with $\dot s=2(1-s)$, $s(t)=1-e^{-2t}$, and $W_2(\al_t,\be)=(1-s(t))W_2(\al_0,\be)=e^{-2t}W_2(\al_0,\be)$: the flow travels the geodesic, slowing down exponentially. on the line, JKO steps of $10^{-3}$ for $W_2^2(\cdot,\be)$ in quantile coordinates (each step is $x\leftarrow\frac{x+2\tau q_\be}{1+2\tau}$), from $\Gauss(0,1)$ to the mixture of Fig. 15.11: $W_2(\al_1,\be)/W_2(\al_0,\be)=0.1356$ against $e^{-2}=0.1353$, and the iterates lie on the McCann interpolation (gap below 10⁻¹²).

Exercise 8 — $W_2^2(\cdot,\be)$ is not geodesically convex (OT4ML Ex. 15.23)§3 · checked

In $\R^2$, let $\be=\frac12\delta_{(-\frac12,-\frac12)}+\frac12\delta_{(\frac12,\frac12)}$, $\al_0=\frac12\delta_{(-1,0)}+\frac12\delta_{(1,0)}$, $\al_1=\frac12\delta_{(0,-1)}+\frac12\delta_{(0,1)}$. (1) Find the optimal couplings between $\al_0$ and $\al_1$ and the midpoints of the corresponding geodesics. (2) Compute $W_2^2(\al_0,\be)$, $W_2^2(\al_1,\be)$ and $W_2^2(\al_{1/2},\be)$ and conclude. (3) Check the 2-semiconcavity inequality of OT4ML Remark 15.24.

Hint
Between two pairs of equal-mass Diracs there are two matchings; compare their costs. All four distances between the supports of $\al_0$ and $\al_1$ equal $\sqrt2$.
Solution

(1) Both matchings cost $2$, so both are optimal, and so is every mixture of them. The matching $(-1,0)\to(0,1)$, $(1,0)\to(0,-1)$ gives the midpoint $\al_{1/2}=\frac12\delta_{(-\frac12,\frac12)}+\frac12\delta_{(\frac12,-\frac12)}$; the other one gives $\be$ itself.

(2) $W_2^2(\al_0,\be)=W_2^2(\al_1,\be)=\frac12$, while $W_2^2(\al_{1/2},\be)=1\gt\frac12\cdot\frac12+\frac12\cdot\frac12$: along the first geodesic the function is not convex (along the second it vanishes at the midpoint). Geodesic convexity asks for every geodesic, so $W_2^2(\cdot,\be)$ is not geodesically convex in $\R^2$; on the line it is, since $W_2^2$ is the squared $L^2$ distance between quantile functions.

(3) $1\ge\frac12\cdot\frac12+\frac12\cdot\frac12-\frac14W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1)=\frac12-\frac12=0$: the semiconcavity bound holds. POT’s exact solver: 0.50, 0.50, 1.00 and 0.00 for the two midpoints, $W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1)=2.00$; the semiconcavity margin is 1.00.

Exercise 9 — Mean shift on a Gaussian cloud§7 · checked

Let $K_\eps(x,y)=e^{-\norm{x-y}^2/(2\eps)}$ and $M_\eps[\al](x)=\frac{\int(y-x)K_\eps(x,y)\dd\al(y)}{\int K_\eps(x,y)\dd\al(y)}$. (1) For $\al=\Gauss(m,\Sigma)$, show $M_\eps[\al](x)=-\eps(\Sigma+\eps\Id)^{-1}(x-m)$. (2) Deduce that the mean-shift flow keeps $\al_t$ Gaussian with fixed mean, and on the line $\dot v=-\frac{2\eps v}{v+\eps}$. (3) Solve this ODE implicitly and describe the two regimes $v\gg\eps$ and $v\ll\eps$.

Hint 1
$K_\eps(x,y)\Gauss(y;m,\Sigma)$ is, as a function of $y$, proportional to a Gaussian density whose precision is $\eps^{-1}\Id+\Sigma^{-1}$.
Hint 2
Separate the variables: $\frac{v+\eps}v\dd v=-2\eps\,\dd t$.
Solution

(1) The tilted law has covariance $C=(\eps^{-1}\Id+\Sigma^{-1})^{-1}=\eps\Sigma(\Sigma+\eps\Id)^{-1}$ and mean $C(\eps^{-1}x+\Sigma^{-1}m)=\Sigma(\Sigma+\eps\Id)^{-1}x+\eps(\Sigma+\eps\Id)^{-1}m$. Subtracting $x$: $M_\eps=-\eps(\Sigma+\eps\Id)^{-1}(x-m)$.

(2) The field is affine with $b=0$ and $A=-\eps(\Sigma+\eps\Id)^{-1}$, symmetric and commuting with $\Sigma$; by Proposition 16.22 the law stays $\Gauss(m,\Sigma_t)$ with $\dot\Sigma=-2\eps\Sigma(\Sigma+\eps\Id)^{-1}$, and on the line $\dot v=-\frac{2\eps v}{v+\eps}$.

Exercise 10 — A convex energy that beats its $O(1/t)$ bound (OT4ML Rem. 15.34)§3 · checked

Let $f(\al)=\frac14\int\abs x^4\dd\al$ on $\Pc_2(\R)$. (1) Show that $f$ is geodesically convex but not $\lambda$-convex for any $\lambda\gt0$. (2) From $\al_0=\delta_1$, compute the flow and $f(\al_t)$, and compare with the bound of Proposition 15.26. (3) Compare with the Łojasiewicz rate of OT4ML Remark 15.34.

Hint
The flow of a potential energy moves each particle by $\dot x=-V'(x)$, here $\dot x=-x^3$.
Solution

(1) $\frac14x^4$ is convex with second derivative $3x^2$, which vanishes at 0, so it is not $\lambda$-convex for $\lambda\gt0$; by Proposition 15.17, $f$ is geodesically convex and not strongly so.

(2) $\dot x=-x^3$, $x(0)=1$ gives $x(t)=(1+2t)^{-1/2}$ and $f(\al_t)=\frac1{4(1+2t)^2}$. The minimizer is $\delta_0$, $W_2^2(\delta_1,\delta_0)=1$, and Proposition 15.26 gives $f(\al_t)\le\frac1{2t}$: true, and loose by a factor of order $t$.

JKO step$\al^{(k+1)}\in\argmin_\al\frac1{2\tau}W_2^2(\al^{(k)},\al)+f(\al)$; for one Dirac, the implicit Euler step; Euler–Lagrange: $\frac{T-\Id}\tau=\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al^{(k+1)})$, $T$ the Brenier map to $\al^{(k)}$; first order in $\tau$, stable for every $\tau$.
On the line$W_2^2=\int_0^1\abs{Q_\al-Q_\be}^2$; entropy $=-\int_0^1\log Q'$; the heat JKO step is convex in $Q$; explicit particle steps need $\tau\lesssim\frac12\min\Delta x^2$.
First variation$f((1-\vartheta)\al+\vartheta\be)=f(\al)+\vartheta\int\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}\dd(\be-\al)+o(\vartheta)$, up to a constant.
Gradient, flow$\nabla_Wf=\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$ (Riesz representative in $L^2(\al)$, Prop. 15.7); $\partial_t\al=\operatorname{div}(\al\nabla_Wf(\al))$; $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}f(\al_t)=-\int\norm{\nabla_Wf}^2\dd\al_t$.
Examples$\int V\dd\al\to\dot x=-\nabla V$; $\int\rho\log\rho\to\partial_t\rho=\Delta\rho$; $\int g(\rho)\to\Delta P(\rho)$, $P'=sg''$ ($\Delta\rho^m$); $\KL(\cdot\mid e^{-V})\to\operatorname{div}(\rho\nabla V)+\Delta\rho$; $\iint k\to-2\int\nabla_xk\,\dd\al$; $\mathrm{MMD}^2\to$ interaction + attraction; $W_2^2(\cdot,\be)\to2(T-\Id)$.

Particles$\dot x_i=-\nabla_Wf(\al)(x_i)=-n\nabla_{x_i}F(X)$; plain gradient descent on positions runs $n$ times slower; entropy is not defined on clouds.

§14Self-test

  1. Write the JKO step and its restriction to Dirac masses.
    Answer
    $\al^{(k+1)}\in\argmin\frac1{2\tau}W_2^2(\al^{(k)},\al)+f(\al)$. For $\al=\delta_x$, $W_2^2(\delta_{x_k},\delta_x)=\norm{x-x_k}^2$ and the step is the implicit Euler step $x_{k+1}=x_k-\tau\nabla h(x_{k+1})$ of $h(x)=f(\delta_x)$.
  2. Why is an implicit step preferable to an explicit one for the heat equation on particles?
    Answer
    The particle system is stiff: its fastest modes have rates of order $1/\min\Delta x^2$, so explicit steps must be below about $\frac12\min\Delta x^2$ (Lab 1: 1.3×10⁻⁵ for 200 particles). The JKO step is a convex problem solved by Newton for every $\tau$, with an error of order $\tau$.
  3. What is the Euler–Lagrange equation of a JKO step, and how does it make the scheme an implicit Euler method?
    Answer
    $\frac{T(x)-x}\tau=\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al^{(k+1)})(x)$ with $T$ the Brenier map from $\al^{(k+1)}$ to $\al^{(k)}$: each particle moved by $-\tau\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$ evaluated at the new measure.
  4. Compute the first variation of $\int\rho\log\rho$ and of $\iint k\,\dd\al\,\dd\al$.
    Answer
    $\log\rho+1$ (up to a constant), and $\int(k(x,y)+k(y,x))\dd\al(y)$, i.e. $2\int k(x,y)\dd\al(y)$ for symmetric $k$.
  5. Why is $\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$ called a gradient?
    Answer
    For a velocity $v$, $\frac{\dd}{\dd\tau}f((\Id+\tau v)_\sharp\al)|_0=\int\dotp{\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}}v\dd\al$ (Prop. 15.7): it is the Riesz representative of the derivative for the $L^2(\al)$ metric on velocities, the metric that defines $W_2$.
  6. Derive the heat equation as a Wasserstein gradient flow.
    Answer
    $\partial_t\rho=\operatorname{div}(\rho\nabla(\log\rho+1))=\operatorname{div}(\nabla\rho)=\Delta\rho$.
  7. Give two different gradient-flow structures for the heat equation.
    Answer
    $W_2$ flow of $\int\rho\log\rho$, and $L^2(\dd x)$ flow of $\frac12\int\norm{\nabla\rho}^2$ (first variation $-\Delta\rho$). A PDE does not determine the energy and the metric.
  8. Which PDE is the flow of $\int g(\rho)$?
    Answer
    $\partial_t\rho=\Delta P(\rho)$ with $P'(s)=sg''(s)$: the heat equation for $g=s\log s$, the porous medium equation $\Delta\rho^m$ for $g=s^m/(m-1)$.
  9. Show that the law of $\dd X=-\nabla V\dd t+\sqrt2\dd B$ follows the gradient flow of $\KL(\cdot\mid e^{-V})$.
    Answer
    Itô: $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\E\psi(X_t)=\E(\Delta\psi-\dotp{\nabla V}{\nabla\psi})(X_t)$; integrating by parts, $\partial_t\mu=\Delta\mu+\operatorname{div}(\mu\nabla V)=\operatorname{div}(\mu\nabla\log\frac\mu{e^{-V}})$, the KL flow.
  10. What bias does the Euler–Maruyama scheme have on the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process?
    Answer
    $X\leftarrow(1-h)X+\sqrt{2h}\xi$ has stationary variance $\frac{2h}{1-(1-h)^2}=\frac1{1-h/2}$ instead of 1: a bias of about $h/2$, whatever the number of particles.
  11. Why does the deterministic score flow need a density estimate, and what does a kernel estimate do to the stationary law?
    Answer
    The velocity $-\nabla V-\nabla\log\rho_t$ needs the score of the current law, which a cloud has not. With a Gaussian kernel of width $b$ the fixed point solves $\rho*\Gauss(0,b^2)=\gamma$, so the law is too narrow by $b^2$ in variance (plus a finite-$n$ effect).
  12. Why does plain gradient descent on particle positions run the Wasserstein flow $n$ times slower?
    Answer
    With $F(X)=f(\frac1n\sum\delta_{x_i})$, $\nabla_{x_i}F=\frac1n\nabla_Wf(\al)(x_i)$: the mass $\frac1n$ of each particle appears in the derivative. The Wasserstein flow is $\dot x_i=-n\nabla_{x_i}F$.
  13. What makes the energy-distance kernel better than a Gaussian kernel for pulling particles towards a distant target?
    Answer
    $\nabla_x(-\norm{x-y})$ has unit norm at every distance, while the Gaussian kernel’s force decays like $e^{-\norm{x-y}^2/(2\sigma^2)}$; a target 8 widths away exerts no measurable force (Lab 4: the cloud spreads by self-repulsion instead).
  14. State $\lambda$-geodesic convexity and give an energy that has it and one that does not.
    Answer
    $f(\al_t)\le(1-t)f(\al_0)+tf(\al_1)-\frac\lambda2t(1-t)W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1)$ along geodesics. $\KL(\cdot\mid\Gauss(0,\Id))$ is 1-convex; $W_2^2(\cdot,\be)$ is not convex in $\R^2$ (Exercise 8).
  15. Why is the entropy displacement convex although $\rho\mapsto\rho\log\rho$ is not affine along the geodesic?
    Answer
    Along $T_t=(1-t)\Id+tT$ the density is $\rho_0/J_t$ with $J_t=\det((1-t)\Id+t\nabla T)$, and $\int\rho_t\log\rho_t=\int\rho_0\log\rho_0-\int\rho_0\log J_t$; $\log\det$ is concave on positive matrices, so $-\log J_t$ is convex in $t$ (McCann’s argument with $\psi(r)=-d\log r$).
  16. What rate does geodesic convexity alone give, and what does strong convexity add?
    Answer
    $f(\al_t)-f^\star\le W_2^2(\al_0,\al^\star)/(2t)$ (Prop. 15.26); $\lambda$-convexity gives PL with $\kappa=\lambda$, hence $e^{-2\lambda t}$ for the energy and $e^{-\lambda t}$ in $W_2$.
  17. Why is the logarithmic Sobolev inequality a PL inequality?
    Answer
    For $f=\KL(\cdot\mid\be)$, $\nabla_Wf=\nabla\log\frac{\dd\al}{\dd\be}$ and $\int\norm{\nabla_Wf}^2\dd\al=\mathcal I(\al\mid\be)$; LSI $\KL\le\frac1{2\lambda}\mathcal I$ is $\int\norm{\nabla_Wf}^2\ge2\lambda(f-f^\star)$ with $f^\star=0$.
  18. Along the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck flow from a mixture with mean $m_0\ne0$, what is the late decay rate of the entropy, and why can a centered start decay faster than the LSI bound?
    Answer
    $e^{-2t}$: the slowest mode is the mean, $H\approx\frac12m_0^2e^{-2t}$, and translates are the equality case of LSI. A centered law has no mean mode; its slowest mode is the variance, which decays like $e^{-4t}$ (Lab 3: slope −4.00).
  19. When does a Gaussian stay Gaussian under a Wasserstein flow, and what does the flow become?
    Answer
    When the Wasserstein gradient is affine on Gaussians (first variation quadratic in $x$). The flow becomes $\dot m=-b_f$, $\dot\Sigma=-(A_f\Sigma+\Sigma A_f)$, and on the line the Euclidean gradient flow of $g(m,\sigma)$ in the flat half-plane.
  20. Show that the flow of $W_2^2(\cdot,\be)$ follows the McCann geodesic, and give the clock.
    Answer
    The velocity is $2(T_s-\Id)=2(1-s)\times$ the geodesic velocity $\frac{T_s-\Id}{1-s}$, so $\dot s=2(1-s)$ and $s(t)=1-e^{-2t}$; $W_2(\al_t,\be)=e^{-2t}W_2(\al_0,\be)$ (Exercise 6).
  21. Why is the risk of a two-layer network convex in the measure of neurons, and why does that not guarantee global convergence of training?
    Answer
    $G_\al$ is linear in $\al$ and the square loss is convex, so $f$ is convex along mixtures. The Wasserstein flow only moves existing mass; a stationary measure may have a negative first variation in a direction its support does not reach (Lab 6: a Dirac start stalls at the best one-neuron network).
  22. What is the certificate of Proposition 15.52 and what does it say at a stall?
    Answer
    $h=\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$, 2-homogeneous, vanishes on the support of a stationary $\al$; $\al$ is a global minimizer iff $\min_{\norm\omega=1}h\ge0$. A negative minimum gives the direction (here the missing teacher, with negative output weight) in which new neurons would lower the risk.
  23. Write residual $L^2$ attention as a particle flow and explain why tokens cluster.
    Answer
    $\dot x_i=M_\eps[\al](x_i)=\eps\nabla\log\rho_\eps(x_i)$, mean shift. Each step averages tokens with a row-stochastic matrix; the Dobrushin coefficient bounds the contraction of the cloud’s width, but it is close to 1 when the cloud is wide compared with $\sqrt\eps$, so tokens first gather around local modes (clusters) and merge much later.
  24. How does one test a transport reconstruction of a single-cell time course?
    Answer
    Hold out a collection day, interpolate its neighbours by optimal transport at the right time fraction, and compare with the held-out population (energy distance, $W_2$), against the mixture of the neighbours as baseline; repeat with other subsamples (Lab 8: the average gain is stable, day-by-day wins are not).
