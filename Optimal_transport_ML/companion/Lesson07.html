Entropic regularization

Adding an entropy penalty to the transport cost turns a linear program into a smooth, strictly convex problem whose solution is a rescaled Gibbs kernel. Rescaling rows and columns in turn computes it, with matrix–vector products only. Entropy buys speed, differentiability and better statistics; it costs a bias, which can be measured and largely removed.

§0Where this lesson sits

Lessons 2 to 4 solved optimal transport as a linear program and through its dual. Exact solvers cost about $n^3$ operations, and their solution is a vertex of the transport polytope: sparse, often not unique, and jumping when the data move. This lesson adds an entropy penalty, weighted by a temperature $\eps>0$, to the transport cost. The problem becomes strictly convex and smooth in the data. Its solution is a dense matrix of Gibbs form, computed by alternately rescaling rows and columns (Sinkhorn’s algorithm), each step a matrix–vector product. The price is a bias: the entropic value is not the transport cost. The lesson measures this bias and removes most of it (Sinkhorn divergences). It also measures how fast Sinkhorn converges, why it slows down as $\eps\to0$, and what entropy changes when transport is estimated from samples.

Watch out: four ways to write the penalty, one minimizer, four values

On the couplings of $(a,b)$ the total mass is 1, so $$\begin{aligned}\KL(P\mid a\otimes b)&=\sum_{ij}P_{ij}\log P_{ij}-\sum_ia_i\log a_i-\sum_jb_j\log b_j\\&=-\mathrm H(P)+\mathrm H(a)+\mathrm H(b)\end{aligned}$$ with the Shannon entropy $\mathrm H(P)=-\sum P\log P$, and $\sum P(\log P-1)=-\mathrm H(P)-1$. The four penalties differ by constants: they have the same minimizer, and their values differ by $\eps(\mathrm H(a)+\mathrm H(b))$ or $\eps(1+\mathrm H(a)+\mathrm H(b))$. Values matter in §6–§9 (expansions, debiasing, statistics): there this page always uses $\KL(P\mid a\otimes b)$, which also makes sense between general measures. With it, $L^\eps\ge L_C$ always, and the Sinkhorn divergence does not depend on the choice of reference measure (OT4ML Rem. 8.38).

A question that comes back

What does entropy buy, and what does it cost? It buys a unique, positive, smooth solution (§1), an algorithm made of matrix–vector products (§2–§4), gradients for free (§9), and statistics that do not degrade with the dimension at fixed $\eps$ (§8). It costs a bias of order $\eps\log(1/\eps)$ on the transport value (§6), a blur of width $\sqrt\eps$ on the plan (§1), iterations that grow like $1/\eps$ (§5), and constants in the statistical bounds that explode as $\eps\to0$ (§8). Debiasing (§7) removes the first-order bias, and keeps the gains. The last section asks the same question of the marginal constraints: relaxing them buys robustness to mass that should not be transported.

§1The discrete Schrödinger problem

The problem

The Kantorovich problem $L_C(a,b)=\min_{P\in\Couplings(a,b)}\dotp CP$ is a linear program. Its solutions sit on vertices of the transport polytope (Lesson 2 §2): they have at most $n+m-1$ non-zero entries, they need not be unique, and they jump from one vertex to another when $a$, $b$ or $C$ move. The value $L_C$ is concave in $C$ and convex in $(a,b)$ but not differentiable. Exact solvers (network simplex, Hungarian method) cost about $n^3$ operations for $n$ points and do not parallelize well. For machine learning one wants instead a value that is smooth in the data, a plan that depends continuously on them, and an algorithm made of operations a GPU does well.

Adding entropy

Definition (Entropic optimal transport (discrete))OT4ML Def. 8.2 and (8.8), pp. 136, 143 (128, 135); compact Def. 8.2, p. 34; CompOT (4.2), p. 62 (58); slides 4–5; sheet Ex. 1

Let $a\in\R^n$, $b\in\R^m$ be probability vectors with positive entries, $C\in\R^{n\times m}$ and $\eps>0$. The entropic transport problem is $$\begin{gathered}L^\eps(a,b)=\min_{P\in\Couplings(a,b)}\ \dotp CP+\eps\KL(P\mid a\otimes b),\\\KL(P\mid Q)=\sum_{i,j}P_{ij}\log\frac{P_{ij}}{Q_{ij}}-P_{ij}+Q_{ij},\end{gathered}$$ with $0\log0=0$. The parameter $\eps$ is called the temperature.

Write $(X,Y)$ for a pair of random indices with joint law $P$. Then $\KL(P\mid a\otimes b)$ is the mutual information $I(X;Y)$, which vanishes exactly when $X$ and $Y$ are independent (OT4ML Def. 8.13, p. 145 (137); slide 6). The problem reads $$L^\eps(a,b)=\min\big\{\E\,C_{XY}+\eps\,I(X;Y):\ X\sim a,\ Y\sim b\big\}.$$ The transport plan pays $\eps$ for each nat of information that $Y$ carries about $X$. A deterministic plan (a permutation, for uniform weights of equal size) carries the most information, $\log n$; the independent coupling $a\otimes b$ carries none. Small $\eps$ allows plans close to deterministic; large $\eps$ pushes towards independence.

Proposition (existence, uniqueness and positivity)OT4ML Prop. 8.3, p. 137 (129); compact Prop. 8.3, p. 34; CompOT p. 62 (58)

For every $\eps>0$ the problem has a unique minimizer $P_\eps$, and every entry of $P_\eps$ is positive.

Proof (compactness, strict convexity, and the infinite slope of $t\log t$ at 0)

The polytope $\Couplings(a,b)$ is compact and non-empty, and the objective is continuous on it ($t\log t\to0$ as $t\to0$), so a minimizer exists. The function $t\mapsto t\log t$ is strictly convex, so the objective is strictly convex and the minimizer is unique.

Positivity. Let $P$ be feasible with $P_{ij}=0$ for some pair, and let $Q=a\otimes b$, whose entries are all positive. Along $P_t=(1-t)P+tQ$, which stays feasible, the derivative at $t=0^+$ of every term with $P_{ij}>0$ is finite, while for each zero entry $$\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\Big[tQ_{ij}\log\frac{tQ_{ij}}{a_ib_j}\Big]=Q_{ij}\big(\log t+1\big)\longrightarrow-\infty$$ as $t\to0^+$. So the objective decreases when one moves from $P$ towards $Q$, and $P$ is not a minimizer. $\square$

The last step is why entropy works as a barrier: the slope of $t\log t$ is infinite at 0, so the constraints $P_{ij}\ge0$ are never active at the optimum. The first-order conditions then involve only the marginal constraints, and they give the product form of §2. The interior-point barrier $-\sum\log P_{ij}$ of Lesson 2 §5 also keeps entries positive, but its Newton steps are dense, while the entropic problem is solved by diagonal scalings (OT4ML Rem. 8.4, p. 138 (130)).

Between the linear program and independence

Proposition (convergence with $\eps$)OT4ML Prop. 8.10, p. 144 (136); compact Prop. 8.8, p. 35; CompOT Prop. 4.1, p. 62 (58)

As $\eps\to0$, $L^\eps(a,b)\to L_C(a,b)$ and $P_\eps$ converges to the optimal plan of the linear program with the smallest $\KL(\cdot\mid a\otimes b)$ (the optimal plan of maximal entropy). As $\eps\to\infty$, $P_\eps\to a\otimes b$.

Proof (compare with one optimal plan, then with all of them)

$\eps\to\infty$. Testing $P=a\otimes b$ gives $\dotp C{P_\eps}+\eps\KL(P_\eps\mid a\otimes b)\le\dotp C{a\otimes b}$, so $\KL(P_\eps\mid a\otimes b)\le(\max C-\min C)/\eps\to0$, and Pinsker’s inequality gives $P_\eps\to a\otimes b$.

$\eps\to0$. Let $P^\star$ be any optimal plan. Since $\KL\ge0$, $$\begin{aligned}L_C\le\dotp C{P_\eps}\le L^\eps&\le\dotp C{P^\star}+\eps\KL(P^\star\mid a\otimes b)\\&=L_C+\eps\KL(P^\star\mid a\otimes b),\end{aligned}$$ so $L^\eps\to L_C$ and every cluster point $\bar P$ of $(P_\eps)$ is optimal. Subtracting $\dotp C{P_\eps}\ge\dotp C{P^\star}$ from the comparison $L^\eps\le\dotp C{P^\star}+\eps\KL(P^\star)$ gives $\KL(P_\eps\mid a\otimes b)\le\KL(P^\star\mid a\otimes b)$ for every optimal $P^\star$; by continuity the same holds for $\bar P$. So $\bar P$ minimizes $\KL(\cdot\mid a\otimes b)$ on the convex set of optimal plans, where this function is strictly convex: the minimizer is unique, and the whole family converges to it. $\square$

The sandwich in the proof also gives the size of the bias on a fixed finite problem. When the optimal plan $P^\star$ is unique, $\KL(P_\eps\mid a\otimes b)\to\KL(P^\star\mid a\otimes b)$, so $$L^\eps-L_C=\eps\KL(P^\star\mid a\otimes b)+o(\eps),$$ while $\dotp C{P_\eps}-L_C$ is exponentially small in $1/\eps$: the sheet’s Exercise 1 computes it for a $2\times2$ problem, where the plan approaches its vertex like $e^{-2/\eps}$ (§12). For histograms that discretize densities, $\KL(P^\star\mid a\otimes b)$ grows like the logarithm of the number of cells, and in the continuum the bias becomes $\frac d2\eps\log(1/\eps)$ (Prop. 8.17, §6). Lab 1 measures both regimes on the same problem and finds where one hands over to the other.

Why Schrödinger

In 1931–32 Schrödinger asked for the most likely evolution of a cloud of independent Brownian particles observed with one distribution at time 0 and an unexpectedly different one at time 1 (slides 8–9). By the chain rule of the relative entropy, the answer keeps Brownian bridges between the endpoints and optimizes only the endpoint coupling, by minimizing $\eps\KL(\pi\mid R_{01})$ with $R_{01}$ the Brownian endpoint law. For noise of variance $\sigma^2$ per unit time this is entropic transport with cost $\norm{x-y}^2$ and $\eps=2\sigma^2$ (OT4ML §14.3, pp. 334–339 (326–331)). Lesson 6 §9 proves the reduction. The problem of this section is the static, discrete version; the continuous one is in §6.

Key idea: a temperature, two biases

Entropy replaces the vertex of the linear program by a positive plan whose conditional laws $Y\mid X=x$ have a width of order $\sqrt\eps$. On a fixed finite problem the bias of the value is $\eps$ times the mutual information of the optimal plan. When the problem discretizes densities, this mutual information grows with the resolution, and the bias is $\frac d2\eps\log(1/\eps)$ plus entropies, which §6 computes and §7 removes.

§2Sinkhorn’s algorithm

The optimal plan is a scaled Gibbs kernel

Since the positivity constraints are inactive (Prop. 8.3), only the two marginal constraints carry multipliers, and the optimality conditions take a product form.

Proposition (scaling form)OT4ML Prop. 8.5, p. 138 (130); compact Prop. 8.4, p. 34; CompOT Prop. 4.3, p. 67 (63); slide 14

Let $K=e^{-C/\eps}$ (entrywise), the Gibbs kernel. A coupling $P\in\Couplings(a,b)$ is the minimizer $P_\eps$ if and only if there are vectors $u\in\R^n$, $v\in\R^m$ with positive entries such that $$P=\diag(u)\,K\,\diag(v),\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\begin{gathered}u\odot(Kv)=a,\\v\odot(K^\top u)=b.\end{gathered}$$ The pair $(u,v)$ is unique up to $(\lambda u,v/\lambda)$, $\lambda>0$.

Proof (Lagrange multipliers, then an exact Pythagorean identity)

Necessity. The minimizer is positive, so it is an interior critical point of the objective under the affine constraints $P\mathbb 1=a$, $P^\top\mathbb 1=b$. With multipliers $f\in\R^n$, $g\in\R^m$, the derivative in $P_{ij}$ of $\dotp CP+\eps\KL(P\mid a\otimes b)-\dotp f{P\mathbb 1-a}-\dotp g{P^\top\mathbb 1-b}$ vanishes: $$\begin{gathered}C_{ij}+\eps\log\frac{P_{ij}}{a_ib_j}-f_i-g_j=0,\quad\text{so}\\P_{ij}=a_ib_j\,e^{(f_i+g_j-C_{ij})/\eps}=u_iK_{ij}v_j,\end{gathered}$$ with $u_i=a_ie^{f_i/\eps}$ and $v_j=b_je^{g_j/\eps}$.

Sufficiency. Let $P=\diag(u)K\diag(v)$ be a coupling of $(a,b)$, and define $f,g$ by the same formulas, so that $\log\frac{P_{ij}}{a_ib_j}=\frac{f_i+g_j-C_{ij}}\eps$. For any coupling $Q$, the masses of $Q$, $P$ and $a\otimes b$ are all 1, so $$\begin{aligned}&\dotp CQ+\eps\KL(Q\mid a\otimes b)\\&\quad=\dotp CQ+\eps\sum_{ij}Q_{ij}\log\frac{Q_{ij}}{P_{ij}}+\eps\sum_{ij}Q_{ij}\log\frac{P_{ij}}{a_ib_j}\\&\quad=\eps\KL(Q\mid P)+\sum_{ij}Q_{ij}(f_i+g_j)\\&\quad=\eps\KL(Q\mid P)+\dotp fa+\dotp gb.\end{aligned}$$ The right side is minimal exactly at $Q=P$, since $\KL(Q\mid P)\ge0$ with equality only for $Q=P$. So $P$ is the minimizer, and the minimal value is $\dotp fa+\dotp gb$.

Uniqueness of the scalings. If $\diag(u)K\diag(v)=\diag(u')K\diag(v')$ with $K>0$, then $u_iv_j=u'_iv'_j$ for all $i,j$, so $u'=\lambda u$ and $v'=v/\lambda$. $\square$

OT4ML states the proposition as an equivalence but proves only the necessity; the identity above supplies the converse, and it will be used three more times. It says that the objective, restricted to couplings, is $\eps$ times the relative entropy to the optimal plan, plus a constant (§3, Prop. 9.3), and that the optimal value is $\dotp fa+\dotp gb$ (§4, Lemma 8.39).

Alternating the two scalings

The two equations $u\odot(Kv)=a$ and $v\odot(K^\top u)=b$ are coupled, but each is trivial to solve for one vector when the other is fixed. Sinkhorn’s algorithm alternates the two exact solutions.

Algorithm (Sinkhorn scaling)OT4ML Alg. 8.1, p. 139 (131); CompOT (4.15), p. 68 (64); slide 15; notebook 2-sinkhorn, cell 31

Start from $v^{(0)}=\mathbb 1_m$. For $\ell=0,1,2,\ldots$ $$u^{(\ell+1)}=\frac a{Kv^{(\ell)}},\qquad v^{(\ell+1)}=\frac b{K^\top u^{(\ell+1)}}$$ (entrywise divisions), and stop when $\norm{u^{(\ell)}\odot(Kv^{(\ell)})-a}_1$ is small. The plan is $P^{(\ell)}=\diag(u^{(\ell)})K\diag(v^{(\ell)})$; after each $v$-update its column sums are exactly $b$, and only the row sums remain to converge.

Each half-step fixes one marginal exactly and breaks the other (slide 18; OT4ML Fig. 8.3, p. 140 (132)). An iteration costs two matrix–vector products, $O(nm)$ operations, and nothing else: no line search, no step size, no linear system. Batches of problems with the same kernel become matrix–matrix products (CompOT Rem. 4.16, p. 77 (73)), and the same few lines run on a GPU. The iteration is also differentiable, so a loss computed with a fixed number of iterations can be differentiated by automatic differentiation (CompOT Rem. 4.27, p. 86 (82)); §9 shows that the gradient is available without it.

History. The same alternating normalization was used by Yule (1912) for contingency tables, by Kruithof (1937) for telephone traffic, and by Deming and Stephan (1940) as iterative proportional fitting (IPFP) in survey statistics; economists know it as the RAS method (slide 17; CompOT Rem. 4.5, p. 68 (64)). Sinkhorn (1964) proved that a positive square matrix can be scaled to be doubly stochastic, and Sinkhorn and Knopp (1967) treated non-negative matrices. Its use for optimal transport in machine learning starts with Cuturi (2013), whose title promised “lightspeed computation of optimal transport”. What the transport problem adds is the choice of the matrix: the Gibbs kernel $e^{-C/\eps}$.

What one iteration costs, and what structure saves

For a dense cost an iteration costs $nm$ multiplications. On a grid of $N=q^d$ points with the cost $\norm{x-y}^2$, the kernel factorizes along the axes, $K=K_1\otimes\cdots\otimes K_1$, and applying it costs $O(dN^{1+1/d})$ instead of $O(N^2)$ (OT4ML Rem. 8.6, p. 142 (134); CompOT Rem. 4.17, p. 78 (74)). For images, $Kv$ is a Gaussian blur of $v$, which can also be computed by recursive filters or on meshes by heat diffusion (OT4ML §8.7, beyond the course). The barycenter part of 2-sinkhorn uses the factorization: K = lambda x: np.dot(np.dot(K1,x),K1).

How many iterations are needed is the subject of §5. Two facts are worth knowing now. To approximate the unregularized value $L_C$ within $\delta$ one may take $\eps\approx\delta/\log(nm)$ and about $\norm C_\infty^2\log(nm)/\delta^2$ iterations, so $O(n^2\log n\,\norm C_\infty^2/\delta^2)$ operations in total (OT4ML Cor. 9.14, p. 179 (171); Altschuler, Weed and Rigollet 2017): fewer than the $n^3$ of exact solvers when a moderate accuracy suffices. And the iterates are feasible for one marginal only; a feasible plan is obtained by a cheap rounding step (CompOT Rem. 4.6, p. 69 (65)).

From a plan to a map: barycentric projection

An entropic plan is never a map: every entry is positive. A map is read off it by averaging, for each source point, the targets it sends mass to: $$T_\eps(x_i)=\frac1{a_i}\sum_jP_{ij}\,y_j=\E[Y\mid X=x_i]$$ (sheet Ex. 11; CompOT Rem. 4.11, p. 73 (69)). With scalings it costs one more kernel product, $T_\eps=\big(u\odot K(v\odot y)\big)/a$ (notebook 2-sinkhorn, cell 64). If the unregularized problem has a unique optimal plan supported on the graph of a map $T$, then $P_\eps\to(\mathrm{Id},T)_\sharp a$ by Prop. 8.10 and $T_\eps(x_i)\to T(x_i)$ for every $i$ (sheet Ex. 11(1)). For the quadratic cost it is also the gradient of a convex potential and the “force” that the entropic loss exerts on $x_i$ (§9, sheet Ex. 11(2)). StatOT calls $T_\eps$ the entropic map and proves that it is estimated from samples at the parametric rate (StatOT Thms. 4.10–4.11, pp. 132–135 (126–129)).

Watch out: the barycentric projection does not transport $\al$ onto $\be$

Averaging contracts. In general $(T_\eps)_\sharp\al\ne\be$: the image is a shrunk version of $\be$ (for Gaussians in §6 its variance is $\rho_\eps^2\,\sigma_\be^2$ with $\rho_\eps<1$ the correlation of the plan). It converges to the Monge map as $\eps\to0$, at a rate that depends on the smoothness of the problem (OT4ML Props. 10.14–10.15, p. 206 (198)). StatOT stresses the same point: $b^\star(x)=\E_{\gamma^\star}[Y\mid X=x]$ is not a transport map (StatOT §4.3, pp. 123–124 (117–118)).

§3KL, Bregman divergences and projections

The problem

Why the Kullback–Leibler divergence, and not another penalty? Every strictly convex penalty makes the minimizer unique. What KL adds is structure: the objective becomes a KL distance to a fixed matrix, and projecting on one marginal constraint in that distance is a diagonal scaling. Sinkhorn is the alternating projection method for this distance, and its convergence follows from a Pythagorean identity.

Two families of divergences, and their intersection

A $\varphi$-divergence compares densities through a convex function of their ratio, $D_\varphi(P\mid Q)=\sum_iQ_i\,\varphi(P_i/Q_i)$ with $\varphi(1)=0$ (Lesson 4 §8; sheet Ex. 10). A Bregman divergence measures the gap between a convex function and its tangent.

Definition (Bregman divergence and projection)OT4ML Defs. 9.1–9.2, p. 172 (164); compact Defs. 9.1–9.2, p. 46; OT4ML Def. 8.31, p. 162 (154); slide 28

For a strictly convex differentiable $\Phi$, $B_\Phi(P\mid Q)=\Phi(P)-\Phi(Q)-\dotp{\nabla\Phi(Q)}{P-Q}\ge0$. The Bregman projection of $Q$ on a closed convex set $\mathcal C$ is $\operatorname{Proj}^{B_\Phi}_{\mathcal C}(Q)=\argmin_{P\in\mathcal C}B_\Phi(P\mid Q)$.

With $\Phi(P)=\frac12\norm P^2$ one gets $\frac12\norm{P-Q}^2$ and Euclidean projections. With the negative entropy $\Phi(P)=\sum_{ij}P_{ij}\log P_{ij}-P_{ij}$, $\nabla\Phi(Q)=\log Q$ and $B_\Phi(P\mid Q)=\KL(P\mid Q)$. With $\varphi(t)=t\log t-t+1$, $D_\varphi=\KL$ too. KL is the only divergence in both families.

Proposition (KL is the common Bregman and $\varphi$-divergence)OT4ML Prop. 8.34, p. 163 (155); compact Prop. 8.29, p. 43

If a Bregman divergence $B_\Phi$ coincides with a $\varphi$-divergence $D_\varphi$ on probability densities, then $\varphi(t)=\kappa\,t\log t+c(t-1)$ for constants $\kappa>0$, $c$: the divergence is $\kappa\KL$.

Each family brings one property used below. Bregman divergences satisfy a Pythagorean identity for projections on affine sets, which drives the convergence proof. $\varphi$-divergences depend on ratios, so a projection on a marginal constraint acts by multiplying rows or columns.

The cost is only a tilt of the reference

Proposition (linear tilt of the reference)OT4ML Prop. 9.3 and (9.1), p. 173 (165); OT4ML (8.9), p. 143 (135); CompOT (4.6)–(4.7), p. 63 (59)

Let $\tilde K=(a\otimes b)\odot e^{-C/\eps}$. For every coupling $P$ of $(a,b)$, $$\dotp CP+\eps\KL(P\mid a\otimes b)=\eps\KL(P\mid\tilde K)+\eps\Big(1-\sum_{ij}\tilde K_{ij}\Big),$$ so $P_\eps=\operatorname{Proj}^{\KL}_{\Couplings(a,b)}(\tilde K)$: the entropic plan is the KL projection of the tilted Gibbs kernel on the couplings.

Proof (expand the logarithm)

$\KL(P\mid\tilde K)=\sum P_{ij}\log\frac{P_{ij}}{a_ib_j}+\frac1\eps\sum P_{ij}C_{ij}-\sum P_{ij}+\sum\tilde K_{ij}$, and $\KL(P\mid a\otimes b)=\sum P_{ij}\log\frac{P_{ij}}{a_ib_j}$ because both $P$ and $a\otimes b$ have mass 1. Multiply by $\eps$ and subtract. $\square$

Projections on one marginal constraint are scalings

Proposition (KL projections on the marginal sets)OT4ML Prop. 9.5, p. 175 (167); compact Prop. 9.5, p. 46; slides 19, 28; CompOT Rem. 4.8, p. 70 (66)

Let $\mathcal C^1_a=\{Q\ge0:Q\mathbb 1=a\}$ and $\mathcal C^2_b=\{Q\ge0:Q^\top\mathbb 1=b\}$. For $P$ with positive entries, $$\begin{gathered}\operatorname{Proj}^{\KL}_{\mathcal C^1_a}(P)=\diag\Big(\frac a{P\mathbb 1}\Big)P,\\\operatorname{Proj}^{\KL}_{\mathcal C^2_b}(P)=P\diag\Big(\frac b{P^\top\mathbb 1}\Big).\end{gathered}$$

Proof (the problem splits row by row)

$\KL(Q\mid P)=\sum_i\sum_j\big(Q_{ij}\log\frac{Q_{ij}}{P_{ij}}-Q_{ij}+P_{ij}\big)$ and the constraint $Q\mathbb 1=a$ involves each row separately. For row $i$, a multiplier $\lambda_i$ for $\sum_jQ_{ij}=a_i$ gives $\log\frac{Q_{ij}}{P_{ij}}=\lambda_i$, so $Q_{ij}=e^{\lambda_i}P_{ij}$, and the constraint fixes $e^{\lambda_i}=a_i/(P\mathbb 1)_i$. The function is strictly convex, so this critical point is the minimizer. Columns are the same. $\square$

So the $u$-update of Sinkhorn is the KL projection of the current plan on $\mathcal C^1_a$ and the $v$-update its projection on $\mathcal C^2_b$. Starting from $K$ (slide 28, notebook 2) or from $\tilde K$ (OT4ML) changes the iterates but not the limit: the two matrices are diagonal rescalings of each other, projections only multiply rows and columns, and the limit is the unique coupling of the form $\diag(u)K\diag(v)$ (§2). Sinkhorn is the method of alternating Bregman projections (Bregman 1967) applied to two affine sets, and its limit is the projection on their intersection.

Proposition (convergence of Sinkhorn, by alternating projections)OT4ML Prop. 9.4, p. 174 (166) (general Bregman version); compact Prop. 9.4, p. 46; StatOT Ex. 4.6-5, p. 137 (131)

The Sinkhorn plans $P^{(k)}$ (one index per half-step) converge to $P_\eps$, and $\KL(P_\eps\mid P^{(k)})$ decreases at every half-step.

Proof (Pythagoras, summability, and closedness of the scalings)

1. Pythagoras. Let $P'=\diag(r)P$ with $r=a/(P\mathbb 1)$ be the row projection of $P$. For any $Q\in\mathcal C^1_a$, $\sum_{ij}Q_{ij}\log r_i=\sum_ia_i\log r_i$, so $$\begin{aligned}\KL(Q\mid P)-\KL(Q\mid P')&=\sum_ia_i\log r_i-\textstyle\sum P'+\sum P\\&=\KL(P'\mid P).\end{aligned}$$ The same holds for column projections and $Q\in\mathcal C^2_b$.

2. Summability. Take $Q=P_\eps$, which lies in both sets: $\KL(P_\eps\mid P^{(k)})=\KL(P_\eps\mid P^{(k+1)})+\KL(P^{(k+1)}\mid P^{(k)})$. Summing, $\sum_k\KL(P^{(k+1)}\mid P^{(k)})\le\KL(P_\eps\mid P^{(0)})<\infty$.

3. Marginals. For $k\ge1$, after a row step, $P^{(k+1)}$ has row sums $a$ and $P^{(k)}$ has column sums $b$, both have mass 1, and the column error of $P^{(k+1)}$ is at most $\norm{P^{(k+1)}-P^{(k)}}_1\le\sqrt{2\KL(P^{(k+1)}\mid P^{(k)})}\to0$ (Pinsker). So both marginal errors tend to 0.

4. Limit. The iterates have mass 1, and $\KL(P_\eps\mid P^{(k)})$ stays bounded with $P_\eps>0$, so no entry of $P^{(k)}$ can tend to 0. Every cluster point $\bar P$ is therefore positive and, by step 3, a coupling of $(a,b)$. Every $P^{(k)}$ is of the form $\diag(u)K\diag(v)$, i.e. $\log P^{(k)}-\log K$ lies in the linear space $\{(\phi_i+\psi_j)_{ij}\}$, which is closed; so $\bar P=\diag(\bar u)K\diag(\bar v)$. By the scaling form (sufficiency), $\bar P=P_\eps$. All cluster points coincide, so the sequence converges. $\square$

The proof gives no rate; §5 does. It also explains why the order of the projections and the starting point do not matter, and why the same scheme extends to any number of affine constraints (multimarginal transport, barycenters: sheet Ex. 3, notebook 2). For a non-KL Bregman divergence the projections on $\mathcal C^1_a$ are not scalings and do not keep entries positive, so positivity must be imposed as a third constraint and Dykstra’s correction is needed (OT4ML §9.1, pp. 175–176 (167–168); CompOT Rem. 4.10, p. 71 (67)).

Other regularizers

Replacing $\eps\KL(P\mid a\otimes b)$ by $\eps D_\varphi(P\mid a\otimes b)$ (slide 23; OT4ML Def. 8.29, p. 161 (153)) keeps a unique minimizer and a dual of the same shape, but changes the plan. With $\varphi(t)=\frac12(t-1)^2$ (quadratic regularization) the optimal plan is sparse: its density $P_{ij}/(a_ib_j)$ is the positive part of $1+(f_i+g_j-C_{ij})/\eps$, because the derivative of $\varphi$ is finite at 0 and the positivity constraint can be active (Blondel, Seguy and Rolet 2018; OT4ML Fig. 8.14, p. 165 (157)). Burg’s entropy $-\log t$ gives a barrier closer to interior-point methods. None of them gives scaling updates; KL is the only choice with both closed-form projections and the Pythagorean identity.

Beyond the course: other regularizers

OT4ML §8.8 (pp. 161–165 (153–157)) treats $\varphi$- and Bregman-regularized transport with their duals and generalized soft $c$-transforms; CompOT Rem. 4.10 (p. 71 (67)) points to quadratic and Tsallis regularizations. The live demo regularized OT beyond KL compares the plans.

§4The dual: soft $c$-transforms and the log domain

The problem

Sinkhorn updates two vectors of scalings, $u=a\odot e^{f/\eps}$ and $v=b\odot e^{g/\eps}$. Their logarithms $f,g$ are dual potentials, as in Lesson 4 §1, and three things become clear in these variables: Sinkhorn is exact coordinate ascent on a concave function; its updates are smoothed versions of the $c$-transforms of Lesson 4; and it can be run without ever forming $e^{-C/\eps}$, which underflows when $\eps$ is small.

An unconstrained, smooth dual

Proposition (dual of entropic transport)OT4ML Prop. 8.18, p. 150 (142); compact Prop. 8.16, p. 38; CompOT Prop. 4.4, p. 81 (77); slide 29; sheet Ex. 2 and 6

$$\begin{aligned}L^\eps(a,b)=\max_{f\in\R^n,\,g\in\R^m}\ &\dotp fa+\dotp gb\\&-\eps\sum_{i,j}a_ib_j\Big(e^{(f_i+g_j-C_{ij})/\eps}-1\Big).\end{aligned}$$ The maximizers are unique up to $(f+\lambda,g-\lambda)$, and $P_\eps=\big(a_ib_je^{(f_i+g_j-C_{ij})/\eps}\big)_{ij}$. At the optimum, $L^\eps(a,b)=\dotp fa+\dotp gb$.

Proof (a pointwise Fenchel–Young inequality)

For $p\ge0$, $q>0$ and $s\in\R$, the convex function $p\mapsto p\log\frac pq-p+q$ has conjugate $s\mapsto q(e^s-1)$, so $$p\log\frac pq-p+q\ \ge\ ps-q(e^s-1),$$ with equality if and only if $p=qe^s$. Weak duality. Apply it with $p=P_{ij}$, $q=a_ib_j$, $s=(f_i+g_j-C_{ij})/\eps$, sum, multiply by $\eps$ and add $\dotp CP$. For a coupling $P$, $\sum P_{ij}(f_i+g_j)=\dotp fa+\dotp gb$, which gives primal $\ge$ dual for every $P$, $f$, $g$.

No gap. The potentials of the scaling form (§2) give $P_\eps=a\otimes b\odot e^{(f\oplus g-C)/\eps}$, so equality holds entry by entry. The sum of the $q\,e^s$ terms is the mass of $P_\eps$, 1, so the penalty vanishes and the common value is $\dotp fa+\dotp gb$.

Uniqueness. Any maximizer gives equality in weak duality with $P=P_\eps$, hence $a_ib_je^{(f_i+g_j-C_{ij})/\eps}=(P_\eps)_{ij}$ for all $i,j$: $f_i+g_j$ is determined, and $f$, $g$ are determined up to $(f+\lambda,g-\lambda)$. $\square$

Compare with the dual of the linear program, $\max\dotp fa+\dotp gb$ under $f_i+g_j\le C_{ij}$ (Lesson 4 §1). The hard constraint is replaced by a smooth exponential penalty: $\eps(e^{s/\eps}-1)$ tends to 0 for $s<0$ and to $+\infty$ for $s>0$ as $\eps\to0$. StatOT derives entropic transport from exactly this smoothing of the dual (StatOT §4.1, Lemma 4.1, p. 117 (111)). The dual is unconstrained, concave, and smooth, with gradient $a-P\mathbb 1$ in $f$ and $b-P^\top\mathbb 1$ in $g$: marginal errors are dual gradients.

Two consequences are used later. The value is a maximum of functions affine in $(a,b)$, so $L^\eps$ is jointly convex in $(a,b)$ (sheet Ex. 6(1)); and by the envelope theorem its gradient in $a$ is $f$, up to an additive constant that does not matter along the simplex (sheet Ex. 5(3); CompOT Prop. 4.6, p. 85 (81)).

Exact block ascent: soft $c$-transforms

Definition (soft-min and soft $c$-transforms)OT4ML Def. 8.19, p. 151 (143); compact Def. 8.17, p. 38; CompOT Rem. 4.22, p. 82 (78); slide 30; notebook 3-sinkhorn-advanced, cell 11

For a probability vector $b$ and $h\in\R^m$, $\operatorname{min}^\eps_b(h)=-\eps\log\sum_jb_je^{-h_j/\eps}$. The soft $c$-transforms of $g$ and of $f$ are $$f_i=\operatorname{min}^\eps_b\big(C_{i\cdot}-g\big),\qquad g_j=\operatorname{min}^\eps_a\big(C_{\cdot j}-f\big).$$

Setting the derivative of the dual in $f_i$ to zero gives $a_i=a_ie^{f_i/\eps}\sum_jb_je^{(g_j-C_{ij})/\eps}$, which is exactly the first soft transform: maximizing the dual in $f$ for fixed $g$ is a soft $c$-transform. In scalings it reads $u=a/(Kv)$. So Sinkhorn is exact block coordinate ascent on the dual (CompOT Rem. 4.21, p. 81 (77)), and the dual value increases at every half-step.

The soft minimum lies between two hard ones: $\min_jh_j\le\operatorname{min}^\eps_b(h)\le\min_j\big(h_j+\eps\log\frac1{b_j}\big)$, the first because $\sum_jb_je^{-h_j/\eps}\le e^{-\min h/\eps}$, the second by keeping one term of the sum. As $\eps\to0$ the soft transform tends to the hard $c$-transform $f_i=\min_j(C_{ij}-g_j)$ of Lesson 4 §3, whose alternation stalls after one cycle. The soft transforms do not stall: the soft minimum mixes all entries, and the iteration converges to the unique maximizer. Slide 37 and OT4ML §9.2 (pp. 176–178 (168–170)) give the order-theoretic reason. The full cycle $f\mapsto\big(f^{c,\eps}\big)^{\bar c,\eps}$ is order-preserving and commutes with adding constants, so it does not expand the oscillation $\max f-\min f$ of differences of potentials; this gives convergence on general compact spaces (Fortet’s argument, OT4ML Prop. 9.11).

The log domain

With scalings, Sinkhorn manipulates $K_{ij}=e^{-C_{ij}/\eps}$ and $u_i=a_ie^{f_i/\eps}$. In double precision $e^{-x}$ underflows to 0 for $x>745$ (in single precision, as on most GPUs, for $x>103$): when $\eps$ is small, whole rows of $K$ vanish, $Kv$ has zero entries and $a/(Kv)$ is infinite (CompOT Rem. 4.7, p. 70 (66)). The fix is to iterate on $f,g$ and to compute the soft minimum with the log-sum-exp trick, $$\operatorname{min}^\eps_b(h)=\underline h-\eps\log\sum_jb_je^{-(h_j-\underline h)/\eps},$$ with $\underline h=\min_jh_j$, so that the largest exponential is $b_j\le1$ and nothing underflows that matters (OT4ML Alg. 8.2, p. 152 (144); CompOT Rem. 4.23, p. 83 (79); slides 32–33). Each iteration now costs an exponential per entry instead of a multiplication, a constant factor that buys stability at any $\eps$. Two practical additions are common. One is $\eps$-scaling: solve for a decreasing sequence of temperatures, each run started from the potentials of the previous one (Lab 1 does this). The other is to recover the plan only at the end, as $P_{ij}=a_ib_je^{(f_i+g_j-C_{ij})/\eps}$. Lab 2 measures where the plain iteration breaks.

General measures

Everything above holds for arbitrary probability measures, with sums replaced by integrals.

Proposition (continuous entropic duality)OT4ML Prop. 8.20, p. 152 (144) and Def. 8.21, p. 153 (145); compact Prop. 8.18, p. 38; StatOT Prop. 4.3, p. 120 (114); CompOT Rem. 4.24, p. 83 (79)

For probability measures $\al,\be$ and a continuous cost $c$ on compact spaces, $$\begin{aligned}L^\eps(\al,\be)=\sup_{f,g}\ &\int f\dd\al+\int g\dd\be\\&-\eps\iint\Big(e^{(f(x)+g(y)-c(x,y))/\eps}-1\Big)\dd\al(x)\dd\be(y),\end{aligned}$$ the supremum is attained by potentials unique up to $(f+\lambda,g-\lambda)$ (OT4ML Prop. 8.22), and the optimal plan is $\dd\pi_\eps=e^{(f\oplus g-c)/\eps}\dd(\al\otimes\be)$. Optimal potentials are soft $c$-transforms of each other: $g(y)=-\eps\log\int e^{(f(x)-c(x,y))/\eps}\dd\al(x)$, and symmetrically (the Schrödinger system, StatOT (4.7), (4.10), pp. 121–122 (115–116)).

The soft transform defines $g$ at every $y$, not only on the support of $\be$, and it is as smooth in $y$ as the cost: for smooth costs on compact sets the potentials are $C^\infty$, with derivatives of order $s$ bounded by $C_s\eps^{1-s}$ (StatOT Ex. 4.6-7(a), p. 138 (132)). This regularity is what makes entropic quantities estimable from samples at the parametric rate (§8), and it lets one evaluate potentials and barycentric maps at new points. For $c=\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$, soft transforms preserve the structure of Lesson 4: $\frac12\norm x^2-f$ is convex and its gradient is the barycentric projection (OT4ML Rem. 8.23, p. 154 (146); StatOT Ex. 4.6-3, §12).

§5How fast Sinkhorn converges

The problem

Alternating projections converge (§3), but how fast, and how does the speed depend on $\eps$? Three answers are available, and they differ by orders of magnitude. A global linear rate in Hilbert’s projective metric holds for every problem but is exponentially slow in $1/\eps$. A sublinear rate holds uniformly in the conditioning of the kernel. A local linear rate, given by a correlation coefficient of the optimal plan, is what one observes.

Hilbert’s projective metric and Birkhoff’s contraction

Definition (Hilbert metric)OT4ML Def. 9.15 and Prop. 9.16, p. 180 (172); compact Def. 9.13, p. 49; CompOT Rem. 4.12, p. 73 (69); slide 35

For vectors $u,u'$ with positive entries, $d_H(u,u')=\log\max_i\frac{u_i}{u'_i}-\log\min_i\frac{u_i}{u'_i}=\norm{\log u-\log u'}_V$, where $\norm h_V=\max h-\min h$. It is a distance between rays: $d_H(u,u')=0$ iff $u'=\lambda u$.

It ignores global scalings, which is exactly the indeterminacy $(\lambda u,v/\lambda)$ of Sinkhorn. It is also invariant under the two nonlinear steps of the algorithm: $d_H(a/x,a/x')=d_H(x,x')$ for any fixed positive vector $a$. Only the linear steps $v\mapsto Kv$ can contract it, and they do.

Theorem (Birkhoff’s contraction theorem)OT4ML Thm. 9.17, pp. 180–181 (172–173); compact Thm. 9.15, p. 49; CompOT Thm. 4.1, p. 74 (70); slide 36

Let $K$ have positive entries and $\eta(K)=\max_{i,j,k,l}\frac{K_{ik}K_{jl}}{K_{jk}K_{il}}$. Then for all positive $v,v'$, $$\begin{gathered}d_H(Kv,Kv')\le\lambda(K)\,d_H(v,v'),\\\lambda(K)=\frac{\sqrt{\eta(K)}-1}{\sqrt{\eta(K)}+1}=\tanh\Big(\frac{\log\eta(K)}4\Big)<1.\end{gathered}$$

The image of the positive cone under $K$ has Hilbert diameter $\log\eta(K)$, and a positive linear map of the cone into a set of diameter $\Delta$ contracts by $\tanh(\Delta/4)$; OT4ML proves it through the Dobrushin coefficient. Given the theorem, the rate of Sinkhorn is two lines.

Theorem (linear convergence in the Hilbert metric (Franklin–Lorenz))OT4ML Thm. 9.18, p. 182 (174); compact Thm. 9.16, p. 49; CompOT Thm. 4.2, p. 75 (71)

With $v^\star$ the scaling of the solution, $d_H(v^{(\ell)},v^\star)\le\lambda(K)^{2\ell}\,d_H(v^{(0)},v^\star)$.

Proof (invariance under division, contraction under $K$ and $K^\top$)

The fixed point satisfies $u^\star=a/(Kv^\star)$ and $v^\star=b/(K^\top u^\star)$. Using the invariance of $d_H$ under division and Birkhoff for $K^\top$ (note $\eta(K^\top)=\eta(K)$) and for $K$: $$\begin{aligned}d_H(v^{(\ell+1)},v^\star)&=d_H(K^\top u^{(\ell+1)},K^\top u^\star)\le\lambda\,d_H(u^{(\ell+1)},u^\star)\\&=\lambda\,d_H(Kv^{(\ell)},Kv^\star)\le\lambda^2d_H(v^{(\ell)},v^\star).\ \square\end{aligned}$$

For the Gibbs kernel, $\log\eta(K)=\Delta/\eps$ with $\Delta=\max_{i,j,k,l}(C_{jk}+C_{il}-C_{ik}-C_{jl})\le2(\max C-\min C)$, so $\lambda=\tanh(\Delta/(4\eps))$, and the guaranteed gain per cycle is $$1-\lambda^2=\frac1{\cosh^2(\Delta/(4\eps))}\approx4e^{-\Delta/(2\eps)}.$$ The convergence is linear, but the bound needs about $e^{\Delta/(2\eps)}/4$ cycles per factor $e$ of accuracy: hopeless as soon as $\eps$ is small compared with the spread of the cost. (OT4ML writes the bound as $\lambda\le\tanh(R/(2\eps))$ with $R=\max C-\min C$, which is the same estimate after $\Delta\le2R$.)

A rate that does not see the conditioning

Proposition (robust sublinear rate)OT4ML Prop. 9.13 and Cor. 9.14, pp. 178–180 (170–172); compact Prop. 9.11 and Cor. 9.12, p. 48

For $C\ge0$ and $g^{(0)}=0$, the dual gap after $\ell$ cycles satisfies $0\le\Delta^{(\ell)}\le2\norm C_\infty^2/(\eps\ell)$. Consequently, with $\eps=\delta/\log(nm)$ and $\ell\ge2\norm C_\infty^2\log(nm)/\delta^2$ cycles, the dual value approximates the unregularized $L_C(a,b)$ within $\delta$.

This $O(1/\ell)$ rate is polynomial in $1/\eps$ and holds for every kernel; it is the basis of the complexity estimate of §2. The proof bounds the oscillation of the potentials by $\norm C_\infty$, shows that a row update increases the dual by $\eps\KL(a\mid P\mathbb 1)$, and uses Pinsker’s inequality.

The rate one observes

Near the solution, linearize one cycle in the potentials. At the fixed point, differentiating the soft transforms gives $\delta g_j=-\sum_i\frac{P_{ij}}{b_j}\delta f_i$ and $\delta f_i=-\sum_j\frac{P_{ij}}{a_i}\delta g_j$: each half-step applies minus a conditional expectation under the plan. A full cycle multiplies $\delta f$ by the matrix $M=\diag(a)^{-1}P\diag(b)^{-1}P^\top$, which is similar to $AA^\top$ with $$A=\diag(a)^{-1/2}\,P_\eps\,\diag(b)^{-1/2}.$$ The largest singular value of $A$ is 1, with singular vectors $\sqrt a$, $\sqrt b$; it corresponds to adding a constant to $f$, which changes nothing. On the other directions the error is multiplied, asymptotically, by $\sigma_2(A)^2$ per cycle.

Proposition (local rate of Sinkhorn)OT4ML Def. 9.19 and Prop. 9.20, pp. 184–186 (176–178); Prop. 9.21, p. 186 (178) (§9.5, beyond the course)

The local linear rate of a Sinkhorn cycle is $\sigma_\eps^2$, where $\sigma_\eps=\sigma_2(A)$ is the maximal correlation of the optimal plan, $\sigma_\eps=\sup\{\operatorname{Corr}(\phi(X),\psi(Y)):(X,Y)\sim P_\eps\}$. It is bounded by the global factor: $\sigma_\eps\le\lambda(K)$.

The maximal correlation explains the slowdown. As $\eps\to0$ the plan concentrates on the graph of a map, $Y$ becomes a function of $X$, and the correlation tends to 1. For smooth problems with a Monge solution the conditional laws have width of order $\sqrt\eps$ (OT4ML p. 186 (178)). By the variance form of the maximal correlation, $1-\sigma_\eps^2=\inf_k\int\Var(k(Y)\mid X)\dd\al$ over normalized centered $k$ (OT4ML (9.19)), the gain per cycle is then at most of order $\eps$: the number of iterations grows at least like $1/\eps$, and Labs 1 and 2 measure a growth close to $1/\eps$, not like $e^{\Delta/(2\eps)}$. For one-dimensional Gaussians this is exact: $O(\eps^{-1}\log(1/\delta))$ cycles suffice (OT4ML Prop. 9.28, p. 191 (183)). Lab 2 measures $\sigma_\eps^2$ and the observed rate on the clouds of 3-sinkhorn-advanced.

Key idea: Sinkhorn is slow exactly when the plan is nearly a map

The local rate is the squared maximal correlation of the plan. It tends to 1 when the plan approaches a deterministic coupling, which is what one asks for when $\eps\to0$. The iteration count then grows like $1/\eps$, which is what makes small temperatures expensive; the Hilbert-metric bound is exponentially more pessimistic and only matters as a proof that the iteration converges.

Beyond the course: acceleration

Over-relaxation, $u\leftarrow u^{1-\omega}(a/Kv)^\omega$, with the optimal $\omega^\star=2/(1+\sqrt{1-\sigma_\eps^2})$, improves the local factor from $\sigma_\eps^2$ to $\omega^\star-1$, of order $1-2\sqrt{1-\sigma_\eps^2}$ (OT4ML Prop. 9.22, p. 187 (179), Fig. 9.3; figure notebook sinkhorn-overrelaxation). Quasi-Newton methods on the semi-dual are another option (OT4ML Rem. 9.23, Prop. 9.24).

§6Entropic transport between general measures

The general formulation

Definition (entropic transport between measures)OT4ML Defs. 8.11–8.12, p. 145 (137); compact Defs. 8.9–8.10, p. 36; CompOT Rem. 4.2, (4.9), p. 65 (61); StatOT (4.3), p. 116 (110); slide 7

For probability measures $\al$ on $\X$, $\be$ on $\Y$, a cost $c$ and $\eps>0$, $$\begin{gathered}L^\eps(\al,\be)=\inf_{\pi\in\Couplings(\al,\be)}\int c\dd\pi+\eps\KL(\pi\mid\al\otimes\be),\\\KL(\pi\mid\xi)=\int\log\frac{\dd\pi}{\dd\xi}\dd\pi-\pi(\X\times\Y)+\xi(\X\times\Y)\end{gathered}$$ if $\pi$ has a density with respect to $\xi$, and $+\infty$ otherwise. For $\al=\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i}$, $\be=\sum_jb_j\delta_{y_j}$ it is the problem of §1 with $C_{ij}=c(x_i,y_j)$.

Finite entropy forces the plan to have a density with respect to $\al\otimes\be$: an entropic plan never concentrates on a graph, whatever $\eps>0$. The reference $\al\otimes\be$ can be replaced by another product $\eta\otimes\zeta$ (Lebesgue measure, say): the minimizer does not change, and the value changes by $\eps\KL(\al\mid\eta)+\eps\KL(\be\mid\zeta)$ (OT4ML Rem. 8.38, p. 167 (159); CompOT Prop. 4.2, p. 65 (61)). The reference is irrelevant for the plan and matters for the value: with $\al\otimes\be$, $L^\eps\ge L_c$ always, and §7 shows that the correction terms cancel in the Sinkhorn divergence. The duality of §4 holds as stated there, and Sinkhorn becomes the alternation of two integral operators, $u=1/\mathcal K(v)$ and $v=1/\mathcal K^*(u)$ with $\mathcal K(v)(x)=\int e^{-c(x,y)/\eps}v(y)\dd\be(y)$ (OT4ML (8.15)–(8.17), p. 146 (138)).

A closed form: adding Gaussian noise

Proposition (Gibbs coupling)OT4ML Prop. 8.14, pp. 146–147 (138–139); compact p. 36

Let $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2$ on $\R^d$, $\al$ any probability measure, and $\be_\eps=\al*\Gauss(0,\frac\eps2\Id)$. The entropic plan between $\al$ and $\be_\eps$ is the law of $(X,X+\sqrt{\eps/2}\,G)$ with $X\sim\al$ and $G\sim\Gauss(0,\Id)$ independent.

Proof (the plan is of scaling form and has the right marginals)

The law $\pi$ of $(X,X+\sqrt{\eps/2}G)$ has marginals $\al$ and $\be_\eps$ by construction. Its density with respect to $\al\otimes\Leb$ is $(\pi\eps)^{-d/2}e^{-\norm{x-y}^2/\eps}$, so with respect to $\al\otimes\be_\eps$ it is $e^{-\norm{x-y}^2/\eps}\cdot(\pi\eps)^{-d/2}/\rho_\eps(y)$, where $\rho_\eps$ is the density of $\be_\eps$. This is $u(x)e^{-c(x,y)/\eps}v(y)$ with $u=1$ and $v=(\pi\eps)^{-d/2}/\rho_\eps$. The sufficiency argument of §2 (the identity $\text{objective}(Q)=\eps\KL(Q\mid\pi)+\text{const}$ for couplings $Q$) works verbatim for measures, so $\pi$ is optimal. $\square$

So when the target is the source blurred by the kernel itself, entropic transport is “add noise”, the first step of a diffusion model (Lesson 6 §6). In general the conditional laws $Y\mid X=x$ of an entropic plan have a spread of order $\sqrt{\eps/2}$, stretched by the local slope of the transport map; the Gaussian case below makes this exact.

The two limits

Proposition (convergence with $\eps$ for measures)OT4ML Prop. 8.15, pp. 147–148 (139–140); compact Prop. 8.13, p. 37; CompOT Rem. 4.4, p. 66 (62)

On compact spaces with a continuous cost, $L^\eps(\al,\be)\to L_c(\al,\be)$ as $\eps\to0$, and cluster points of $\pi_\eps$ are optimal plans; for the quadratic cost and $\al$ with a density, $\pi_\eps$ converges weakly to $(\mathrm{Id},T)_\sharp\al$, with $T$ the Brenier map. As $\eps\to\infty$, $\pi_\eps\to\al\otimes\be$ in total variation and $L^\eps(\al,\be)\to\iint c\dd\al\dd\be$.

The first-order corrections are explicit. At large temperature, $L^\eps=\bar c-\frac1{2\eps}\iint c_0^2\dd\al\dd\be+O(\eps^{-2})$, where $\bar c=\iint c\dd\al\dd\be$ and $c_0$ is the doubly centered cost (OT4ML Prop. 8.16, p. 148 (140)). At small temperature the correction depends on the dimension and on the entropies of the two measures.

Proposition (small-temperature expansion)OT4ML Prop. 8.17, p. 149 (141); compact Prop. 8.15, p. 37; Conforti–Tamanini 2021, Chizat et al. 2020

For $c=\norm{x-y}^2$ and compactly supported bounded densities on $\R^d$ with finite $\mathcal I_{\rm geo}$, $$\begin{aligned}L^\eps(\al,\be)=W_2^2(\al,\be)&-\frac{d\eps}2\log(\pi\eps)-\frac\eps2\big(H(\al)+H(\be)\big)\\&+\frac{\eps^2}{16}\,\mathcal I_{\rm geo}(\al,\be)+o(\eps^2),\end{aligned}$$ where $H(\al)=\int\rho_\al\log\rho_\al$ (minus the differential entropy) and $\mathcal I_{\rm geo}=\int_0^1\!\!\int\norm{\nabla\log\rho_t}^2\rho_t\dd x\dd t$ is the Fisher information integrated along McCann’s interpolation $\rho_t$ between $\al$ and $\be$.

The leading correction is $\frac d2\eps\log(1/\eps)$, larger than $\eps$ for small $\eps$, and it grows with the dimension: this is the bias Lab 1 measured on histograms. The next terms depend on $\al$ and $\be$ separately through their entropies, which is why subtracting $\frac12L^\eps(\al,\al)+\frac12L^\eps(\be,\be)$ removes them (§7). Note the sign convention: here $H$ is $\int\rho\log\rho$, the opposite of the Shannon entropy $\mathrm H(P)=-\sum P\log P$ used for discrete plans in OT4ML Def. 8.1.

Compared with Prop. 8.17, the logarithmic term has the opposite sign and a different argument, the entropy term has coefficient $\eps$ instead of $\frac\eps2$ (whichever sign convention is used for $H$), and the second-order term of the divergence is 16 times too large. Gaussians

As for unregularized transport (Lesson 1 §8), Gaussians give closed forms. The reason is the same: the cost only sees second moments, and among couplings with given second moments the Gaussian one has the most entropy.

Proposition (entropic transport between Gaussians on the line)OT4ML Ex. 8.45, pp. 170–171 (162–163) and Prop. 9.26, p. 190 (182); compact Prop. 9.23, p. 52; sheet Ex. 4; StatOT Ex. 4.6-4, p. 137 (131); Janati et al. 2020

For $\al=\Gauss(m_\al,s_\al^2)$, $\be=\Gauss(m_\be,s_\be^2)$ and $c=(x-y)^2$, the entropic plan is Gaussian with cross-covariance $$k_\eps=\frac{\sqrt{\eps^2+16s_\al^2s_\be^2}-\eps}4,$$ and $L^\eps(\al,\be)=(m_\al-m_\be)^2+s_\al^2+s_\be^2-2k_\eps-\frac\eps2\log\Big(1-\frac{k_\eps^2}{s_\al^2s_\be^2}\Big)$.

Proof (maximum entropy, then a one-variable minimization)

Let $\pi$ be any coupling with finite entropy and cross-covariance $k$, and $\pi_G$ the Gaussian coupling with the same means and covariance matrix. The cost $\int(x-y)^2\dd\pi=(m_\al-m_\be)^2+s_\al^2+s_\be^2-2k$ depends only on $k$, so it is the same for both. The mutual information is $h(X)+h(Y)-h(X,Y)$ in terms of differential entropies; the marginals are fixed, and the Gaussian has the largest entropy for a given covariance, so $\pi_G$ has the smallest mutual information. Hence the optimum is a Gaussian coupling, whose mutual information is $-\frac12\log(1-\rho^2)$ with $\rho=k/(s_\al s_\be)$. It remains to minimize $$F(k)=-2k-\frac\eps2\log\Big(1-\frac{k^2}{S}\Big),\qquad S=s_\al^2s_\be^2,\quad|k|<\sqrt S.$$ $F'(k)=-2+\eps k/(S-k^2)=0$ gives $2k^2+\eps k-2S=0$, whose root in $(0,\sqrt S)$ is $k_\eps$. $\square$

Three readings. As $\eps\to0$, $k_\eps\to s_\al s_\be$ and $L^\eps\to(m_\al-m_\be)^2+(s_\al-s_\be)^2=W_2^2$, the one-dimensional Bures formula. The conditional variance is $\Var(Y\mid X)=s_\be^2-k_\eps^2/s_\al^2=\frac\eps2\frac{s_\be}{s_\al}+O(\eps^2)$: a blur of width $\sqrt{\eps/2}$ stretched by the slope $s_\be/s_\al$ of the Brenier map. And the barycentric projection $x\mapsto m_\be+\frac{k_\eps}{s_\al^2}(x-m_\al)$ has slope $\rho_\eps\,s_\be/s_\al$ with $\rho_\eps=k_\eps/(s_\al s_\be)<1$: it contracts, as the Watch out of §2 said. In dimension $d$ the plan is Gaussian with a cross-covariance built from the singular values $\sigma_i$ of $\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be^{1/2}$, each shrunk by the same one-dimensional formula, $s_i=(\sqrt{\eps^2+16\sigma_i^2}-\eps)/(4\sigma_i)$ (OT4ML Prop. 9.26).

§7The entropic bias, and Sinkhorn divergences

The problem

As a loss between measures, $L^\eps$ has a defect: $L^\eps(\al,\al)>0$, and $\be\mapsto L^\eps(\al,\be)$ is not minimized at $\be=\al$. Fitting a model by minimizing $L^\eps$ to the data therefore aims at the wrong target. At large temperature the target degenerates completely.

Proposition (large-temperature collapse)OT4ML Prop. 8.35 and Ex. 8.36, p. 166 (158)

On compact spaces with a continuous cost, $L^\eps(\al,\be)\to\iint c\dd\al\dd\be$ as $\eps\to\infty$. Minimizers $\be_\eps$ of $L^\eps(\al,\cdot)$ concentrate on the minimizers of $y\mapsto\int c(x,y)\dd\al(x)$; for the quadratic cost, on the mean of $\al$.

For the quadratic cost, $\iint\norm{x-y}^2\dd\al\dd\be=\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+\Var\al+\Var\be$, minimized over $\be$ by the Dirac mass at $m_\al$. The defect is present at small temperature too. For $\al=\Gauss(0,s_\al^2)$ and $\be=\Gauss(0,s^2)$, the envelope theorem applied to the closed form of §6 gives $\partial_sL^\eps=2(s_\al^2-k_\eps)/s_\al>0$ at $s=s_\al$: shrinking $\be$ below the width of $\al$ lowers the loss. The minimizer of the raw loss is a contracted copy of the data.

The Sinkhorn divergence

Definition (Sinkhorn divergence)OT4ML Def. 8.37, p. 166 (158); compact Def. 8.31, p. 44; slides 47–50; notebook 3-sinkhorn-advanced, cell 35; CompOT §8.5, p. 135 (131); StatOT §4.5, p. 136 (130)

$$S_\eps(\al,\be)=L^\eps(\al,\be)-\tfrac12L^\eps(\al,\al)-\tfrac12L^\eps(\be,\be).$$

By construction $S_\eps(\al,\al)=0$. The reference measure drops out: changing $\al\otimes\be$ into $\eta\otimes\zeta$ adds $\eps\KL(\al\mid\eta)+\eps\KL(\be\mid\zeta)$ to the first term, and half of each to the other two (OT4ML Rem. 8.38). By Lemma 8.39 each term is a sum of potentials, $L^\eps(\al,\be)=\int f_{\al\be}\dd\al+\int g_{\al\be}\dd\be$, so computing $S_\eps$ costs three Sinkhorn runs, two of them symmetric.

Watch out: the self terms converge slowly

When $\eps$ is small against the gaps between points, the plan from a cloud to itself is close to the identity permutation, so its local rate (§5) is close to $1$. On the first sample of Lab 5 (16 points of $\mathcal N(0,\mathrm{Id}_3)$, $\eps=0.2$), the self plan has $\sigma_2^2=1-{}$3.8×10⁻⁸, so 20000 alternating cycles shrink the slow mode only by the factor 0.9992 and still leave a marginal error of 7.6×10⁻⁷. The averaged update $f\leftarrow\frac12\big(f+T_\eps f\big)$, with $T_\eps$ the soft $c$-transform of §4, keeps the two potentials equal and reaches an error below 10⁻¹³ in 34 steps (CompOT Rem. 4.5, PDF p. 69 (65): "averaged projection iterations to maintain symmetry"). The two values differ by 3.4×10⁻⁸ (relative), so the slow mode carries almost no value; but a stopping rule on the marginal error would never fire.

Proposition (limits of Sinkhorn divergences)OT4ML Prop. 8.40 and Rem. 8.41, p. 168 (160); compact Prop. 8.33, p. 44; CompOT Prop. 8.3, p. 135 (131)

On compact spaces with a continuous cost with $c(x,x)=0$: $S_\eps(\al,\be)\to L_c(\al,\be)$ as $\eps\to0$, and $S_\eps(\al,\be)\to-\frac12\iint c\,\dd(\al-\be)\dd(\al-\be)$ as $\eps\to\infty$.

For $c=\norm{x-y}^2$ the large-$\eps$ limit is computed by expanding $\norm{x-y}^2=\norm x^2+\norm y^2-2\dotp xy$: the first two terms integrate to 0 against $\al-\be$, which has total mass 0, and the last gives $\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2$. At large temperature the Sinkhorn divergence with the quadratic cost only compares means, which Lab 3 measured. For $c=\norm{x-y}^p$ with $0<p<2$ the limit is an energy distance, a kernel norm (MMD) of Lesson 4 §8: Sinkhorn divergences interpolate between optimal transport and MMD. In between, they are true divergences.

Proposition (positivity and metrization)OT4ML Prop. 8.42, pp. 168–169 (160–161); compact Prop. 8.34, p. 45; Feydy et al. 2019

If $e^{-c/\eps}$ is a positive definite kernel, then $S_\eps\ge0$. If it is moreover universal (on a compact space, with $c$ continuous), then $S_\eps(\al,\be)=0$ iff $\al=\be$, and $S_\eps(\al_n,\al)\to0$ iff $\al_n$ converges weakly to $\al$.

The Gaussian kernel $e^{-\norm{x-y}^2/\eps}$ is positive definite and universal, so for the quadratic cost $S_\eps$ is a genuine divergence that metrizes weak convergence (Lesson 3 §4). The small-$\eps$ expansion of §6 shows how much bias is left: the terms in $\eps\log\eps$ and $\eps H$ cancel, and $$\begin{aligned}S_\eps(\al,\be)=W_2^2(\al,\be)&+\frac{\eps^2}{16}\Big(\mathcal I_{\rm geo}(\al,\be)-\frac{\mathcal I_{\rm geo}(\al,\al)+\mathcal I_{\rm geo}(\be,\be)}2\Big)\\&+o(\eps^2).\end{aligned}$$ For Gaussians on the line the bracket is $-\frac12(1/s_\al-1/s_\be)^2$ (OT4ML Cor. 9.27, pp. 190–191 (182–183), gives the Gaussian divergence in every dimension). For a translation $\be=\al(\cdot-h)$ of any measure the debiasing is exact at every $\eps$: translating the second marginal of a coupling of $(\al,\al)$ adds $\norm h^2$ to its cost (the cross term vanishes because both marginals have the same mean) and leaves its relative entropy unchanged, so $L^\eps(\al,\be)=L^\eps(\al,\al)+\norm h^2$ and $S_\eps(\al,\be)=\norm h^2=W_2^2(\al,\be)$. Chizat et al. (2020) use this second-order accuracy to estimate $W_2^2$ by $S_\eps$ with a larger $\eps$ than $L^\eps$ would allow.

What the bias does to a fitted measure

Let $\al_x=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$ be a cloud of particles that one moves to minimize a loss against a fixed target $\be$. For $c=\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$, the gradient of $L^\eps(\al_x,\be)$ in $x_i$ is $\frac1n(x_i-\bar y_i)$, with $\bar y_i$ the barycentric projection of $x_i$ through the plan to $\be$ (§9 derives it). The self term $\frac12L^\eps(\al_x,\al_x)$ moves both of its arguments, and its gradient is $\frac1n(x_i-\bar x_i)$, with $\bar x_i$ the barycentric projection through the plan from the cloud to itself. A gradient step rescaled by $n$ is therefore $$\begin{aligned}\text{raw: }&x_i\leftarrow x_i-\tau(x_i-\bar y_i),\\\text{debiased: }&x_i\leftarrow x_i-\tau(\bar x_i-\bar y_i).\end{aligned}$$ The raw step pulls each particle to a blurred average of the target; at large $\eps$ that average is the mean of $\be$ for every particle. The debiased step compares two blurred averages, and the blur cancels.

Key idea: subtract the self-transport

The raw entropic loss compares a measure with a blurred version of the other, and is minimized by a contracted measure. The Sinkhorn divergence subtracts what each measure pays to be transported to itself. The terms of order $\eps\log\eps$ and $\eps H$ cancel, positivity and definiteness hold for the quadratic cost, and the loss interpolates between $W_2^2$ (small $\eps$) and a kernel norm (large $\eps$).

§8Statistics: transport estimated from samples

The problem

In machine learning the measures are known through samples: $\hat\al_n=\frac1n\sum_{i\le n}\delta_{X_i}$ with $X_i$ independent draws from $\al$, and similarly $\hat\be_n$. A loss is computed between the empirical measures, and one needs to know how far $D(\hat\al_n,\hat\be_n)$ is from $D(\al,\be)$. For exact transport the answer degrades with the dimension; for kernel norms (MMD) it does not; entropic transport at fixed $\eps$ sides with the kernel norms, at a price in the constants. The three are compared on the same experiment in Lab 5.

Exact transport: the curse of dimension

Proposition (empirical rates for $W_p$)OT4ML Prop. 10.5, p. 198 (190) and Prop. 10.8, p. 200 (192); compact Prop. 10.5, p. 56; CompOT §8.4, pp. 132–133 (128–129); slides 52, 54

On a compact set of $\R^d$, $\sup_\al\E\big[W_p(\hat\al_n,\al)^p\big]^{1/p}\le C\,r_{n,p,d}$ with $r_{n,p,d}=n^{-1/(2p)}$ if $d<2p$, $n^{-1/(2p)}(\log(1+n))^{1/p}$ if $d=2p$, and $n^{-1/d}$ if $d>2p$. For $d\ge3$ no estimator does better uniformly over bounded densities: the minimax rate is of order $n^{-1/d}$.

To halve the error in dimension 10 one needs about $2^{10}$ times more samples. The dimension that matters is the intrinsic dimension of the supports (OT4ML Rem. 10.6), which is why transport still works on data that lie near low-dimensional sets. Lesson 5 §1 measured the curse and its absence for sliced distances. For a kernel norm the rate is parametric in every dimension:

Proposition (MMD has a parametric rate)OT4ML Prop. 10.11, p. 203 (195); compact Prop. 10.9, p. 57; CompOT §8.4, p. 134 (130)

For a kernel bounded by $\kappa^2$, $\E\big|\mathrm{MMD}_k(\hat\al_n,\hat\be_m)-\mathrm{MMD}_k(\al,\be)\big|\le\kappa\,(n^{-1/2}+m^{-1/2})$.

Entropic transport at fixed temperature

Proposition (Sinkhorn divergences have a parametric rate)OT4ML Prop. 10.12, pp. 203–204 (195–196); compact Prop. 10.10, p. 58; Genevay et al. 2019; Mena and Niles-Weed 2019; slides 53–54

For $\sigma^2$-subgaussian $\al,\be$ on $\R^d$ and $c=\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$, $$\begin{aligned}&\E\big|S_\eps(\hat\al_n,\hat\be_m)-S_\eps(\al,\be)\big|\\&\qquad\le C_d\,\eps\Big[1+\Big(\frac\sigma{\sqrt\eps}\Big)^{q_d}\Big]\big(n^{-1/2}+m^{-1/2}\big),\end{aligned}$$ with $q_d=\lceil5d/2\rceil+6$.

The dimension has moved from the exponent of $n$ to the constant, which grows polynomially as $\eps\to0$. The mechanism is the regularity of §4: the optimal potentials are soft $c$-transforms, smooth with derivatives of order $s$ bounded by $C_s\eps^{1-s}$, and a class of uniformly smooth functions is small enough for empirical averages to converge at the rate $n^{-1/2}$ (StatOT Ex. 4.6-7 runs this argument for one sample). StatOT takes another route, strong concavity of the dual, and obtains rates free of the dimension but with constants exponential in $1/\eps$: for supports in a ball of diameter 1, the entropic value satisfies $0\le\E L^\eps(\hat\mu_n,\hat\nu_n)-L^\eps(\mu,\nu)\le2\eps e^{7/\eps}/n$ and $\Var L^\eps(\hat\mu_n,\hat\nu_n)\le8\eps^2e^{4/\eps}/n$ (StatOT Thm. 4.9, pp. 129–131 (123–125)).

Entropy does not beat the curse for the unregularized cost. To estimate $L_c$ itself with $L^\eps$ or $S_\eps$, the temperature must go to zero with $n$, and the regularization bias must be balanced against a fluctuation of order $\eps^{-p_d}n^{-1/2}$: the best choice $\eps\sim n^{-1/(2(p_d+1))}$ gives no better rate than exact transport in the worst case (OT4ML Rem. 10.13, p. 205 (197), “no free lunch”). What entropy offers is a different, smoother object, estimable at the parametric rate. Slide 57 lists the three errors that add up in practice: sampling, regularization and optimization (a finite number of Sinkhorn iterations).

On finite spaces the fluctuations are Gaussian: $\sqrt n\big(L^\eps(\hat\al_n,\be)-L^\eps(\al,\be)\big)$ converges to $\Gauss(0,\Var_\al(f^\star))$, with $f^\star$ the optimal potential, and the bias is $O(1/n)$ for $\eps>0$ (OT4ML Prop. 10.18, p. 208 (200)). For two samples of the same measure the first-order term vanishes ($S_\eps$ is minimal at $\be=\al$), and $S_\eps(\hat\al_n,\hat\al'_n)$ is of order $1/n$, like $\mathrm{MMD}^2$: this null case is what Fig. 10.2 shows and Lab 5 measures.

§9Learning with Sinkhorn losses

The problem

To fit a model $\al_\theta$ to data $\be$ by gradient descent on $L^\eps(\al_\theta,\be)$ or $S_\eps(\al_\theta,\be)$, one needs the gradient of the loss with respect to the positions and weights of $\al_\theta$, and then with respect to $\theta$. One could differentiate through the Sinkhorn iterations; the envelope theorem says that this is unnecessary at convergence.

The envelope theorem gives the gradients

Proposition (envelope theorem)sheet Ex. 5; CompOT Prop. 4.6, p. 85 (81)

Let $V(\theta)=\min_zF(z,\theta)$ with $F$ smooth and a unique minimizer $z^\star(\theta)$ depending smoothly on $\theta$. Then $\nabla V(\theta)=\nabla_\theta F(z^\star(\theta),\theta)$.

Proof in one line: $\nabla V=\nabla_\theta F+(\mathrm Dz^\star)^\top\nabla_zF$, and $\nabla_zF(z^\star,\theta)=0$ at an unconstrained minimizer (for the constrained problem here, the constraints do not depend on $C$, and the same argument applies to the Lagrangian). Applied to the primal and the dual of entropic transport (sheet Ex. 5):

  • $\nabla_CL^\eps(a,b)=P_\eps$: the optimal plan is the sensitivity of the value to the cost;
  • $\nabla_aL^\eps(a,b)=f$, up to a constant: the optimal potential is the sensitivity to the weights (from the dual, §4);
  • for $C_{ij}=\frac12\norm{x_i-y_j}^2$, by the chain rule, $$\begin{aligned}\nabla_{x_i}L^\eps&=\sum_jP_{ij}(x_i-y_j)=a_ix_i-\sum_jP_{ij}y_j\\&=a_i\big(x_i-T_\eps(x_i)\big),\end{aligned}$$ so the barycentric projection is a gradient step with step $1/a_i$: $T_\eps(x_i)=x_i-\frac1{a_i}\nabla_{x_i}L^\eps$ (sheet Ex. 11(2)). For $C_{ij}=\norm{x_i-y_j}^2$ the gradient doubles.

For the Sinkhorn divergence, add the gradient of $-\frac12L^\eps(\al_x,\al_x)$, whose two arguments both move: with the self-plan $Q$, it is $-\big(a_ix_i-\sum_kQ_{ik}x_k\big)$ for $c=\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$, which gives the debiased step of §7. With a model $x_i=g_\theta(z_i)$ the chain rule finishes the job: $\nabla_\theta=\sum_i\partial_\theta g_\theta(z_i)^\top\nabla_{x_i}$ (notebook 3, cell 37). Slides 60–62 describe the resulting training loop: sample a batch, build the cost, run Sinkhorn, backpropagate.

Watch out: what the envelope theorem needs

It needs the optimal plan. With a fixed, small number of iterations the formula is only approximate; automatic differentiation through the iterations gives the exact gradient of the truncated loss instead (CompOT Rem. 4.27). Mini-batch Sinkhorn losses are biased estimators of the full loss (the expectation over batches of $S_\eps$ is not $S_\eps$ of the full measures), which is one more reason to debias.

§10Unbalanced transport: relaxing the marginals

The problem

Balanced transport needs measures of equal mass, and it moves all the mass. An outlier, a mode present in one measure only, or a population that grows between two observations, must then be matched at whatever distance. Unbalanced transport replaces the marginal constraints by penalties: mass can be created or destroyed at a price.

Penalized marginals

Definition (unbalanced transport with KL penalties)OT4ML Def. 11.1, p. 217 (209); compact Def. 11.1, p. 63; slide 40; sheet Ex. 8; CompOT §10.2, (10.7), pp. 166–167 (162–163); notebook 5-unbalanced

For non-negative vectors $a$, $b$ (any masses) and $\tau>0$, $$\mathrm{UW}_\tau(a,b)=\min_{P\ge0}\ \dotp CP+\tau\KL(P\mathbb 1\mid a)+\tau\KL(P^\top\mathbb 1\mid b).$$

The penalty depends on $P$ only through its marginals $r=P\mathbb 1$ and $s=P^\top\mathbb 1$, so the problem is balanced transport between relaxed marginals chosen at best: $\mathrm{UW}_\tau(a,b)=\min_{r,s}L_C(r,s)+\tau\KL(r\mid a)+\tau\KL(s\mid b)$ over $r,s$ of equal mass (sheet Ex. 8(1); OT4ML Prop. 11.2). As $\tau\to\infty$ the penalties become constraints and one recovers $L_C(a,b)$ when the masses agree. As $\tau\to0$, transport becomes too expensive compared with creation and destruction: on a common grid with $C_{ii}=0$, $\mathrm{UW}_\tau(a,b)/\tau\to\sum_i(\sqrt{a_i}-\sqrt{b_i})^2$, the squared Hellinger distance (OT4ML Prop. 11.5, pp. 218–220 (210–212)), because the plan becomes diagonal and, cell by cell, $\min_p\big[\KL(p\mid a_i)+\KL(p\mid b_i)\big]=(\sqrt{a_i}-\sqrt{b_i})^2$, attained at $p=\sqrt{a_ib_i}$. Other divergences give other behaviors: the total variation penalty $\tau\norm{P\mathbb 1-a}_1$ gives partial transport, where each unit of mass is either moved at cost below $2\tau$ or destroyed (notebook 5; sheet Ex. 10).

The dual keeps the constraint $f_i+g_j\le C_{ij}$ of balanced transport and replaces the linear terms by concave ones: $$\begin{aligned}\mathrm{UW}_\tau(a,b)=\max_{f_i+g_j\le C_{ij}}\ &\tau\sum_ia_i\big(1-e^{-f_i/\tau}\big)\\&+\tau\sum_jb_j\big(1-e^{-g_j/\tau}\big),\end{aligned}$$ and since $\tau(1-e^{-f/\tau})\to f$ as $\tau\to\infty$, it tends to the Kantorovich dual (OT4ML Prop. 11.6, p. 221 (213); sheet Ex. 8(3)–(4), corrected in §11).

Entropic unbalanced transport and its Sinkhorn iteration

Proposition (generalized Sinkhorn for KL penalties)OT4ML §8.6, Prop. 8.26 and Alg. 8.3, pp. 155–157 (147–149); §11.1, pp. 224–225 (216–217); compact Prop. 8.22, p. 40; slide 42; sheet Ex. 9; notebook 5-unbalanced, cells 36–45

The minimizer of $\dotp CP+\eps\KL(P\mid a\otimes b)+\tau\KL(P\mathbb 1\mid a)+\tau\KL(P^\top\mathbb 1\mid b)$ is $P=\diag(u)\tilde K\diag(v)$ with the tilted kernel $\tilde K=(a\otimes b)\odot e^{-C/\eps}$, and the scalings are the limit of $$u\leftarrow\Big(\frac a{\tilde Kv}\Big)^{\frac\tau{\tau+\eps}},\quad v\leftarrow\Big(\frac b{\tilde K^\top u}\Big)^{\frac\tau{\tau+\eps}}.$$ In potentials, $f\leftarrow\frac\tau{\tau+\eps}\,g^{\bar c,\eps}$ and $g\leftarrow\frac\tau{\tau+\eps}\,f^{c,\eps}$: damped soft $c$-transforms.

The exponent $\frac\tau{\tau+\eps}$ comes from balancing the two terms that a row update trades off: the first-order condition in $f_i$ reads $e^{f_i/\eps}\sum_jb_je^{(g_j-C_{ij})/\eps}=e^{-f_i/\tau}$ (sheet Ex. 9(2) does this computation for the counting-measure entropy). As $\tau\to\infty$ the exponent tends to 1 and one recovers Sinkhorn. The damping also helps convergence: the KL penalties remove the indeterminacy $(f+\lambda,g-\lambda)$, and each half-step contracts the sup norm by the factor $\frac\tau{\tau+\eps}$ (OT4ML Prop. 11.12, pp. 225–226 (217–218)).

Watch out: the tilt matters once the marginals are free

Slide 42 writes the update with $K=e^{-C/\eps}$ (slide 13) for the objective of slide 40, which uses $\eps\KL(P\mid a\otimes b)$. In balanced transport the difference is harmless, since a diagonal rescaling of the kernel is absorbed by the scalings (§3). Here it is not. Writing $P=\diag(u)K\diag(v)=\diag(u/a)\tilde K\diag(v/b)$ in the slide’s update shows that its fixed point solves slide 40’s problem with $a$ and $b$ replaced by $a^{1-\eps/\tau}$ and $b^{1-\eps/\tau}$ in the two penalties; equivalently, it solves the problem of sheet Ex. 9, whose entropy is $\sum P(\log P-1)$. The distortion is small when $\eps\ll\tau$: on Lab 7’s histograms ($\eps/\tau=0.004$) the masses are 0.964 and 0.949, and slide 40’s objective is 0.0512 against 0.0511. It is large otherwise: on a $12\times15$ instance with $\eps=0.05$ and $\tau=0.3$, the slide’s fixed point has mass 1.476 against 1.020 and objective 0.1344 against the minimum 0.0746. (The equivalence with the modified penalties holds to below 10⁻¹⁴.)

§11Beyond this lesson, and what the resources get wrong

What the resources add that the course does not require

  • Heat kernels and convolutional Sinkhorn. For the squared Euclidean cost the Gibbs kernel is a heat kernel, $K_\eps=(\pi\eps)^{d/2}h_{\eps/4}$, so on grids and meshes $Kv$ can be replaced by a few steps of heat diffusion; Varadhan’s formula recovers geodesic distances from it (“geodesics in heat”). The soft Hopf–Lax operator is a Gaussian convolution followed by a logarithm, the Hopf–Cole transform (OT4ML §8.7, pp. 157–160 (149–152); CompOT Rems. 4.17–4.19, pp. 78–79 (74–75); Solomon et al. 2015).
  • Other regularizers: $\varphi$-divergences, Bregman divergences, quadratic regularization and sparse plans (OT4ML §8.8, §3 here).
  • Complex temperatures. The scalings extend holomorphically to complex $\eps$ near any real configuration, but not globally (OT4ML §8.10, Thm. 8.43, pp. 169–171 (161–163)).
  • Acceleration and the semi-dual: over-relaxation, quasi-Newton methods on the entropic semi-dual (OT4ML §9.5, the Beyond callout of §5).
  • The continuous Sinkhorn flow. With a time step $\eps$ per cycle, Sinkhorn on the torus converges as $\eps\to0$ to a parabolic Monge–Ampère equation whose stationary points are Brenier maps (OT4ML §9.7, pp. 192–194 (184–186); Berman 2020).
  • Sketching. Low-rank and random-feature approximations of the kernel give Sinkhorn iterations in linear time; positive features are needed to keep the iteration well defined, and they connect with linear-time attention (OT4ML §10.4, pp. 210–216 (202–208)).
  • Estimating maps. The entropic map (barycentric projection) of empirical measures converges to the Brenier map, with rates under smoothness (OT4ML Props. 10.14–10.15, p. 206 (198); StatOT Thms. 4.10–4.11).
  • Barycenters and multimarginal problems by iterated Bregman projections (notebook 2-sinkhorn; sheet Ex. 3; CompOT §9.2; Lesson 6 §8).
  • Unbalanced transport, in full: the conic formulation and the Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao (Hellinger–Kantorovich) metric, linear convergence of the damped iteration in the sup norm (OT4ML §11.1, pp. 217–229 (209–221); CompOT §10.2).
  • Neural dual solvers parametrize the potentials by input-convex networks (OT4ML §8.5, p. 154 (146)).

Exercise 1 — A $2\times2$ entropic problem§1 · checked

Let $C=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, $a=(p,1-p)$ with $p\in(0,1)$, $b=(\frac12,\frac12)$, $\eps>0$, and consider $\min\dotp CP+\eps\sum_{ij}P_{ij}(\log P_{ij}-1)$ over $P\in\R_+^{2\times2}$ with $P\mathbb 1=a$, $P^\top\mathbb 1=b$. (1) Show that every admissible plan is $P(x)=\begin{pmatrix}x&y(x)\\z(x)&w(x)\end{pmatrix}$ for one scalar $x$; give $y,z,w$ and the admissible interval $I(p)$. (2) Write the objective as $F_\eps(x)$ and compute $F_\eps'(x)$. (3) Explain why the optimal $x_\eps(p)$ lies in the interior of $I(p)$; give it from $F'_\eps(x)=0$. (4) For $p\lt\frac12$, expand $x_\eps(p)$ and show $x_\eps(p)\sim p+ce^{-2/\eps}$.

Hint 1
Fix $x=P_{11}$: the first row and the first column then determine the other three entries. Each entry must be non-negative.
Hint 2
$F'_\eps$ is $-2$ plus $\eps$ times the logarithm of a ratio of entries. Near an end of $I(p)$ one entry vanishes and the logarithm is infinite. For (4), the small parameter is $e^{-2/\eps}$ (the sheet prints $e^{-\eps/2}$, §11).
Solution

(1) $P(x)=\begin{pmatrix}x&p-x\\\frac12-x&\frac12-p+x\end{pmatrix}$, admissible for $x\in I(p)=[\max(0,p-\frac12),\min(p,\frac12)]$.

(2) $\dotp C{P(x)}=(p-x)+(\frac12-x)$, so $$\begin{gathered}F_\eps(x)=p+\tfrac12-2x+\eps\sum_{\text{4 entries }e}e\,(\log e-1),\\F'_\eps(x)=-2+\eps\log\frac{x\,(\frac12-p+x)}{(p-x)(\frac12-x)}.\end{gathered}$$

(3) $F'_\eps\to-\infty$ at the left end of $I(p)$ (an entry in the numerator vanishes) and $+\infty$ at the right end, and $F_\eps$ is strictly convex, so the minimizer is the unique interior root. $F'_\eps(x)=0$ reads $x(\frac12-p+x)=E\,(p-x)(\frac12-x)$ with $E=e^{2/\eps}$, a quadratic equation: $$(1-E)x^2+\big(\tfrac12-p+E(p+\tfrac12)\big)x-\tfrac{Ep}2=0,$$ and $x_\eps(p)$ is its root in $I(p)$.

(4) For $p\lt\frac12$ the unregularized optimum is the vertex $x=p$ (all of $a_1$ on $b_1$, cost $\frac12-p$). Write $x=p-\delta$: the optimality condition is $\delta=E^{-1}\frac{x(\frac12-p+x)}{\frac12-x}$, and at leading order $x=p$ gives $$x_\eps(p)=p-\frac p{1-2p}e^{-2/\eps}+O(e^{-4/\eps}),\qquad c(p)=-\frac p{1-2p}.$$ The entropic plan approaches its vertex exponentially fast in $1/\eps$, as on every fixed finite problem with a unique optimum (§1). for $p=0.3$ ($c=−0.75$), the exact root against the expansion: $p-x_\eps$ is 1.21×10⁻² vs 1.37×10⁻² at $\eps=0.5$, 2.51×10⁻⁴ vs 2.52×10⁻⁴ at $0.25$, 1.55×10⁻⁹ vs 1.55×10⁻⁹ at $0.1$; mpmath at 50 digits agrees.

Exercise 2 — Dual of an entropic linear program§4 · checked

Consider $\min\dotp CP+\eps H(P)$ over $P\ge0$ with $A(P)=b$, where $H(P)=\sum_iP_i(\log P_i-1)$, $P\in\R^N$, $A:\R^N\to\R^m$ linear and $\eps>0$. (1) Write the Lagrangian. (2) Assuming that $\inf_P$ and $\sup_\lambda$ can be swapped, write the dual. (3) Give the primal–dual relation. (4) For the marginal constraints of transport, write $A$ and $A^\top$ and recover the dual of entropic transport.

Hint 1
$\mathcal L(P,\lambda)=\dotp CP+\eps H(P)+\dotp\lambda{b-A(P)}$. The minimization over $P$ separates coordinate by coordinate.
Hint 2
$\min_{p\ge0}\ sp+\eps p(\log p-1)$ is attained at $p=e^{-s/\eps}$ and equals $-\eps e^{-s/\eps}$.
Solution

(1)–(2) With $s=C-A^\top\lambda$, the coordinatewise minimization gives $P_i=e^{-s_i/\eps}$ and the dual $$\max_{\lambda\in\R^m}\ \dotp\lambda b-\eps\sum_ie^{(A^\top\lambda-C)_i/\eps}.$$ (3) $P=e^{(A^\top\lambda-C)/\eps}$ entrywise: the primal solution is the exponential of an affine function of the multipliers.

(4) For $P\in\R^{n\times m}$, $A(P)=(P\mathbb 1_m,P^\top\mathbb 1_n)$ and $b=(a,b)$. Then $\dotp{(f,g)}{A(P)}=\sum_{ij}(f_i+g_j)P_{ij}$, so $A^\top(f,g)=(f_i+g_j)_{ij}=f\oplus g$, and the dual is $$\max_{f,g}\ \dotp fa+\dotp gb-\eps\sum_{ij}e^{(f_i+g_j-C_{ij})/\eps},$$ the dual written in the sheet’s Exercises 5–6. Writing these potentials as $f+\eps\log a$ and $g+\eps\log b$ turns it into the dual of Prop. 8.18 (§4), up to the constant $\eps(1+\mathrm H(a)+\mathrm H(b))$ that separates the two conventions (§0). on a random $5\times6$ problem at $\eps=0.3$, the dual at the Sinkhorn potentials equals the primal value; the difference is below 10⁻¹⁴.

For $a\in\R_+^{n_1}$, $b\in\R_+^{n_2}$, $c\in\R_+^{n_3}$ and a cost tensor $C\in\R^{n_1\times n_2\times n_3}$, consider $\min_{T\ge0}\dotp CT+\eps\sum T_{ijk}(\log T_{ijk}-1)$ under $\pi_1(T)=a$, $\pi_2(T)=b$, $\pi_3(T)=c$. (1) Write the Lagrangian with multipliers $f,g,h$. (2) Write the dual. (3) Write the log-domain Sinkhorn iterations. (4) (Bonus) Explain why the Hilbert-metric argument for two marginals does not give a contraction here.

Hint 1
Exercise 2 with $A^\top(f,g,h)=(f_i+g_j+h_k)_{ijk}$.
Hint 2
For (4): the update of $f$ applies a positive linear map to the product $v\otimes w$ of the two other scalings. What does Birkhoff’s theorem say about $d_H(v\otimes w,v'\otimes w')$?
Solution

(1)–(2) The Lagrangian is $$\begin{aligned}\mathcal L&=\dotp CT+\eps\sum T(\log T-1)\\&\quad+\dotp f{a-\pi_1T}+\dotp g{b-\pi_2T}+\dotp h{c-\pi_3T},\end{aligned}$$ and the dual is $$\begin{gathered}\max_{f,g,h}\ \dotp fa+\dotp gb+\dotp hc-\eps\sum_{ijk}e^{(f_i+g_j+h_k-C_{ijk})/\eps},\\T_{ijk}=e^{(f_i+g_j+h_k-C_{ijk})/\eps}.\end{gathered}$$ (3) Maximizing in each block in turn: $$f_i\leftarrow\eps\log a_i-\eps\log\sum_{j,k}e^{(g_j+h_k-C_{ijk})/\eps},$$ and the same for $g$ and $h$, each computed with the log-sum-exp trick. Each update costs $n_1n_2n_3$ operations: the tensor is the bottleneck unless the cost has structure (OT4ML p. 269 (261); Lesson 6 §8).

(4) With scalings $u=e^{f/\eps}$, the update is $u=a/\mathcal K(v\otimes w)$, a positive linear map applied to $v\otimes w$. Birkhoff contracts $d_H(v\otimes w,v'\otimes w')=d_H(v,v')+d_H(w,w')$ by $\lambda<1$, so the error on $f$ is bounded by $\lambda$ times the sum of the errors on $g$ and $h$. One update can therefore multiply the largest error by up to $2\lambda$, which exceeds 1 as soon as $\lambda\ge\frac12$. The two-marginal argument used that each half-step involves a single vector; here the bound does not even show that the errors decrease when $\eps$ is small. (The algorithm still converges: it is an alternating Bregman projection, §3.) on a random $4\times5\times6$ tensor at $\eps=0.2$, the three-block iteration reaches a marginal error below 10⁻¹³ in 20 cycles.

Exercise 14 — Sinkhorn’s scaling theorem (StatOT Ex. 4.6-6)§2, §3 · checked

Let $M$ be an $n\times n$ matrix with positive entries. Show that there are positive vectors $\kappa,\lambda$ such that $n\,\diag(\kappa)M\diag(\lambda)$ is doubly stochastic, by minimizing $\sum_{ij}\gamma_{ij}\log\frac{\gamma_{ij}}{M_{ij}}-\gamma_{ij}+M_{ij}$ over the couplings of the uniform marginals.

Hint 1
This is the KL projection of $M$ on $\Couplings(\frac1n\mathbb 1,\frac1n\mathbb 1)$: entropic transport with $C=-\eps\log M$, for any $\eps$.
Hint 2
Existence and positivity of the minimizer: Prop. 8.3. Its form: the Lagrange conditions, as in Prop. 8.5.
Solution

The objective is continuous and strictly convex on the compact polytope of couplings of $(\frac1n\mathbb 1,\frac1n\mathbb 1)$, so it has a unique minimizer $\gamma$, positive by the argument of Prop. 8.3 (the slope of $t\log t$ is infinite at 0). With multipliers $\phi_i$, $\psi_j$ for the row and column constraints, $\log\frac{\gamma_{ij}}{M_{ij}}=\phi_i+\psi_j$, so $\gamma=\diag(e^\phi)M\diag(e^\psi)$ and $n\gamma$ is doubly stochastic: take $\kappa=e^\phi$ and $\lambda=e^\psi$. Sinkhorn computes them by alternately normalizing rows and columns. §15Cheat sheet

Problem$L^\eps(a,b)=\min_{P\in\Couplings(a,b)}\dotp CP+\eps\KL(P\mid a\otimes b)$; $\KL(P\mid a\otimes b)$ = mutual information; on couplings $=-\mathrm H(P)+\mathrm H(a)+\mathrm H(b)$, so the other conventions ($-\eps\mathrm H$, $+\eps\sum P(\log P-1)$) have the same minimizer and shifted values.
Solutionunique, positive (slope of $t\log t$ infinite at 0); $P_\eps=\diag(u)K\diag(v)$, $K=e^{-C/\eps}$; $P_{ij}=a_ib_je^{(f_i+g_j-C_{ij})/\eps}$; objective on couplings $=\eps\KL(Q\mid P_\eps)+\dotp fa+\dotp gb$.
Limits$\eps\to0$: $L^\eps\to L_C$, $P_\eps\to$ the optimal plan of maximal entropy; fixed finite problem: $L^\eps-L_C\sim\eps\KL(P^\star\mid a\otimes b)$, plan within $e^{-\Delta/\eps}$; $\eps\to\infty$: $P_\eps\to a\otimes b$.
Sinkhorn$u\leftarrow a/(Kv)$, $v\leftarrow b/(K^\top u)$, $O(nm)$ per iteration; = alternating KL projections on $\{P\mathbb 1=a\}$, $\{P^\top\mathbb 1=b\}$ (projections are diagonal scalings); = exact block ascent on the dual.
Dual$\max\dotp fa+\dotp gb-\eps\sum a_ib_j(e^{(f_i+g_j-C_{ij})/\eps}-1)$, unconstrained, smooth; gradients = marginal errors; value $=\dotp fa+\dotp gb$ at the optimum; $\nabla_aL^\eps=f$, $\nabla_CL^\eps=P_\eps$.
Soft $c$-transform$f_i=-\eps\log\sum_jb_je^{(g_j-C_{ij})/\eps}$; between $\min_j(C_{ij}-g_j)$ and that plus $\eps\log\frac1{b_j}$; log-sum-exp trick: subtract the minimum; plain iteration breaks when $\min_jC_{ij}/\eps>745$ (double) or $103$ (single).

§16Self-test

  1. Write the entropic transport problem and explain the term $\eps\KL(P\mid a\otimes b)$ in probabilistic words.
    Answer
    $\min_{P\in\Couplings(a,b)}\dotp CP+\eps\KL(P\mid a\otimes b)$. For $(X,Y)\sim P$ the KL term is the mutual information $I(X;Y)$: the plan pays $\eps$ per nat of information shared by $X$ and $Y$. It vanishes for the independent coupling and is largest for deterministic plans.
  2. Why is the entropic plan unique and positive?
    Answer
    Uniqueness: $t\log t$ is strictly convex. Positivity: its slope is $-\infty$ at 0, so moving a plan with a zero entry towards $a\otimes b$ decreases the objective (Prop. 8.3).
  3. Show that $-\eps\mathrm H(P)$, $\eps\sum P(\log P-1)$ and $\eps\KL(P\mid a\otimes b)$ give the same minimizer. Do they give the same value?
    Answer
    On couplings, $\KL(P\mid a\otimes b)=-\mathrm H(P)+\mathrm H(a)+\mathrm H(b)$ and $\sum P(\log P-1)=-\mathrm H(P)-1$: they differ by constants. The values differ by $\eps(\mathrm H(a)+\mathrm H(b))$ or $\eps(1+\mathrm H(a)+\mathrm H(b))$; only the KL version satisfies $L^\eps\ge L_C$ and gives a reference-free Sinkhorn divergence.
  4. Derive the scaling form of the optimal plan.
    Answer
    Multipliers $f,g$ for the marginal constraints; stationarity $C_{ij}+\eps\log\frac{P_{ij}}{a_ib_j}=f_i+g_j$, so $P_{ij}=a_ie^{f_i/\eps}\,e^{-C_{ij}/\eps}\,b_je^{g_j/\eps}=u_iK_{ij}v_j$. Conversely a coupling of this form is optimal because the objective equals $\eps\KL(Q\mid P)+\dotp fa+\dotp gb$ on couplings.
  5. Write Sinkhorn’s algorithm. What does one half-step do to the marginals?
    Answer
    $u\leftarrow a/(Kv)$, $v\leftarrow b/(K^\top u)$ from $v=\mathbb 1$. Each half-step makes one marginal exact and perturbs the other; it is the KL projection on that marginal constraint.
  6. Why is a KL projection on $\{P\mathbb 1=a\}$ a row scaling?
    Answer
    The problem splits row by row; a multiplier $\lambda_i$ gives $\log(Q_{ij}/P_{ij})=\lambda_i$, so $Q=\diag(e^\lambda)P$ with $e^{\lambda_i}=a_i/(P\mathbb 1)_i$.
  7. What is the dual of entropic transport, and how does Sinkhorn act on it?
    Answer
    $\max_{f,g}\dotp fa+\dotp gb-\eps\sum a_ib_j(e^{(f_i+g_j-C_{ij})/\eps}-1)$: concave, smooth, unconstrained. Maximizing exactly in $f$ for fixed $g$ is the soft $c$-transform $f_i=-\eps\log\sum_jb_je^{(g_j-C_{ij})/\eps}$, which is the $u$-update. Sinkhorn is exact block ascent.
  8. Why does the plain iteration fail for small $\eps$, and what is the fix?
    Answer
    $e^{-C_{ij}/\eps}$ underflows (below $e^{-745}$ in double precision): rows of $K$ vanish, $a/(Kv)$ is infinite or NaN. Iterate on the potentials and compute soft minima with the log-sum-exp trick. In Lab 2 rows vanish below $\eps$ equal to 7.46×10⁻⁴.
  9. State Birkhoff’s contraction theorem and the resulting rate of Sinkhorn. Why is it pessimistic?
    Answer
    $d_H(Kv,Kv')\le\tanh(\frac14\log\eta(K))\,d_H(v,v')$; Sinkhorn contracts $d_H$ by $\lambda^2$ per cycle. For $K=e^{-C/\eps}$ the gain $1-\lambda^2\approx4e^{-\Delta/(2\eps)}$ is exponentially small: 8.2×10⁻¹¹³ per cycle at $\eps=0.01$ in Lab 2, while the observed gain is 1.53%.
  10. What governs the observed rate of Sinkhorn, and how does it depend on $\eps$?
    Answer
    The local rate is $\sigma_\eps^2$, the squared second singular value of $\diag(a)^{-1/2}P_\eps\diag(b)^{-1/2}$ (the maximal correlation of the plan). It tends to 1 as the plan becomes deterministic, with $1-\sigma_\eps^2=O(\eps)$: the iteration count grows like $1/\eps$.
  11. How large is the bias $L^\eps-L_C$? Distinguish a fixed finite problem from densities.
    Answer
    Fixed finite problem with a unique optimum: $\eps\KL(P^\star\mid a\otimes b)+o(\eps)$. Densities on $\R^d$ (quadratic cost): $-\frac{d\eps}2\log(\pi\eps)-\frac\eps2(H(\al)+H(\be))+O(\eps^2)$. Lab 1 sees both on one problem, with the hand-over where $\sqrt{\eps/2}$ reaches the cell size.
  12. Give the entropic plan between $\al$ and $\al*\Gauss(0,\frac\eps2\Id)$.
    Answer
    The law of $(X,X+\sqrt{\eps/2}G)$: its density with respect to $\al\otimes\be_\eps$ has the scaling form, so it is optimal (Prop. 8.14). Entropic transport to a blurred copy is adding noise.
  13. Why is the entropic plan between Gaussians Gaussian?
    Answer
    The cost only depends on second moments, and among couplings with given covariance the Gaussian one has the largest entropy, hence the smallest mutual information. Equivalently, soft transforms map quadratic potentials to quadratic ones (sheet Ex. 4).
  14. What is wrong with $L^\eps$ as a loss, and how does the Sinkhorn divergence fix it?
    Answer
    $L^\eps(\al,\al)>0$, and $\be\mapsto L^\eps(\al,\be)$ is minimized by a contracted copy of $\al$ (a Dirac at the mean when $\eps\to\infty$). $S_\eps=L^\eps(\al,\be)-\frac12L^\eps(\al,\al)-\frac12L^\eps(\be,\be)$ vanishes at $\be=\al$, is non-negative and definite for the quadratic cost, and differs from $W_2^2$ by $O(\eps^2)$.
  15. What are the limits of $S_\eps$ as $\eps\to0$ and $\eps\to\infty$?
    Answer
    $L_c(\al,\be)$, and $-\frac12\iint c\,\dd(\al-\be)\dd(\al-\be)$, a kernel norm (energy distance for $\norm{x-y}^p$, $p\lt2$); for $\norm{x-y}^2$ it is $\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2$, so very large temperatures only compare means (Lab 3).
  16. Why is the debiasing exact for translations?
    Answer
    Translating the second marginal of a coupling of $(\al,\al)$ by $h$ adds $\norm h^2$ to its cost and does not change its relative entropy, so $L^\eps(\al,\al(\cdot-h))=L^\eps(\al,\al)+\norm h^2$ and $S_\eps=\norm h^2=W_2^2$.
  17. Compare the sample complexities of $W_p$, MMD and $S_\eps$.
    Answer
    $W_p$: $n^{-1/d}$ for $d>2p$ (minimax for densities). MMD: $n^{-1/2}$ in every dimension. $S_\eps$ at fixed $\eps$: $n^{-1/2}$ with a constant growing like $\eps^{-q_d/2}$. In Lab 5 ($d=6$), $W_2$ decreases like $n^{−0.17}$, MMD like $n^{−0.53}$, $\sqrt{S_\eps}$ like $n^{−0.42}$ at the figure’s $\eps$ and $n^{−0.20}$ at one tenth of it.
  18. Does entropy beat the curse of dimension for estimating $W_2^2$?
    Answer
    No: estimating the unregularized cost forces $\eps\to0$ with $n$, and balancing the bias against the $\eps$-dependent fluctuations gives no better worst-case rate (OT4ML Rem. 10.13). Entropy gives a different, smoother quantity that is estimable at the parametric rate.
  19. Give the gradient of $L^\eps$ with respect to the cost, the weights and the positions.
    Answer
    By the envelope theorem: $\nabla_CL^\eps=P_\eps$, $\nabla_aL^\eps=f$ (up to a constant), and for $\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$, $\nabla_{x_i}L^\eps=a_ix_i-\sum_jP_{ij}y_j=a_i(x_i-T_\eps(x_i))$.
  20. Why is the barycentric projection not a transport map, and when does it become one?
    Answer
    Averaging contracts: $(T_\eps)_\sharp\al\ne\be$ in general (for Gaussians, the slope is multiplied by the correlation $\rho_\eps\lt1$). As $\eps\to0$, if the optimal plan is the graph of a map $T$, $T_\eps\to T$ (sheet Ex. 11).
  21. In Lab 6, what does the raw loss do to the generated cloud, and why?
    Answer
    It shrinks it, and at large temperature collapses it to a point: the raw loss is minimized by a contracted measure (§7). At $\eps=0.01$ it collapses the horizontal spread (target 0.0099, fit 0.0001), while the divergence finds 0.0112.
  22. Write the unbalanced entropic problem and its Sinkhorn update. Why must the kernel be tilted?
    Answer
    Minimize $\dotp CP+\eps\KL(P\mid a\otimes b)$ plus $\tau\KL(P\mathbb 1\mid a)+\tau\KL(P^\top\mathbb 1\mid b)$ over $P\ge0$; $u\leftarrow(a/\tilde Kv)^{\tau/(\tau+\eps)}$ with $\tilde K=(a\otimes b)\odot e^{-C/\eps}$. The marginals are free, so a diagonal rescaling of the kernel is no longer absorbed: with $e^{-C/\eps}$ (slide 42) the iteration solves the problem with $a^{1-\eps/\tau}$, $b^{1-\eps/\tau}$ in the penalties.
  23. What are the limits $\tau\to\infty$ and $\tau\to0$ of unbalanced transport?
    Answer
    $\tau\to\infty$: balanced transport (equal masses). $\tau\to0$: $\mathrm{UW}_\tau/\tau\to\sum_i(\sqrt{a_i}-\sqrt{b_i})^2$ on a common grid, the Hellinger distance: no transport, only creation and destruction (notebook 5 states this limit for $\rho\to+\infty$ by mistake).
  24. In the $2\times2$ problem of sheet Ex. 1, how fast does $x_\eps(p)$ approach $p$?
    Answer
    Exponentially: $p-x_\eps(p)\approx\frac p{1-2p}e^{-2/\eps}$ for $p\lt\frac12$. At $p=0.3$, $\eps=0.25$: 2.51×10⁻⁴ against 2.52×10⁻⁴.
