§1The course

What the outline says

The outline is the file Syllabus Teaching - 2026-2027 - Computational OT - IASD_MVA.docx. Its description of the course reads:

“Optimal Transport (OT) is a foundational mathematical theory that connects optimization, partial differential equations, and probability. It offers a powerful framework for comparing probability distributions and has recently become an important tool in machine learning, especially for designing and evaluating generative models. This course covers the fundamental mathematical aspects of OT, including the Monge and Kantorovich formulations, Brenier’s theorem, the dual and dynamic formulations, the Bures metric on Gaussian distributions, and gradient flows. It also introduces numerical methods such as linear programming, semi-discrete solvers, and entropic regularization. Applications in machine learning will include topics like training neural networks via gradient flows, token dynamics in transformers, and the structure of GANs and diffusion models. The course will focus primarily on mathematical content rather than deep learning techniques. However, it will include hands-on numerical programming sessions using PyTorch, along with exercise sessions. Assessment will be based on a written exam reflecting the material covered in both the numerical and exercise components.”

  • Validation. Attend all courses and do the numerical parts (not graded). Final written exam on 16 December 2026, with no documents allowed. Only the exam is graded and no sheet is marked as graded, so every lesson page gives hints and then a full worked solution for each exercise.
  • When and where. Wednesdays, 9:00 to 12:15, PSL amphitheatre Estrapade, 16 bis rue de l’Estrapade, 75005 Paris.
  • Lecturers. Julie Delon and Gabriel Peyré; Lesson 5 by Kimia Nadjahi.

Where the outline is silent or inconsistent, and what the pages do

  • Lessons 5 and 6 have no topic list. Lesson 5 follows K. Nadjahi’s lecture notes, slides and exercise sheet. Lesson 6 follows its sheet (“flow matching and dynamic OT”), the notebook 6-diffusion, slides 2–34 of deck 4 and OT4ML Ch. 14 and §16.1. The dates list adds “Multimarginal”, which gets a short section based on OT4ML §12.2.
  • An older plan. The block “More detailed content” at the end of the outline is an older plan: it mentions a project, a presentation and “4 numerical tours”, which contradicts the written exam, and it numbers two parts “3/”. The pages use it only to order topics inside a lesson, never to add or move topics. For example it puts “Gaussian case any dimension, expression for W2” and “Monge–Ampère” in its part 2; the pages treat them in Lesson 1, where “Detailed content” puts “OT between Gaussian, Bures metric”.
  • Lesson 4 starts with statistics. “Variational inference, comparison with KL, mixture estimation, OT on mixture of Gaussians” come before duality in the outline. The resources treat variational inference with gradient flows (StatOT §6.1), which is Lesson 8 material, and Gaussian mixtures in OT4ML §13.2 and StatOT §5.6. Lesson 4 covers what can be said with the tools available at that point: maximum likelihood as a forward KL, variational inference as a reverse KL, and OT between Gaussian mixtures (Lesson 4 §9). The Bures–Wasserstein flows of Gaussian variational inference are a pointer beyond the course in Lesson 8 §5.
  • Notebooks without a lesson. The outline attaches six of the eight teaching notebooks to lessons. 5-unbalanced fits Lesson 7 (introduction to unbalanced OT) and 8-discrete_diffusion is beyond the course; see §8.
  • Links in the outline. The gradient-flow notebook link points to github.com/gpeyre/ot4ml/blob/main/python/7-wasserstein-flows.ipynb, which no longer exists: the folder python/ was renamed notebooks/ on 9 June 2026 (commit 18e3343). The other “Numerics” links point to the repository’s root. The older plan links Numerical Tours notebooks (optimaltransp_5_entropic, _6_entropic_adv, _7_semidiscrete) whose course equivalents are notebooks 2, 3 and 4. The pages link the local copies in ot4ml/.
  • Bibliography. Santambrogio’s Optimal Transport for Applied Mathematicians is in the outline’s bibliography but not in the resources folder, so no page cites it. The four other books and notes are in ressources/ (§6).
  • Glossary language. The specification left the second glossary language as a placeholder. The course is taught in Paris, so each glossary gives the French terms.

The lessons in one table

1 Monge When can mass be moved by a map, and what does the optimal map look like? The assignment problem and its certificate (§2); sorting solves the line for convex costs (§3); the Monge problem and three ways it fails (§5); quantile maps (§6); Brenier’s theorem: the optimal map is the gradient of a convex function (§7); Gaussian maps and the Bures metric (§8); McCann’s interpolation (§9). On the line, sorting is optimal for $p\ge{}$1.00 and not below: 24.98% too expensive at $p=\frac12$ (Lab 3). The $n$-cell maps of OT4ML Example 2.18 cost $1+\frac1{12n^2}$: the infimum $1$ is approached, never attained (Lab 5).
2 Kantorovich How does splitting mass turn transport into a linear program, and when does the relaxation lose nothing? Couplings and the transport polytope, whose vertices have at most $n+m-1$ non-zero entries (§2); the north-west rule on the line (§3); Birkhoff–von Neumann: for uniform weights a permutation is optimal (§4); the network simplex and its dual certificate (§5); when Monge equals Kantorovich (§7); $c$-cyclical monotonicity (§8). With atoms in the source the Monge value is not continuous in the measures: from $k=9$ to $k=10$ central atoms it drops from 1.508333 to 0.397037, while the Kantorovich value moves from 0.397222 to 0.397037 (Lab 7).
3 Metric In what sense is optimal transport a distance between probability measures, and which convergence does it measure? $W_p$ is a distance, by the gluing lemma (§1); couplings of general measures, Gaussian couplings (§2); displacement interpolation is a geodesic, the mixture is not (§3); convergence in $W_p$ is weak convergence plus $p$-th moments (§5); the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem, measured in $W_1$ (§6, §7). For $\Gauss(0,1)$, $\sqrt n\,\E W_1(\al_n,\al)$ increases to the limit 1.28838. For the heavy-tailed $t(2)$ it keeps growing, by 0.5642 per unit of $\ln n$, as a heuristic predicts ($1/\sqrt\pi={}$0.5642) (Lab 6).
4 Duality What certifies that a transport plan is optimal, and what do the certificates compute? Duality and complementary slackness (§1, §2); $c$-transforms (§3); Brenier’s theorem, proved (§4); semi-discrete transport and Laguerre cells (§5); quantization (§6); $W_1$ and Lipschitz potentials (§7); dual norms, $\varphi$-divergences and GANs (§8); maximum likelihood, variational inference, OT between Gaussian mixtures (§9). Alternating hard $c$-transforms stall: the dual value is −0.099127 after one cycle and still −0.099127 after four, against the optimum 0.522569 (Lab 2). The notebook 4-semidiscrete samples its two blobs ½–½ while its grid gives them the masses 0.391 and 0.609, so its stochastic ascent stays 2.9% below the optimum (Lab 3).
5 Sliced How much of optimal transport survives if we only compare one-dimensional projections? $SW_p$ averages one-dimensional transports over directions (§2); a distance with the topology of $W_p$ (§3), but weaker: $SW_2^2\le W_2^2/d$ (§4); Gaussians (§5); Monte Carlo over directions (§6); a sample rate $n^{-1/2}$ in every dimension (§7); plans from slices, Min-SW and max-sliced (§8); sliced flows (§9). For two samples of $\Gauss(0,\Id_d)$ of size 500, $SW_2$ is 0.106, 0.108 and 0.109 for $d=2$, 10 and 50, while the log–log slope of $W_2$ in $n$ goes from −0.37 ($d=2$) to −0.04 ($d=50$) (Lab 1).
6 Dynamic When a path joins two distributions, what does it say about the map at its end? The continuity equation and the least-squares velocity (§1); Benamou–Brenier: $W_2^2$ is the least kinetic energy (§2), a convex problem with a Hamilton–Jacobi dual (§3), solved on a grid (§4); flow matching (§5) and diffusion models (§6); when the generated map is optimal (§7); multimarginal transport (§8). When the target covariance is rotated by 30°, the flow-matching map between two Gaussians costs 19.2% more than $W_2^2$ (Lab 6). The map of 6-diffusion is not the optimal map but is close to it: 0.302% of the mass goes to another atom, for an excess of 0.0013% of $W_2^2$ (Lab 5).
7 Sinkhorn What does entropy buy (speed, smoothness, statistics), and what does it cost? The entropic problem, its unique positive plan and its limits (§1); Sinkhorn’s algorithm (§2), as KL projections (§3) and as block ascent on a smooth dual (§4); its linear rate (§5); general measures, Gaussians and the bias $\eps\log(1/\eps)$ (§6); Sinkhorn divergences (§7); statistics (§8); gradients and learning (§9); unbalanced transport (§10). The rate of Sinkhorn per cycle is $\sigma_\eps^2$: 0.984742 measured against 0.984743 predicted at $\eps=0.01$; from $\eps=0.01$ to $0.001$ the iterations needed for an error $10^{-5}$ grow by a factor 8.7 (Lab 2). At large $\eps$, a particle descent on the raw loss collapses the cloud (spread 0.000 of the target’s), while the Sinkhorn divergence keeps it (1.001) (Lab 4).
8 Flows What does it mean to run gradient descent on a measure, and when does the descent reach the minimum? JKO steps (§1); first variations, the Wasserstein gradient and the limit PDE, Fokker–Planck and Langevin (§2); geodesic convexity, rates, and the log-Sobolev, Talagrand and HWI inequalities (§3); particle flows of MMD and energy distance (§4); Gaussian closures (§5); two-layer networks (§6); tokens in transformers (§7); populations of cells (§8). JKO steps for the heat equation are first order, with an error of about 0.45$\,\tau$ (Lab 1). A two-layer ReLU network started from a single point stays a Dirac and stops at risk 3.58×10⁻², with a negative certificate pointing at the missing teacher neuron (Lab 6). Attention groups the tokens into two clusters by $t=5$ and needs about 850 times longer to merge them (Lab 7).

The seven questions that come back, answered

Each thread of §4 asks a question. Here is what the course answers, read across all the lessons; the thread list of §4 gives every station.

T1 · Relaxation and convexity. When a problem has no solution or is not convex, which relaxation makes it solvable, and when does the relaxation lose nothing? Three relaxations carry the course. Couplings replace maps and turn transport into a linear program (L2 §1, §2). Nothing is lost for uniform discrete weights, where a permutation is optimal (L2 §4), or for a source without atoms, where the Monge infimum equals the Kantorovich minimum (L3 §2); with atoms the Monge value can be three times larger, and it is not continuous in the measures (L2 §7). Entropy makes the problem strictly convex and smooth (L7 §1). It always loses something, and the loss is measured: a blur of width $\sqrt\eps$ on the plan and a bias of order $\eps\log(1/\eps)$ on the value (L7 §6), which the Sinkhorn divergence removes at first order (L7 §7). Lifting to measures makes problems convex: the momentum $m=\rho v$ for Benamou–Brenier (L6 §3), the measure of the neurons for a two-layer network (L8 §6). This convexity along mixtures is not the one that makes a flow converge: rates need convexity along $W_2$ geodesics (L8 §3), and the network’s flow can stall at a stationary measure that is not a minimizer (L8 Lab 6).

T2 · Duality and potentials. What do dual variables certify, and how do they turn into algorithms and gradients? Potentials with $f(x)+g(y)\le c(x,y)$ whose total equals the cost of a plan prove that the plan is optimal, entry by entry through complementary slackness (L1 §2, L2 §5, L4 §1, §2). The same potentials compute the objects of the course: their gradient gives Brenier’s map (L4 §4), their weights give the semi-discrete map by balancing cell masses (L4 §5), and their Lipschitz form gives $W_1$ and the critic of a GAN (L4 §7, §8). As algorithms: the network simplex updates potentials (L2 §5); alternating hard $c$-transforms stall after one cycle (L4 §3), while their soft version, Sinkhorn, is exact block ascent on a smooth dual and converges (L7 §4). As gradients: the potentials and the plan give the gradients of a transport loss, which makes Sinkhorn losses trainable (L7 §9); the first variation of $\frac12W_2^2(\cdot,\be)$ is a Kantorovich potential, which gives the Euler–Lagrange equation of a JKO step (L8 §1); first variations drive every flow of Lesson 8 (L8 §2), and the first variation of a network’s risk is its optimality certificate (L8 §6).

T3 · Monotonicity and convex gradients. Why are optimal maps monotone, and what structure does monotonicity force on them? Optimality means that no cyclic exchange of destinations lowers the cost: $c$-cyclical monotonicity (L2 §8). On the line, for a convex cost, this is sorting (L1 §3, L2 §3). For the quadratic cost in $\R^d$, it makes the map the gradient of a convex function (Brenier, L1 §7, proved in L4 §4). Later lessons use this structure. Slices are solved by sorting, and the projection of an optimal plan is optimal for a slice only when it is monotone (L5 §2, §8). A flow generates an optimal map only when that map is a gradient, which holds on the line and for Gaussians with commuting covariances (L6 §5, §7). The entropic barycentric projection is still the gradient of a convex function and tends to Brenier’s map (L7 §4). Displacement convexity comes from the Jacobian of the Brenier map along McCann’s interpolation (L8 §3).

T4 · Closed forms as test beds. Which cases can be solved by hand, and how do they test the general theory and the code? Two families recur. On the line everything reduces to quantile functions: maps, plans with atoms, $W_1$ by distribution functions, empirical rates, quantization, slices (L1 §6, L2 §3, L3 §6, L4 §6, §7, L5 §2). For Gaussians everything reduces to matrices: the Bures metric (L1 §8), couplings (L3 §2), sliced distances (L5 §5), flow matching (L6 §7), entropic transport (L7 §6), gradient flows (L8 §5). These cases test the theory: on them the sheet’s Gaussian-coupling formula fails (L3 Lab 2), the small-$\eps$ expansion of slide 56 is wrong (L7 Lab 3), and the flow-matching map is not optimal (L6 Lab 6). They also test the code: the Bures formula against POT (L1 Lab 9), the Benamou–Brenier solver against the exact geodesic (L6 Lab 3), JKO steps against the exact heat flow (L8 Lab 1).

T5 · Lagrangian and Eulerian. Do we move particles or change densities, and how do the two descriptions match? Particles and maps on one side, densities and plans on the other; the continuity equation $\partial_t\al+\operatorname{div}(\al v)=0$ connects them: a velocity field moves the particles and transports the density (L6 §1). The course goes back and forth. McCann’s interpolation moves points (L1 §9), while a mixture fades mass in place and is not a geodesic (L3 §3). Benamou–Brenier is solved on a space–time grid (L6 §4), while flow matching learns a velocity and integrates it for particles (L6 §5). On the line, a JKO step is a Lagrangian problem in quantile functions, and its limit is an Eulerian PDE (L8 §1, §2). The Fokker–Planck equation, Langevin particles and score particles are three representations of one flow (L8 §2), and MMD descents, neurons and tokens are particle systems whose mean-field limits are continuity equations (L8 §4, §6, §7). The sliced flow (L5 §9) and the barycentric steps of Sinkhorn descents (L7 §7) are particle methods too.

T6 · Computation and statistics. What does optimal transport cost to compute, and how well can it be estimated from samples? Exactly, a discrete problem is a linear program: $O(n^3)$ for the Hungarian method (L1 §2), the network simplex for general weights (L2 §5), a sort on the line (L1 §3). Two routes go faster: slicing, a sort per direction with a Monte Carlo error $\sqrt{\Var/K}$ over $K$ directions (L5 §6), and entropy, matrix–vector products whose number of iterations grows like $1/\eps$ (L7 §2, §5). For densities, finite-dimensional formulations take over: one weight per point in semi-discrete transport (L4 §5), a space–time grid for Benamou–Brenier (L6 §4). From samples, the plug-in estimate converges at the rate $n^{-1/2}$ on the line under a tail condition (L3 §6), and only like $n^{-1/d}$ in dimension $d\gt2p$ (L5 §7); slicing, and entropy at fixed $\eps$, give $n^{-1/2}$ in every dimension, for a different quantity (L5 §7, L7 §8). The labs add a practical warning: sampling noise easily dominates a comparison, as with the 200 particles of 6-diffusion (L6 §6), the bias of Euler–Maruyama under 400 samples (L8 Lab 2) and the win counts on held-out days (L8 Lab 8).

T7 · Geometry of measures. What geometry does optimal transport put on probability measures, compared with other distances? $W_p$ measures how far mass has to move. It metrizes weak convergence plus $p$-th moments (L3 §5). It stays finite and informative between disjoint supports, where KL is infinite and total variation and Jensen–Shannon saturate (L4 §8, Lab 6). It has geodesics, McCann’s interpolations, which are the cheapest paths of Benamou–Brenier (L3 §3, L6 §2). The other geometries of the course are compared with it: Fisher–Rao and Bures on Gaussians (L1 §8), dual norms and $\varphi$-divergences (L4 §8), sliced distances, with the same topology but weaker (L5 §3, §4), and Sinkhorn divergences, between $W_2^2$ and kernel norms (L7 §7). Lesson 8 uses this geometry for optimization: a gradient $\nabla_WF=\nabla\frac{\delta F}{\delta\al}$ and the dissipation of energy (L8 §2), then the log-Sobolev, Talagrand and HWI inequalities, which relate entropy, Fisher information and $W_2$ along the flow (L8 §3).

§4Threads: seven ideas that come back

Seven ideas run through the whole course. The map shows in which lessons each one appears (a dot is a “station”). Each lesson page repeats the map with its own column shaded and says what it contributes. The list gives every station, and section numbers link into the lesson pages; §3 answers each question with what the whole course says.

  1. T1 · Relaxation & convexity. When a problem has no solution or is not convex, which relaxation makes it solvable, and when does the relaxation lose nothing?

    • L1 The Monge problem is non-convex: maps may not exist and infima may not be attained (§5).
    • L2 Couplings make the problem a linear program; its vertices are sparse trees, and permutations for uniform weights (§1, §2, §4).
    • L3 Couplings between general measures; for an atomless source, the Monge infimum equals the Kantorovich minimum, which is attained even when no map attains the infimum (§2).
    • L4 The semi-dual is concave and unconstrained, a finite-dimensional problem when one side is discrete (§5); quantization is non-convex in the positions, and Lloyd reaches stationary points only (§6).
    • L6 The action is not convex in $(\al,v)$; with the momentum $m=\rho v$ and the perspective $\norm m^2/a$, Benamou–Brenier becomes a convex problem with a linear constraint (§3); multimarginal transport is again a linear program, over a tensor (§8).
    • L7 Entropy makes transport strictly convex and smooth: a unique positive plan, a value differentiable in the data, and the vertex of the linear program recovered as $\eps\to0$, exponentially fast on a fixed problem (§1); the Sinkhorn divergence restores definiteness (§7); penalizing the marginals relaxes the problem once more (§10).
    • L8 Convexity along mixtures is not enough for flows: convexity along $W_2$ geodesics (McCann) gives $O(1/t)$, and $\lambda$-convexity gives the PL inequality and exponential rates (§3); the risk of a two-layer network is convex in the measure of its neurons but not along geodesics, and its flow can stall at a stationary measure that is not a minimizer (§6).

  2. T2 · Duality & potentials. What do dual variables certify, and how do they turn into algorithms and gradients?

    • L1 The Hungarian certificate $u_i+v_j\le C_{i,j}$ and the discrete Brenier potential (§2, §7).
    • L2 LP duality and complementary slackness; the network simplex carries potentials that certify the optimum (§5).
    • L3 Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality used as a tool: the Berry–Esseen bound in $W_1$ is a bound over all 1-Lipschitz test functions (§7); Lesson 4 proves it.
    • L4 Kantorovich duality and complementary slackness (§1–§2), $c$-transforms and the one-cycle stall of their alternation (§3), the proof of Brenier’s theorem (§4), semi-discrete weights found by mass balancing (§5), the Lipschitz potential of $W_1$ and the GAN critic (§7–§8).
    • L6 The dual of Benamou–Brenier is a Hamilton–Jacobi inequality; its endpoint values form a Kantorovich pair, the Hopf–Lax formula is a $c$-transform, and $v=\nabla\varphi/2$ (§3); the endpoint coupling of a Schrödinger bridge is an entropic plan (§9).
    • L7 The dual becomes unconstrained and smooth; Sinkhorn is exact block ascent on it, by soft $c$-transforms, whose hard limit stalls in Lesson 4 (§4); the potentials give the gradients of the loss (§9).
    • L8 The first variation of $\frac12W_2^2(\cdot,\be)$ is a Kantorovich potential: it gives the Euler–Lagrange equation of a JKO step (§1) and the flow of $W_2^2$ along the McCann geodesic (Exercise 6); the first variation of the network risk is the optimality certificate of Chizat and Bach (§6).

  3. T3 · Monotonicity & convex gradients. Why are optimal maps monotone, and what structure does monotonicity force on them?

    • L1 Sorting on the line, cyclical monotonicity, Brenier’s $\nabla\varphi$, Monge–Ampère (§3, §7, §9).
    • L2 The weighted sweep on the line, and $c$-cyclical monotonicity of optimal plans, checked by negative cycles (§3, §8).
    • L3 The optimal Gaussian coupling is the graph of a symmetric positive linear map, the Brenier map; in 1D, gluing monotone plans through an atomless middle gives the monotone plan (§1, §2).
    • L4 $c$-concave potentials: $\frac12\norm x^2-f$ is convex and its gradient is Brenier’s map (§3–§4); the Laguerre map is the gradient of a piecewise affine convex function (§5).
    • L5 Each slice is solved by its monotone matching; the projection of an optimal plan is optimal for a slice only when it is monotone, and sorting along an eigenvector of the Gaussian Brenier map reproduces that map (§8, §11).
    • L6 Optimal flows move every particle straight at constant speed (§2); a one-dimensional flow-matching map is monotone, hence optimal, while in higher dimension the generated map is optimal only when it is a gradient (§5, §7); on the line the multimarginal plan is comonotone (§8).
    • L7 Soft transforms keep $c$-concavity: $\frac12(\norm x^2-f)$ is convex and its gradient is the barycentric projection, which tends to the Brenier map (§4, Exercise 13).
    • L8 Displacement convexity of internal energies comes from the Jacobian of the Brenier map along the McCann interpolation (§3); on the line, JKO steps are convex problems in the quantile function and one-dimensional deterministic flows keep particles in order (§1, §2).

  4. T4 · Closed forms as test beds. Which cases can be solved by hand, and how do they test the general theory and the code?

    • L1 Quantiles in 1D, affine maps and the Bures metric for Gaussians (§6, §8).
    • L2 The 1D north-west sweep, and the quantile coupling that handles atoms (§3, §7).
    • L3 Gaussian couplings and the correction of the sheet’s formula; $W_p$ of Diracs and two-atom measures; exact 1D values of $W_1$ for empirical measures and CLT sums (§1, §2, §6, §7).
    • L4 1D potentials $f'=2(x-T(x))$ (§2); quantile intervals as optimal Laguerre cells and the constant $\frac1{12}\int\frac{\dd x}\rho$ of quantization (§5–§6); $W_1=\int\abs{F_\al-F_\be}$, KL between Gaussians, OT between Gaussian mixtures (§7–§9).
    • L5 Sorting and quantile functions slice by slice (§1–§2); closed forms between Gaussians for isotropic, proportional and rank-one covariances, and the split into a mean part and a shape part (§5, §11).
    • L6 Gaussian paths and the Lyapunov equation (§1); the action of a mixture path with the logarithmic mean (§2); flow-matching velocities for Gaussians and mixtures, and the commutation criterion (§5, §7, §10); Gaussian noise to atoms, against semi-discrete transport (§6).
    • L7 Entropic transport between Gaussians in closed form; it tests the small-temperature expansion and finds slide 56 wrong, and shows that the Sinkhorn divergence is exact for translations (§6, Lab 3).
    • L8 Gaussian closures: affine velocities keep Gaussians Gaussian and reduce flows to matrix ODEs; on the line the Gaussians form a flat half-plane where KL, $W_2^2$, Sinkhorn and Fisher flows are explicit (§5, Exercises 3–5); the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck flow of a mixture tests the functional inequalities exactly (§3).

  5. T5 · Lagrangian vs Eulerian. Do we move particles or change densities, and how do the two descriptions match?

    • L1 Moving points vs moving weights; McCann displacement interpolation (§1, §9).
    • L2 Plans move mass between fixed supports; interpolation along a plan, without collisions for quadratic optimal plans (§9).
    • L3 Displacement interpolation is a constant-speed $W_2$ geodesic; the mixture fades mass in place and has infinite $W_2$ length (§3).
    • L4 The Beckmann flux, an Eulerian description of $W_1$ without time (§7); interpolating mixtures component by component or as densities (§9).
    • L5 The sliced gradient flow moves particles by sorting, while Radon barycenters average quantile functions on a grid and return by back-projection (§9).
    • L6 The continuity equation as the Eulerian form of a Lagrangian flow (§1); Benamou–Brenier solved on a space-time grid (§4), flow matching learned as a velocity and integrated for particles (§5); diffusion trajectories against straight OT rays (§6).
    • L7 Particle descents on the raw loss and on the Sinkhorn divergence: the barycentric projection is a Lagrangian gradient step (§7, §9).
    • L8 The JKO step is Lagrangian in quantiles, the limit is an Eulerian PDE (§1–§2); the Fokker–Planck equation, Langevin particles and score particles are three representations of one flow (§2); MMD, neurons and tokens move as particle systems whose mean-field limit is a continuity equation (§4, §6, §7).

  6. T6 · Computation & statistics. What does optimal transport cost to compute, and how well can it be estimated from samples?

    • L1 $n!$ permutations, $O(n\log n)$ sorting, $O(n^3)$ Hungarian method; plug-in bias (§2, §3, §6).
    • L2 Network simplex, interior points and $n+m-1$ sparsity; Hungarian and Sinkhorn in context (§2, §5).
    • L3 Empirical measures converge in $W_p$; on the line at rate $n^{-1/2}$ when $\int\sqrt{F(1-F)}\lt\infty$; sharp CLT rates in $W_1$ for lattice, symmetric and skewed laws (§6, §7).
    • L4 Deterministic and stochastic semi-discrete ascent, and a sampling pitfall in the notebook (§5); quantization rates $m^{-2}$ against $m^{-1}$ for i.i.d. samples (§6); maximum likelihood as a KL projection (§9).
    • L5 A sort per direction, $O(Kdn+Kn\log n)$, and a Monte Carlo error $\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}/K}$ (§6); a sample rate $n^{-1/2}$ in every dimension, against a signal that shrinks like $1/\sqrt d$ (§1, §7).
    • L6 A staggered-grid Douglas–Rachford solver: a cubic per cell, a DCT Poisson solve, tridiagonal projections, first-order accuracy in space (§4); flow matching trains by regression, without simulating the ODE (§5); a 200-particle comparison is dominated by sampling noise (§6); multimarginal tensors have $\prod_sn_s$ entries (§8).
    • L7 Matrix–vector iterations, the log domain, Birkhoff and local rates ($\propto1/\eps$ iterations) (§2–§5); the bias $\frac d2\eps\log\frac1\eps$ (§6); parametric statistics at fixed $\eps$ and no free lunch for $W_2$ (§8).
    • L8 Implicit steps are stable for every step, explicit ones need $\tau\lesssim\Delta x^2$ (§1); Euler–Maruyama is biased by $h/2$ and Monte Carlo errors scale like $n^{-1/2}$ (§2); finite particle systems only reach kernel quadratures of the target (§4); held-out days test trajectory reconstructions from samples (§8).

  7. T7 · Geometry of measures. What geometry does optimal transport put on probability measures, compared with other distances?

    • L1 Directed Monge distance, geodesics, Bures vs Fisher–Rao (§5, §8, §9).
    • L2 The Kantorovich value is convex in the marginals and stable, while the Monge value jumps when atoms are split (§6, §7).
    • L3 $W_p$ is a distance on $\mathcal P_p$ by gluing; it metrizes weak convergence plus $p$-th moments; comparisons with total variation (§1, §4, §5).
    • L4 Dual norms (total variation, $W_1$, MMD) against $\varphi$-divergences; Pinsker; Jensen–Shannon saturates on disjoint supports (§8); maximum likelihood and variational inference use the two directions of KL (§9).
    • L5 $SW_p$ metrizes the topology of $W_p$ (§3) but is weaker: $SW_2^2\le W_2^2/d$, reverse bounds with a degraded exponent, and a smaller ratio for smooth deformations (§4); Min-SW is not a distance, and max-sliced keeps the best direction (§8).
    • L6 $W_2$ is the length of the cheapest path, and a mixture of densities is a longer path (§2); learned flows are valid transports, not geodesics (§5–§7); Schrödinger bridges add a Fisher-information term (§9).
    • L7 KL is the only Bregman and $\varphi$-divergence (§3); Sinkhorn divergences interpolate between $W_2^2$ and kernel norms and metrize weak convergence (§7).
    • L8 Gradient flows in the Wasserstein space: the Otto metric, $\nabla_W=\nabla\frac{\delta}{\delta\al}$, energy dissipation (§2); logarithmic Sobolev, Talagrand and HWI relate entropy, Fisher information and $W_2$ (§3); attention contracts the token cloud in Hilbert’s projective geometry (§7).

§5How the lessons depend on each other

An arrow from A to B means that Lesson B uses a result of Lesson A. Each box links to its lesson page. Lesson 1 feeds almost everything: the 1D quantile formula, Brenier’s theorem and the Gaussian formulas come back in Lessons 2 to 8.

  • L1 → L2. The assignment problem becomes a linear program over couplings; permutations are its vertices.
  • L2 → L3. Discrete couplings become couplings of general measures, which define the distances $W_p$.
  • L1 → L4. Brenier’s theorem, stated in Lesson 1, is proved with $c$-transforms.
  • L3 → L4. The Kantorovich problem between general measures is studied through its dual.
  • L1 → L5. The 1D quantile formula is applied to every projection.
  • L3 → L5. The metric and topological properties of $W_p$ are carried over to sliced distances.
  • L1 → L6. Displacement interpolation and Gaussian maps become flows of densities.
  • L3 → L6. Geodesics of the Wasserstein space are the curves that dynamic OT minimises over.
  • L2 → L7. The transport linear program is regularised by entropy.
  • L3 → L7. Weak convergence and the central limit theorem lead to sample-complexity results.
  • L4 → L7. Sinkhorn maximises the regularised dual; $c$-transforms become soft minima.
  • L4 → L8. First variations are dual potentials; the Wasserstein gradient of $\frac12W_2^2(\cdot,\be)$ is $x-T(x)$, with $T$ the Brenier map.
  • L6 → L8. The continuity equation carries the gradient flows (JKO scheme).

§9How a lesson page is organised

  • Header. The lesson strip (current lesson highlighted, finished lessons linked), the contents, and links to the previous and next lessons.
  • §0 Where this lesson sits. What the lesson reuses and what it prepares, with links to exact sections; its sources and their pages; a notation table mapping the companion’s notation to each resource; the thread map with this lesson’s stations; and a question that comes back in later lessons.
  • Sections. They follow the outline’s topics. Each goes from the problem to the intuition, the precise statement, why it is true, when it fails and how it is used. Statement boxes keep the source’s numbering and page (“OT4ML Thm. 2.23, p. 29 (21)”). Proofs are given in full when they teach something, folded otherwise.
  • Callouts. Key idea, Watch out (a trap or a common error), Link (to another lesson), In the notebook (what to run and what to look at), Beyond the course.
  • Labs. Live experiments computed in the page. Each one states a question and its setting (usually the data of a teacher’s notebook), then “What we found”, with numbers measured by the same code and checked against NumPy, SciPy and POT. Drag the points, or focus a plot with Tab and use the arrow keys ([ and ] choose the point; Shift makes bigger steps). Sliders and buttons work with the keyboard.
  • Exercises. The lesson’s sheet: statement, hints, then the worked solution.
  • End of the page. References, reference implementations in Python/NumPy with their printed outputs, a cheat sheet, a self-test with worked answers, and a glossary in English and French.

The theme button in the header switches between light and dark; the choice is remembered.

§10How the pages are built and checked

  • One source for numbers. The labs run in the page (plain JavaScript). The numbers quoted in the text are produced by the same code run outside the browser, then recomputed by independent Python code (NumPy, SciPy, POT). A headless-browser test reads every lab’s values back from the rendered page and compares them with the numbers in the text.
  • Page checks. For every page: no JavaScript error, every formula rendered, no horizontal scrolling at widths 500, 700 and 1400 px, every link and anchor valid (including links into ot4ml/ and the PDFs), and screenshots of every section in both themes, inspected by eye.
  • Files. The pages are self-contained: their code and styles are inside the file, and only the formula renderer (MathJax) and the fonts come from the web. Scratch work, the notebook runs and the checking code are in _build/; the working notes, with the conventions and all measured numbers, are in _companion_notes.md.
