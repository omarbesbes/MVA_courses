8. Computing $SW_p$ in practice: Monte Carlo over directions notes §2, pp. 4; slides, frame 12/12

Replace the average over the sphere by an average over $K$ random directions, each drawn by normalizing a standard Gaussian vector: the estimate of $SW_p^p$ is unbiased, costs $O(Kdn+Kn\log n)$, and its error decreases like $\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}/K}$ — whatever the dimension.

An average over random directions

In the course Nadjahi notes, §2, p. 4

2. Sliced Optimal Transport in Practice. Consider $\mu=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{x_i}$ and $\nu=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{y_i}$, with each $x_i,y_i\in\R^d$ (practical setting: we only observe data points).

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf{SW}_p(\mu,\nu)^p&=\E_{\theta\sim\mathcal U_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}}\big[\mathbf W_p\big((p_\theta)_\sharp\mu,(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu\big)^p\big]&(22)\\&\approx\frac1K\sum_{j=1}^K\mathbf W_p\big((p_{\theta_j})_\sharp\mu,(p_{\theta_j})_\sharp\nu\big)^p&(23)\\&=\widehat{\mathbf{SW}}_p(\mu,\nu)^p&(24)\end{aligned}$$

Main steps to compute SW in practice:

  1. Sample: $(\theta_j)_{j=1}^K$, which are $K$ independent and identically distributed samples from $\mathcal U_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}$
  2. Project: For each $j\in\{1,\dots,K\}$, compute $(p_{\theta_j})_\sharp\mu$ and $(p_{\theta_j})_\sharp\nu$
  3. Sort and average: Compute $\mathbf W_p\big((p_{\theta_j})_\sharp\mu,(p_{\theta_j})_\sharp\nu\big)$
(22)
The definition, written as an expectation over a random direction (Remark 3).
(23)
The expectation is replaced by the empirical average over $K$ random directions — Monte Carlo integration. The “$\approx$” is random: another draw of directions gives another number.
$\widehat{\mathbf{SW}}_p$
The resulting estimator; its $p$-th power estimates $SW_p^p$. In OT4ML the number of directions is $L$, on the slides $k$.
“Sort and average”
Each $\mathbf W_p$ between two uniform measures on $n$ numbers is computed by sorting (§2); then the $p$-th powers are averaged over the $K$ directions and the $p$-th root is taken.
Algorithm — Monte Carlo sliced Wasserstein between two clouds of $n$ points
  1. Draw $K$ standard Gaussian vectors $g_1,\dots,g_K\in\R^d$ and set $\theta_j=g_j/\norm{g_j}$. Cost $O(Kd)$.
  2. Compute the $K\times n$ projections $\ps{\theta_j}{x_i}$ and $\ps{\theta_j}{y_i}$ (a matrix product). Cost $O(Kdn)$.
  3. Sort each of the $2K$ rows. Cost $O(Kn\log n)$.
  4. Return $\widehat{SW}_p^p=\frac1K\sum_{j=1}^K\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\abs{u_{j,(i)}-v_{j,(i)}}^p$, where $u_{j,(i)}$, $v_{j,(i)}$ are the sorted projections. Cost $O(Kn)$.

With unequal numbers of points or weights, step 4 uses the quantile formula of §2 instead. In POT: ot.sliced_wasserstein_distance(X, Y, n_projections=K) returns $\widehat{SW}_2$ (with the root).

Worked example — three directions on the cross

For the cross of §4 the slice cost in the direction $\varphi$ is $1-\abs{\sin2\varphi}$. With the three directions $0°$, $30°$, $60°$ (as if they had been drawn at random):

$$\widehat{SW}_2^2=\frac13\Big[(1-0)+\big(1-\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\big)+\big(1-\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\big)\Big]=\frac{1+0.134+0.134}3\approx0.423,$$

against the exact $SW_2^2=1-\frac2\pi\approx0.363$. With the four directions $0°,45°,90°,135°$ one gets $0.5$; with $22.5°,67.5°,112.5°,157.5°$, $0.293$. A handful of directions gives a rough estimate; how rough is quantified below.

Drawing uniform directions: normalize a Gaussian vector

In the course Nadjahi notes, Prop. 2 and its proof, p. 4

Proposition 2 (How to uniformly sample on $\mathbb S^{d-1}$?). Let $X_1,X_2,\dots,X_d\sim\N(0,1)$ and be independent. Then, the vector

$$\tilde X=\Big(\frac{X_1}Z,\frac{X_2}Z,\dots,\frac{X_d}Z\Big)$$

is a uniform random vector on $\mathbb S^{d-1}$, where $Z=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^dX_i^2}$.

Proof. Let $f:\mathbb S^{d-1}\to\R$ be a bounded, continuous function. We want to show that,

$$\E[f(\tilde X)]=\frac1{\mathcal A(\mathbb S^{d-1})}\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}f(\theta)\,\dd\sigma(\theta)\qquad(25)$$

where $\sigma$ is the surface measure on $\mathbb S^{d-1}$, and $\mathcal A(\mathbb S^{d-1})$ denotes the surface area of $\mathbb S^{d-1}$, i.e., $\mathcal A(\mathbb S^{d-1})=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}$ with $\Gamma(d/2)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-t}t^{\frac d2-1}\,\dd t$. Using the Gaussian density and switching to spherical coordinates $x=r\theta$ with $r\in(0,+\infty)$ and $\theta\in\mathbb S^{d-1}$, and using $\dd x=r^{d-1}\dd r\,\dd\sigma(\theta)$,

$$\begin{aligned}\E[f(\tilde X)]&=\int_{\R^d}f(x_1/z,x_2/z,\dots,x_d/z)\,(2\pi)^{-d/2}e^{-z^2/2}\,\dd x_1\dd x_2\dots\dd x_d&(26)\\&=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\int_0^{+\infty}\Big[\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}f(\theta)\,\dd\sigma(\theta)\Big]e^{-r^2/2}r^{d-1}\,\dd r&(27)\\&=c_d\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}f(\theta)\,\dd\sigma(\theta).&(28)\end{aligned}$$

where $c_d=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-r^2/2}r^{d-1}\,\dd r=\frac1{2\pi^{d/2}}\Gamma(d/2)$. Thus, $c_d=\frac1{\mathcal A(\mathbb S^{d-1})}$, and this concludes the proof. $\square$

$\tilde X$
A standard Gaussian vector of $\R^d$ divided by its length: a point of the unit sphere.
$z$ in (26)
Short for $\norm{x}=\sqrt{x_1^2+\dots+x_d^2}$, both inside $f$ and in the density $(2\pi)^{-d/2}e^{-\norm x^2/2}$.
$\sigma$
Here, as in Remark 1, the surface measure ($\tilde\sigma$ on this page). (25) says: $\tilde X$ has the law $\tilde\sigma/\mathcal A(\mathbb S^{d-1})=\mathcal U_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}$.
“bounded, continuous $f$”
Two probability measures that give the same expectation to all bounded continuous functions are equal; so (25) identifies the law of $\tilde X$.
Idea of the proof

The density of a standard Gaussian vector, $(2\pi)^{-d/2}e^{-\norm x^2/2}$, depends only on the radius $\norm x$: it gives the same weight to all directions. In polar coordinates the integral factorizes into (radius part) × (direction part), and the direction part is the uniform law. Equivalently: the Gaussian law is invariant under rotations, so the law of its direction is a rotation-invariant probability on the sphere — and the uniform law is the only one.

The proof, step by step (with the radius integral)
  1. $$\E[f(\tilde X)]=\int_{\R^d}f\Big(\frac x{\norm x}\Big)(2\pi)^{-d/2}e^{-\norm x^2/2}\,\dd x .$$Expectation of a function of a random vector with a density (the set $\{x=0\}$ has measure zero). This is (26).
  2. $$=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\int_0^\infty\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}f(\theta)\,e^{-r^2/2}r^{d-1}\,\dd\tilde\sigma(\theta)\,\dd r .$$Polar coordinates (Remark 1, formula (4)) with $x=r\theta$: $x/\norm x=\theta$ and $\norm x=r$. This is (27).
  3. $$\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2/2}r^{d-1}\,\dd r=2^{\frac d2-1}\Gamma\big(\tfrac d2\big).$$Substitute $t=r^2/2$, $r=\sqrt{2t}$, $\dd r=\dd t/\sqrt{2t}$: the integral becomes $\int_0^\infty e^{-t}(2t)^{\frac{d-1}2}(2t)^{-\frac12}\,\dd t=2^{\frac d2-1}\int_0^\infty e^{-t}t^{\frac d2-1}\,\dd t$.
  4. $$c_d=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\,2^{\frac d2-1}\Gamma\big(\tfrac d2\big)=\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{2\pi^{d/2}}=\frac1{\mathcal A(\mathbb S^{d-1})}.$$$(2\pi)^{-d/2}2^{d/2}=\pi^{-d/2}$, and the formula of Remark 1 for the area. So $\E[f(\tilde X)]=\frac1{\mathcal A}\int f\,\dd\tilde\sigma$: (25)–(28). $\square$

Check. $d=2$: $c_2=\frac{\Gamma(1)}{2\pi}=\frac1{2\pi}$, the density of a uniform angle. $d=3$: $c_3=\frac{\sqrt\pi/2}{2\pi^{3/2}}=\frac1{4\pi}$. And the factorization shows more: the direction $\tilde X$ is independent of the radius $Z$ (the joint density is a product). This is used in Exercise 4 of §13.

Pitfall — two wrong ways to draw directions
  • Normalize a uniform point of the cube. $U/\norm U$ with $U$ uniform in $[-1,1]^d$ is not uniform: the corners of the cube stick out, so diagonal directions are too frequent. In the plane, the probability of falling within $5°$ of the diagonal direction $45°$ is $0.0402$, against $\frac{10}{360}=0.0278$ for the uniform law (exact formula $\frac14(1-\tan40°)$; Monte Carlo agrees).
  • Draw the spherical angles uniformly. In $\R^3$, taking the polar angle $\psi$ uniform on $[0,\pi]$ gives a height $\cos\psi$ with density $\frac1{\pi\sqrt{1-z^2}}$, not the uniform $\frac12$: $\P(\abs{\theta_3}>0.9)=\frac{2\arccos0.9}\pi\approx0.287$ instead of $0.1$.

Both bias the estimate of $SW_p$ towards some directions. Normalized Gaussian vectors (Proposition 2) are exact, cheap, and work in every dimension. (In $\R^2$ only, a uniform angle $\varphi\in[0,2\pi)$ is also exact.)

What it costs

In the course Nadjahi notes, Remark 5, p. 4

Remark 5 (Computational complexity).

  • Computing $\widehat{\mathbf{SW}}_p(\mu,\nu)$ costs $O(Kdn+Kn\log n)$ operations (projecting + sorting).
  • Recall that computing $\mathbf W_p(\mu,\nu)$ scales in $O(n^3)$ operations when $d>1$.

$\Rightarrow$ Computing $\widehat{\mathbf{SW}}_p$ is much cheaper than $\mathbf W_p$ for $n\gg d$ and moderate $K$

$Kdn$
The projections: $K$ directions × $n$ points × $d$ multiplications each (times $2$ for the two clouds).
$Kn\log n$
The sorts: $K$ directions, a sort of $n$ numbers each.
“$n\gg d$ and moderate $K$”
The condition matters. Exact transport costs $O(n^2d)$ to build the cost matrix plus $O(n^3)$ to solve; slicing costs $O(Kdn)$ to project. When $n\gg d$ and $K$ is moderate, slicing wins by far.
Worked example — when is slicing cheaper?
  • Point clouds ($d=3$, $n=2\,048$ points per shape, $K=100$): exact $n^3\approx8.6\times10^9$; sliced: projections $Kdn\approx6.1\times10^5$, sorts $Kn\log_2n\approx2.3\times10^6$. About $3\,000$ times cheaper.
  • Small batches of images ($d=3\,072$, $n=256$, $K=1\,000$): exact: cost matrix $n^2d\approx2.0\times10^8$ and assignment $n^3\approx1.7\times10^7$; sliced: projections $Kdn\approx7.9\times10^8$, sorts $\approx2.0\times10^6$. Here $nnot cheaper in operation count — its advantages are elsewhere (no assignment to solve, a dense matrix product that runs well on a GPU, a gradient that is easy to back-propagate, the statistical rate of §9). This is the regime of the notes’ warning “for $n\gg d$”.

How many directions? The Monte Carlo error

In the course slides, frame 12/12 (p. 18)

Still Some Practical Challenges! The previous rate applies to $\mathbf{SW}_p(\mu,\nu)=\big[\E_{\theta\sim\mathcal U(\mathbb S^{d-1})}\mathbf W_p^p(\mu^\theta,\nu^\theta)\big]^{1/p}$, which is intractable in practice. Practical approximation uses $k$ random projections:

$$\widehat{\mathbf{SW}}_p^p(\mu,\nu)=\frac1k\sum_{j=1}^k\mathbf W_p^p(\mu^{\theta_j},\nu^{\theta_j})$$

Monte Carlo approximation error

$$\E\big[\abs{\widehat{\mathbf{SW}}_p^p(\mu,\nu)-\mathbf{SW}_p^p(\mu,\nu)}\big]\le\frac1k\mathrm{Var}_\theta\big[\mathbf W_p^p(\mu^\theta,\nu^\theta)\big]$$

Trade-off: more projections $\Rightarrow$ better accuracy, but higher computational cost.

“the previous rate”
The statistical rate of frame 11/12 (§9 of this page): the slides discuss statistics before computation.
$\mu^\theta$
The slice $(p_\theta)_\sharp\mu$.
$\mathrm{Var}_\theta[\cdots]$
The variance of the slice cost $V(\theta)=W_p^p(\mu^\theta,\nu^\theta)$ when $\theta$ is a uniform random direction: how much the directions disagree.
$\E[\abs{\cdots}]$
Average over the random directions (the measures are fixed).
The correct statement

$\widehat{SW}_p^p$ is an average of $K$ independent copies of $V(\theta)=W_p^p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)$, whose mean is $SW_p^p$. Hence (refresher)

$$\E\big[\widehat{SW}_p^p\big]=SW_p^p,\qquad\E\big[\abs{\widehat{SW}_p^p-SW_p^p}^2\big]=\frac{\mathrm{Var}_\theta[V]}K,\qquad\E\big[\abs{\widehat{SW}_p^p-SW_p^p}\big]\le\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{Var}_\theta[V]}K}.$$
Note on the sources — the square root is missing on the slide

The slide bounds the mean absolute error by $\frac1k\mathrm{Var}_\theta$; that is the bound on the mean squared error. The correct bound on the mean absolute error is $\big(\mathrm{Var}_\theta/k\big)^{1/2}$, which is much larger when $k$ is large. Two checks:

  • Units. Multiply both clouds by $\lambda>0$: every slice cost is multiplied by $\lambda^p$, so the error is multiplied by $\lambda^p$ — and so is $\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}}$, while $\mathrm{Var}$ is multiplied by $\lambda^{2p}$. A bound “error $\le\mathrm{Var}/k$” cannot hold at all scales.
  • Numbers (the cross, $\mathrm{Var}=\frac12-\frac4{\pi^2}\approx0.0947$, $4\,000$ independent runs per $k$): the measured mean absolute error is $0.274$, $0.0617$, $0.0157$, $0.0077$ for $k=1,16,256,1024$, while $\mathrm{Var}/k=0.0947$, $0.0059$, $0.00037$, $0.00009$: the slide’s bound is violated by factors $3$ to $83$. The correct bound $\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}/k}=0.308$, $0.077$, $0.019$, $0.0096$ holds, and the ratio tends to $\sqrt{2/\pi}\approx0.80$ as the central limit theorem predicts.

The book has the correct square root (OT4ML Remark 10.17 below).

In the course OT4ML Remark 10.17, p. 207

Remark 10.17 (Directions are another sample budget). […] In computation, draw i.i.d. directions $\theta_1,\dots,\theta_L$ with law $\sigma$, independently of the data, and use the random estimator $\mathcal{SW}_1^L(\hat\al_n,\hat\be_m):=\frac1L\sum_{r=1}^L\mathcal W_1\big((P_{\theta_r})_\sharp\hat\al_n,(P_{\theta_r})_\sharp\hat\be_m\big)$. Conditionally on the samples, the summands are i.i.d., take values in $[0,2R]$, and have mean $\mathcal{SW}_1(\hat\al_n,\hat\be_m)$. Their conditional variance is at most $R^2$, so their mean absolute Monte-Carlo error is at most $R/\sqrt L$.

$[0,2R]$
Both measures live in the ball of radius $R$, so their slices live in $[-R,R]$, and no unit of mass moves by more than $2R$.
variance $\le R^2$
A random variable with values in an interval of length $\ell$ has variance at most $\ell^2/4$; here $\ell=2R$.
$R/\sqrt L$
$\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}/L}\le\sqrt{R^2/L}$: a bound that does not depend on the dimension, only on the size of the support.
Worked example — how many directions for 1%?

For the cross, the slice costs have mean $0.3634$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{0.0947}=0.308$: individual directions disagree a lot. The root-mean-square error with $K$ directions is $0.308/\sqrt K$. For a relative error of $10\%$ ($0.0363$) one needs $K\ge(0.308/0.0363)^2\approx72$ directions; for $1\%$, about $7\,200$. Each factor $10$ in accuracy costs a factor $100$ in directions — the price of Monte Carlo, but independent of the dimension $d$.

Pitfall — the root makes the estimator biased

$\widehat{SW}_p^p$ is unbiased for $SW_p^p$, but $\widehat{SW}_p=\big(\widehat{SW}_p^p\big)^{1/p}$ underestimates $SW_p$ on average: $s\mapsto s^{1/p}$ is concave, so $\E\big[(\widehat{SW}_p^p)^{1/p}\big]\le\big(\E\widehat{SW}_p^p\big)^{1/p}$ (Jensen). For the cross: $\E\,\widehat{SW}_2=0.527$, $0.587$, $0.599$, $0.6026$ with $K=1,4,16,256$, against $SW_2=0.6028$ (simulation, $10^5$ runs). Average $p$-th powers, take the root at the end, and use many directions when the value itself matters.

In machine learning — sliced losses for generative models

The Monte Carlo estimate is a differentiable loss. For a generator $x_i=T(w)(z_i)$ and a batch of data $y_i$: draw $K$ directions, project both batches, sort, and use $\mathcal L(w)=\frac1K\sum_j\frac1n\sum_i\big(\ps{\theta_j}{x}_{(i)}-\ps{\theta_j}{y}_{(i)}\big)^2$. The sort is a permutation that is constant almost everywhere, so the gradient simply flows through the sorted values: the point $x_i$ receives the gradient $\frac2{Kn}\sum_j\big(\ps{\theta_j}{x_i}-\text{its sorted partner along }\theta_j\big)\,\theta_j$, which is back-propagated into $w$. New directions are drawn at every step. This is the principle of sliced-Wasserstein generators and auto-encoders (slide 10/12: Deshpande et al. 2018; Wu et al. 2019; Kolouri et al. 2019b). In a sliced-Wasserstein auto-encoder, the sliced loss replaces the KL term of a variational auto-encoder: it pushes the batch of latent codes towards samples of the prior (a Gaussian, a circle…), with no adversarial network. On the slide, a progressive-growing GAN trained with a sliced loss reaches FID scores $5.5$ (CelebA-HQ faces) and $8.0$ (LSUN bedrooms), against $7.5$ and $8.4$ for its Wasserstein-GAN counterpart (Wu et al. 2019; lower is better). For 3D point clouds ($d=3$, a few thousand points per shape) the sliced distance is a natural reconstruction loss, cheap compared with exact transport (first example above); Nguyen, Bariletto and Ho (2024, in the slides’ references) study better-than-random directions for exactly this case.

Reflexes
  • Uniform directions → normalize standard Gaussian vectors (never a cube, never uniform angles in $d\ge3$).
  • Monte Carlo error → $\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}/K}$; $10\times$ more accuracy costs $100\times$ more directions.
  • Unbiased → average $W_p^p$ over directions; take the root only at the end.
  • Cost → $O(Kdn+Kn\log n)$; compare with $O(n^2d+n^3)$ before claiming a speed-up.

Two clouds live in the unit ball of $\R^{50}$. How many directions guarantee a mean absolute Monte Carlo error at most $0.01$ on $\widehat{SW}_1$?

Answer

By OT4ML Remark 10.17 with $R=1$, the error is at most $1/\sqrt L$; $1/\sqrt L\le0.01$ requires $L\ge10\,000$ directions. The dimension $50$ plays no role in this bound (it only enters the cost of each projection). In practice the variance is often much smaller than $R^2$ and far fewer directions suffice.
