Exercise 1 — Comparing isotropic centered Gaussians

In the course exercise sheet, Exercise 1

Exercise 1 (Comparing isotropic centered Gaussians). Consider $\mu=\N(\mathbf0,\sigma^2\mathbf I_d)$ and $\nu=\N(\mathbf0,\tau^2\mathbf I_d)$, with $\sigma\ge0$, $\tau\ge0$.

  1. Compute the Sliced-Wasserstein distance of order 2 between $\mu$ and $\nu$.
  2. Compare the obtained expression with the Wasserstein distance of order 2 between $\mu$ and $\nu$.
Reminder — $W_2$ between Gaussians (Lesson 1)

On the line, $W_2^2\big(\N(a,s^2),\N(b,t^2)\big)=(a-b)^2+(s-t)^2$ ($s,t\ge0$ standard deviations). In $\R^d$, $W_2^2\big(\N(m_1,\Sigma_1),\N(m_2,\Sigma_2)\big)=\norm{m_1-m_2}^2+\tr\big(\Sigma_1+\Sigma_2-2(\Sigma_2^{1/2}\Sigma_1\Sigma_2^{1/2})^{1/2}\big)$; when the two covariances commute (for instance, both diagonal), the trace term is $\sum_i(\sqrt{\lambda_i}-\sqrt{\mu_i})^2$ over their common eigenvalues. And a linear form of a Gaussian vector is Gaussian: $\ps{\theta}{X}\sim\N(\ps{\theta}{m},\theta\T\Sigma\theta)$. See the refresher and Reminders: closed forms.

Hint

(a) What is the slice of $\N(0,\sigma^2\Id)$ in the direction $\theta$? Note that $\theta\T(\sigma^2\Id)\theta=\sigma^2\norm\theta^2$. Then use the 1D formula $W_2^2(\N(a,s^2),\N(b,t^2))=(a-b)^2+(s-t)^2$. (b) The covariances commute: the Bures term is computed eigenvalue by eigenvalue. Or exhibit the map $x\mapsto\frac\tau\sigma x$.

Solution

(a)

  1. For every $\theta\in\mathbb S^{d-1}$: $(p_\theta)_\sharp\mu=\N(0,\sigma^2)$ and $(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu=\N(0,\tau^2)$.Slice of a Gaussian: $\N\big(\ps{\theta}{0},\theta\T(\sigma^2\Id)\theta\big)=\N(0,\sigma^2\norm\theta^2)$ and $\norm\theta=1$ (§3). (If $\sigma=0$, $\mu=\delta_0$ and its slices are $\delta_0=\N(0,0)$: the formulas below still hold.)
  2. $$\mathbf W_2^2\big((p_\theta)_\sharp\mu,(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu\big)=(0-0)^2+(\sigma-\tau)^2=(\sigma-\tau)^2\qquad\text{for every }\theta.$$One-dimensional Gaussian formula, with standard deviations $\sigma,\tau\ge0$.
  3. $$\mathbf{SW}_2(\mu,\nu)=\Big(\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}(\sigma-\tau)^2\,\dd\mathcal U_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}(\theta)\Big)^{1/2}=\abs{\sigma-\tau}.$$The integrand does not depend on $\theta$, and $\mathcal U$ is a probability.

So the sliced distance between two isotropic centred Gaussians is $\abs{\sigma-\tau}$ in every dimension.

(b)

  1. $$\mathbf W_2^2(\mu,\nu)=\tr\big(\sigma^2\Id+\tau^2\Id-2\big((\tau\Id)\sigma^2\Id(\tau\Id)\big)^{1/2}\big)=\tr\big((\sigma^2+\tau^2-2\sigma\tau)\Id\big)=d(\sigma-\tau)^2.$$Gaussian formula with equal means: $W_2^2=\B(\Sigma_\mu,\Sigma_\nu)^2$ (refresher), and $(\sigma^2\tau^2\Id)^{1/2}=\sigma\tau\Id$.
  2. Direct check without the formula: the map $T(x)=\frac\tau\sigma x$ (for $\sigma>0$) pushes $\mu$ to $\nu$, and it is the gradient of the convex function $\frac\tau{2\sigma}\norm x^2$, hence optimal (Brenier); its cost is $\E\norm{X-\frac\tau\sigma X}^2=\big(1-\frac\tau\sigma\big)^2\sigma^2d=d(\sigma-\tau)^2$.$\E\norm X^2=\tr(\sigma^2\Id)=d\sigma^2$ for $X\sim\mu$.
  3. $$\mathbf W_2(\mu,\nu)=\sqrt d\,\abs{\sigma-\tau}=\sqrt d\;\mathbf{SW}_2(\mu,\nu).$$Steps 1 and (a).

Comment. $\mathbf{SW}_2^2=\frac1d\mathbf W_2^2$: the comparison $\mathbf{SW}_2^2\le c_{d,2}\mathbf W_2^2$ with $c_{d,2}=\frac1d$ (§6) is an equality here. The reason: the optimal map $x\mapsto\frac\tau\sigma x$ projects, on every slice, to the increasing map $s\mapsto\frac\tau\sigma s$, so the projected optimal plan is optimal on every slice (this is the equality case of Exercise 2). In high dimension the sliced distance between isotropic Gaussians is $\sqrt d$ times smaller than $W_2$: for $\sigma=0.5$, $\tau=1.5$ in $\R^3$, $\mathbf{SW}_2=1$ and $\mathbf W_2=\sqrt3\approx1.732$; in $\R^{100}$, $\mathbf{SW}_2=1$ and $\mathbf W_2=10$. (Checked with SciPy’s matrix square root.) This is the proportional case of OT4ML Remark 11.31 (§7).

Exercise 2 — Projected optimal transport plans

In the course exercise sheet, Exercise 2

Exercise 2 (Projected optimal transport plans). Let $\mu,\nu\in\mathcal P_2(\R^d)$ (measures on $\R^d$ with finite second moments). Denote by $\gamma$ an optimal transport plan between $\mu$ and $\nu$. For any $\theta\in\mathbb S^{d-1}$, define $\gamma_\theta=(p_\theta\otimes p_\theta)_\sharp\gamma$.

  1. Show that $\gamma_\theta$ is a transport plan between $(p_\theta)_\sharp\mu$ and $(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu$.
  2. Is $\gamma_\theta$ an optimal transport plan between $(p_\theta)_\sharp\mu$ and $(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu$?
Reminder — marginals and push-forwards

A plan $\gamma$ on $\R^d\times\R^d$ is a coupling of $\mu$ and $\nu$ when its marginals are $\mu$ and $\nu$: $\int g(x)\,\dd\gamma(x,y)=\int g\,\dd\mu$ and $\int g(y)\,\dd\gamma(x,y)=\int g\,\dd\nu$ for every bounded measurable $g$. To identify a push-forward $\pf{T}{\gamma}$, integrate a test function: $\int h\,\dd(\pf{T}{\gamma})=\int h\circ T\,\dd\gamma$. Two measures that give the same integral to every bounded measurable function are equal. See Reminders: couplings and push-forward.

Hint

(a) Compute the first marginal of $\gamma_\theta$: test it against a bounded function $g$ of one variable, and use the change of variables (1) twice. Or think with random variables: if $(X,Y)\sim\gamma$, what is the law of $\ps{\theta}{X}$? (b) On the line, a plan is optimal for the quadratic cost exactly when it is monotone. Look for an optimal plan in the plane whose two arrows cross after projection: two pairs of points suffice.

Solution

(a) Write $p_\theta\otimes p_\theta$ for the map $(x,y)\mapsto\big(\ps{\theta}{x},\ps{\theta}{y}\big)$ from $\R^d\times\R^d$ to $\R\times\R$.

  1. For every bounded measurable $g:\R\to\R$, $\ \displaystyle\int_{\R\times\R}g(s)\,\dd\gamma_\theta(s,t)=\int_{\R^d\times\R^d}g\big(\ps{\theta}{x}\big)\,\dd\gamma(x,y).$Change of variables for the push-forward by $p_\theta\otimes p_\theta$, applied to the function $(s,t)\mapsto g(s)$.
  2. $$=\int_{\R^d}g\big(\ps{\theta}{x}\big)\,\dd\mu(x)$$The integrand depends on $x$ only, and the first marginal of $\gamma$ is $\mu$ ($\gamma$ is a coupling of $\mu$ and $\nu$).
  3. $$=\int_\R g(s)\,\dd\big((p_\theta)_\sharp\mu\big)(s).$$Property (1) of the notes (§3).
  4. So the first marginal of $\gamma_\theta$ is $(p_\theta)_\sharp\mu$; in the same way (with $g(t)$ and the second marginal $\nu$), the second marginal is $(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu$. $\gamma_\theta$ is a probability on $\R\times\R$ (push-forward of a probability), hence a transport plan between the two slices. It has finite second moments because $\abs{\ps{\theta}{x}}\le\norm x$. $\square$Two measures that integrate all bounded measurable functions in the same way are equal.

In one line, with random variables: if $(X,Y)\sim\gamma$ then $X\sim\mu$ and $Y\sim\nu$, so $\ps{\theta}{X}\sim(p_\theta)_\sharp\mu$ and $\ps{\theta}{Y}\sim(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu$, and $\gamma_\theta$ is the joint law of $\big(\ps{\theta}{X},\ps{\theta}{Y}\big)$. Note that optimality of $\gamma$ was not used: every plan projects to a plan.

(b) No, not in general.

  1. Take $d=2$, $\mu=\frac12(\delta_{(0,0)}+\delta_{(1,3)})$, $\nu=\frac12(\delta_{(1,0)}+\delta_{(0,3)})$. The optimal plan is $\gamma=\frac12\big(\delta_{((0,0),(1,0))}+\delta_{((1,3),(0,3))}\big)$, of cost $\frac{1+1}2=1$.There are two couplings that are permutations: the straight one costs $1$ per pair, the crossed one $(0,0)\to(0,3)$, $(1,3)\to(1,0)$ costs $9$ per pair; with equal weights an optimal plan can be taken among these two (Birkhoff, Lesson 2). An LP solver gives $1$.
  2. For $\theta=e_1=(1,0)$: $(p_\theta)_\sharp\mu=(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu=\frac12(\delta_0+\delta_1)$, and $\gamma_\theta=\frac12(\delta_{(0,1)}+\delta_{(1,0)})$: it sends $0\to1$ and $1\to0$, at cost $\frac{1+1}2=1$.Project both ends of each arrow on the first axis: $(0,0)\to(1,0)$ becomes $0\to1$, $(1,3)\to(0,3)$ becomes $1\to0$.
  3. But the two slices are equal, so the optimal plan between them costs $0$ (keep every point in place). Hence $\gamma_\theta$ is not optimal.$W_2$ of a measure with itself is $0$. Equivalently: $\gamma_\theta$ crosses (it is decreasing), and on the line optimal plans are monotone (§2).
  4. For $\theta=e_2$: the slices are $\frac12(\delta_0+\delta_3)$ for both, and $\gamma_\theta$ sends $0\to0$, $3\to3$: optimal. In this example $\gamma_\theta$ is optimal exactly for the directions whose angle is between $\arctan\frac13\approx18.4°$ and $161.6°$ — about $79.5\%$ of them — that is, when the two arrows keep their order after projection.Monotone iff $\ps{\theta}{(1,3)-(0,0)}$ and $\ps{\theta}{(0,3)-(1,0)}$ have the same sign, i.e. $(\cos\varphi+3\sin\varphi)(3\sin\varphi-\cos\varphi)\ge0$. Checked on $36\,000$ angles.

When is the projected plan optimal for every $\theta$? When the optimal plan is a map that every projection turns into an increasing map: in dimension $1$; for $x\mapsto\lambda x+b$ with $\lambda>0$ (translations and dilations, Exercise 1 and Exercise 3); for Gaussians, only along the eigenvectors of the Brenier matrix (this is how Min-SW recovers $W_2$ on Gaussians, §10).

What remains true is the inequality used in Proposition 1: $\mathbf W_2^2\big((p_\theta)_\sharp\mu,(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu\big)\le\int\abs{s-t}^2\,\dd\gamma_\theta=\int\ps{\theta}{x-y}^2\,\dd\gamma$, and the right-hand side averages to $\frac1d\mathbf W_2^2$. Consequently $\mathbf{SW}_2^2=\frac1d\mathbf W_2^2$ if and only if $\gamma_\theta$ is optimal for almost every $\theta$. In the example, $\mathbf{SW}_2^2\approx0.364<\frac12\mathbf W_2^2=0.5$, while the projected plan costs exactly $0.5$ on average.

Exercise 3 — Translation decomposition for $\mathbf{SW}_2$

In the course exercise sheet, Exercise 3

Exercise 3 (Translation decomposition for $\mathbf{SW}_2$). Let $\mu,\nu\in\mathcal P_2(\R^d)$ (measures on $\R^d$ with finite second moments), and denote by $\mu_0,\nu_0$ the respective centered versions.

  1. Prove that for any $\theta\sim\mathcal U_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}$, $\E_{s\sim(p_\theta)_\sharp\mu}[s]=\ps{\theta}{\E_{x\sim\mu}[x]}$ and $\E_{t\sim(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu}[t]=\ps{\theta}{\E_{y\sim\nu}[y]}$.
  2. Prove that $((p_\theta)_\sharp\mu)_0=(p_\theta)_\sharp\mu_0$ and $((p_\theta)_\sharp\nu)_0=(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu_0$, with $\mu_0=(T_{-u})_\sharp\mu$ and $\nu_0=(T_{-v})_\sharp\nu$ for a unique pair $(u,v)\in\R^d\times\R^d$ (whose values must be precised).
  3. Show that $\mathbf{SW}_2(\mu,\nu)$ admits the following decomposition, $$\mathbf{SW}_2(\mu,\nu)^2=\frac1d\norm{u-v}^2+\mathbf{SW}_2(\mu_0,\nu_0)^2$$

(Hint: $\E_{\theta\sim\mathcal U_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}}[\theta\theta^T]=(1/d)\,\mathbf I_d$.)

Reading the sheet

In (a), “for any $\theta\sim\mathcal U_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}$” should be read “for any $\theta\in\mathbb S^{d-1}$”: the identity holds for every fixed direction, there is nothing random in it (the uniform law only appears in (c)). $T_w(x)=x+w$ is the translation by $w$, and a centered measure is one with mean $0$.

Reminder — translating a measure on the line

If $\rho$ is a probability on $\R$ and $\rho'=\pf{(T_w)}{\rho}$ is its translate by $w$ ($T_w(s)=s+w$), then the mean moves by $w$ and the quantile function too: $F_{\rho'}^{-1}=F_\rho^{-1}+w$. The mean is the integral of the quantile function, $\int_0^1F_\rho^{-1}(r)\,\dd r=\int s\,\dd\rho(s)$, because $F_\rho^{-1}$ pushes the uniform law on $(0,1)$ to $\rho$. And $W_2^2(\rho,\eta)=\int_0^1\abs{F_\rho^{-1}-F_\eta^{-1}}^2$ (§2). See Reminders: CDF and quantiles.

Hint

(a) Property (1) with $g(s)=s$. (b) The mean of $(T_{-w})_\sharp\mu$ is $m_\mu-w$; and $p_\theta\circ T_{-w}=T_{-\ps{\theta}{w}}\circ p_\theta$ (projecting a translated point = translating the projection). (c) On the line, if $\rho$ has mean $a$, its quantile function is $a$ plus that of $\rho_0$, and $\int_0^1F_{\rho_0}^{-1}=0$ (the mean of $\rho_0$). Expand the square in the quantile formula.

Solution

Write $m_\mu=\E_{x\sim\mu}[x]$ and $m_\nu=\E_{y\sim\nu}[y]$ (finite since $\mu,\nu\in\mathcal P_2\subset\mathcal P_1$).

(a) $\ \E_{s\sim(p_\theta)_\sharp\mu}[s]=\int_\R s\,\dd\big((p_\theta)_\sharp\mu\big)(s)=\int_{\R^d}\ps{\theta}{x}\,\dd\mu(x)=\Big\langle\theta,\int x\,\dd\mu(x)\Big\rangle=\ps{\theta}{m_\mu}$: property (1) with $g(s)=s$ (integrable because $\abs{\ps{\theta}{x}}\le\norm x$), then linearity of the integral. Same for $\nu$.

(b)

  1. The mean of $(T_{-w})_\sharp\mu$ is $m_\mu-w$, so it is centred if and only if $w=m_\mu$.$\int x\,\dd(T_{-w})_\sharp\mu=\int(x-w)\,\dd\mu=m_\mu-w$. Hence the unique choice is $u=m_\mu$, $v=m_\nu$: $(u,v)=(\E_\mu[x],\E_\nu[y])$.
  2. $p_\theta\circ T_{-u}=T_{-\ps{\theta}{u}}\circ p_\theta$, as maps $\R^d\to\R$.$\ps{\theta}{x-u}=\ps{\theta}{x}-\ps{\theta}{u}$.
  3. $$(p_\theta)_\sharp\mu_0=(p_\theta\circ T_{-u})_\sharp\mu=(T_{-\ps{\theta}{u}})_\sharp\big((p_\theta)_\sharp\mu\big).$$Push-forwards compose: $(f\circ g)_\sharp=f_\sharp\circ g_\sharp$; then step 2.
  4. This is $(p_\theta)_\sharp\mu$ translated by $-\ps{\theta}{u}=-\ps{\theta}{m_\mu}$, which by (a) is minus its mean: it is the centred version $((p_\theta)_\sharp\mu)_0$. Same for $\nu$ with $v$. $\square$On the line, centring means translating by minus the mean, and the mean of the slice is $\ps{\theta}{m_\mu}$ by (a).

(c) First a one-dimensional lemma: if $\rho,\eta$ are measures on $\R$ with finite second moments and means $a,b$, then $W_2^2(\rho,\eta)=(a-b)^2+W_2^2(\rho_0,\eta_0)$.

  1. $F_\rho^{-1}=a+F_{\rho_0}^{-1}$ and $F_\eta^{-1}=b+F_{\eta_0}^{-1}$.Translating a measure by $a$ translates its quantile function by $a$.
  2. $$W_2^2(\rho,\eta)=\int_0^1\big((a-b)+F_{\rho_0}^{-1}(r)-F_{\eta_0}^{-1}(r)\big)^2\,\dd r .$$Quantile formula (§2).
  3. $$=(a-b)^2+2(a-b)\int_0^1\big(F_{\rho_0}^{-1}-F_{\eta_0}^{-1}\big)\,\dd r+W_2^2(\rho_0,\eta_0)=(a-b)^2+W_2^2(\rho_0,\eta_0).$$Expand the square. $\int_0^1F_{\rho_0}^{-1}(r)\,\dd r$ is the mean of $\rho_0$ (the quantile function pushes the uniform law on $(0,1)$ to $\rho_0$), which is $0$; same for $\eta_0$.

Now the decomposition:

  1. $$\mathbf W_2^2\big((p_\theta)_\sharp\mu,(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu\big)=\ps{\theta}{u-v}^2+\mathbf W_2^2\big((p_\theta)_\sharp\mu_0,(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu_0\big)\qquad\text{for every }\theta.$$The lemma with $\rho=(p_\theta)_\sharp\mu$, $\eta=(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu$: their means are $\ps{\theta}{u}$, $\ps{\theta}{v}$ by (a), and their centred versions are $(p_\theta)_\sharp\mu_0$, $(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu_0$ by (b).
  2. $$\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}\ps{\theta}{u-v}^2\,\dd\mathcal U_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}(\theta)=(u-v)\T\E[\theta\theta\T](u-v)=\frac1d\norm{u-v}^2.$$$\ps{\theta}{z}^2=z\T\theta\theta\T z$, linearity of the expectation, and the sheet’s hint.
  3. $$\mathbf{SW}_2(\mu,\nu)^2=\frac1d\norm{u-v}^2+\mathbf{SW}_2(\mu_0,\nu_0)^2.\qquad\square$$Average step 1 over $\theta\sim\mathcal U_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}$ and use step 2.

Numerical check. $\mu=\frac13(\delta_{(0,0)}+\delta_{(2,1)}+\delta_{(1,3)})$ and $\nu=\frac13(\delta_{(4,1)}+\delta_{(5,3)}+\delta_{(6,0)})$: $u=(1,\frac43)$, $v=(5,\frac43)$, $\frac12\norm{u-v}^2=8$. A quadrature on $200\,000$ directions gives $\mathbf{SW}_2^2(\mu,\nu)=8.2027$ and $\mathbf{SW}_2^2(\mu_0,\nu_0)=0.2027$: the difference is $8$ up to $10^{-12}$.

Comment. The same argument in $\R^d$ (expand $\E\norm{(X-u)-(Y-v)+(u-v)}^2$ under any coupling) gives $\mathbf W_2^2(\mu,\nu)=\norm{u-v}^2+\mathbf W_2^2(\mu_0,\nu_0)$; here $16.667=16+0.667$. So slicing divides the mean part by $d$ and keeps a sliced shape part: this is why the signal of a shift decays like $1/\sqrt d$ (§9), and the Gaussian formula of §7 is the case where both $\mu,\nu$ are Gaussian.
