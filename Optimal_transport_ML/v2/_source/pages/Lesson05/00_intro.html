What you will be able to do after this page
  1. Explain why exact optimal transport is a poor loss for machine learning — about $n^3$ operations per evaluation, and an estimation error that decays like $n^{-1/d}$ — and why the real line is the exception: there, transport is a sort.
  2. Slice a measure: compute $(P_\theta)_\#\al$ for point clouds, Gaussians and densities, and relate the family of all slices to the Radon transform and to tomography.
  3. Define the sliced Wasserstein distance $SW_p$, explain why one averages over the sphere with the uniform law, and compute it by hand on small examples (Diracs, translations, a “cross”).
  4. Prove that $SW_p$ is a distance (Minkowski, then the Fourier slice identity and Cramér–Wold), and compare it with $W_p$: $SW_p^p\le\kappa_{d,p}W_p^p$ with $\kappa_{d,2}=1/d$, and only a dimension-dependent reverse bound.
  5. Compute $SW_p$ in practice by Monte Carlo over directions (normalized Gaussian vectors), with cost $O(Kdn+Kn\log n)$ and error $\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}/K}$, and prove the dimension-free statistical rate $n^{-1/2}$ of $SW_1$.
  6. Know the variants (max-sliced, subspace, Min-SW and lifted plans, generalized), the sliced gradient flow and Radon barycenters, what all this is used for in machine learning, and solve the three exercises of the sheet.

The story of the lecture in seven steps

Optimal transport in dimension $d$ is slow and data-hungry, but on the line it is a sort; so project both distributions on many lines, sort, and average: the result is a true distance, cheap to compute and estimated at the same rate in every dimension — at the price of a weaker geometry.

  1. The obstacle (§1). Machine learning uses transport as a loss between a model and data, at every training step. Exactly, it is a linear program with $n^2$ unknowns, solved in $O(n^3)$; and from samples it converges like $n^{-1/d}$: the curse of dimensionality.
  2. The exception (§2). On the line, the optimal plan matches the $i$-th smallest point of one cloud with the $i$-th smallest of the other: one sort, $O(n\log n)$.
  3. Slicing (§3–§4). Project both measures on a direction $\theta$ (push-forward by $x\mapsto\ps{\theta}{x}$), solve the one-dimensional problem, and average over all directions of the sphere: this is $SW_p$. The family of all slices is the Radon transform, the mathematics of the CT scanner.
  4. Is it a good distance? (§5–§7). Yes: it is a distance (Minkowski, then Fourier and Cramér–Wold), with the same convergent sequences as $W_p$. But it is smaller — $SW_2\le W_2/\sqrt d$ — and the reverse comparison loses a power that depends on $d$. Gaussians give closed forms.
  5. Computation and statistics (§8–§9). Replace the average over the sphere by $K$ random directions (normalized Gaussian vectors): cost $O(Kdn+Kn\log n)$, error of order $K^{-1/2}$; from $n$ samples, the error is of order $n^{-1/2}$ whatever the dimension.
  6. Variants (§10). Keep the best direction (max-sliced), project on subspaces, lift one slice to a transport plan (Min-SW), bend the projections (generalized slicing).
  7. Moving and averaging (§11). Move particles by sorting along random directions (the sliced flow: colour transfer, sampling), average slices direction by direction (Radon barycenters). Then the exercise sheet (§12) and more exercises (§13).

Refresher — optimal transport on the line: sort, or match quantiles (Lesson 1)

For a probability $\mu$ on $\R$, the cumulative distribution function is $F_\mu(x)=\mu\big((-\infty,x]\big)$ and the quantile function is its generalized inverse $F_\mu^{-1}(t)=\inf\{x:\ F_\mu(x)\ge t\}$, $t\in(0,1)$. For a cost $\abs{x-y}^p$ with $p\ge1$, the optimal plan on the line is the monotone one (the $t$-th quantile goes to the $t$-th quantile), and

$$W_p^p(\mu,\nu)=\int_0^1\abs{F_\mu^{-1}(t)-F_\nu^{-1}(t)}^p\,\dd t .$$

For two clouds of $n$ points with equal weights $\frac1n$, this reads: sort both lists and match by rank, $W_p^p=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\abs{x_{(i)}-y_{(i)}}^p$, where $x_{(1)}\le\dots\le x_{(n)}$ are the sorted points. Used here in every slice. See Lesson 1, Lesson 3, and Reminders: CDF and quantiles, sorting.

Refresher — push-forward and the change of variables

If $T:\X\to\Y$ is a map and $\al$ a measure on $\X$, the push-forward $\pf{T}{\al}$ moves the mass at $x$ to $T(x)$: $(\pf{T}{\al})(B)=\al\big(T^{-1}(B)\big)$. Two equivalent ways to use it:

$$\int g\,\dd(\pf{T}{\al})=\int g\big(T(x)\big)\,\dd\al(x)\quad\text{for every bounded }g,\qquad X\sim\al\ \Longrightarrow\ T(X)\sim\pf{T}{\al}.$$

Discrete measures: $\pf{T}{\big(\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i}\big)}=\sum_ia_i\delta_{T(x_i)}$ — the weights travel with their points (and add up if two points land on the same image). Used here to define the slices $(P_\theta)_\#\al$ (§3) and to project couplings (§6, Exercise 2 of the sheet). See Reminders: push-forward.

Refresher — couplings, $W_p$, and Minkowski’s inequality (Lessons 2–3)

A coupling of $\al$ and $\be$ is a joint law $\pi$ of a pair $(X,Y)$ with $X\sim\al$ and $Y\sim\be$; the set of couplings is $\U(\al,\be)$. For $p\ge1$,

$$W_p(\al,\be)=\Big(\min_{\pi\in\U(\al,\be)}\int\norm{x-y}^p\,\dd\pi(x,y)\Big)^{1/p}$$

is a distance (Lesson 3 §2). Two reflexes: any coupling gives an upper bound on $W_p^p$; and the $p$-th root is what makes the triangle inequality true, through Minkowski’s inequality $\E\big[\abs{U+V}^p\big]^{1/p}\le\E\big[\abs{U}^p\big]^{1/p}+\E\big[\abs{V}^p\big]^{1/p}$, valid for $p\ge1$ (refresher in Lesson 3, Reminders: norms and inequalities). Used here in §5 (triangle inequality of $SW_p$) and §6 (comparison with $W_p$).

Refresher — Gaussians: affine images and $W_2$ (Lesson 1)

If $X\sim\N(m,\Sigma)$ in $\R^d$ and $\theta\in\R^d$, then $\ps{\theta}{X}$ is a real Gaussian with mean $\ps{\theta}{m}$ and variance $\theta\T\Sigma\theta$: $\ps{\theta}{X}\sim\N\big(\ps{\theta}{m},\,\theta\T\Sigma\theta\big)$ (a linear image of a Gaussian vector is Gaussian; mean and variance by linearity). On the line,

$$W_2^2\big(\N(a,s^2),\N(b,t^2)\big)=(a-b)^2+(s-t)^2,$$

the optimal map being $x\mapsto b+\frac ts(x-a)$. In $\R^d$: $W_2^2=\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+\B(\Sigma_\al,\Sigma_\be)^2$ with the Bures term $\B(\Sigma,\Lambda)^2=\tr\big(\Sigma+\Lambda-2(\Lambda^{1/2}\Sigma\Lambda^{1/2})^{1/2}\big)$. Used here in §7 and in Exercise 1 of the sheet. See Lesson 3’s refresher, Reminders: Gaussian vectors, closed forms.

Refresher — the sphere, polar coordinates and the Gamma function

The unit sphere of $\R^d$ is $\mathbb S^{d-1}=\{u\in\R^d:\ \norm{u}=1\}$ (it has dimension $d-1$: a circle in $\R^2$, the usual sphere in $\R^3$). Every $x\ne0$ is written $x=r\theta$ with $r=\norm{x}>0$ and $\theta=x/\norm{x}\in\mathbb S^{d-1}$, and Lebesgue measure splits as $\dd x=r^{d-1}\,\dd r\,\dd\tilde\sigma(\theta)$, where $\tilde\sigma$ is the surface measure of the sphere (in $\R^2$: $\dd x=r\,\dd r\,\dd\varphi$). Its total mass is the surface area

$$\abs{\mathbb S^{d-1}}=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)},\qquad\Gamma(s)=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-t}t^{s-1}\,\dd t,$$

with $\Gamma(1)=1$, $\Gamma(\frac12)=\sqrt\pi$, $\Gamma(s+1)=s\,\Gamma(s)$. Checks: $d=2$ gives $2\pi/\Gamma(1)=2\pi$ (the length of the unit circle), $d=3$ gives $2\pi^{3/2}/(\frac{\sqrt\pi}2)=4\pi$. The uniform probability on the sphere is $\sigma=\tilde\sigma/\abs{\mathbb S^{d-1}}$; in $\R^2$ it is $\theta=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)$ with $\varphi$ uniform on $[0,2\pi)$. Two facts are used again and again:

  • Rotation invariance: if $\theta\sim\sigma$ and $O$ is an orthogonal matrix, then $O\theta\sim\sigma$. Consequence: for a fixed unit vector $e$, $\ps{\theta}{e}$ has the same law as the first coordinate $\theta_1$.
  • Second moments: $\E[\theta\theta\T]=\frac1d\Id$, i.e. $\E[\theta_i\theta_j]=0$ for $i\ne j$ and $\E[\theta_i^2]=\frac1d$. Proof: flipping the sign of the $i$-th coordinate is orthogonal, so $\E[\theta_i\theta_j]=-\E[\theta_i\theta_j]=0$; permuting coordinates is orthogonal, so all $\E[\theta_i^2]$ are equal; and they sum to $\E\norm{\theta}^2=1$.

See Reminders: change of variables.

Refresher — the Fourier transform of a probability measure

With the convention of the notes, the Fourier transform of a probability measure $\mu$ on $\R^d$ is the function

$$\mathcal F\mu(\xi)=\int_{\R^d}e^{-2i\pi\ps{\xi}{x}}\,\dd\mu(x),\qquad\xi\in\R^d .$$

(Probabilists use the characteristic function $\phi_X(t)=\E\big[e^{i\ps{t}{X}}\big]$ of $X\sim\mu$: the same object, $\phi_X(t)=\mathcal F\mu(-t/2\pi)$.) Three facts: $\abs{\mathcal F\mu}\le1$; $\mathcal F\mu$ is continuous (dominated convergence: the integrand is continuous in $\xi$ and bounded by $1$); and it determines the measure: $\mathcal F\mu=\mathcal F\nu$ everywhere implies $\mu=\nu$ (injectivity). Example: for $\N(m,s^2)$ on $\R$, $\mathcal F\mu(\omega)=e^{-2i\pi\omega m-2\pi^2s^2\omega^2}$. Used here in §5 to prove that $SW_p(\al,\be)=0$ forces $\al=\be$.

Refresher — Monte Carlo averages: mean, variance, error

Let $V_1,\dots,V_K$ be independent copies of a random variable $V$ with mean $m$ and variance $v$. The average $\bar V=\frac1K\sum_kV_k$ satisfies

$$\E\bar V=m,\qquad\mathrm{Var}\,\bar V=\frac vK,\qquad\E\abs{\bar V-m}\le\big(\E\abs{\bar V-m}^2\big)^{1/2}=\sqrt{\frac vK}.$$

The inequality is Cauchy–Schwarz (or Jensen for the convex function $s\mapsto s^2$). By the central limit theorem, $\sqrt K(\bar V-m)$ is approximately $\N(0,v)$ for large $K$, so $\E\abs{\bar V-m}\approx\sqrt{2/\pi}\,\sqrt{v/K}\approx0.80\sqrt{v/K}$. Used here in §8 (random directions) and §9 (random samples). See Reminders: laws of large numbers.
