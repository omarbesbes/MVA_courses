7. Gaussians OT4ML Prop. 11.30 and Remark 11.31, pp. 234–235

Every slice of a Gaussian is a one-dimensional Gaussian, so $SW_2$ between Gaussians is an average over directions of the 1D formula $(a-b)^2+(s-t)^2$: the means contribute $\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2/d$, the covariances an angular integral with closed forms in a few cases.

The notes and slides do not treat Gaussians; the exercise sheet does (Exercise 1), and the book gives the general formula. It is the best test bed to see how far $SW_2$ is from $W_2$.

The formula

In the course OT4ML Prop. 11.30, p. 234

Proposition 11.30 (Sliced Wasserstein between Gaussians). Let $\al=\N(m_\al,\Sigma_\al)$ and $\be=\N(m_\be,\Sigma_\be)$ on $\R^d$, with positive-semidefinite covariance matrices. Then

$$\mathcal{SW}_2(\al,\be)^2=\frac{\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2}d+\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}\Big(\sqrt{\theta\T\Sigma_\al\theta}-\sqrt{\theta\T\Sigma_\be\theta}\Big)^2\,\dd\sigma(\theta).\qquad(11.29)$$
$\theta\T\Sigma\theta$
The variance of the slice in the direction $\theta$; its square root is the standard deviation of the slice.
first term
The means: they only contribute $\frac1d$ of their squared distance — the translation computation of §4.
second term
The shapes: in each direction, the squared difference of the two standard deviations, averaged over the sphere.
Proof
  1. $\al_\theta=\N\big(\ps{\theta}{m_\al},\ \theta\T\Sigma_\al\theta\big)$ and $\be_\theta=\N\big(\ps{\theta}{m_\be},\ \theta\T\Sigma_\be\theta\big)$.Linear image of a Gaussian vector (refresher; §3).
  2. $$W_2^2(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)=\ps{\theta}{m_\al-m_\be}^2+\Big(\sqrt{\theta\T\Sigma_\al\theta}-\sqrt{\theta\T\Sigma_\be\theta}\Big)^2.$$One-dimensional Gaussian formula $(a-b)^2+(s-t)^2$ with $s,t$ the standard deviations (also valid when one of them is $0$: a Dirac).
  3. $$\int\ps{\theta}{m_\al-m_\be}^2\,\dd\sigma(\theta)=(m_\al-m_\be)\T\E[\theta\theta\T](m_\al-m_\be)=\frac{\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2}d.$$$\ps{\theta}{z}^2=z\T\theta\theta\T z$ and $\E[\theta\theta\T]=\frac1d\Id$ (refresher).
  4. Integrate step 2 over $\theta$ and use step 3: this is (11.29). $\square$Definition of $SW_2^2$ as the average of the slice costs.

Cases with closed forms

In the course OT4ML Remark 11.31, p. 235

Remark 11.31 (Proportional and rank-one covariances). If $\Sigma_\al=s^2\Sigma$ and $\Sigma_\be=t^2\Sigma$ for $s,t\ge0$, then

$$\mathcal{SW}_2(\al,\be)^2=\frac{\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+(s-t)^2\tr(\Sigma)}d .$$

For the rank-one covariances $\Sigma_\al=a^2uu\T$ and $\Sigma_\be=b^2vv\T$, where $u$ and $v$ are unit vectors, set $\chi=\abs{\ps{u}{v}}$. Then

$$\begin{aligned}\mathcal{SW}_2(\al,\be)^2&=\frac1d\Big(\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+a^2+b^2-\frac{4ab}\pi\big(\sqrt{1-\chi^2}+\chi\arcsin(\chi)\big)\Big)\\&\le\frac1d\mathcal W_2(\al,\be)^2=\frac1d\big(\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+a^2+b^2-2ab\chi\big).\end{aligned}$$

[…] Aligned directions attain this upper bound. For centered, orthogonal, equal-scale covariances, $\mathcal{SW}_2^2=2a^2(1-2/\pi)/d$.

Worked examples — five Gaussian pairs
  • Isotropic, centred (Exercise 1 of the sheet): $\N(0,s^2\Id)$ and $\N(0,t^2\Id)$. Proportional case with $\Sigma=\Id$, $\tr\Sigma=d$: $SW_2^2=(s-t)^2$, so $SW_2=\abs{s-t}$ in every dimension, while $W_2=\sqrt d\abs{s-t}$. For $s=0.5$, $t=1.5$ in $\R^3$: $SW_2=1$, $W_2=\sqrt3\approx1.732$.
  • Translation: same covariance, means $m_\al\ne m_\be$: $SW_2=\norm{m_\al-m_\be}/\sqrt d$, $W_2=\norm{m_\al-m_\be}$.
  • Rank one, orthogonal ($\chi=0$, $a=b=1$, centred) in $\R^2$: $SW_2^2=\frac{2(1-2/\pi)}2=1-\frac2\pi\approx0.3634$ — exactly the cross of §4! The slices of $\N(0,e_1e_1\T)$ are $\N(0,\cos^2\varphi)$, those of $\frac12(\delta_{-e_1}+\delta_{e_1})$ are $\frac12(\delta_{-\cos\varphi}+\delta_{\cos\varphi})$: in both cases a fixed centred shape scaled by $\abs{\cos\varphi}$ (and by $\abs{\sin\varphi}$ for $\be$). Between two scaled copies $\pf{(a\Id)}{\rho}$, $\pf{(b\Id)}{\rho}$ of the same centred shape $\rho$ of variance $1$, the scaling map is monotone, so $W_2^2=(a-b)^2$, whatever the shape: both profiles are $(\abs{\cos\varphi}-\abs{\sin\varphi})^2$.
  • Rank one, $\chi=\frac12$, $a=1$, $b=2$, centred, $d=2$: the formula gives $SW_2^2\approx1.0640$, below $W_2^2/d=\frac{1+4-2}2=1.5$ (quadrature on $200\,000$ angles: $1.0640$).
  • A generic pair: $\al=\N(0,\mathrm{diag}(4,1))$, $\be=\N((1,0),\Id)$ in $\R^2$. The mean term is $\frac12$; the covariance term $\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\big(\sqrt{4\cos^2\varphi+\sin^2\varphi}-1\big)^2\,\dd\varphi\approx0.4161$ has no elementary closed form (it involves the perimeter of an ellipse). Total $SW_2^2\approx0.9161$, against $W_2^2=1+(2-1)^2+(1-1)^2=2$: ratio $0.458<\frac12$.

Intuition — concentration on the sphere

In high dimension, a random unit vector is almost orthogonal to any fixed vector: $\ps{\theta}{e}$ has mean $0$ and variance $\frac1d$, so it is typically of size $1/\sqrt d$. A difference between $\al$ and $\be$ that lives in one direction is seen by few slices. For a translation by $z$, the slice sees the shift $\ps{\theta}{z}$, and $SW_2^2=\norm z^2/d$. A stretch along one axis is diluted even more: stretching $e_1$ by $2$ changes the standard deviation of the slice from $1$ to $\sqrt{1+3\theta_1^2}\approx1+\frac32\theta_1^2$, a second-order effect in $\theta_1$, so $SW_2^2$ decays like $1/d^2$ ($0.039$ for $d=10$ and $0.0006$ for $d=100$, against $W_2^2/d=0.1$ and $0.01$; computed by quadrature). A difference spread over all directions (an isotropic dilation) is seen by every slice, but $W_2$ adds it up over the $d$ coordinates while $SW_2$ sees one coordinate at a time. In all cases $SW_2\le W_2/\sqrt d$, with equality for translations and isotropic dilations.

Pitfall — no Bures matrix square root here

$W_2$ between Gaussians has the closed form $\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+\B(\Sigma_\al,\Sigma_\be)^2$. $SW_2$ does not: “for generic anisotropic covariances its spherical integral has no matrix-square-root simplification and is evaluated by angular quadrature or Monte Carlo” (OT4ML, p. 234). Do not try to replace the angular average by a formula in $\Sigma_\al^{1/2}$ and $\Sigma_\be^{1/2}$ except in the proportional and rank-one cases above.

Show that the Gaussian formula gives $SW_2^2(\al,\be)\le\frac1dW_2^2(\al,\be)$ directly when $\Sigma_\al$ and $\Sigma_\be$ are diagonal.

Answer

Write $\Sigma_\al=\mathrm{diag}(a_i^2)$ and $\Sigma_\be=\mathrm{diag}(b_i^2)$ with $a_i,b_i\ge0$. The covariances commute, so $W_2^2=\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+\sum_i(a_i-b_i)^2$ (Bures term coordinate by coordinate). In (11.29), $\sqrt{\theta\T\Sigma_\al\theta}$ is the Euclidean norm of the vector $u=(\theta_ia_i)_i$ and $\sqrt{\theta\T\Sigma_\be\theta}$ that of $v=(\theta_ib_i)_i$. The reverse triangle inequality $\big\lvert\norm u-\norm v\big\rvert\le\norm{u-v}$ gives $\big(\sqrt{\theta\T\Sigma_\al\theta}-\sqrt{\theta\T\Sigma_\be\theta}\big)^2\le\sum_i\theta_i^2(a_i-b_i)^2$, and averaging with $\E[\theta_i^2]=\frac1d$ gives at most $\frac1d\sum_i(a_i-b_i)^2$. Adding the mean term $\frac1d\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2$: $SW_2^2\le\frac1dW_2^2$, as §6 predicts.
