4. The sliced Wasserstein distance notes Def. 2 and Remarks 1–3, pp. 1–2

Slice both measures in the direction $\theta$, compute the one-dimensional transport cost $W_p^p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)$, average it over all directions of the sphere with the uniform law, and take the $p$-th root: that is $SW_p(\al,\be)$.

Average the slices over the sphere

The problem: one slice sees only one “shadow” of the measures, and two different measures can have the same slice (§5). To compare $\al$ and $\be$ with a single number that sees everything, compare their slices in every direction and average the costs.

In the course Nadjahi notes, Def. 2, p. 1

Definition 2 (Sliced-Wasserstein distance, Rabin et al. [2012]). Let $\mu,\nu$ be two distributions on $\R^d$. Denote by $\mathbf W_c$ the one-dimensional Wasserstein distance based on the cost function $c:\R\times\R\to\R_+$. The Sliced-Wasserstein distance (based on $c$) between $\mu$ and $\nu$ is defined as,

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf{SW}_c(\mu,\nu)&=\E_{\theta\sim\mathcal U_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}}\big[\mathbf W_c\big((p_\theta)_\sharp\mu,(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu\big)\big]&(2)\\&=\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}\mathbf W_c\big((p_\theta)_\sharp\mu,(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu\big)\,\dd\mathcal U_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}(\theta)&(3)\end{aligned}$$

where $\mathbb S^{d-1}=\{u\in\R^d:\ \norm{u}_2=1\}$, and $\mathcal U_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}$ is the uniform distribution on $\mathbb S^{d-1}$.

In the course Nadjahi notes, Remark 3, p. 2

Remark 3 (The Sliced-Wasserstein distance of order $p$). If $c(x,y)=\abs{x-y}^p$ with $p\in[1,+\infty)$, the resulting SW distance is denoted by $\mathbf{SW}_p$ and given by,

$$\mathbf{SW}_p(\mu,\nu)=\Big(\E_{\theta\sim\mathcal U_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}}\big[\mathbf W_p\big((p_\theta)_\sharp\mu,(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu\big)^p\big]\Big)^{1/p}\qquad(5)$$

From now on, we will focus on $\mathbf{SW}_p$. The following theoretical properties and proofs are based on results from Bonnotte [2013].

In the course OT4ML Def. 11.23, p. 230

Definition 11.23 (Sliced Wasserstein distance). Let $p\ge1$, let $\al,\be\in\Pc_p(\R^d)$, and let $\sigma$ be the uniform probability measure on the sphere $\mathbb S^{d-1}$. The sliced $p$-Wasserstein distance is

$$\mathcal{SW}_p^p(\al,\be):=\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}\mathcal W_p^p\big((P_\theta)_\sharp\al,(P_\theta)_\sharp\be\big)\,\dd\sigma(\theta).\qquad(11.27)$$
$\mathbf W_c$
The one-dimensional transport cost for the cost $c$ — no root: $\mathbf W_c(\mu,\nu)=\min_{\pi}\int c\,\dd\pi$, written $\Lc$ in the rest of the course. With $c(x,y)=\abs{x-y}^p$ it is $W_p^p$.
$\theta\sim\mathcal U_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}$
A random direction, uniform on the unit sphere (written $\sigma$ on this page). (2) and (3) are the same thing, written as an expectation and as an integral.
$\mathbf{SW}_c$ vs $\mathbf{SW}_p$
$\mathbf{SW}_c$ averages the costs; $\mathbf{SW}_p$ averages the $p$-th powers and then takes the $p$-th root: $SW_p^p=\mathbf{SW}_c$ for $c=\abs{x-y}^p$. The root is what makes $SW_p$ a distance (§5).
$\Pc_p(\R^d)$
Measures with $\int\norm{x}^p\,\dd\al<\infty$. Then every slice has a finite $p$-th moment ($\abs{\ps{\theta}{x}}\le\norm{x}$), and all the quantities are finite.
“Rabin et al. [2012]”, “Bonnotte [2013]”
The paper that introduced the construction (for texture mixing; the slides date it 2011 — same paper, see the notes on the sources in §14), and the PhD thesis that proved its main properties.
Reminder — what is computed inside, for two clouds

For $\al=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$ and $\be=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{y_i}$, each slice is a uniform measure on $n$ numbers, and its transport cost is a sort (§2). Writing $\sigma_\theta,\tau_\theta$ for the permutations that sort $\big(\ps{\theta}{x_i}\big)_i$ and $\big(\ps{\theta}{y_i}\big)_i$,

$$SW_p^p(\al,\be)=\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\abs{\ps{\theta}{x_{\sigma_\theta(i)}-y_{\tau_\theta(i)}}}^p\,\dd\sigma(\theta).$$

The matching $\sigma_\theta(i)\leftrightarrow\tau_\theta(i)$ changes with $\theta$: every direction has its own sorted matching. See Reminders: sorting.

The uniform law on the sphere

In the course Nadjahi notes, Remark 1, pp. 1–2

Remark 1 (Uniform distribution on $\mathbb S^{d-1}$). There is a unique Borel measure $\sigma$ on $\mathbb S^{d-1}$ such that for every non-negative Borel measurable function $f$ on $\R^d$

$$\int_{\R^d}f(x)\,\dd x=\int_0^{+\infty}\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}f(r\theta)\,r^{d-1}\,\dd\sigma(\theta)\,\dd r.\qquad(4)$$

This is the change of variable formula from Cartesian coordinates to polar coordinates in $(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb S^{d-1}$. The uniform distribution on $\mathbb S^{d-1}$ is then defined as, for any Borel set $B\subset\mathbb S^{d-1}$,

$$\mathcal U_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}(B)=\frac{\sigma(B)}{\mathcal A(\mathbb S^{d-1})},$$

where $\mathcal A(\mathbb S^{d-1})=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}$ is the surface area of the sphere, with $\Gamma(d/2)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-t}t^{d/2-1}\,\dd t$.

$\sigma$ (in the notes)
The surface measure of the sphere, written $\tilde\sigma$ on this page: it is not a probability, its total mass is $\mathcal A(\mathbb S^{d-1})$. Everywhere else on this page, $\sigma$ is the uniform probability.
(4)
Polar coordinates: a point $x=r\theta$ is described by its distance $r$ to the origin and its direction $\theta$; the volume element is $r^{d-1}\,\dd r\,\dd\tilde\sigma(\theta)$ (in $\R^2$, $r\,\dd r\,\dd\varphi$). See the refresher.
$\mathcal U_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}$
The normalized surface measure: “every patch of the sphere gets a probability proportional to its area”. In $\R^2$: $\theta=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)$ with $\varphi$ uniform on $[0,2\pi)$. It is the only probability on the sphere that is invariant under all rotations.
$\mathcal A(\mathbb S^{d-1})$
$2\pi$ for $d=2$, $4\pi$ for $d=3$, $2\pi^2\approx19.74$ for $d=4$ (computed from the formula with $\Gamma(1)=1$, $\Gamma(\frac32)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}2$, $\Gamma(2)=1$).

How to draw directions from this law on a computer is Proposition 2 of the notes (§8): normalize a standard Gaussian vector.

Why the sphere, and not all of $\R^d$?

In the course Nadjahi notes, Remark 2, p. 2

Remark 2 (Why uniformly sample on $\mathbb S^{d-1}$ instead of $\R^d$?). In polar coordinates, every vector $x\in\R^d$ is described as $x=r\theta$ where $r\ge0$ is a radius and $\theta\in\mathbb S^{d-1}$ the direction. Projection along $r\theta$ rescales the 1-D Wasserstein by $\abs{r}^p$, so integrating over $\R^d$ repeats directional information and gives a divergent radial factor with the Lebesgue measure. Hence, we average over $\mathbb S^{d-1}$ instead. (Rigorous justification left as an exercise!)

Intuition

The vectors $\theta$, $2\theta$, $3\theta$, … all define the same line; projecting on $r\theta$ gives the slice along $\theta$ stretched by $r$, whose transport cost is multiplied by $r^p$ (stretching both measures by $r$ multiplies every distance by $r$). So a vector of $\R^d$ carries the same information as its direction, plus an irrelevant scale. Integrating over all of $\R^d$ would count each direction infinitely many times with ever larger weights $r^{p}\cdot r^{d-1}$: the integral is $+\infty$ as soon as $\al\ne\be$. Keeping only unit vectors removes the redundancy. The full computation is Exercise 4 of §13; it also shows that drawing $\theta$ as a standard Gaussian vector without normalizing it would give $d\,SW_2^2$ instead of $SW_2^2$ for $p=2$.

First computations

Worked examples — four cases you can do by hand
  • Dimension one. $\mathbb S^0=\{-1,1\}$ and $\sigma=\frac12(\delta_{-1}+\delta_{1})$. The slice along $-1$ is the mirror image of the measure, and a mirror image does not change transport costs. So $SW_p=W_p$ on the line.
  • Two Diracs. The slices of $\delta_x$ and $\delta_y$ are $\delta_{\ps{\theta}{x}}$ and $\delta_{\ps{\theta}{y}}$, at distance $\abs{\ps{\theta}{x-y}}$. Hence $SW_p^p(\delta_x,\delta_y)=\int\abs{\ps{\theta}{x-y}}^p\,\dd\sigma(\theta)$. For $p=2$ this is $(x-y)\T\E[\theta\theta\T](x-y)=\frac1d\norm{x-y}^2$: $\ SW_2(\delta_x,\delta_y)=\norm{x-y}/\sqrt d$, while $W_2(\delta_x,\delta_y)=\norm{x-y}$.
  • A translation. If $\be$ is $\al$ translated by $z$, each slice of $\be$ is the slice of $\al$ translated by $\ps{\theta}{z}$ (§3), and on the line a translation by $t$ costs exactly $\abs{t}^p$. So $SW_p^p(\al,\be)=\int\abs{\ps{\theta}{z}}^p\,\dd\sigma$, the same as for two Diracs: $SW_2=\norm{z}/\sqrt d$.
  • The cross (figure below). $\al=\frac12(\delta_{(-1,0)}+\delta_{(1,0)})$, $\be=\frac12(\delta_{(0,-1)}+\delta_{(0,1)})$. For $\theta=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)$ the slices are $\frac12(\delta_{-\cos\varphi}+\delta_{\cos\varphi})$ and $\frac12(\delta_{-\sin\varphi}+\delta_{\sin\varphi})$. Sorting matches the negative atoms together and the positive ones together: $$W_2^2(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)=\big(\abs{\cos\varphi}-\abs{\sin\varphi}\big)^2=1-2\abs{\cos\varphi\sin\varphi}=1-\abs{\sin2\varphi}.$$ Averaging over $\varphi$ uniform on $[0,2\pi)$, with $\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\abs{\sin2\varphi}\,\dd\varphi=\frac2\pi$: $$SW_2^2(\al,\be)=1-\frac2\pi\approx0.3634,\qquad SW_2\approx0.6028,$$ while every coupling of $\al$ and $\be$ costs $2$ (all four squared distances equal $2$), so $W_2^2=2$, $W_2\approx1.414$. (Checked by a quadrature on $200\,000$ angles.)

Pitfall — four conventions for “SW”

Sources differ by a root and by a normalization. The notes and OT4ML: $SW_p=\big(\int W_p^p\,\dd\sigma\big)^{1/p}$ with the uniform probability $\sigma$ — a distance. Some papers call $SW_2^2$ “the sliced Wasserstein distance” (it is not a distance, §5). CompOT (10.13) integrates against the surface measure of the sphere, so its values are $\abs{\mathbb S^{d-1}}$ times larger (and it writes the sphere $\mathbb S^d$; see §14). Libraries usually return the Monte Carlo estimate of $SW_p$ with the root. Always check which one a number refers to.

Pitfall — each slice has its own matching

$SW_p$ is an average of optimal costs, not the cost of one plan. In the cross, the direction $\varphi=10°$ sorts $(-1,0)$ first among the points of $\al$ and $(0,-1)$ first among those of $\be$, so it matches $(-1,0)$ with $(0,-1)$; the direction $\varphi=100°$ gives $(-1,0)$ the coordinate $+0.17$ (it is now the larger one) and matches it with $(0,1)$. There is no single transport plan between $\al$ and $\be$ behind $SW_p$. How to get one is the subject of §10 (lifted plans).

Reflexes
  • To compute $SW_p$ by hand → compute the slices (§3), solve each 1D problem by sorting, integrate over the angle.
  • Translations and Diracs → the slice cost is $\abs{\ps{\theta}{z}}^p$; for $p=2$ use $\E[\theta\theta\T]=\frac1d\Id$.
  • Any claim about $SW_p$ → test it in dimension $1$ (where $SW_p=W_p$) and on Diracs.

In the cross, which directions see the largest difference, and which see none? Why is the maximum $1$ and not $W_2^2=2$?

Answer

The profile $1-\abs{\sin2\varphi}$ is maximal ($=1$) on the axes $\varphi=0°,90°$: along $e_1$, $\al$ keeps its atoms at $\pm1$ while $\be$ collapses to $\delta_0$, and $W_2^2(\frac12(\delta_{-1}+\delta_1),\delta_0)=1$. It vanishes on the diagonals, where both slices are $\frac12(\delta_{-1/\sqrt2}+\delta_{1/\sqrt2})$. A single direction can never see the full cost $2$: the displacement from $(\pm1,0)$ to $(0,\pm1)$ has a component along $\theta$ and one orthogonal to it, and the slice only sees the first.
