5. $SW_p$ is a distance notes Thm. 1, pp. 2–3

Symmetry and the triangle inequality come slice by slice (plus Minkowski’s inequality over the directions); the hard part — $SW_p(\al,\be)=0$ forces $\al=\be$ — holds because the Fourier transforms of the slices fill the whole Fourier transform of the measure.

The statement and the proof of the notes

Why do we care? A distance is what lets us speak of convergence, of “close to the data”, of minimizing a loss with a unique minimum at the target. If $SW_p(\al,\be)$ could vanish for $\al\ne\be$, a generative model could reach zero loss without reproducing the data.

In the course Nadjahi notes, Thm. 1 and its proof, pp. 2–3

Theorem 1. The Sliced-Wasserstein distance of order $p$ satisfies all metric axioms (hence the name distance!)

Proof. Let $\mu,\nu,\xi$ be three arbitrary probability distributions on $\R^d$.

(a) Symmetry. $\mathbf{SW}_p(\mu,\nu)=\mathbf{SW}_p(\nu,\mu)$. Indeed,

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf{SW}_p(\mu,\nu)&=\big(\E_{\theta\sim\mathcal U_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}}\big[\mathbf W_p\big((p_\theta)_\sharp\mu,(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu\big)^p\big]\big)^{1/p}\\&=\big(\E_{\theta\sim\mathcal U_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}}\big[\mathbf W_p\big((p_\theta)_\sharp\nu,(p_\theta)_\sharp\mu\big)^p\big]\big)^{1/p}\quad\text{(by the symmetry of }\mathbf W_p)\\&=\mathbf{SW}_p(\nu,\mu).\end{aligned}$$

(b) Triangle inequality. $\mathbf{SW}_p(\mu,\nu)\le\mathbf{SW}_p(\mu,\xi)+\mathbf{SW}_p(\xi,\nu)$. Indeed,

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf{SW}_p(\mu,\nu)&=\big(\E_{\theta\sim\mathcal U_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}}\big[\mathbf W_p\big((p_\theta)_\sharp\mu,(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu\big)^p\big]\big)^{1/p}\\&=\big(\E_{\theta\sim\mathcal U_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}}\big[\{\mathbf W_p\big((p_\theta)_\sharp\mu,(p_\theta)_\sharp\xi\big)+\mathbf W_p\big((p_\theta)_\sharp\xi,(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu\big)\}^p\big]\big)^{1/p}\end{aligned}$$

since $\mathbf W_p$ satisfies the triangle inequality. We conclude by using Minkowski’s inequality: for every real-valued random variables $X$ and $Y$, $\ \E[\abs{X+Y}^p]^{1/p}\le\E[\abs{X}^p]^{1/p}+\E[\abs{Y}^p]^{1/p}$.

(c) Identity of indiscernibles (Séparation) $\mathbf{SW}_p(\mu,\nu)=0\Leftrightarrow\mu=\nu$.

($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $\mu=\nu$. Then $\ \mathbf{SW}_p(\mu,\mu)=\big(\E_{\theta}\big[\mathbf W_p\big((p_\theta)_\sharp\mu,(p_\theta)_\sharp\mu\big)^p\big]\big)^{1/p}=0\quad(6)\ $ since $\mathbf W_p(\mu',\mu')=0$ for any probability distribution $\mu'$.

($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $\mathbf{SW}_p(\mu,\nu)=0$. Since $\mathbf W_p(\mu',\nu')\ge0$ for any distributions $\mu',\nu'$, then for almost every $\theta\in\mathbb S^{d-1}$, $\ \mathbf W_p\big((p_\theta)_\sharp\mu,(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu\big)^p=0,\quad(7)\ $ thus $(p_\theta)_\sharp\mu=(p_\theta)_\sharp\nu$ (since $\mathbf W_p$ is a metric). Taking the Fourier transform of $\mu$,

$$\begin{aligned}\mathcal F\mu(s\theta)&=\int_{\R^d}e^{-2i\pi s\ps{\theta}{x}}\,\dd\mu(x)&(8)\\&=\int_{\R^d}e^{-2i\pi t}\,\dd[(p_\theta)_\sharp\mu](t)&(9)\\&=\mathcal F\big((p_\theta)_\sharp\mu\big)(s)&(10)\\&=\mathcal F\big((p_\theta)_\sharp\nu\big)(s)&(11)\\&=\mathcal F\nu(s\theta)&(12)\end{aligned}$$

By injectivity of the Fourier transform, we conclude that $\mu=\nu$. $\square$

“all metric axioms”
$SW_p\ge0$; $SW_p(\al,\be)=SW_p(\be,\al)$; $SW_p(\al,\ga)\le SW_p(\al,\be)+SW_p(\be,\ga)$; $SW_p(\al,\be)=0\iff\al=\be$. Non-negativity is obvious (an average of non-negative numbers). Finiteness needs $\al,\be\in\Pc_p(\R^d)$.
$\xi$
The intermediate measure of the triangle inequality ($\ga$ on this page).
Minkowski’s inequality
The triangle inequality of the norm $\E[\abs{X}^p]^{1/p}$ (the $L^p$ norm of a random variable, here a function of the random direction $\theta$). It needs $p\ge1$.
“for almost every $\theta$”
Except on a set of directions of $\sigma$-probability zero. A non-negative function with zero average vanishes almost everywhere — not necessarily everywhere.
$\mathcal F\mu(s\theta)$
The Fourier transform of $\mu$ at the point $s\theta$ of the line $\R\theta$ (refresher). Lines (8)–(10) are the Fourier slice identity of §3.
“injectivity of the Fourier transform”
Two probability measures with the same Fourier transform at every point are equal.
Idea of the proof
  1. Everything is done slice by slice, where $W_p$ is already a distance (Lesson 3).
  2. Symmetry: the slices of $(\al,\be)$ and of $(\be,\al)$ have the same costs.
  3. Triangle inequality: apply the 1D triangle inequality in every direction, then the triangle inequality of the $L^p(\sigma)$ norm over directions (Minkowski) — the same two-step pattern as for $W_p$ itself.
  4. Separation: zero average forces almost every slice of $\al$ to equal that of $\be$; continuity in $\theta$ upgrades “almost every” to “every”; the Fourier transforms of the slices are the values of $\mathcal F\al$ on all lines through the origin, i.e. everywhere.

The proof, step by step

Proof of the triangle inequality

Write $f(\theta)=W_p(\al_\theta,\ga_\theta)$, $g(\theta)=W_p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)$, $h(\theta)=W_p(\be_\theta,\ga_\theta)$ — three non-negative functions of the direction. Then $SW_p(\al,\ga)=\big(\int f^p\,\dd\sigma\big)^{1/p}=\norm{f}_{L^p(\sigma)}$, and similarly for the two others.

  1. $$f(\theta)\le g(\theta)+h(\theta)\qquad\text{for every }\theta.$$Triangle inequality of $W_p$ between the three measures $\al_\theta,\be_\theta,\ga_\theta$ on the line (Lesson 3, gluing).
  2. $$\Big(\int f^p\,\dd\sigma\Big)^{1/p}\le\Big(\int(g+h)^p\,\dd\sigma\Big)^{1/p}$$$t\mapsto t^p$ is increasing on $[0,\infty)$, integration preserves inequalities, and $t\mapsto t^{1/p}$ is increasing. (This is the line written “$=$” in the notes; it is an inequality — see the note below.)
  3. $$\le\Big(\int g^p\,\dd\sigma\Big)^{1/p}+\Big(\int h^p\,\dd\sigma\Big)^{1/p}$$Minkowski’s inequality in $L^p(\sigma)$, valid for $p\ge1$ (refresher).
  4. $$SW_p(\al,\ga)\le SW_p(\al,\be)+SW_p(\be,\ga).\qquad\square$$The three integrals are the definitions of the three sliced distances.
Proof of separation: $SW_p(\al,\be)=0\Rightarrow\al=\be$
  1. $g(\theta)=W_p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)=0$ for $\sigma$-almost every $\theta$.$\int g^p\,\dd\sigma=SW_p^p=0$ and $g^p\ge0$: a non-negative function with zero integral vanishes almost everywhere.
  2. $g$ is continuous on the sphere.Lemma below (this step is missing in the notes).
  3. $g(\theta)=0$ for every $\theta$.The set $\{g>0\}$ is open (continuity). A non-empty open subset of the sphere has positive $\sigma$-probability. So it is empty.
  4. $\al_\theta=\be_\theta$ for every $\theta$.$W_p$ is a distance on the line.
  5. $\mathcal F\al(\omega\theta)=\mathcal F(\al_\theta)(\omega)=\mathcal F(\be_\theta)(\omega)=\mathcal F\be(\omega\theta)$ for all $\omega\in\R$, $\theta\in\mathbb S^{d-1}$.The Fourier slice identity (lines (8)–(10) and (11)–(12) of the notes), applied to $\al$ and to $\be$.
  6. $\mathcal F\al(\xi)=\mathcal F\be(\xi)$ for every $\xi\in\R^d$.$\xi=0$: both equal $1$. $\xi\ne0$: write $\xi=\omega\theta$ with $\omega=\norm\xi$, $\theta=\xi/\norm\xi$, and use step 5.
  7. $\al=\be$. $\square$Injectivity of the Fourier transform on probability measures (refresher).

Lemma (continuity in the direction; StatOT, proof of Thm. 2.25, p. 87–88). For $\al\in\Pc_p(\R^d)$ and directions $\theta,\theta'$: $\ W_p(\al_\theta,\al_{\theta'})\le\big(\E\norm{X}^p\big)^{1/p}\norm{\theta-\theta'}$ with $X\sim\al$. Indeed, the pair $\big(\ps{\theta}{X},\ps{\theta'}{X}\big)$ — the same random point projected twice — is a coupling of $\al_\theta$ and $\al_{\theta'}$, of cost $\E\abs{\ps{\theta-\theta'}{X}}^p\le\norm{\theta-\theta'}^p\E\norm{X}^p$ (Cauchy–Schwarz). Then, by the triangle inequality of $W_p$ used twice,

$$\abs{g(\theta)-g(\theta')}\le W_p(\al_\theta,\al_{\theta'})+W_p(\be_\theta,\be_{\theta'})\le\Big(\big(\E\norm{X}^p\big)^{1/p}+\big(\E\norm{Y}^p\big)^{1/p}\Big)\norm{\theta-\theta'},$$

so $g$ is even Lipschitz. (Alternative, StatOT footnote 6 and OT4ML: equality of the slices for almost every $\theta$ gives $\mathcal F\al=\mathcal F\be$ almost everywhere in $\R^d$, and Fourier transforms are continuous.)

Note on the sources — three small slips in the proof of Theorem 1
  • In (b), the second line is written with “$=$”; the triangle inequality of $\mathbf W_p$ only gives “$\le$”. Check with $\mu=\delta_{e_1}$, $\xi=\delta_{e_2}$, $\nu=\delta_{-e_1}$ in $\R^2$ and $p=2$: the slice costs are $2\abs{\cos\varphi}$ on the left and $\abs{\cos\varphi-\sin\varphi}+\abs{\cos\varphi+\sin\varphi}=2\max(\abs{\cos\varphi},\abs{\sin\varphi})$ on the right, so the first line is $\sqrt{4\cdot\frac12}=\sqrt2\approx1.41$ and the second $\sqrt{4(\frac12+\frac1\pi)}\approx1.81$.
  • In (9), the integral is over $\R$, not $\R^d$: the measure $(p_\theta)_\sharp\mu$ lives on the line.
  • From (7) (“for almost every $\theta$”) the notes go directly to the Fourier identity for every $\theta$: the continuity argument above is needed in between. OT4ML (proof of Prop. 11.24, p. 230) and StatOT (Thm. 2.25, pp. 87–88) state it.

Cramér–Wold: all directions are needed

The proof is also known as the Cramér–Wold theorem: a probability measure on $\R^d$ is determined by the laws of all its one-dimensional projections. (OT4ML calls the full family of slices injective “by the Cramér–Wold theorem”, p. 232.) But finitely many directions are not enough, and the next example shows it on two points.

Worked example — two measures that two axes cannot tell apart

Let $\al=\frac12(\delta_{(0,0)}+\delta_{(1,1)})$ and $\be=\frac12(\delta_{(1,0)}+\delta_{(0,1)})$: the two diagonals of the unit square.

  • On $e_1$: $\al_{e_1}=\frac12(\delta_0+\delta_1)=\be_{e_1}$. On $e_2$: the same. Both coordinate axes see exactly the same slices.
  • On the diagonal $\theta=\frac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)$: $\al_\theta=\frac12(\delta_0+\delta_{\sqrt2})$ but $\be_\theta=\delta_{1/\sqrt2}$ (both points of $\be$ project to $\frac1{\sqrt2}$). Different.
  • For a general direction $\theta=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)$, the sorted matching gives $W_2^2(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)=\min(\cos^2\varphi,\sin^2\varphi)$, zero only on the axes. Averaging, $\ SW_2^2(\al,\be)=\frac4\pi\int_0^{\pi/4}\sin^2\varphi\,\dd\varphi=\frac12-\frac1\pi\approx0.1817$, while $W_2^2(\al,\be)=1$ (all four squared distances equal $1$).

(Checked by quadrature on $200\,000$ angles.) A computation that would use only the directions $e_1$ and $e_2$ would declare $\al=\be$. With random directions this never happens — the bad directions have probability zero — but it explains why a finite set of directions only gives a pseudo-distance, and why the estimate of §8 must use fresh random directions.

Same convergent sequences as $W_p$

In the course OT4ML Prop. 11.24, p. 230 (first part)

Proposition 11.24 (Metric properties of sliced Wasserstein). For $p\ge1$, $\mathcal{SW}_p$ is a distance on $\Pc_p(\R^d)$. Moreover, $\mathcal{SW}_p$ metrizes weak convergence together with convergence of the $p$th moment.

In words: $SW_p(\al_n,\al)\to0$ if and only if $\al_n\wto\al$ and $\int\norm{x}^p\,\dd\al_n\to\int\norm{x}^p\,\dd\al$ — exactly the convergence that $W_p$ metrizes on $\Pc_p(\R^d)$ (Lesson 3 §4). So $SW_p$ and $W_p$ have the same convergent sequences; §6 shows that they nevertheless measure very different numbers.

Going further — the proof of the topological statement (OT4ML, p. 231)
  1. If $W_p(\al_n,\al)\to0$ then $SW_p(\al_n,\al)\to0$.$SW_p\le W_p$ (§6).
  2. Conversely, if $SW_p(\al_n,\al)\to0$, the $p$-th moments of $\al_n$ are bounded.The spherical identity $\int\!\!\int\abs{\ps{\theta}{x}}^p\,\dd\al_n(x)\,\dd\sigma(\theta)=\kappa_{d,p}\int\norm{x}^p\,\dd\al_n$ (§6) shows that the moment is $\kappa_{d,p}^{-1}SW_p^p(\al_n,\delta_0)$, and $SW_p(\al_n,\delta_0)\le SW_p(\al_n,\al)+SW_p(\al,\delta_0)$ is bounded.
  3. So $(\al_n)$ is tight, and every subsequence has a further subsequence converging weakly to some $\al'$, whose slices are those of $\al$ for almost every $\theta$.Bounded moments give tightness (Markov’s inequality); along a subsequence, $W_p((\al_n)_\theta,\al_\theta)\to0$ for almost every $\theta$ (an $L^p(\sigma)$-convergent sequence has an a.e. convergent subsequence), and weak limits commute with the continuous projections.
  4. $\al'=\al$, hence $\al_n\wto\al$; finally the $p$-th moments converge.Cramér–Wold, as in the separation proof. For the moments: $SW_p(\al_n,\delta_0)\to SW_p(\al,\delta_0)$ by the triangle inequality, and the spherical identity turns this into convergence of $\int\norm x^p\,\dd\al_n$.

For $p=2$: a Euclidean distance in disguise

In the course OT4ML Remark 11.26, p. 232

Remark 11.26 (Hilbert embedding for $\mathcal{SW}_2$). In one dimension, $\mathcal W_2$ is the $L^2(0,1)$ distance between quantile functions. Hence

$$\mathcal{SW}_2(\al,\be)^2=\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}\int_0^1\big\lvert F_{\theta,\al}^{-1}(u)-F_{\theta,\be}^{-1}(u)\big\rvert^2\,\dd u\,\dd\sigma(\theta),$$

where $F_{\theta,\al}^{-1}$ is the quantile function of $(P_\theta)_\sharp\al$. Thus $\mathcal{SW}_2$ is the Hilbertian distance obtained by embedding each measure into its field of projected quantile functions. Consequently, $\exp(-\gamma\,\mathcal{SW}_2^2)$ is a positive-definite kernel on probability measures for every $\gamma>0$.

“embedding”
The map $\al\mapsto Q_\al$, $Q_\al(\theta,u)=F_{\theta,\al}^{-1}(u)$, sends each measure to a function of two variables, and $SW_2(\al,\be)=\norm{Q_\al-Q_\be}_{L^2(\sigma\otimes\dd u)}$: an ordinary Euclidean (Hilbert) distance between these functions.
positive-definite kernel
A similarity $k(\al,\be)$ such that every matrix $\big(k(\al_i,\al_j)\big)_{ij}$ is positive semi-definite — what kernel methods (SVM, kernel ridge regression, Gaussian processes) require. Gaussian functions $e^{-\gamma\norm{u-v}^2}$ of a Hilbert distance are such kernels.
In machine learning — kernels and embeddings for sets of points

Suppose each data item is itself a distribution: a 3D shape given as a cloud of $1\,024$ points, a document as a cloud of word embeddings, an image as the cloud of its pixel colours. Fix $K$ directions $\theta_1,\dots,\theta_K$ and $M$ quantile levels $u_1,\dots,u_M$, and map each item $\al$ to the vector $\big(F_{\theta_k,\al}^{-1}(u_m)\big)_{k,m}\in\R^{KM}$ — one sort per direction. Then the squared Euclidean distance between two such vectors, divided by $KM$, approximates $SW_2^2$, and any Euclidean method applies directly: $k$-means, PCA, nearest neighbours, or an SVM with the Gaussian kernel $e^{-\gamma SW_2^2}$, which is positive definite (OT4ML Remark 11.26; “sliced Wasserstein kernels”, CompOT §10.4, p. 173, after Kolouri, Zou and Rohde, 2016). Exact $W_2$ does not allow this: in dimension $d\ge2$ it is not Hilbertian, and $e^{-\gamma W_2^2}$ is not positive definite in general (CompOT Prop. 8.2, p. 130).

Pitfall — $SW_2^2$ is not a distance

The square loses the triangle inequality, as for $W_2$. With $d=2$: $SW_2^2(\delta_0,\delta_{2e_1})=\frac{4}{2}=2$, but $SW_2^2(\delta_0,\delta_{e_1})+SW_2^2(\delta_{e_1},\delta_{2e_1})=\frac12+\frac12=1$. Use $SW_2$ (with the root) whenever you need a triangle inequality.

Reflexes
  • A property of $SW_p$ → prove it slice by slice for $W_p$ on the line, then integrate over directions (Minkowski for the triangle inequality).
  • “The slices determine the measure” → Fourier slice identity + injectivity of the Fourier transform (Cramér–Wold); “almost every direction” needs a continuity argument.
  • Finitely many directions → only a pseudo-distance: two diagonals of a square fool both axes.

Where exactly did the proof use $p\ge1$? And which step breaks if the uniform law $\sigma$ is replaced by the uniform law on the four directions $\pm e_1,\pm e_2$?

Answer

$p\ge1$ is used twice: in the triangle inequality of $W_p$ on the line, and in Minkowski’s inequality over the directions. With four directions, symmetry and the triangle inequality still hold (the same proof works for any law of directions), but separation breaks at step 3: “zero for almost every direction” now only means “zero on the four axes”, and the Cramér–Wold pair has a zero average without being equal. The argument needs a law that charges every open set of directions, as $\sigma$ does.
