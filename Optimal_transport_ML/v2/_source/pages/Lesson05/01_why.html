1. Why slice: the price of exact transport slides, frames 3/12–7/12

Machine learning wants a transport loss, evaluated on mini-batches at every training step; exactly, it costs about $n^3$ operations per evaluation, and when the clouds are samples it approaches the true value only like $n^{-1/d}$ — slicing is designed to remove both obstacles.

Transport as a loss between a model and data

The lecture starts from an application. A generative model is a machine that produces new samples looking like the data: images, molecules, point clouds. In the version drawn on the slides, it is a neural network that turns random noise into samples, and training it means moving the distribution of its outputs towards the distribution of the data. Optimal transport gives a natural way to measure how far apart the two distributions are.

OT for Machine Learning. [drawing: $\N(0,\mathrm{Id})$ → a neural network $T(w)$ → a distribution $\mu(w)$, compared with the data $\hat\mu_n$ (crosses); below: $\min_w\mathrm D\big(\mu(w),\hat\mu_n\big)$] Generative models.

Some OT-based deep generative models:

  • Wasserstein GANs [Arjovsky et al., 2017]: GANs with objective function based on the dual of $\mathbf W_1(\mu(w),\hat\mu_n)$
  • Wasserstein Auto-Encoders [Tolstikhin et al., 2018]: Auto-encoders trained by minimizing a regularized $\mathbf W_1(\mu(w),\hat\mu_n)$
$\N(0,\mathrm{Id})$
The latent noise: a standard Gaussian vector $z$ in some $\R^k$, easy to sample.
$T(w)$
A neural network with weights $w$ (the drawing shows convolution layers). It maps a noise vector $z$ to a sample $x=T(w)(z)\in\R^d$, for instance an image.
$\mu(w)$
The distribution of the generated samples: the push-forward $\pf{T(w)}{\N(0,\mathrm{Id})}$. It depends on $w$.
$\hat\mu_n$
The data, seen as the empirical measure $\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{y_i}$ of $n$ training examples (the red crosses).
$\min_w\mathrm D(\mu(w),\hat\mu_n)$
Training: choose the weights so that a discrepancy $\mathrm D$ between model and data is as small as possible. With $\mathrm D=W_p$, this is “minimum Wasserstein estimation”.
WGAN, WAE
Two famous ways to use $W_1$: through its dual formula (a second network, the critic, plays the role of the dual potential — Lesson 4), or through a relaxed primal problem (auto-encoders).
In machine learning — what one training step needs

Take a generator of $32\times32$ colour images, so samples live in $\R^d$ with $d=3\,072$, and mini-batches of $n=256$. At each step: draw $256$ noise vectors $z_i$, generate $x_i=T(w)(z_i)$, take $256$ real images $y_j$, and evaluate the loss $W_2^2\big(\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i},\frac1n\sum_j\delta_{y_j}\big)$. With equal weights the optimal plan is a permutation $\sigma^\star$, and the gradient with respect to $x_i$ is $\frac2n\big(x_i-y_{\sigma^\star(i)}\big)$ (the optimal assignment is frozen when differentiating: Danskin’s theorem), which is then back-propagated into $w$. So each step solves a new assignment problem: about $n^2d\approx2.0\times10^{8}$ operations to build the cost matrix and $n^3\approx1.7\times10^{7}$ for the Hungarian algorithm — over tens of thousands of steps. This is the setting the whole lecture has in mind.

The cost of exact transport

In the course slides, frame 5/12 (p. 10)

OT in Multi-Dimensions. Now, $\mu,\nu$ are distributions on $\R^d$.

Discrete measures. Let $\mu_n=\sum_{i=1}^na_i\delta_{x_i}$ and $\nu_n=\sum_{j=1}^nb_j\delta_{y_j}$.

$$\mathbf W_2^2(\mu_n,\nu_n)=\min_{\pi\in\Pi(a,b)}\Big\{\langle C,\pi\rangle\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^nC_{ij}\pi_{ij}\Big\}$$

with $C\in\R^{n\times n}$ the cost matrix s.t. $C_{ij}=c(x_i,y_j)$, and $\Pi(a,b)\triangleq\{\pi\in\R_+^{n\times n}\ \text{s.t.}\ \pi\ind_n=a,\ \pi\T\ind_n=b\}$.

OT is a linear program. Linear program with $n^2$ variables and $2n$ constraints. Classical solvers: Hungarian algorithm, interior-point method. Computational complexity $\mathcal O(n^3)$.

In the course slides, frame 6/12 (p. 11)

Standard OT: Too Costly in ML. Computational bottleneck: Computing $\mathbf W_2(\mu_n,\nu_n)$ costs $\mathcal O(n^3)$ $\Rightarrow$ Expensive for modern ML pipelines.

Example: Training OT-based neural networks. Mini-batch $n=256$ $\Rightarrow$ 1 evaluation of $\mathbf W_2(\mu_n,\nu_n)\sim10^7$ operations. Needed at every training step, over thousands of iterations.

Standard OT is expensive $\Rightarrow$ Approximate/regularized OT: Entropy-regularized OT (Sinkhorn algorithm) [Cuturi, 2013]; Sliced OT [Rabin et al., 2011].

$C_{ij}=c(x_i,y_j)$
The price of moving a unit of mass from $x_i$ to $y_j$; for $W_2^2$, $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2$.
$\Pi(a,b)$
The transport plans: non-negative matrices whose row sums are $a$ and column sums are $b$ ($\ind_n$ is the vector of ones). It is the set $\U(a,b)$ of Lesson 3 §1.
“$n^2$ variables, $2n$ constraints”
The unknowns are the $n^2$ entries $\pi_{ij}\ge0$; the equality constraints are the $n$ row sums and the $n$ column sums.
$\mathcal O(n^3)$
The number of elementary operations grows like $n^3$. For equal weights, the problem is an assignment problem, and the Hungarian algorithm solves it in $O(n^3)$ operations (OT4ML Prop. 1.8, p. 18). For general weights, the network simplex has a bound of order $n^3\log n$ up to a logarithm of the costs (CompOT §3.5) — this is the “$O(n^3\log n)$” you will also meet.
Reminder — transport between histograms is a linear program (Lesson 2)

The objective $\ps{C}{\pi}=\sum_{ij}C_{ij}\pi_{ij}$ is linear in $\pi$, and the constraints are linear equalities and inequalities: the minimum is attained at a vertex of the polytope $\Pi(a,b)$, and for uniform weights $a=b=\frac1n\ind_n$ the vertices are the permutation matrices divided by $n$ (Birkhoff). This is why “optimal transport between two clouds of $n$ points” and “optimal assignment” are the same problem. See Lesson 2 and Reminders: linear programming.

Worked example — orders of magnitude
  • $n=256$: $n^3=16\,777\,216\approx1.7\times10^{7}$ — the “$\sim10^7$” of the slide. Sorting $256$ numbers costs about $n\log_2n=2\,048$ comparisons: $8\,000$ times fewer.
  • $n=1\,000$ points in $\R^3$: $n^3=10^9$ operations for the exact plan, against $3n=3\,000$ multiplications to project the points on one line and about $n\log_2n\approx10^4$ comparisons to sort them.
  • Memory: the cost matrix has $n^2$ entries; for $n=10^5$ points that is $10^{10}$ numbers, $40$ GB in single precision. Exact transport is simply out of reach.

The curse of dimensionality

Speed is not the only problem. In practice we never see the distributions themselves, only samples, and we compute the distance between the two empirical measures. Is that number close to the true distance?

In the course slides, frame 7/12 (p. 12)

The Curse of Dimensionality. In practice, we only observe finite samples $(x_i)_{i=1}^n\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}\sim\mu$, $(y_i)_{i=1}^n\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}\sim\nu$: $\ \mathbf W_p(\mu_n,\nu_n)$ vs. $\mathbf W_p(\mu,\nu)$. How fast does $\mathbf W_p(\mu_n,\nu_n)\to\mathbf W_p(\mu,\nu)$ as $n\to+\infty$?

Sample complexity of $\mathbf W_p$ [Dudley, 1969]. If $\mu,\nu$ are supported on a bounded domain of $\R^d$, $d>2$,

$$\E\big[\abs{\mathbf W_p(\mu_n,\nu_n)-\mathbf W_p(\mu,\nu)}\big]=\mathcal O\big(n^{-\frac1d}\big)$$

with $\E$ taken over $(x_i,y_i)_{i=1}^n$. Consequences: Higher dimension $d$ $\Rightarrow$ slower convergence. Accurate estimates may require prohibitively large $n$.

$\mu_n,\nu_n$
The empirical measures $\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$ and $\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{y_i}$: random measures, because the samples are random.
$\E[\abs{\cdots}]$
The average error, over all possible draws of the samples, made by replacing the true distance by the computable one.
$\mathcal O(n^{-1/d})$
The error is at most a constant times $n^{-1/d}$. In dimension $d=10$, it decreases like $n^{-0.1}$: almost not at all.
“bounded domain”
Both measures live in a ball of $\R^d$. (Without such an assumption, mass far away can make things even worse.)
Intuition — why samples are so far from their law in high dimension

$n$ points cannot cover a $d$-dimensional region finely: to put a point within distance $\eps$ of every location of the unit cube, you need about $\eps^{-d}$ points. Conversely, with $n$ points, the typical distance from a location to the nearest sample is about $n^{-1/d}$ — and transporting the true law onto the samples costs about that much. The Riemann sums of Lesson 3 are the case $d=1$: there $W_1=O(1/n)$ for a well-spread grid, and $O(n^{-1/2})$ for random samples (§2).

Worked example — what $n^{-1/d}$ means

If the error behaves like $c\,n^{-1/d}$, dividing it by $2$ requires multiplying $n$ by $2^d$: by $4$ in dimension $2$, by $1\,024$ in dimension $10$, by about $1.1\times10^{15}$ in dimension $50$. With a rate $n^{-1/2}$ — the rate we will get for sliced distances in every dimension (§9) — it always suffices to multiply $n$ by $4$.

Note on the sources — the condition “$d>2$” is for $p=1$

Dudley’s theorem (1969) is about $W_1$. For $W_p$ with $p>1$ the rate $n^{-1/d}$ holds only when $d>2p$; for $d<2p$ the worst-case rate is $n^{-1/(2p)}$ (StatOT, display (2.21), p. 89). For instance with $p=2$ and $d=3$ the rate is $n^{-1/4}$, not $n^{-1/3}$: two atoms at distance $1$ already give $\E\,W_2(\hat\al_n,\al)\approx0.58\,n^{-1/4}$ in any dimension (Exercise 11 of §13, checked by an exact binomial sum). The message of the slide — the rate degrades with the dimension — is unchanged.

You can see the curse on real numbers in §9 (figure “rates”): between two independent samples of the same Gaussian, whose true distance is $0$, the computed $W_2$ is still $7.6$ in dimension $50$ with $1\,000$ points.

Two ways out, and the idea of slicing

The slide lists two remedies. Entropic regularization (Lesson 7) smooths the linear program; it costs $O(n^2)$ per iteration. Slicing, the subject of this lecture, uses the only case where transport is trivial — the line — and builds everything from it:

  1. Pick a direction $\theta$ (a unit vector) and replace every point $x$ by the number $\ps{\theta}{x}$.This costs $d$ multiplications per point; the two clouds become two lists of numbers.
  2. Solve transport between the two lists by sorting them (§2).$O(n\log n)$ operations, instead of $O(n^3)$.
  3. Average the result over many directions $\theta$ (§4, §8).One direction sees only one “shadow” of the clouds; all directions together see everything (§5).

With $n=10\,000$ points in $\R^{3}$ and $K=100$ directions, compare the number of operations of the exact assignment and of the sliced computation.

Answer

Exact: $n^3=10^{12}$ (plus $n^2d=3\times10^8$ for the cost matrix). Sliced: projections $Kdn=3\times10^6$, sorts about $Kn\log_2n\approx100\times10^4\times13.3\approx1.3\times10^7$, in total about $1.6\times10^7$. About $60\,000$ times fewer operations — and no $n\times n$ matrix in memory.
