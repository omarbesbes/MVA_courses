9. Statistics: a rate that does not depend on the dimension slides, frame 11/12; OT4ML Thm. 10.16

Each slice of an empirical measure is the empirical measure of the projected samples, and on the line empirical measures converge at rate $n^{-1/2}$; averaging over directions keeps this rate in every dimension — whereas $W_p$ between samples converges like $n^{-1/d}$.

What the slides show

The problem: in practice $\al$ and $\be$ are known only through samples, and §1 showed that $W_p$ between samples approaches its true value only like $n^{-1/d}$. Does slicing also remove this curse of dimensionality?

Sample complexity of $\mathbf{SW}_p$. For $\mu,\nu$ supported on a bounded domain of $\R^d$,

$$\E\big[\abs{\mathbf{SW}_p(\mu_n,\nu_n)-\mathbf{SW}_p(\mu,\nu)}\big]=\mathcal O\big(n^{-\frac1{2p}}\big)$$

$\Rightarrow$ Dimension-free rate, unlike $\mathbf W_p$ ($\mathcal O(n^{-\frac1d})$).

the plot
Both samples come from the same Gaussian, so the true distance is $0$: the curves show the estimation error alone.
$\mathcal O(n^{-1/(2p)})$
A bound valid for every pair of measures with bounded support; for $p=1$ it is the parametric rate $n^{-1/2}$ of the line. The constant depends on the radius of the support, not on $d$.
“dimension-free”
The exponent of $n$ does not depend on $d$. Compare with $n^{-1/d}$ for $W_p$ (§1).

Why do the $SW_2$ curves coincide exactly (up to noise)? Every slice of $\N(0,\Id_d)$ is $\N(0,1)$, and every slice of a sample of $\N(0,\Id_d)$ is a sample of $\N(0,1)$. So the law of $W_2^2$ of a slice does not depend on $d$, and neither does its average.

The theorem and its proof

In the course OT4ML Thm. 10.16 and its proof, p. 207

Theorem 10.16 (Dimension-free empirical rate for $\mathcal{SW}_1$). Let $\al,\be\in\Pc(\R^d)$ satisfy $\supp(\al)\cup\supp(\be)\subset B(0,R)$, and let $\hat\al_n$ and $\hat\be_m$ be their empirical measures from $n$ and $m$ i.i.d. samples, respectively. Then

$$\E\,\big\lvert\mathcal{SW}_1(\hat\al_n,\hat\be_m)-\mathcal{SW}_1(\al,\be)\big\rvert\le R\Big(\frac1{\sqrt n}+\frac1{\sqrt m}\Big).$$

Proof. The triangle inequality for $\mathcal{SW}_1$ gives $\ \abs{\mathcal{SW}_1(\hat\al_n,\hat\be_m)-\mathcal{SW}_1(\al,\be)}\le\mathcal{SW}_1(\hat\al_n,\al)+\mathcal{SW}_1(\hat\be_m,\be)$. Fix $\theta\in\mathbb S^{d-1}$, set $\al_\theta=(P_\theta)_\sharp\al$, and denote the cumulative functions of $\al_\theta$ and its empirical measure by $F_\theta$ and $\hat F_{\theta,n}$. Both measures are supported on $[-R,R]$. The one-dimensional identity (2.12) and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality give

$$\E\,\mathcal W_1\big((P_\theta)_\sharp\hat\al_n,\al_\theta\big)=\int_{-R}^R\E\,\big\lvert\hat F_{\theta,n}(s)-F_\theta(s)\big\rvert\,\dd s\le\int_{-R}^R\sqrt{\frac{F_\theta(s)(1-F_\theta(s))}n}\,\dd s\le\frac R{\sqrt n}.$$

Indeed, $n\hat F_{\theta,n}(s)$ is binomial with success probability $F_\theta(s)$. Integrating this bound over $\theta$ yields $\E\,\mathcal{SW}_1(\hat\al_n,\al)\le R/\sqrt n$. The same argument for $\be$, followed by the preceding triangle inequality, proves the result. $\square$

$B(0,R)$
The ball of radius $R$: all samples have norm at most $R$, so all projections lie in $[-R,R]$.
$\hat\al_n$
$\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{X_i}$ with $X_1,\dots,X_n$ i.i.d. of law $\al$; it is random.
(2.12)
The one-dimensional identity $W_1(\mu,\nu)=\int_\R\abs{F_\mu(s)-F_\nu(s)}\,\dd s$: the area between the two CDFs (§2).
$n\hat F_{\theta,n}(s)$
The number of samples whose projection is $\le s$: each sample falls there independently with probability $F_\theta(s)$, hence a binomial law $\mathcal B(n,F_\theta(s))$.
Reminder — the empirical CDF is a binomial proportion

Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be i.i.d. real variables with CDF $F$, and fix $s$. Each indicator $\ind_{X_i\le s}$ is a Bernoulli variable with parameter $F(s)$, so the count $N=\#\{i:\ X_i\le s\}$ is binomial $\mathcal B(n,F(s))$, with mean $nF(s)$ and variance $nF(s)(1-F(s))$. The empirical CDF $\hat F(s)=N/n$ therefore has mean $F(s)$ and variance $F(s)(1-F(s))/n\le\frac1{4n}$. And for any random variable $U$, $\E\abs U\le\sqrt{\E U^2}$ (Cauchy–Schwarz). See Reminders: CDF and quantiles and laws of large numbers.

Idea of the proof

(1) Split the error into “$\al$ against its sample” plus “$\be$ against its sample” with the triangle inequality. (2) The slice of the sample is the sample of the slice. (3) On the line, $W_1$ is the area between CDFs, and an empirical CDF is a binomial proportion: its error at each point is at most $1/(2\sqrt n)$. (4) Integrate over $s\in[-R,R]$, then average over $\theta$: the dimension never appears.

The proof, step by step
  1. $$\big\lvert SW_1(\hat\al_n,\hat\be_m)-SW_1(\al,\be)\big\rvert\le SW_1(\hat\al_n,\al)+SW_1(\hat\be_m,\be).$$Reverse triangle inequality for the distance $SW_1$ (§5): $\abs{\mathrm d(a,b)-\mathrm d(a',b')}\le\mathrm d(a,a')+\mathrm d(b,b')$.
  2. $(P_\theta)_\sharp\hat\al_n=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{\ps{\theta}{X_i}}$ is the empirical measure of the i.i.d. sample $\ps{\theta}{X_1},\dots,\ps{\theta}{X_n}$ of $\al_\theta$.Slices of discrete measures (§3), and $\ps{\theta}{X_i}\sim\al_\theta$.
  3. $$\E\,W_1\big((P_\theta)_\sharp\hat\al_n,\al_\theta\big)=\int_{-R}^R\E\,\big\lvert\hat F_{\theta,n}(s)-F_\theta(s)\big\rvert\,\dd s .$$$W_1$ on the line is the area between the CDFs; both CDFs are $0$ before $-R$ and $1$ after $R$; then exchange $\E$ and $\int$ (Tonelli).
  4. $$\E\,\big\lvert\hat F_{\theta,n}(s)-F_\theta(s)\big\rvert\le\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}\,\hat F_{\theta,n}(s)}=\sqrt{\frac{F_\theta(s)(1-F_\theta(s))}n}\le\frac1{2\sqrt n}.$$Cauchy–Schwarz $\E\abs U\le\sqrt{\E U^2}$ for the centred variable $U=\hat F-F$; the variance of a binomial proportion is $F(1-F)/n$; and $F(1-F)\le\frac14$.
  5. $$\E\,W_1\big((P_\theta)_\sharp\hat\al_n,\al_\theta\big)\le2R\cdot\frac1{2\sqrt n}=\frac R{\sqrt n},\qquad\E\,SW_1(\hat\al_n,\al)\le\frac R{\sqrt n}.$$Integrate step 4 over the interval $[-R,R]$ of length $2R$; then average over $\theta$ (Tonelli again).
  6. $$\E\,\big\lvert SW_1(\hat\al_n,\hat\be_m)-SW_1(\al,\be)\big\rvert\le\frac R{\sqrt n}+\frac R{\sqrt m}.\qquad\square$$Take expectations in step 1 and apply step 5 to $\al$ and to $\be$.

[…] Combining this estimate with Theorem 10.16 gives the joint bound

$$\E\,\big\lvert\mathcal{SW}_1^L(\hat\al_n,\hat\be_m)-\mathcal{SW}_1(\al,\be)\big\rvert\le R\Big(\frac1{\sqrt L}+\frac1{\sqrt n}+\frac1{\sqrt m}\Big).\qquad(10.7)$$

Thus the directional approximation preserves the empirical rate provided $L\gtrsim\min\{n,m\}$.

Worked example — the error budget

Two clouds of $n=m=100$ points in the unit ball ($R=1$) of any $\R^d$: the statistical error of $SW_1$ is at most $\frac1{10}+\frac1{10}=0.2$; with $L=100$ directions the total bound (10.7) is $0.3$. With $10^4$ points and $10^4$ directions: $0.03$. The directions are “a third sample”: there is no point in using many fewer directions than points.

Note on the sources — the rate $n^{-1/(2p)}$ is a worst case

For $p=1$ the slide’s rate is $n^{-1/2}$ (Theorem 10.16). For $p>1$, the rate $n^{-1/(2p)}$ comes from the line, where it is attained by measures with gaps. Example: $\mu=\frac12(\delta_0+\delta_1)$ on $\R$. If $k$ of the $n$ samples fall at $0$, the empirical measure must move a mass $\abs{\frac kn-\frac12}$ by a distance $1$, so $W_p(\hat\mu_n,\mu)=\abs{\frac kn-\frac12}^{1/p}$; an exact binomial sum gives $n^{1/4}\,\E\,W_2(\hat\mu_n,\mu)=0.527,0.571,0.580,0.581$ for $n=10,10^2,10^3,10^4$: the rate $n^{-1/4}$ (constant $0.58$). For measures without gaps — like the Gaussians of the plot — the observed rate is close to $n^{-1/2}$ (slopes $-0.46$ to $-0.49$ above). Either way, the exponent does not depend on $d$.

Pitfall — a dimension-free error, but a signal that shrinks

The estimation error of $SW_2$ does not grow with $d$; the distance itself can shrink. A unit translation has $SW_2=1/\sqrt d$ (§6). With $n=500$ samples of $\N(0,\Id_d)$ and of the same law shifted by $e_1$ ($10$ pairs, $500$ directions), we measured $SW_2=0.718$, $0.344$, $0.176$ for $d=2,10,50$, against the true values $0.707$, $0.316$, $0.141$ — while two samples of the same law already give $SW_2\approx0.11$ at $n=500$. In dimension $50$ the signal is barely above the noise: detecting a shift of size $\delta$ needs roughly $n\gtrsim d/\delta^2$ samples. (The plug-in $W_2$ is worse: $1.04$, $2.44$, $7.84$ for a true value of $1$.)

In machine learning — comparing large datasets and embedding distributions

A model in production receives inputs whose distribution may drift away from the training data; two text corpora, two hospitals, two cameras produce feature distributions that one wants to compare. Typical sizes: $n=10^5$ embeddings in $d=768$. Exact transport needs a $10^5\times10^5$ cost matrix ($4\times10^{10}$ bytes in single precision) and an assignment of cost $\sim n^3=10^{15}$: impossible. The sliced distance with $K=256$ directions needs about $2Kdn\approx3.9\times10^{10}$ multiply-adds (one matrix product, seconds on a GPU) and $2K$ sorts of $10^5$ numbers. And thanks to Theorem 10.16 its value from samples is accurate at the rate $n^{-1/2}$, so it can be used as the statistic of a two-sample test or of a drift monitor. Keep the pitfall above in mind: a shift along a single feature is diluted by the factor $\sqrt d$; max-sliced and subspace variants (§10) are designed to catch such low-dimensional differences.

In machine learning — domain adaptation with sliced losses

In unsupervised domain adaptation, a classifier is trained on labelled source data (say, synthetic images) and must work on unlabelled target data (real images). A classical recipe adds to the classification loss a term that aligns the distribution of the features computed on source and target mini-batches; optimal transport is a natural choice for that term (Courty et al. 2017, in the slides’ references). On mini-batches of a few hundred high-dimensional features, the sliced distance is a cheap, differentiable alignment term computed exactly as in §8. Lee et al. (CVPR 2019, “sliced Wasserstein discrepancy”) use a sliced distance between the output distributions of two classifiers on target data, inside an adversarial scheme. The statistical rate above is what makes a mini-batch estimate meaningful.

Reflexes
  • Rate of a sliced quantity → it inherits the one-dimensional rate: slice of the sample = sample of the slice.
  • $W_1$ on the line → area between CDFs; empirical CDF → binomial, error $\le1/(2\sqrt n)$.
  • Budget → $R(L^{-1/2}+n^{-1/2}+m^{-1/2})$: take $L$ of the order of $n$.
  • High dimension → the error does not grow, but the distance between laws that differ in few directions shrinks like $1/\sqrt d$.

Why does the proof fail for $W_1$ itself in $\R^d$, $d\ge2$?

Answer

Step 3 is one-dimensional: on the line, $W_1=\int\abs{F_\mu-F_\nu}$, and each point $s$ involves only a binomial count. In $\R^d$ there is no such formula; the dual formula $W_1=\sup_{\Lip(f)\le1}\int f\,\dd(\mu-\nu)$ (Lesson 4) involves a supremum over a huge class of test functions, and controlling $\sup_f\big(\frac1n\sum_if(X_i)-\E f(X)\big)$ uniformly over $1$-Lipschitz $f$ in $d$ dimensions is what produces the rate $n^{-1/d}$.
