Graded from ★ (direct application) to ★★★ (needs an idea). Exercises 4 and 5 are the two proofs that the notes leave “as an exercise” (Remarks 2 and 4). Try before opening the hint; open the hint before the solution. All numbers were checked by script.

Exercise 1 — Two Diracs ★

In $\R^2$, compute $SW_1$, $SW_2$ and $W_2$ between $\delta_{(0,0)}$ and $\delta_{(3,4)}$. Check the triangle inequality of $SW_2$ with the intermediate Dirac $\delta_{(3,0)}$.

Hint

Slices of Diracs are Diracs: $SW_p^p(\delta_x,\delta_y)=\int\abs{\ps{\theta}{x-y}}^p\,\dd\sigma=\kappa_{d,p}\norm{x-y}^p$ (§6), with $\kappa_{2,1}=\frac2\pi$, $\kappa_{2,2}=\frac12$.

Solution

$\norm{x-y}=5$. $W_2=5$ (only one coupling). $SW_2=\sqrt{\kappa_{2,2}}\cdot5=\frac5{\sqrt2}\approx3.536$ and $SW_1=\kappa_{2,1}\cdot5=\frac{10}\pi\approx3.183$. Triangle inequality: $SW_2(\delta_x,\delta_y)=\frac1{\sqrt2}\norm{x-y}$ for all Diracs, so it reduces to the Euclidean one: $\frac5{\sqrt2}\le\frac3{\sqrt2}+\frac4{\sqrt2}=\frac7{\sqrt2}$. ✓ On Diracs, $SW_p$ is the Euclidean distance multiplied by $\kappa_{d,p}^{1/p}$.

Exercise 2 — A Monte Carlo estimate by hand ★

Let $\al=\frac13(\delta_{(0,0)}+\delta_{(2,1)}+\delta_{(1,3)})$ and $\be=\frac13(\delta_{(1,0)}+\delta_{(3,3)}+\delta_{(0,2)})$. Compute $W_2^2(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)$ for $\theta=e_1$, $e_2$ and $(\frac35,\frac45)$, and the resulting three-direction estimate of $SW_2^2$. Compute $W_2^2(\al,\be)$ and check the bound of §6 against the exact value $SW_2^2\approx0.590$.

Hint

Project, sort, match by rank (§2–§3). For $W_2^2$, there are only $3!=6$ assignments.

Solution
  • $e_1$: $\al\mapsto\{0,2,1\}$, sorted $\{0,1,2\}$; $\be\mapsto\{1,3,0\}$, sorted $\{0,1,3\}$. Cost $\frac{0+0+1}3=\frac13$.
  • $e_2$: $\{0,1,3\}$ against $\{0,3,2\}\to\{0,2,3\}$. Cost $\frac{0+1+0}3=\frac13$.
  • $(\frac35,\frac45)$: $\{0,2,3\}$ against $\{0.6,4.2,1.6\}\to\{0.6,1.6,4.2\}$. Cost $\frac{0.36+0.16+1.44}3=\frac{49}{75}\approx0.653$.

Estimate: $\frac13\big(\frac13+\frac13+\frac{49}{75}\big)=\frac{11}{25}=0.44$, against the exact $SW_2^2\approx0.590$ (quadrature): three directions are too few. $W_2^2$: the six assignments cost $\frac13\{8,10,10,18,22,32\}$; the best one, $(0,0)\to(1,0)$, $(2,1)\to(3,3)$, $(1,3)\to(0,2)$, gives $W_2^2=\frac83\approx2.667$. Bound: $SW_2^2\le\frac12W_2^2=\frac43$. ✓

Exercise 3 — Dimension one, and $SW_p\le W_p$ ★

(a) Show that $SW_p=W_p$ on the line. (b) Show that $SW_p\le W_p$ in any dimension, for every $p\ge1$ (StatOT, §2.10, Exercise 4). Is the inequality tight for $d\ge2$? What is the sharp constant?

Hint

(a) $\mathbb S^0=\{-1,1\}$. (b) Project an optimal plan; $\abs{\ps{\theta}{z}}\le\norm z$. For the sharp constant, recall §6.

Solution

(a) The uniform law on $\mathbb S^0=\{-1,1\}$ is $\frac12(\delta_{-1}+\delta_1)$. The slice along $+1$ is the measure itself; along $-1$ it is its mirror image $\pf{(-\Id)}{\al}$. The map $x\mapsto-x$ is an isometry of $\R$, so $W_p(\pf{(-\Id)}{\al},\pf{(-\Id)}{\be})=W_p(\al,\be)$ (push an optimal plan by $(-\Id,-\Id)$, and back). So $SW_p^p=\frac12W_p^p+\frac12W_p^p=W_p^p$.

(b) Let $(X,Y)$ follow an optimal plan. For every $\theta$, $W_p^p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)\le\E\abs{\ps{\theta}{X-Y}}^p\le\E\norm{X-Y}^p=W_p^p(\al,\be)$ (Cauchy–Schwarz: $\abs{\ps{\theta}{z}}\le\norm\theta\norm z=\norm z$). Average over $\theta$: $SW_p\le W_p$. For $d\ge2$ it is never an equality unless $\al=\be$: §6 gives the sharper $SW_p^p\le\kappa_{d,p}W_p^p$ with $\kappa_{d,p}<1$ (for instance $\kappa_{2,2}=\frac12$). The sharp constant is $\kappa_{d,p}^{1/p}$, attained by translations ($SW_p^p=\kappa_{d,p}\norm z^p$, $W_p=\norm z$).

Exercise 4 — Why the sphere? (notes, Remark 2) ★★

For any vector $x\in\R^d$ (not necessarily of norm $1$), let $P_x(y)=\ps{x}{y}$ and $\al_x=\pf{(P_x)}{\al}$. Let $p\ge1$ and $\al,\be\in\Pc_p(\R^d)$.

  1. Show that $W_p(\al_{r\theta},\be_{r\theta})=r\,W_p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)$ for $r>0$ and $\theta\in\mathbb S^{d-1}$.
  2. Deduce that $\int_{\R^d}W_p^p(\al_x,\be_x)\,\dd x=+\infty$ unless $\al=\be$.
  3. If the random direction is $x=R\theta$ with $\theta\sim\sigma$ and $R\ge0$ independent of $\theta$, show that $\E\,W_p^p(\al_x,\be_x)=\E[R^p]\,SW_p^p(\al,\be)$. What do unnormalized standard Gaussian vectors give for $p=2$?
Hint

(a) $P_{r\theta}=r\,P_\theta$: the slice along $r\theta$ is the slice along $\theta$ dilated by $r$. (b) Polar coordinates (formula (4) of the notes). (c) If $G\sim\N(0,\Id_d)$, then $G/\norm G\sim\sigma$, independent of $\norm G$ (proof of Proposition 2, §8), and $\E\norm G^2=d$.

Solution
  1. (a) $\al_{r\theta}=\pf{(D_r)}{\al_\theta}$ with $D_r(s)=rs$, because $\ps{r\theta}{y}=r\ps{\theta}{y}$. The map $D_r\times D_r$ is a bijection between the couplings of $(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)$ and those of $(\al_{r\theta},\be_{r\theta})$, and it multiplies the cost $\int\abs{s-t}^p\,\dd\pi$ by $r^p$. Taking the minimum: $W_p^p(\al_{r\theta},\be_{r\theta})=r^pW_p^p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)$.Dilating both measures by $r$ dilates all distances by $r$.
  2. (b) $$\begin{aligned}\int_{\R^d}W_p^p(\al_x,\be_x)\,\dd x&=\int_0^\infty\!\!\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}r^pW_p^p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)\,r^{d-1}\,\dd\tilde\sigma(\theta)\,\dd r\\&=\abs{\mathbb S^{d-1}}\,SW_p^p(\al,\be)\int_0^\infty r^{p+d-1}\,\dd r .\end{aligned}$$Polar coordinates $x=r\theta$ (Remark 1), then (a), then $\tilde\sigma=\abs{\mathbb S^{d-1}}\,\sigma$.
  3. The last integral is $+\infty$, so the total is $+\infty$ as soon as $SW_p(\al,\be)>0$, i.e. as soon as $\al\ne\be$ (§5). If $\al=\be$ it is $0$.$\int_0^\infty r^{p+d-1}\,\dd r$ diverges at infinity. Every point of a ray carries the same slice, rescaled: integrating over $\R^d$ counts each direction infinitely often.
  4. (c) $\E\,W_p^p(\al_{R\theta},\be_{R\theta})=\E\big[R^pW_p^p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)\big]=\E[R^p]\,\E\big[W_p^p(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)\big]=\E[R^p]\,SW_p^p(\al,\be).$(a), then independence of $R$ and $\theta$.
  5. For $G\sim\N(0,\Id_d)$: $R=\norm G$, $\theta=G/\norm G$, $\E[R^2]=d$. So averaging the slice costs over unnormalized Gaussian directions gives $d\,SW_2^2$, not $SW_2^2$.For the cross of §4 ($d=2$): $400\,000$ Gaussian directions give $0.725$, against $d\,SW_2^2=2(1-\frac2\pi)=0.727$.

Conclusion: any rotation-invariant law of directions with a finite $p$-th moment gives $SW_p^p$ up to a constant factor; the uniform law on the sphere is the normalization with factor $1$.

Exercise 5 — The constant $c_{d,p}$ (notes, Remark 4) ★★

The notes define $c_{d,p}=\frac1d\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}\norm\theta_p^p\,\dd\mathcal U_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}(\theta)$, with $\norm\theta_p^p=\sum_i\abs{\theta_i}^p$.

  1. Show that $c_{d,p}=\kappa_{d,p}=\int\abs{\ps{e}{\theta}}^p\,\dd\sigma(\theta)$ for any unit vector $e$, and that $c_{d,p}\le1$.
  2. Show that $c_{d,p}\le\frac1d$ for $p\ge2$, with equality if and only if $p=2$ (the claim of Remark 4).
  3. Does it hold for $1\le p<2$?
Hint

(a) All coordinates of a uniform direction have the same law (symmetry), and so does $\ps{e}{\theta}$ (rotation invariance). (b) $\abs{\theta_i}\le1$, so $\abs{\theta_i}^p\le\theta_i^2$ when $p\ge2$. (c) Compare $\norm\theta_1$ with $\norm\theta_2=1$.

Solution

(a) Permuting the coordinates is an orthogonal map, so all $\theta_i$ have the law of $\theta_1$: $c_{d,p}=\frac1d\sum_i\E\abs{\theta_i}^p=\E\abs{\theta_1}^p$. A rotation sending $e$ to $e_1$ shows that $\ps{e}{\theta}$ also has the law of $\theta_1$: $c_{d,p}=\kappa_{d,p}$. Since $\abs{\theta_1}\le1$, $\kappa_{d,p}\le1$ — the bound printed in Proposition 1. (This also shows that line (15) of the notes is an equality.)

(b) For $p\ge2$ and $\abs{\theta_i}\le1$: $\abs{\theta_i}^p=\theta_i^2\abs{\theta_i}^{p-2}\le\theta_i^2$. Summing, $\norm\theta_p^p\le\norm\theta_2^2=1$, hence $c_{d,p}\le\frac1d$. For $p=2$ it is an equality ($\norm\theta_2^2=1$ exactly). For $p>2$ it is strict: $\abs{\theta_i}^p<\theta_i^2$ as soon as $0<\abs{\theta_i}<1$, which happens with probability $1$ when $d\ge2$. Consequence: $SW_p^p\le\frac1dW_p^p$ for all $p\ge2$.

(c) No. For $p=1$: $\norm\theta_1\ge\norm\theta_2=1$, so $c_{d,1}\ge\frac1d$, strictly for $d\ge2$. In fact $d\,\kappa_{d,1}=1.273$ ($d=2$), $1.5$ ($d=3$), $2.587$ ($d=10$), $5.67$ ($d=50$), growing like $\sqrt{2d/\pi}$; for $p=1.5$: $1.113$ and $1.558$ for $d=2,10$. Over $2\le d\le50$ and $p\in[2,8]$, the largest value of $d\,\kappa_{d,p}$ is $1$, at $p=2$ (computed from the Gamma formula).

Exercise 6 — Archimedes and $\kappa_{3,p}$ ★★

(a) Parametrize the unit sphere of $\R^3$ by the height $z\in(-1,1)$ and the longitude $\varphi$: $x=(\sqrt{1-z^2}\cos\varphi,\sqrt{1-z^2}\sin\varphi,z)$. Show that the area element is $\dd z\,\dd\varphi$, and deduce that the height $\theta_3$ of a uniform direction is uniform on $[-1,1]$. (b) Deduce $\kappa_{3,p}=\frac1{p+1}$. (c) Compute $SW_1$ and $SW_2$ between $\delta_0$ and $\delta_z$ in $\R^3$.

Hint

(a) Compute the norm of the cross product $\partial_zx\times\partial_\varphi x$. Geometric version: the circle at height $z$ is shorter by the factor $\sqrt{1-z^2}$, but the sphere is steeper there by the factor $1/\sqrt{1-z^2}$.

Solution

(a) With $\rho=\sqrt{1-z^2}$ and $\rho'=-z/\rho$: $\partial_zx=(\rho'\cos\varphi,\rho'\sin\varphi,1)$, $\partial_\varphi x=(-\rho\sin\varphi,\rho\cos\varphi,0)$, and $\partial_zx\times\partial_\varphi x=(-\rho\cos\varphi,-\rho\sin\varphi,\rho\rho')$, of norm $\sqrt{\rho^2+\rho^2\rho'^2}=\sqrt{\rho^2+z^2}=1$. So the area of the zone $\{a\le z\le b\}$ is $2\pi(b-a)$ (Archimedes’ “hat-box” theorem), and the total area is $4\pi$: $\P(a\le\theta_3\le b)=\frac{b-a}2$, the uniform law on $[-1,1]$. (Monte Carlo with $10^6$ normalized Gaussian vectors: each of $10$ bins of length $0.2$ receives a frequency between $0.0993$ and $0.1004$.)

(b) $\kappa_{3,p}=\E\abs{\theta_3}^p=\frac12\int_{-1}^1\abs t^p\,\dd t=\frac1{p+1}$; the Gamma formula of §6 gives the same.

(c) $SW_p^p=\kappa_{3,p}\norm z^p$: $SW_1=\frac12\norm z$ and $SW_2=\frac1{\sqrt3}\norm z\approx0.577\norm z$.

Exercise 7 — Drawing directions the wrong way ★★

(a) Let $U$ be uniform on the square $[-1,1]^2$ and $\Phi$ the angle of $U$. Show that for $\abs\varphi\le\frac\pi4$, $\Phi$ has the density $\frac1{8\cos^2\varphi}$, and compare with $\frac1{2\pi}$. (b) In $\R^3$, draw the polar angle $\psi$ uniformly in $[0,\pi]$ and the longitude uniformly. Show that the height $z=\cos\psi$ has density $\frac1{\pi\sqrt{1-z^2}}$ and compute $\P(\abs z>0.9)$. Compare with Exercise 6.

Hint

(a) The probability that $\Phi\in[\varphi,\varphi+\dd\varphi]$ is the area of a thin wedge of the square, divided by $4$. For $\abs\varphi\le\frac\pi4$ the wedge leaves the square through the side $x=1$, at distance $1/\cos\varphi$. (b) Change of variables $z=\cos\psi$.

Solution

(a) The wedge is a thin triangle of radius $r(\varphi)=1/\cos\varphi$ and angle $\dd\varphi$, of area $\frac12r^2\dd\varphi=\frac{\dd\varphi}{2\cos^2\varphi}$; dividing by the area $4$ of the square gives the density $\frac1{8\cos^2\varphi}$ (and by symmetry the same pattern in each quarter of $[0,2\pi)$). It ranges from $\frac18=0.125$ on the axes to $\frac14=0.25$ on the diagonals, against the uniform $\frac1{2\pi}\approx0.159$: diagonal directions are twice as likely as axis directions. Within $5°$ of the direction $45°$: $2\int_{40°}^{45°}\frac{\dd\varphi}{8\cos^2\varphi}=\frac14(1-\tan40°)\approx0.0402$, against $\frac{10}{360}\approx0.0278$.

(b) $\psi$ has density $\frac1\pi$ on $[0,\pi]$; with $z=\cos\psi$, $\abs{\dd z}=\sin\psi\,\dd\psi=\sqrt{1-z^2}\,\dd\psi$, so $z$ has density $\frac1\pi\cdot\frac1{\sqrt{1-z^2}}$ (the arcsine law). $\P(\abs z>0.9)=\P(\psi<\arccos0.9)+\P(\psi>\pi-\arccos0.9)=\frac{2\arccos0.9}\pi\approx0.287$. For the uniform law on the sphere (Exercise 6), $z$ is uniform and $\P(\abs z>0.9)=0.1$: the wrong recipe puts almost three times too many directions near the poles. (Monte Carlo with $2\times10^6$ draws: $0.287$ and $0.0998$.)

Exercise 8 — Two directions are not enough ★★

Let $\al=\frac12(\delta_{(0,0)}+\delta_{(1,1)})$ and $\be=\frac12(\delta_{(1,0)}+\delta_{(0,1)})$. (a) Show that their slices on $e_1$ and on $e_2$ coincide. (b) For $\theta=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)$, show that $W_2^2(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)=\min(\cos^2\varphi,\sin^2\varphi)$. (c) Compute $SW_2^2(\al,\be)$ and $W_2^2(\al,\be)$. (d) What would a “sliced” computation using only the directions $e_1,e_2$ conclude?

Hint

(b) Treat $\varphi\in[0,\frac\pi2]$ first: the slices are $\{0,\cos\varphi+\sin\varphi\}$ and $\{\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi\}$. Then $\varphi\in[\frac\pi2,\pi]$, and use that $\theta$ and $-\theta$ give the same cost.

Solution

(a) On $e_1$: $\al\mapsto\{0,1\}$, $\be\mapsto\{1,0\}$; on $e_2$: $\al\mapsto\{0,1\}$, $\be\mapsto\{0,1\}$. In both cases $\frac12(\delta_0+\delta_1)$.

(b) Write $c=\cos\varphi$, $s=\sin\varphi$; $\al_\theta=\frac12(\delta_0+\delta_{c+s})$, $\be_\theta=\frac12(\delta_c+\delta_s)$. If $c,s\ge0$: sorted, $0\le c+s$ and $\min(c,s)\le\max(c,s)$; the cost is $\frac12\big[\min^2+(c+s-\max)^2\big]=\frac12[\min^2+\min^2]=\min(c,s)^2$. If $c<0\le s$: when $c+s\ge0$ (i.e. $c^2\le s^2$), the sorted matching is $0\to c$, $c+s\to s$, cost $\frac12[c^2+c^2]=c^2$; when $c+s<0$, it is $c+s\to c$, $0\to s$, cost $s^2$. In all cases $\min(c^2,s^2)$. Directions $\varphi+\pi$ give the reflected slices, hence the same cost.

(c) The profile has period $\frac\pi2$ and is symmetric about $\frac\pi4$: $SW_2^2=\frac4\pi\int_0^{\pi/4}\sin^2\varphi\,\dd\varphi=\frac4\pi\Big(\frac\pi8-\frac14\Big)=\frac12-\frac1\pi\approx0.182$. All four squared distances between a point of $\al$ and a point of $\be$ equal $1$, so $W_2^2=1$ (and $SW_2^2\le\frac12$ ✓).

(d) It would find $0$ and declare $\al=\be$. The profile vanishes only on the axes, a set of directions of probability zero, so random directions do detect the difference — but any fixed finite set of directions defines only a pseudo-distance (Cramér–Wold needs all directions, §5).

Exercise 9 — Max-sliced and lifted plans on two pairs ★★

Let $\al=\frac12(\delta_{(0,0)}+\delta_{(1,3)})$ and $\be=\frac12(\delta_{(1,0)}+\delta_{(0,3)})$ (the pair of §10 and of Exercise 2 of the sheet). (a) Compute $W_2^2(\al,\be)$. (b) Compute the plans lifted from the sorted matchings along $e_1$ and along $e_2$, their costs, and deduce $\text{Min-SW}_2^2(\al,\be)$. (c) Compute $W_2^2(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)$ for $\theta=(3,1)/\sqrt{10}$. (d) Prove $SW_2\le\mathrm{MaxSW}_2\le W_2$ in general.

Hint

(b) Along $e_1$, the order of $\be$ is $(0,3)$ then $(1,0)$. (c) Both points of $\be$ have the same coordinate along $\theta$.

Solution

(a) Straight pairs $(0,0)\to(1,0)$, $(1,3)\to(0,3)$: cost $\frac{1+1}2=1$; crossed pairs: $\frac{9+9}2=9$. So $W_2^2=1$.

(b) Along $e_1$: $\al$ sorted as $(0,0)$ [coordinate $0$], $(1,3)$ [$1$]; $\be$ as $(0,3)$ [$0$], $(1,0)$ [$1$]. Lifted plan: $(0,0)\to(0,3)$, $(1,3)\to(1,0)$, cost $9$. Along $e_2$: $\al$ as $(0,0)$, $(1,3)$; $\be$ as $(1,0)$, $(0,3)$: the optimal plan, cost $1$. Since every lifted plan costs at least $W_2^2=1$ and $e_2$ achieves $1$: $\text{Min-SW}_2^2=1=W_2^2$.

(c) Along $\theta=(3,1)/\sqrt{10}$: $\al\mapsto\{0,\frac6{\sqrt{10}}\}$ and $\be\mapsto\{\frac3{\sqrt{10}},\frac3{\sqrt{10}}\}$, a single atom. Each point of $\al$ moves by $\frac3{\sqrt{10}}$: $W_2^2=\frac9{10}=0.9$. So $\mathrm{MaxSW}_2^2\ge0.9$; a sweep over $2\times10^6$ angles shows that $0.9$ is the maximum (attained at $18.4°$ and $161.6°$). Meanwhile $SW_2^2\approx0.364$.

(d) An average of the values $W_2^2(\al_\theta,\be_\theta)$ is at most their maximum: $SW_2\le\mathrm{MaxSW}_2$. Each slice cost is at most $W_2^2$ (project an optimal plan, $\abs{\ps{\theta}{z}}\le\norm z$): $\mathrm{MaxSW}_2\le W_2$. Here: $0.364\le0.9\le1\le\text{Min-SW}_2^2=1$.

Exercise 10 — The formula for $\kappa_{d,p}$, with Gaussian directions ★★★

Let $G\sim\N(0,\Id_d)$. (a) Using $G=\norm G\cdot\frac G{\norm G}$ and Proposition 2, show that $\E\abs{G_1}^p=\E\norm G^p\cdot\kappa_{d,p}$. (b) Compute $\E\abs{G_1}^p$ and $\E\norm G^p$ with the Gamma function. (c) Deduce $\kappa_{d,p}=\frac{\Gamma(d/2)\,\Gamma((p+1)/2)}{\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma((d+p)/2)}$, and check $p=2$ and $d=3$.

Hint

(a) The direction $G/\norm G$ is uniform and independent of the radius $\norm G$ (§8). (b) Substitute $t=x^2/2$ in $\int_0^\infty x^pe^{-x^2/2}\,\dd x$; $\norm G^2$ follows a $\chi^2_d$ law, whose density is proportional to $t^{d/2-1}e^{-t/2}$.

Solution
  1. $\abs{G_1}^p=\norm G^p\,\abs{\theta_1}^p$ with $\theta=G/\norm G$; independence gives $\E\abs{G_1}^p=\E\norm G^p\,\E\abs{\theta_1}^p=\E\norm G^p\,\kappa_{d,p}$.Proposition 2 and the factorization of its proof (§8).
  2. $$\E\abs{G_1}^p=\frac2{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_0^\infty x^pe^{-x^2/2}\,\dd x=\frac2{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,2^{\frac{p-1}2}\Gamma\Big(\frac{p+1}2\Big)=\frac{2^{p/2}}{\sqrt\pi}\Gamma\Big(\frac{p+1}2\Big).$$Symmetry of the density, then $t=x^2/2$: $\int_0^\infty x^pe^{-x^2/2}\dd x=2^{\frac{p-1}2}\int_0^\infty t^{\frac{p-1}2}e^{-t}\,\dd t$.
  3. $$\E\norm G^p=\E\big[(\norm G^2)^{p/2}\big]=\frac{2^{p/2}\,\Gamma\big(\frac{d+p}2\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac d2\big)}.$$$\norm G^2\sim\chi^2_d$ with density $\frac{t^{d/2-1}e^{-t/2}}{2^{d/2}\Gamma(d/2)}$; integrate $t^{p/2}$ against it and substitute $t=2u$.
  4. $$\kappa_{d,p}=\frac{\E\abs{G_1}^p}{\E\norm G^p}=\frac{\Gamma(d/2)\,\Gamma((p+1)/2)}{\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma((d+p)/2)}.\qquad\square$$Steps 1–3; the powers of $2$ cancel.

Checks: $p=2$: $\E G_1^2=1$, $\E\norm G^2=d$, so $\kappa_{d,2}=\frac1d$. $d=3$: $\frac{\Gamma(3/2)\Gamma((p+1)/2)}{\sqrt\pi\,\frac{p+1}2\Gamma((p+1)/2)}=\frac1{p+1}$ (Exercise 6). Numerically, $\kappa_{2,1}=0.636620=\frac2\pi$, $\kappa_{5,1}=0.375$, $\kappa_{5,3}=0.125$, and Monte Carlo estimates of $\E\abs{G_1}^p$ agree with step 2 ($0.7979$, $1$, $1.596$ for $p=1,2,3$).

Exercise 11 — The worst-case rate $n^{-1/(2p)}$: two atoms ★★★

Let $\mu=\frac12(\delta_0+\delta_1)$ on $\R$, and $\hat\mu_n$ the empirical measure of $n$ i.i.d. samples; $k$ of them fall at $0$. (a) Show that $W_p(\hat\mu_n,\mu)=\abs{\frac kn-\frac12}^{1/p}$. (b) Using the central limit theorem, show that $\E\,W_p(\hat\mu_n,\mu)\approx c_p\,n^{-1/(2p)}$ and compute $c_1$ and $c_2$. (c) In $\R^d$, let $\mu=\frac12(\delta_0+\delta_{e_1})$. What is the rate of $\E\,SW_p(\hat\mu_n,\mu)$? Comment on the slide’s $\mathcal O(n^{-1/(2p)})$ (frame 11/12) and on frame 7/12.

Hint

(a) Keep the common part in place. (b) $\frac kn-\frac12\approx\frac{Z}{2\sqrt n}$ with $Z\sim\N(0,1)$; $\E\abs Z=\sqrt{2/\pi}$ and $\E\abs Z^{1/2}=2^{1/4}\Gamma(3/4)/\sqrt\pi\approx0.822$. (c) Slice: atoms at $0$ and $\theta_1$.

Solution

(a) $\hat\mu_n=\frac kn\delta_0+(1-\frac kn)\delta_1$. The monotone plan keeps $\min(\frac kn,\frac12)$ at $0$, $\min(1-\frac kn,\frac12)$ at $1$, and moves the excess $\abs{\frac kn-\frac12}$ by a distance $1$: $W_p^p=\abs{\frac kn-\frac12}$.

(b) $k\sim\mathcal B(n,\frac12)$, so $\frac kn-\frac12$ has variance $\frac1{4n}$ and is approximately $\frac Z{2\sqrt n}$. Then $\E\,W_p\approx\E\abs{\frac Z2}^{1/p}n^{-1/(2p)}$: $c_1=\frac12\sqrt{2/\pi}\approx0.399$ and $c_2=\frac{0.822}{\sqrt2}\approx0.581$. Exact binomial sums: $\sqrt n\,\E\,W_1=0.389,0.398,0.399,0.399$ and $n^{1/4}\,\E\,W_2=0.527,0.571,0.580,0.581$ for $n=10,10^2,10^3,10^4$. For $p=2$ the error decays like $n^{-1/4}$: to divide it by $2$, multiply $n$ by $16$.

(c) The slice along $\theta$ is $\frac12(\delta_0+\delta_{\theta_1})$, and the slice of $\hat\mu_n$ is $\frac kn\delta_0+(1-\frac kn)\delta_{\theta_1}$: $W_p^p=\abs{\frac kn-\frac12}\abs{\theta_1}^p$, so $SW_p=\kappa_{d,p}^{1/p}\abs{\frac kn-\frac12}^{1/p}$: the same rate $n^{-1/(2p)}$, in every dimension. So the slide’s $\mathcal O(n^{-1/(2p)})$ cannot be improved in general for $p>1$ — it is a worst case, attained by measures with gaps; for $p=1$ it is $n^{-1/2}$ (Theorem 10.16). The same atoms also show that frame 7/12 needs $d>2p$: in $\R^3$ with $p=2$, $W_2(\hat\mu_n,\mu)$ decays like $n^{-1/4}$, slower than the $n^{-1/3}$ claimed there.

Exercise 12 — The sliced flow corrects a shift $d$ times more slowly ★★★

(a) Let $\al=\N(m,\Sigma)$ and $\be=\N(\bar m,\Sigma)$ (same covariance). Show that the sliced velocity (16.21) of §11 is the constant vector $w=\frac1d(\bar m-m)$. Deduce the flow $m_t$ of the means and the time needed to divide $\norm{m_t-\bar m}$ by $10$; compare with the $W_2$ flow of $\frac12W_2^2$, whose velocity is $T(x)-x=\bar m-m$. (b) In the particle algorithm of §11 with $K$ random directions and step $\tau$, show that the expected displacement of a point, for a target translated by $z$, is $\tau z$.

Hint

(a) Between two 1D Gaussians with the same variance, the monotone map is a translation. (b) $\E[\theta\theta\T]=\frac1d\Id$, and the partner along $\theta$ of $x_i$ is $x_i+z$ (worked example of §11).

Solution

(a) The slices are $\N(\ps{\theta}{m},v_\theta)$ and $\N(\ps{\theta}{\bar m},v_\theta)$ with the same variance $v_\theta=\theta\T\Sigma\theta$; the monotone map between them is $T_\theta(s)=s+\ps{\theta}{\bar m-m}$. So $w(x)=\int\ps{\theta}{\bar m-m}\theta\,\dd\sigma(\theta)=\E[\theta\theta\T](\bar m-m)=\frac1d(\bar m-m)$, for every $x$: the flow translates $\al$ rigidly, and $\dot m_t=\frac1d(\bar m-m_t)$, so $m_t-\bar m=e^{-t/d}(m_0-\bar m)$ (in agreement with the row $\mathcal{SW}_2^2$ of Proposition 16.23, which has the factor $\frac2d$ for the functional without the $\frac12$). Dividing the gap by $10$ takes $t=d\ln10$: $4.6$, $23.0$, $230$ for $d=2,10,100$, against $\ln10\approx2.30$ for the $W_2$ flow ($\dot m=\bar m-m$).

(b) For a target translated by $z$, the partner of $x_i$ along any $\theta$ is $x_i+z$, so the step is $\tau\frac dK\sum_k\ps{\theta_k}{z}\theta_k=\tau\frac dK\sum_k\theta_k\theta_k\T z$, of expectation $\tau\frac dK\cdot K\cdot\frac1d z=\tau z$. The factor $\frac dK$ exactly undoes the $\frac1d$ of (a). (With $\tau=1$, $8$ random directions in $\R^2$ and $z=(2,-1)$, one draw moved every point by $(0.94,-0.92)$, equal to $\frac28\sum_k\theta_k\theta_k\T z$ computed directly: far from $z$ for this draw, correct only on average.)
