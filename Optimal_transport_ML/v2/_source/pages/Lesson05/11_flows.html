11. Moving and averaging: sliced flows and barycenters slides 9/12–10/12; CompOT §10.4; OT4ML §16.2

To move a cloud of points towards a target, repeatedly sort both clouds along random directions and move each point towards its partner of the same rank: this is the gradient flow of $\frac12SW_2^2$, the oldest application of slicing (colour transfer); averaging slices direction by direction gives sliced and Radon barycenters.

From colour transfer to generative models

The problem: $SW_p$ is a number, but many applications need to move a cloud of points towards a target (recolour an image, generate samples) or to average several distributions. Slicing gives cheap ways to do both, and this is in fact where it started.

colour transfer
Give a photo the colour palette of another. Each pixel is a point of the colour cube $\R^3$ (red, green, blue): an image is a cloud of $10^6$ points in $\R^3$. Moving the cloud of the first image onto the cloud of the second, pixel by pixel, recolours the first image.
IDT
Iterative Distribution Transfer (Pitié, Kokaram and Dahyot): choose a random rotation of the colour space; in each of the three rotated axes, match the one-dimensional distribution of the image to that of the target (a sort, §2); rotate back; repeat. The marginals along more and more directions agree, and so does the whole distribution.
2012, 2013
The sliced distance itself (Rabin, Peyré, Delon and Bernot, texture mixing; the idea is credited to Marc Bernot) and its mathematical study (Bonnotte’s thesis), including the interpretation of IDT-like schemes as gradient flows.
FID
Fréchet Inception Distance: the squared $W_2$ distance between the Gaussians fitted to the features (computed by a pretrained network) of real and of generated images — Lesson 1’s Gaussian formula. Lower is better.

Moving particles by sorting: the sliced flow

In the course CompOT §10.4, (10.13)–(10.15), pp. 170–171

One can define a distance between two measures $(\al,\be)$ defined on $\R^d$ by aggregating 1-D Wasserstein distances between their projections onto all directions of the sphere. This defines

$$SW(\al,\be)^2:=\int_{\mathbb S^d}W_2(P_{\theta,\sharp}\al,P_{\theta,\sharp}\be)^2\,\dd\theta,\qquad(10.13)$$

where $\mathbb S^d=\{\theta\in\R^d:\ \norm\theta=1\}$ is the $d$-dimensional sphere […]. In the specific case where $m=n$ and $\al=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$, $\be=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{y_i}$, this is achieved by simply sorting points

$$SW(\al,\be)^2=\int_{\mathbb S^d}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n\abs{\ps{x_{\sigma_\theta(i)}-y_{\kappa_\theta(i)}}{\theta}}^2\Big)\dd\theta,$$

where $\sigma_\theta,\kappa_\theta\in\mathrm{Perm}(n)$ are the permutation ordering in increasing order, respectively, $(\ps{x_i}{\theta})_i$ and $(\ps{y_i}{\theta})_i$. Fixing the vector $y$, the function $\mathcal E_\be(x)=SW(\al,\be)^2$ is smooth, and one can use this function to define a mapping by gradient descent

$$x\leftarrow x-\tau\nabla\mathcal E_\be(x)\quad\text{where}\quad\nabla\mathcal E_\be(x)_i=2\int_{\mathbb S^d}\ps{x_i-y_{\kappa_\theta\circ\sigma_\theta^{-1}(i)}}{\theta}\,\theta\,\dd\theta\qquad(10.15)$$

using a small enough step size $\tau>0$. To make the method tractable, one can use a stochastic gradient descent (SGD), replacing this integral with a discrete sum against randomly drawn directions $\theta\in\mathbb S^d$. […] Numerically, one finds that this flow has no local minimizer and that it thus converges to $\al=\be$. […] This method has been used successfully for color transfer and texture synthesis in [Rabin et al., 2011] and is related to the alternate minimization approach detailed in [Pitié et al., 2007].

$\mathbb S^d$, $\dd\theta$
CompOT’s sphere is our $\mathbb S^{d-1}$ (the unit sphere of $\R^d$), with the surface measure, and the sum has no $\frac1n$: its $SW^2$ is $n\abs{\mathbb S^{d-1}}$ times ours. These constants only rescale the step $\tau$ (note on the sources, §14).
$\kappa_\theta\circ\sigma_\theta^{-1}(i)$
The partner of $x_i$ along $\theta$: $\sigma_\theta^{-1}(i)$ is the rank of $x_i$ among the projected $x$’s, and $\kappa_\theta$ of that rank is the index of the $y$ with the same rank.
$\ps{x_i-y_{\text{partner}}}{\theta}\,\theta$
The one-dimensional displacement between $x_i$ and its partner, put back in $\R^d$ along $\theta$. The gradient averages these lifted displacements over directions.
“smooth”
For almost every $\theta$ the ranks do not change under a small move of $x$, so each slice term is a quadratic function of $x$ locally; the gradient exists for almost every configuration.
SGD
Replace the integral by an average over a few random directions at each step (§8): an unbiased estimate of the gradient.
In the course OT4ML §16.2, (16.21), pp. 417–418

Sliced-Wasserstein flow. […] Take $\ \mathcal E_\be(\al)=\frac12\mathcal{SW}_2^2(\al,\be)$. […] If $T_\theta$ denotes the one-dimensional monotone transport from $(P_\theta)_\sharp\al$ to $(P_\theta)_\sharp\be$, then the formal $\mathcal W_2$-gradient-flow velocity is

$$w_{\mathcal{SW}}[\al,\be](x)=\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}\big(T_\theta(P_\theta x)-P_\theta x\big)\,\theta\,\dd\sigma(\theta).\qquad(16.21)$$

[…] This is the sliced-Wasserstein flow studied by Cozzi and Santambrogio, who prove long-time convergence under their hypotheses when the target is Gaussian and also show that the limiting characteristic map is not, in general, the optimal transport map.

$T_\theta(P_\theta x)-P_\theta x$
How far the coordinate of $x$ along $\theta$ must move to reach its partner of the same quantile: the 1D displacement. Lifted by $\theta$ and averaged, it is the velocity of the point $x$.
“gradient flow”
The continuous-time version of gradient descent, $\dot x=w(x)$: each particle follows the steepest descent of $\frac12SW_2^2$ in the Wasserstein geometry (Lesson 8). With particles of mass $\frac1n$, $w(x_i)=-n\nabla_{x_i}\mathcal E$.
“not the optimal map”
The flow does reach the target, but the matching it produces between the initial and final positions is in general not the $W_2$-optimal one.
Algorithm — iterated sliced matching (sliced flow, IDT)

Input: a cloud $x_1,\dots,x_n$ (to be moved) and a target cloud $y_1,\dots,y_n$ in $\R^d$, a step $\tau\in(0,1]$, a number $K$ of directions per step.

  1. Draw $K$ directions: $K$ normalized Gaussian vectors, or ($K=d$) a random orthonormal basis.
  2. For each direction $\theta_k$: sort the projections of both clouds; let $t_{k,i}$ be the projection of the partner of $x_i$ (same rank).
  3. Move every point: $\ x_i\leftarrow x_i+\tau\,\frac dK\sum_{k=1}^K\big(t_{k,i}-\ps{\theta_k}{x_i}\big)\,\theta_k$.
  4. Repeat until the clouds match (monitor $\widehat{SW}_2$).

The factor $\frac dK$ compensates $\E[\theta\theta\T]=\frac1d\Id$: in expectation, a translation by $z$ is corrected by $\tau z$ at each step. With an orthonormal basis and $\tau=1$, step 3 is exactly one iteration of IDT.

Worked example — a translation is corrected in one IDT step

Let the target be the cloud translated by $z$: $y_i=x_i+z$. Along any direction $\theta$, the sorted order of $\ps{\theta}{y_i}=\ps{\theta}{x_i}+\ps{\theta}{z}$ is that of the $\ps{\theta}{x_i}$, so the partner of $x_i$ is $y_i$ and $t_{k,i}-\ps{\theta_k}{x_i}=\ps{\theta_k}{z}$. With an orthonormal basis $\theta_1,\dots,\theta_d$ and $\tau=1$, $K=d$:

$$x_i\leftarrow x_i+\sum_{k=1}^d\ps{\theta_k}{z}\,\theta_k=x_i+z=y_i .$$

One step, exactly (numerically: error $9\times10^{-16}$ on $50$ points). With $K$ random directions instead, the step is $x_i\leftarrow x_i+\tau\frac dK\sum_k\theta_k\theta_k\T z$: correct only on average, since $\frac dK\sum_k\theta_k\theta_k\T\approx\Id$ for large $K$.

Gaussians: the sliced flow is $d$ times slower on the means

Proposition 16.23 (Gaussian closure catalogue). Let $\ga=\N(\bar m,\bar\Sigma)$ on $\R^d$, with $\bar\Sigma\succ0$, and let the initial condition be $\al_0=\N(m_0,\Sigma_0)$, with $\Sigma_0\succ0$. “Gaussian closure”
If you start from a Gaussian, the flow stays Gaussian, and the infinite-dimensional flow reduces to an ODE on the mean and the covariance.
$h=-\frac2d\delta_m$
The mean moves straight towards the target mean, with speed $\frac2d$ times the remaining gap (for the functional $SW_2^2$; for $\frac12SW_2^2$ the speed is $\frac1d$). For $W_2^2$ the factor is $2$ (resp. $1$).
Worked example — how slow?

For the flows of $\frac12W_2^2$ and $\frac12SW_2^2$, the mean error solves $\dot\delta=-\delta$ and $\dot\delta=-\frac1d\delta$: $\delta_t=e^{-t}\delta_0$ versus $e^{-t/d}\delta_0$. Dividing the error by $10$ takes a time $\ln10\approx2.30$ for the $W_2$ flow, and $d\ln10$ for the sliced flow: $4.6$, $23.0$, $230$ for $d=2$, $10$, $100$. This is the dynamical face of $SW_2^2=\frac1d\norm{z}^2$ for a translation (§6) — and the reason for the factor $\frac dK$ in the algorithm above.

Sliced and Radon barycenters

In the course OT4ML §12.1, pp. 262–263

For inputs $\be_s\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$, define the sliced barycenter by replacing $\mathcal W_2$ with $\mathcal{SW}_2$:

$$\min_{\al\in\Pc_2(\R^d)}\sum_{s=1}^S\lambda_s\mathcal{SW}_2^2(\al,\be_s)=\min_{\al\in\Pc_2(\R^d)}\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}\sum_{s=1}^S\lambda_s\mathcal W_2^2\big((P_\theta)_\sharp\al,(P_\theta)_\sharp\be_s\big)\,\dd\sigma(\theta).$$

The constraint that all projected measures come from the same $\al$ is the nontrivial part of this problem. A cheaper Radon-domain approximation drops this consistency constraint and minimizes directly over measurable collections $(\ga_\theta)_{\theta\in\mathbb S^{d-1}}$ with $\ga_\theta\in\Pc_2(\R)$ […]. This problem decouples over directions. For each $\theta$, the minimizer is the one-dimensional barycenter $\ga_\theta$ with quantile $\ \mathcal C_{\ga_\theta}^{-1}(r)=\sum_{s=1}^S\lambda_s\mathcal C_{\be_{s,\theta}}^{-1}(r)$. […] In general, however, independently computed one-dimensional barycenters do not satisfy the range conditions of the Radon transform. One therefore reconstructs a density in a least-squares sense, usually through a regularized Radon pseudoinverse.

sliced barycenter
The Fréchet mean of Lesson 3 §7, with $SW_2^2$ instead of $W_2^2$. CompOT (10.16)–(10.17) computes it by moving particles with the sliced gradient above, one gradient per input.
Radon barycenter
Average the inputs slice by slice: in each direction, the 1D barycenter is obtained by averaging quantile functions (Lesson 3 §8). Then come back to $\R^d$ by inverting the Radon transform (filtered back-projection, as in a CT scanner; CompOT (10.18)–(10.20); OT4ML Fig. 12.3, p. 265).
range conditions
Not every family $(\ga_\theta)_\theta$ of measures on the line is the family of slices of a measure on $\R^d$. Averaged slices may fail to be one — next example.
Worked example — averaged slices that are nobody’s slices

Take $\be_1=\N(0,\mathrm{diag}(4,1))$ and $\be_2=\N(0,\mathrm{diag}(1,4))$ in $\R^2$, weights $\frac12,\frac12$. Each slice is a centred Gaussian, and the 1D barycenter of $\N(0,s^2)$ and $\N(0,t^2)$ is $\N\big(0,(\frac{s+t}2)^2\big)$ (Lesson 3). So the Radon barycenter has the slices $\ga_\theta=\N(0,q(\theta))$ with

$$\begin{aligned}q(\theta)&=\Big(\frac{\sqrt{\theta\T\Sigma_1\theta}+\sqrt{\theta\T\Sigma_2\theta}}2\Big)^2:\\ q(e_1)=q(e_2)&=\Big(\frac{2+1}2\Big)^2=2.25,\qquad q\big(\tfrac{(1,\pm1)}{\sqrt2}\big)=\Big(\frac{\sqrt{2.5}+\sqrt{2.5}}2\Big)^2=2.5 .\end{aligned}$$

If these were the slices of a measure $\al$ with covariance $C$, the variance of the slice would be the quadratic form $q(\theta)=\theta\T C\theta$, and every quadratic form satisfies $q(e_1)+q(e_2)=q\big(\frac{e_1+e_2}{\sqrt2}\big)+q\big(\frac{e_1-e_2}{\sqrt2}\big)$ (both sides equal $C_{11}+C_{22}$). Here $2.25+2.25=4.5\ne2.5+2.5=5$. So no measure has these slices: the reconstruction step can only approximate them. (CompOT, p. 172, notes that numerically the averaged slices “seem in practice” to be almost in the range of the Radon transform.)

In machine learning and imaging — colour transfer and texture synthesis

Colour transfer (slide 9/12): an image with $10^6$ pixels is a cloud of $10^6$ points in the colour cube $\R^3$. Exact transport between two such clouds is out of the question ($n^3=10^{18}$); one IDT iteration is three sorts of $10^6$ numbers, about $6\times10^7$ comparisons, and a few tens of iterations transfer the palette (Pitié et al. 2007). Texture synthesis and mixing: describe a texture by the distribution of local features (wavelet coefficients, or the activations of a pretrained network at every pixel) and push a noise image until its feature distribution matches the target’s, with a sliced distance as the loss (Rabin et al. 2011 for texture mixing with sliced barycenters; Heitz et al., CVPR 2021, use a sliced loss on neural-network features in place of Gram matrices). Each evaluation is a few hundred sorts.

In machine learning — sampling with sliced-Wasserstein flows

The particle scheme above is also a non-parametric generative model: start from $n$ particles drawn from a Gaussian, and move them by the sliced flow towards a training set; at convergence the particles are new samples that follow the data distribution. Liutkus et al. (ICML 2019, “Sliced-Wasserstein flows”) add a small diffusion (noise) term to this flow, which acts as an entropic regularization. In one-step generative models, the same velocity field (16.21) can be distilled into a network (OT4ML §16.2). The figure above is a two-dimensional cartoon of such a flow.

Pitfall — the flow reaches the target, not the optimal map

The sliced flow is a good way to move a cloud onto a target, but the correspondence it produces (initial point → final point) is not the $W_2$-optimal map in general (Cozzi and Santambrogio, cited in OT4ML p. 418; in our figure, only $18$ of $60$ partners agree). If the optimal map itself matters, use exact or entropic transport, or a lifted plan (§10) as a cheap upper bound.

Reflexes
  • Move a cloud towards a target cheaply → iterate: random directions, sort, move each point towards its partner of the same rank.
  • Step size → multiply the averaged 1D displacements by $\frac dK$; an orthonormal basis with step $1$ is IDT.
  • Average distributions slice by slice → average quantiles per direction, then invert the Radon transform; check whether the averaged slices are consistent (variances must be a quadratic form in $\theta$).

Why does the factor $\frac dK$ in step 3 of the algorithm not make the flow unstable when $K=1$?

Answer

With one direction the step is $\tau d\,(t_i-\ps{\theta}{x_i})\theta$: a move along $\theta$ only, by $\tau d$ times the 1D displacement. For $\tau d>2$ this overshoots the partner along $\theta$ and can make the slice cost increase: along $\theta$ the update is $s\leftarrow s+\tau d(t-s)$, which contracts the gap $t-s$ only if $\abs{1-\tau d}<1$. So with $K=1$ one needs $\tau<2/d$, and $\tau\le1/d$ to never overshoot. With $K=d$ orthonormal directions, the moves along different directions do not interfere and $\tau=1$ is safe. In general, take $\tau$ small enough that $\tau\frac dK\sum_k\theta_k\theta_k\T\preceq\Id$.
