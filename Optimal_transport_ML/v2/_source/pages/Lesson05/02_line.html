2. On the line, transport is a sort slides, frame 4/12

Between two clouds of $n$ points on the real line, the optimal plan matches the $i$-th smallest point of one cloud with the $i$-th smallest point of the other: one sort of each list, $O(n\log n)$ operations, gives $W_p$ exactly.

Sort and match

In the course slides, frame 4/12 (p. 9)

OT in One Dimension: Sort and Match. Let $\mu=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nx_i$ and $\nu=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^ny_i$ with $x_i,y_i\in\R$. [drawing: red source points $x_1,\dots,x_4$ and blue target points $y_1,\dots,y_4$ on a line, in the order $x_1 $$\mathbf W_2^2(\mu,\nu)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\abs{x_{(i)}-y_{(i)}}^2\quad\text{with}\quad x_{(1)}\le x_{(2)}\le\dots\le x_{(n)},\ \text{and}\ y_{(1)}\le y_{(2)}\le\dots\le y_{(n)}.$$

Fast to compute: Just sort $\to\mathcal O(n\log(n))$.

$\mu=\frac1n\sum_ix_i$
Read $\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$: the uniform measure on the points $x_1,\dots,x_n$ (the $\delta$ is missing on the slide, a typo).
$x_{(i)}$
The $i$-th smallest of the $x$’s (an order statistic): sort the list, read the $i$-th entry. The parentheses in the index mean “after sorting”.
$\frac1n\sum_i\abs{x_{(i)}-y_{(i)}}^2$
The cost of the plan that sends the $i$-th smallest $x$ to the $i$-th smallest $y$, each unit carrying mass $\frac1n$. The statement is that this monotone plan is optimal.
$\mathcal O(n\log n)$
Sorting $n$ numbers takes about $n\log_2n$ comparisons (merge sort); matching and summing then take $O(n)$.
Worked example — the slide’s configuration with numbers

Take $x=\{0,3,5,6\}$ and $y=\{1,2,4,9\}$: they are in the order of the slide’s drawing, $x_1 $$W_2^2=\frac{(0-1)^2+(3-2)^2+(5-4)^2+(6-9)^2}{4}=\frac{1+1+1+9}4=3,\qquad W_1=\frac{1+1+1+3}4=1.5 .$$

Checked by brute force over the $4!=24$ matchings: $3$ is the smallest value of the cost (the largest is $29$, for the reversed matching $0\to9$, $3\to4$, $5\to2$, $6\to1$), and an exact linear-program solver gives the same $W_2^2=3$. Note that $x_2=3$ is matched to $y_{(2)}=2$, to its left: monotone does not mean “each point moves right”.

Why sorting is optimal

Idea of the proof

Look at two pairs of a plan. If they cross — the smaller source is sent to the larger target — uncrossing them never increases the cost, because the cost $h(x-y)$ is convex. Repeating the uncrossing until no pair crosses produces the sorted matching, so the sorted matching is at least as good as any other.

Proof for $p=2$ (and the general statement)
  1. Let $xy'$ two targets, and compare the crossed pairs $(x\to y,\ x'\to y')$ with the uncrossed pairs $(x\to y',\ x'\to y)$.“Crossed” means: the smaller source goes to the larger target.
  2. $$(x-y)^2+(x'-y')^2-\big[(x-y')^2+(x'-y)^2\big]=-2xy-2x'y'+2xy'+2x'y=2(x'-x)(y-y')>0.$$Expand the four squares: the terms $x^2,x'^2,y^2,y'^2$ appear on both sides and cancel. Then $x'-x>0$ and $y-y'>0$.
  3. So uncrossing strictly decreases the cost, and an optimal matching has no crossed pair: it is increasing, i.e. it is the sorted matching.Among the $n!$ matchings there is an optimal one (finite set); if it had a crossed pair, uncrossing it would give a cheaper matching, a contradiction. A matching without crossed pairs sends the $i$-th smallest $x$ to the $i$-th smallest $y$.

The general statement (OT4ML Prop. 1.2, p. 9): if the cost is $C_{ij}=h(x_i-y_j)$ with $h$ strictly convex (for instance $h(s)=\abs{s}^p$, $p>1$) and the points are distinct, the order-preserving matching is the unique optimum. For $p=1$ it is still optimal, but other matchings can tie. For measures that are not uniform on $n$ points, the same idea gives the quantile formula of the refresher: $W_p^p(\mu,\nu)=\int_0^1\abs{F_\mu^{-1}(t)-F_\nu^{-1}(t)}^p\,\dd t$ (OT4ML Prop. 2.44, p. 38).

Reminder — the quantile formula (Lessons 1 and 3)

For equal weights, the quantile function of $\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$ is the staircase equal to $x_{(i)}$ on $\big(\frac{i-1}n,\frac in\big]$. Plugging two staircases into $\int_0^1\abs{F_\mu^{-1}-F_\nu^{-1}}^p$ gives back $\frac1n\sum_i\abs{x_{(i)}-y_{(i)}}^p$ — the right panel of the figure. The quantile formula also handles different numbers of points and unequal weights, and densities: for two Gaussians on the line it gives $W_2^2=(a-b)^2+(s-t)^2$ (refresher). It even makes $W_2$ on the line a Hilbert distance: the $L^2(0,1)$ distance between quantile functions. We will use this in §5. More in Lesson 3, §8 and Reminders.

On the line, samples are accurate

The line is also the easy case for statistics. On $\R$, $W_1$ is the area between the two CDFs, $W_1(\mu,\nu)=\int_\R\abs{F_\mu(s)-F_\nu(s)}\,\dd s$ (Lesson 1). For an empirical measure $\hat\mu_n$, $nF_{\hat\mu_n}(s)$ counts the samples below $s$: it is a binomial variable with parameters $n$ and $F_\mu(s)$. Hence $\E\abs{F_{\hat\mu_n}(s)-F_\mu(s)}\le\sqrt{F_\mu(s)(1-F_\mu(s))/n}$, and after integration in $s$: on the line, $\E\,W_1(\hat\mu_n,\mu)$ decreases like $n^{-1/2}$ for measures with bounded support. This one-dimensional fact is the whole proof of the dimension-free rate of §9.

Pitfall — there is no “sorting” in dimension 2

In $\R^d$ with $d\ge2$, no ordering of the points is compatible with the cost. Sorting both clouds by their first coordinate and matching by rank gives a coupling, hence an upper bound on $W_p^p$, but in general not the optimum. Example: $\al=\frac12(\delta_{(0,0)}+\delta_{(1,3)})$ and $\be=\frac12(\delta_{(1,0)}+\delta_{(0,3)})$. Sorting by the first coordinate matches $(0,0)\to(0,3)$ and $(1,3)\to(1,0)$, cost $9$, while the optimal plan $(0,0)\to(1,0)$, $(1,3)\to(0,3)$ costs $1$. We meet this pair again in §10 (lifted plans) and in Exercise 2 of the sheet.

Reflexes
  • Transport on the line → sort both lists and match by rank (or match quantiles).
  • Transport in $\R^d$ is hard → find lines on which to solve easy problems: that is slicing.
  • A statistical rate on the line → $W_1=\int\abs{F-G}$ and binomial counts: $n^{-1/2}$.

Compute $W_2^2$ between $\frac13(\delta_0+\delta_1+\delta_3)$ and $\frac13(\delta_0+\delta_2+\delta_3)$, and between $\frac13(\delta_0+\delta_2+\delta_3)$ and $\frac13(\delta_{0.6}+\delta_{1.6}+\delta_{4.2})$.

Answer

First pair, sorted matching $0\to0$, $1\to2$, $3\to3$: $W_2^2=\frac{0+1+0}3=\frac13$. Second pair, $0\to0.6$, $2\to1.6$, $3\to4.2$: $W_2^2=\frac{0.36+0.16+1.44}3=\frac{1.96}3=\frac{49}{75}\approx0.653$. (These are two slices of the clouds of Exercise 2 in §13.)
