3. Slicing a measure: projections and the Radon transform notes Def. 1, p. 1; slides, frame 8/12

To slice a measure $\al$ on $\R^d$ in the direction $\theta$, keep only the coordinate $\ps{\theta}{x}$ of every point: the slice $\al_\theta=\pf{(P_\theta)}{\al}$ is a measure on the line, and the family of all slices — the Radon transform of $\al$ — still determines $\al$.

The slicing operator

In the course Nadjahi notes, Def. 1 and what follows, p. 1

Definition 1 (Slicing operator). Let $u\in\R^d$. The slicing operator based on $u$, $p_u:\R^d\to\R$, is defined for any $x\in\R^d$ as

$$p_u(x)=\ps{x}{u}=\sum_{i=1}^dx^{(i)}u^{(i)},$$

where $y^{(i)}$ denotes the $i$-th component of a vector $y\in\R^d$.

Consider $\mu=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{x_i}$ with each $x_i\in\R^d$. Then, slicing $\mu$ yields a discrete distribution on $\R$, defined as

$$\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{p_u(x_i)}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{\ps{x_i}{u}}$$

We can generalize this to any continuous measure $\mu$ on $\R^d$ using the push-forward operator. Formally, for any $x\sim\mu$, then $\ps{u}{x}\sim(p_u)_\sharp\mu$. In words: the pushforward operator allows us to lift operations on points in $\R^d$ (like the projection $p_u$) to operations on measures.

Useful property: apply the change of variable formula for push-forward measures on $(p_u)_\sharp\mu$: for any measurable function $g$ on $\R$ s.t. $g$ is integrable with respect to $(p_u)_\sharp\mu$ ($\Leftrightarrow g\circ p_u$ is integrable w.r.t. $\mu$), one has

$$\int_\R g(s)\,\dd\big((p_u)_\sharp\mu\big)(s)=\int_{\R^d}(g\circ p_u)(x)\,\dd\mu(x)=\int_{\R^d}g\big(\ps{u}{x}\big)\,\dd\mu(x).\qquad(1)$$
$u$, then $\theta$
Any vector of $\R^d$ in the definition; from Definition 2 on, a unit vector $\theta\in\mathbb S^{d-1}$, a direction. This page writes $P_\theta(x)=\ps{\theta}{x}$, as OT4ML does.
$p_u(x)$
The coordinate of $x$ along $u$: if $\norm{u}=1$, the point of the line $\R u$ closest to $x$ is $p_u(x)\,u$ (orthogonal projection), and $p_u(x)$ is its abscissa on that line.
$(p_u)_\sharp\mu$
The slice of $\mu$: the law of the number $\ps{u}{X}$ when $X\sim\mu$. A measure on $\R$. This page writes $\al_\theta=\pf{(P_\theta)}{\al}$; the slides write $\mu^\theta$.
“continuous measure”
Here: any measure, not necessarily made of Diracs (for instance with a density).
(1)
The change of variables for push-forwards (refresher) with $T=p_u$: to integrate a function of the slice, integrate the same function of $\ps{u}{x}$ against the original measure. In probabilistic words: $\E\big[g(\ps{u}{X})\big]$ computed in two ways.

Slices of clouds, of Gaussians, of densities

Worked example — slicing three points

Let $\al=\frac13(\delta_{(0,0)}+\delta_{(2,1)}+\delta_{(1,3)})$.

  • $\theta=(\frac35,\frac45)$: $\ps{\theta}{x_i}=0,\ \frac65+\frac45=2,\ \frac35+\frac{12}5=3$, so $\al_\theta=\frac13(\delta_0+\delta_2+\delta_3)$.
  • $\theta=e_1=(1,0)$: the first coordinates, $\al_{e_1}=\frac13(\delta_0+\delta_2+\delta_1)$. $\ \theta=e_2$: $\al_{e_2}=\frac13(\delta_0+\delta_1+\delta_3)$.
  • $\theta'=\frac1{\sqrt5}(1,-2)$: $\ps{\theta'}{x_1}=0$, $\ps{\theta'}{x_2}=\frac{2-2}{\sqrt5}=0$, $\ps{\theta'}{x_3}=\frac{1-6}{\sqrt5}=-\sqrt5$: $\al_{\theta'}=\frac23\delta_0+\frac13\delta_{-\sqrt5}$. The weights of the two colliding points add up.
  • Property (1) with $g(s)=s$: the mean of the slice is the projection of the mean. The mean of $\al$ is $m=(1,\frac43)$, and $\ps{\theta}{m}=\frac35+\frac{16}{15}=\frac53$, which is indeed the mean of $\{0,2,3\}$.
Worked example — slices of a Gaussian

If $\al=\N(m,\Sigma)$, then $\al_\theta=\N\big(\ps{\theta}{m},\ \theta\T\Sigma\theta\big)$ (refresher). With $\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(4,1)$ and $\theta=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)$, the slice has variance $4\cos^2\varphi+\sin^2\varphi$: standard deviation $2$ along the long axis ($\varphi=0$), $1$ along the short one. A Gaussian is “slice-wise Gaussian” in every direction — and conversely (Cramér–Wold, §5), a measure all of whose slices are Gaussian is Gaussian.

All the slices together: the Radon transform

In the course OT4ML Def. 11.25, p. 231

Definition 11.25 (Measure-valued Radon transform). For $\al\in\Pc(\R^d)$, define $\ \mathfrak R\al(\theta):=(P_\theta)_\#\al$, $\theta\in\mathbb S^{d-1}$. […] If $\al=\rho(x)\,\dd x$ has a density, then $\mathfrak R\al(\theta)$ has density given by the classical Radon transform

$$R\rho(\theta,s)=\int_{\{x:\ \ps{\theta}{x}=s\}}\rho(x)\,\dd\mathcal H^{d-1}(x).$$
$\mathfrak R\al$
Not one slice but the whole family $\theta\mapsto\al_\theta$: a measure on the line for each direction.
$\{x:\ \ps{\theta}{x}=s\}$
The hyperplane orthogonal to $\theta$ at signed distance $s$ from the origin (a line in $\R^2$).
$\dd\mathcal H^{d-1}$
The $(d-1)$-dimensional area on that hyperplane (length in $\R^2$). So $R\rho(\theta,s)$ is the total density on the hyperplane: the slice is obtained by integrating $\rho$ along the hyperplanes orthogonal to $\theta$.
tomography
An X-ray crossing the body along a line is attenuated according to the integral of the tissue density along that line. A CT scanner records these integrals for many lines and directions — the Radon transform of the density — and reconstructs the density from them (“filtered back-projection”, §11).
Worked example — slicing the uniform disk

Let $\al$ be uniform on the unit disk of $\R^2$, density $\rho=\frac1\pi$ on the disk. For $\abs{s}<1$, the line $\{\ps{\theta}{x}=s\}$ cuts the disk along a chord of length $2\sqrt{1-s^2}$ (Pythagoras: half-chord $\sqrt{1-s^2}$ at distance $\abs s$ from the centre). Hence

$$R\rho(\theta,s)=\frac1\pi\cdot2\sqrt{1-s^2}=\frac2\pi\sqrt{1-s^2},\qquad\abs{s}\le1,$$

the “semicircle law”, whatever $\theta$. Checks: it integrates to $1$; its value at $s=0.5$ is $0.551$; its variance is $\frac14$, equal to $\theta\T\Sigma\theta$ for the covariance $\Sigma=\frac14\Id$ of the uniform disk — property (1) with $g(s)=s^2$. (Both integrals computed numerically.)

Slices and the Fourier transform

Here is the key to “the slices determine the measure”. It is used inside the proof of Theorem 1 of the notes (lines (8)–(12), §5); we isolate it.

Fourier slice identity

For every probability measure $\al$ on $\R^d$, every direction $\theta$ and every frequency $\omega\in\R$,

$$\mathcal F\al(\omega\theta)=\mathcal F(\al_\theta)(\omega).$$

In words: the Fourier transform of the slice $\al_\theta$ is the restriction of the Fourier transform of $\al$ to the line $\R\theta$. (OT4ML (12.27), p. 263, states it for densities: $\widehat{R\rho}(\theta,\omega)=\hat\rho(\omega\theta)$.)

Proof (one line, from property (1))
  1. $$\mathcal F(\al_\theta)(\omega)=\int_\R e^{-2i\pi\omega s}\,\dd\al_\theta(s)$$Definition of the Fourier transform of a measure on $\R$ (refresher).
  2. $$=\int_{\R^d}e^{-2i\pi\omega\ps{\theta}{x}}\,\dd\al(x)$$Property (1) with the bounded function $g(s)=e^{-2i\pi\omega s}$ (apply it to the real and imaginary parts).
  3. $$=\int_{\R^d}e^{-2i\pi\ps{\omega\theta}{x}}\,\dd\al(x)=\mathcal F\al(\omega\theta).$$$\omega\ps{\theta}{x}=\ps{\omega\theta}{x}$, then the definition of $\mathcal F\al$ at the point $\xi=\omega\theta$.

Check on a Gaussian. For $\al=\N(m,\Sigma)$, $\mathcal F\al(\xi)=e^{-2i\pi\ps{\xi}{m}-2\pi^2\xi\T\Sigma\xi}$; at $\xi=\omega\theta$ this is $e^{-2i\pi\omega\ps{\theta}{m}-2\pi^2\omega^2\theta\T\Sigma\theta}$, the Fourier transform of $\N(\ps{\theta}{m},\theta\T\Sigma\theta)$ — the slice. ✓

Intuition — why all the slices see everything

Every point $\xi\ne0$ of $\R^d$ lies on exactly one line through the origin, $\xi=\omega\theta$ with $\theta=\xi/\norm\xi$ and $\omega=\norm\xi$. So knowing the Fourier transforms of all the slices is knowing $\mathcal F\al$ everywhere, hence $\al$ itself. One slice, or finitely many, only reveal $\mathcal F\al$ on finitely many lines: not enough in general (§5).

Pitfall — slices of discrete measures can merge atoms

The slice of $\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$ is $\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{\ps{\theta}{x_i}}$ with repetitions allowed: two points with the same coordinate along $\theta$ give one atom of mass $\frac2n$ (example above). This only happens for directions orthogonal to some difference $x_i-x_j$ — a set of directions of measure zero — but it matters for sorting: ties must be handled, and in §10 they make “lifted plans” ambiguous.

Pitfall — the length of $u$ matters

$p_{ru}=r\,p_u$: projecting on $2\theta$ instead of $\theta$ stretches the slice by a factor $2$, and multiplies every transport cost $\abs{s-t}^p$ by $2^p$. This is why the directions are taken on the unit sphere (§4, notes’ Remark 2).

What is the slice of a Dirac $\delta_x$? Of the translate $\pf{\tau_z}{\al}$ of $\al$ by a vector $z$? Of the dilation $\pf{(\lambda\Id)}{\al}$?

Answer

$\delta_{\ps{\theta}{x}}$. The slice of $\al$ translated by the number $\ps{\theta}{z}$, because $\ps{\theta}{x+z}=\ps{\theta}{x}+\ps{\theta}{z}$. The slice of $\al$ dilated by $\lambda$, because $\ps{\theta}{\lambda x}=\lambda\ps{\theta}{x}$. These three facts are used constantly: slicing commutes with translations and dilations.

Is the slice of a measure with a density always a measure with a density? Is the slice of a discrete measure always discrete?

Answer

Yes to both: if $\al=\rho\,\dd x$, the slice has the density $s\mapsto R\rho(\theta,s)$ (Fubini: integrate $\rho$ first along the hyperplanes); if $\al=\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i}$, the slice is $\sum_ia_i\delta_{\ps{\theta}{x_i}}$. But a measure carried by a line can have Dirac slices: the uniform measure on the segment $[0,1]\times\{0\}$ has the slice $\delta_0$ in the direction $e_2$.
