6. Divergences, dual norms and GANs OT4ML Ch. 7, pp. 125–135

Dual norms compare $\al-\be$ against a class of test functions ($\TV$, $W_1$, MMD) and can compare singular measures; $\varphi$-divergences compare densities point by point (KL, Jensen–Shannon, $\TV$) and saturate or explode when supports are disjoint. A GAN learns the test function: Wasserstein and MMD GANs learn a dual-norm witness, the original GAN a density-ratio one.

The problem: a loss to fit a model to data

A generative model produces samples $g_\theta(z)$ from latent noise $z$; the data are finitely many points $y_1,\dots,y_m$. To fit $\theta$ you need a discrepancy $D(\al_\theta,\be)$ between the model law and the data, with two properties:

  • defined for singular measures: the data measure $\be=\frac1m\sum_j\delta_{y_j}$ has no density, and a model that maps a low-dimensional latent space into a high-dimensional one has none either;
  • informative when the model is wrong: if the model sits slightly to the side of the data, $D$ should decrease when the model moves towards the data — so that gradient descent on $\theta$ knows where to go.

The test case of this section: translate a measure by $t$ and watch how each discrepancy reacts.

Dual norms (integral probability metrics)

In the course OT4ML Def. 7.1, Ex. 7.2–7.4, p. 125

Definition 7.1 (Dual seminorm and integral probability metric). Let $B$ be a nonempty symmetric convex class of measurable functions that are integrable against the measures under consideration. For a signed measure $\xi$, define the extended dual seminorm

$$\norm{\xi}_B:=\sup_f\Big\{\int_\X f(x)\,\dd\xi(x)\ ;\ f\in B\Big\}.\qquad(7.1)$$

It is a norm when it is finite and $B$ separates signed measures. When applied to $\al-\be$ for probability measures, it is called an integral probability metric (IPM); it is a genuine metric precisely when $B$ separates probability measures.

Example 7.2 (Total variation): $B=\{f\in\mathcal C(\X)\ ;\ \norm{f}_\infty\le1\}$. Example 7.3 ($W_1$ norm): $B=\{f\ ;\ \Lip(f)\le1\}$. Example 7.4 (Flat norm and Dudley metric): $B=\{f\ ;\ \Lip(f)\le1\text{ and }\norm{f}_\infty\le1\}$ […]

$B$
A “ball” of test functions (a discriminator class): symmetric ($f\in B\Rightarrow-f\in B$) and convex.
$\norm{\al-\be}_B$
The largest difference of averages $\E_\al f-\E_\be f$ over the class: “the best test in $B$ for telling $\al$ from $\be$”. A maximising $f$ is a witness.
“separates”
$\norm{\al-\be}_B=0$ only if $\al=\be$: the class is rich enough to see every difference.
the examples
Same template, different balls. Bounded functions see any difference of mass (total variation, Lesson 3); 1-Lipschitz functions see how far mass moves ($W_1$, §5); both constraints give the flat norm, which also accepts measures of different masses.

Which topology a dual norm defines is decided by the size of its ball (Prop. 7.5, §5): a ball with dense span and compact closure (Lipschitz, RKHS) gives weak convergence; the sup-norm ball is not compact and total variation is the strong topology.

Kernels and the maximum mean discrepancy

Reminder — positive definite kernels

A symmetric $k(x,y)$ is positive definite if every Gram matrix $\big(k(x_i,x_j)\big)_{ij}$ is positive semi-definite: $\sum_{ij}r_ir_jk(x_i,x_j)\ge0$. Example: the Gaussian kernel $k(x,y)=e^{-\norm{x-y}^2/(2\sigma^2)}$. Such a kernel defines a Hilbert space of functions $\mathcal H$ (the RKHS) in which $h(x)=\ps{h}{k(x,\cdot)}_{\mathcal H}$ (“reproducing property”). More on the Reminders page.

In the course OT4ML Def. 7.9, Prop. 7.10, (7.5), pp. 128–129

Definition 7.9 (Kernel seminorm and MMD), for a positive-definite kernel $k$. […] Its kernel seminorm is $\norm{\xi}_k^2:=\iint_{\X\times\X}k(x,y)\,\dd\xi(x)\,\dd\xi(y)$ (7.4). For two probability measures […] the maximum mean discrepancy associated with $k$ is $\mathrm{MMD}_k(\al,\be):=\norm{\al-\be}_k$. It is a genuine distance when the kernel is characteristic, meaning that $\norm{\al-\be}_k=0$ implies $\al=\be$.

Proposition 7.10 (Kernel seminorm as an RKHS dual norm). […] $\norm{\xi}_k=\sup_{\norm{h}_{\mathcal H}\le1}\int h(x)\,\dd\xi(x)$, so $\norm{\cdot}_k$ is the dual seminorm in the sense of (7.1) associated with the RKHS unit ball.

[p. 129] To compute the discrepancy between two discrete measures, one can use

$$\norm{\al-\be}_k^2=\sum_{i,i'}a_ia_{i'}k(x_i,x_{i'})+\sum_{j,j'}b_jb_{j'}k(y_j,y_{j'})-2\sum_{i,j}a_ib_jk(x_i,y_j).\qquad(7.5)$$
$\iint k\,\dd\xi\,\dd\xi$
With $X,X'\sim\al$ and $Y,Y'\sim\be$ independent: $\mathrm{MMD}^2=\E k(X,X')+\E k(Y,Y')-2\E k(X,Y)$. Two samples from the same law look “as similar as” one sample from each.
RKHS unit ball
The witness is $h^\star\propto\int k(x,\cdot)\,\dd(\al-\be)(x)$: the difference of the two densities smoothed by the kernel.
(7.5)
For samples, three averages of kernel values: cost $O((n+m)^2)$, no optimisation at all — the main practical advantage of MMD.
Worked example — two Diracs

$\al=\delta_t$, $\be=\delta_0$, Gaussian kernel with $\sigma=1$: by (7.5), $\mathrm{MMD}^2=k(t,t)+k(0,0)-2k(t,0)=2-2e^{-t^2/2}$. So $\mathrm{MMD}=0.0999$ for $t=0.1$, $0.887$ for $t=1$, $1.406$ for $t=3$. It is increasing in $\abs{t}$ (a weak metric: close Diracs are close), but it saturates at $\sqrt2=1.414$ once the points are a few $\sigma$ apart — while $W_1=\abs{t}$ keeps growing.

In machine learning — two-sample tests, MMD GANs, FID and KID

Kernel two-sample test (Gretton et al., JMLR 2012). Question: do samples $X_1,\dots,X_n$ and $Y_1,\dots,Y_m$ come from the same law (for instance: did the distribution of user features drift between January and June)? Statistic: the empirical $\mathrm{MMD}^2$ by (7.5) with weights $\frac1n$, $\frac1m$. Calibration: shuffle the $n+m$ points many times, recompute the statistic on random splits, and reject “same law” if the observed value is larger than 95% of the shuffled ones (a permutation test). MMD GANs train a generator by minimising this same statistic between generated and real batches (OT4ML p. 134: “MMD generator losses take $B$ to be a unit ball in an RKHS”). Evaluating generators (OT4ML Example 7.14, p. 129): the Fréchet Inception Distance embeds real and generated images in the feature space of a pretrained network, fits a Gaussian to each cloud, and reports $W_2^2$ between the two Gaussians — mean term plus the Bures term of Lesson 3; the Kernel Inception Distance reports an MMD$^2$ in the same feature space.

$\varphi$-divergences compare densities point by point

In the course OT4ML Defs. 7.15–7.16, Ex. 7.19–7.20, pp. 129–132

Definition 7.15 (Entropy function). A function $\varphi:\R\to\R\cup\{+\infty\}$ is an entropy function if it is proper, lower semicontinuous and convex, with $\mathrm{dom}\,\varphi\subset[0,+\infty)$ and $\mathrm{dom}\,\varphi\cap(0,+\infty)\ne\emptyset$. Its recession slope is $\varphi'_\infty=\lim_{s\to+\infty}\frac{\varphi(s)}s\in\R\cup\{+\infty\}$.

Definition 7.16 ($\varphi$-Divergences). Let $\varphi$ be an entropy function and let $\al,\be\in\mathcal M_+(\X)$. Write the Lebesgue decomposition of $\al$ with respect to $\be$ as $\al=\frac{\dd\al}{\dd\be}\be+\al^\perp$, where $\al^\perp\perp\be$. The associated divergence is

$$\mathcal D_\varphi(\al|\be):=\int_\X\varphi\Big(\frac{\dd\al}{\dd\be}\Big)\dd\be+\varphi'_\infty\,\al^\perp(\X).\qquad(7.6)$$

[…] If $\varphi$ has superlinear growth, e.g. the usual entropy (7.9), then $\varphi'_\infty=+\infty$ so that $\mathcal D_\varphi(\al|\be)=+\infty$ if $\al$ does not have a density with respect to $\be$.

Example 7.19 (Kullback–Leibler divergence): $\varphi_\KL(s)=s\log(s)-s+1$ for $s>0$, $1$ for $s=0$, $+\infty$ otherwise. Example 7.20 (Total variation): $\varphi_\TV(s)=\abs{s-1}$ for $s\ge0$; $\TV(\al|\be)=\norm{\al-\be}_\TV$, “the full variation norm, without the factor $1/2$ sometimes used for probability measures”.

[p. 132] For probability measures, the Jensen–Shannon distance is the square root of $\mathrm{JS}(\al,\be)^2=\frac12\KL\big(\al\big|\frac{\al+\be}2\big)+\frac12\KL\big(\be\big|\frac{\al+\be}2\big)$, and satisfies $0\le\mathrm{JS}(\al,\be)^2\le\log2$. […] Its exact entropy generator is $\varphi_{\mathrm{JS}}(s)=\frac12\big(s\log s-(s+1)\log\frac{s+1}2\big)$.

$\frac{\dd\al}{\dd\be}$
The density ratio (Radon–Nikodym derivative) of the part of $\al$ that $\be$ “sees”. For histograms on common points: $a_i/b_i$; for densities: $\rho_\al/\rho_\be$.
$\al^\perp$
The part of $\al$ living where $\be$ has no mass. A $\varphi$-divergence charges it $\varphi'_\infty$ per unit: $+\infty$ for KL, $1$ for TV.
$\varphi(1)=0$
All generators vanish at $s=1$, so $\mathcal D_\varphi(\al|\al)=0$; convexity and Jensen give $\mathcal D_\varphi\ge0$ (OT4ML Prop. 7.18).
point by point
A $\varphi$-divergence only compares $\al$ and $\be$ at the same place: it does not know how far apart two places are. That is its strength (cheap, $O(n)$ for histograms, invariant under re-labelling of points) and its weakness (blind to geometry).
Reminder — KL, from Lesson 3’s total variation to here

$\KL(p|q)=\int p\log\frac pq\ge0$, $+\infty$ if $p$ has mass where $q$ has none; total variation $\norm{\al-\be}_\TV=\int\abs{\rho_\al-\rho_\be}\in[0,2]$ (Lesson 3, §5). Both are $\varphi$-divergences. See the refresher and the Reminders.

Pinsker’s inequality, and strong versus weak

In the course OT4ML Thm. 7.21, p. 131; Remark 7.22, p. 132

Theorem 7.21 (Pinsker inequality). Let $\al,\be\in\M(\X)$ be probability measures on a measurable space. With the full-variation convention (7.11), $\TV(\al|\be)^2=\norm{\al-\be}_\TV^2\le2\KL(\al|\be)$.

Remark 7.22 (Strong vs. weak topology). […] For probability measures on a compact metric space, one has the sharp elementary comparison $W_1(\al,\be)\le\frac{\diam(\X)}2\norm{\al-\be}_\TV$. Indeed, a 1-Lipschitz test function can be shifted by a constant so that its sup norm is at most $\diam(\X)/2$. […] The converse is false: if $x_n\to x$ with $x_n\ne x$, then $\delta_{x_n}\wto\delta_x$ and $W_1(\delta_{x_n},\delta_x)\to0$, whereas $\norm{\delta_{x_n}-\delta_x}_\TV=2$ for every $n$.

So the hierarchy is $\KL\ \Rightarrow\ \TV\ \Rightarrow\ W_1$: a small KL forces a small total variation, which forces a small $W_1$ on a bounded space — and none of the converses holds.

Idea of the proof of Pinsker

Reduce to two points: lumping $\X$ into “where $\al>\be$” and “elsewhere” can only decrease KL and keeps the total variation. On two points it is a one-variable inequality, proved by a second derivative.

Full proof (OT4ML p. 131, in steps)
  1. Assume $\KL(\al|\be)<\infty$ (else there is nothing to prove). Let $A$ be a set where $\al-\be\ge0$ and $\al-\be\le0$ on its complement (a Hahn set), $a=\al(A)$, $b=\be(A)$. Then $a-b=\frac12\norm{\al-\be}_\TV$.$\norm{\al-\be}_\TV=(\al-\be)(A)-(\al-\be)(A^c)$, and $(\al-\be)(A^c)=-(\al-\be)(A)$ because $(\al-\be)(\X)=0$.
  2. $$\KL(\al|\be)\ge a\log\frac ab+(1-a)\log\frac{1-a}{1-b}.$$Coarse-graining onto $(A,A^c)$: on each piece, Jensen’s inequality (the log-sum inequality $\int_Ap\log\frac pq\ge\big(\int_Ap\big)\log\frac{\int_Ap}{\int_Aq}$) .
  3. For fixed $b$, $h(a):=a\log\frac ab+(1-a)\log\frac{1-a}{1-b}-2(a-b)^2$ satisfies $h(b)=0$ and $h'(b)=0$.$h'(a)=\log\frac ab-\log\frac{1-a}{1-b}-4(a-b)$, which is $0$ at $a=b$.
  4. $h''(a)=\frac1a+\frac1{1-a}-4\ge0$, so $h$ is convex and minimal at $a=b$: $h\ge0$.$\frac1a+\frac1{1-a}=\frac1{a(1-a)}\ge4$ because $a(1-a)\le\frac14$.
  5. $\KL(\al|\be)\ge2(a-b)^2=2\cdot\frac14\norm{\al-\be}_\TV^2$, i.e. $\norm{\al-\be}_\TV^2\le2\KL(\al|\be)$.Steps 1, 2 and 4.

Check on numbers. Gaussians translated by $t=1$: $\norm{\cdot}_\TV^2=0.5865\le2\KL=1$. For the Dirac family the inequality is true but empty ($4\le\infty$). The second inequality of the box is the upper bound of Exercise 2 of the sheet, proved by duality in §9.

GANs: the discriminator is a dual potential

In the course OT4ML §7.4, pp. 133–135

The goal is to fit a generative parametric model $\al_\theta=g_{\theta,\sharp}\zeta$ to empirical data $\be=\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m\delta_{y_j}$, where $\zeta\in\M(\mathcal Z)$ is a fixed latent probability measure and $g_\theta:\mathcal Z\to\X$ is the generator, often a neural network. […] The original vanilla GAN corresponds, up to an additive constant and the usual reparametrization by a discriminator taking values in $(0,1)$, to the unscaled Jensen–Shannon generator $\hat\varphi_{\mathrm{JS}}=2\varphi_{\mathrm{JS}}$ […]. Moreover, density-ratio losses are informative when the measures overlap but can saturate when model and data are mutually singular: $\mathrm{JS}^2$ reaches its maximum $\log2$ on disjoint supports.

Dual norms and integral probability metrics. Instead of a density-ratio divergence, one can minimize a dual norm (7.1),

$$\inf_\theta\norm{\al_\theta-\be}_B=\inf_\theta\sup_{f\in B}\int_{\mathcal Z}f\big(g_\theta(z)\big)\,\dd\zeta(z)-\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^mf(y_j).$$

MMD generator losses take $B$ to be a unit ball in an RKHS; Wasserstein GANs take $B$ to be a Lipschitz ball, following Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality. The advantage is topological […] The corresponding objectives are therefore continuous for weak convergence. They can compare singular empirical and generated measures through test functions instead of requiring pointwise density ratios.

$g_{\theta,\sharp}\zeta$
The law of $g_\theta(z)$ for $z\sim\zeta$ (e.g. $z$ Gaussian in $\R^{100}$, $g_\theta(z)$ an image): sampling is easy, the density is unknown or does not exist.
$f\in B$
The discriminator or critic: a test function, implemented as a second network $f_\xi$. The inner $\sup$ is the dual of §1–§5: the critic is a dual potential.
$\inf_\theta\sup_f$
A two-player game: the critic looks for the test that best separates fake from real, the generator moves its samples to defeat it.
vanilla GAN
Goodfellow et al. (2014): a discriminator outputs a probability “real”; the game is (up to constants) the dual form of $2\mathrm{JS}^2$ (OT4ML Prop. 7.24 gives the dual of any $\varphi$-divergence, the basis of $f$-GANs).
Worked example — parallel segments (why JS fails and $W_1$ works)

In the plane, data on the vertical segment $\{0\}\times[0,1]$ and the model on $\{\theta\}\times[0,1]$ (both uniform); the parameter $\theta$ is the horizontal position. For $\theta\ne0$ the supports are disjoint, so $\mathrm{JS}^2=\log2$, $\norm{\cdot}_\TV=2$ and $\KL=+\infty$, whatever $\theta$: their derivative in $\theta$ is $0$ (or undefined). But $W_1=\abs{\theta}$ (a translation), with derivative $\mathrm{sign}(\theta)$: gradient descent moves the model straight to the data. The optimal critic is $f(x,y)=x\,\mathrm{sign}(\theta)$ (1-Lipschitz), and its gradient at the generated points is the direction in which to push them. (This is Example 1 of Arjovsky, Chintala and Bottou, ICML 2017.)

In machine learning — Wasserstein GANs: the critic is a Kantorovich potential

Data: images $y_1,\dots,y_m$. Model: a generator network $g_\theta:\R^{100}\to\R^{64\times64\times3}$ applied to Gaussian noise. Loss: $W_1(\al_\theta,\be)=\max_{\Lip(f)\le1}\E_zf(g_\theta(z))-\frac1m\sum_jf(y_j)$ (with the sign convention of the book), with $f=f_\xi$ a second network, the critic. Training: alternate a few steps of stochastic gradient ascent on $\xi$ (make the critic a better witness on mini-batches) and one step of descent on $\theta$, whose gradient is $\E_z\big[\partial_\theta g_\theta(z)\T\,\nabla f_\xi(g_\theta(z))\big]$ (CompOT (9.36), p. 160): each generated sample is pushed along the slope of the potential. Why it helps: when model and data lie on thin, disjoint sets — the typical situation for images, which live near low-dimensional manifolds — the JS loss of the original GAN is saturated at $\log2$ and its gradient vanishes, while $W_1$ still measures the distance. The catch is the constraint $\Lip(f_\xi)\le1$ (next box): the original WGAN clipped the weights; later variants penalise $(\norm{\nabla f_\xi}-1)^2$ at points between real and fake samples (Gulrajani et al., 2017) or normalise each layer by its spectral norm (Miyato et al., 2018) — both outside the course resources, and both only approximate the Lipschitz ball, so a trained critic gives an estimate of $W_1$, not its exact value.

In the course OT4ML Remark 7.26, p. 135

Remark 7.26 (Weight clipping is only a proxy). Wasserstein GANs originally used a tunable clipping parameter $c>0$, constraining each critic parameter to $[-c,c]$, equivalently $\norm{\xi}_\infty\le c$, as a proxy for enforcing a Lipschitz bound. The resulting Lipschitz constant depends on the architecture and on $c$ and is not canonically one. The parameter box is convex, but its image through a neural-network parametrization is neither the full unit Lipschitz ball nor generally a convex class of functions, and optimization in the parameters remains non-concave. Clipping is therefore a practical heuristic rather than a faithful implementation of the Kantorovich–Rubinstein constraint.

Pitfall — clipped weights do not make a 1-Lipschitz critic

One hidden layer with $K$ ReLU units and all weights clipped to $[-1,1]$: $f(x)=\sum_{k=1}^K\mathrm{ReLU}(x)$ (all weights equal to $1$) has Lipschitz constant $K$. With $K=10$, the “critic” computes up to $10\,W_1$. A constant factor does not change the minimiser in $\theta$, but the class is not a ball and its size depends on the architecture: the 2019 notes’ claim that clipping “ensures $f_\xi\in B$” (p. 33) is too strong — see the notes on the sources.

Reflexes
  • Data are samples (singular) → use a dual norm ($W_1$, MMD) or a transport cost, not a density-ratio divergence.
  • A loss is flat on disjoint supports (JS, TV) → no gradient for a generator far from the data.
  • A dual norm is a $\sup$ over test functions → in practice the test function is a network (critic), and the class must be controlled (Lipschitz, RKHS norm).
  • Comparing two Gaussians in feature space → FID $=W_2^2$ (mean + Bures).

Show that $\mathrm{JS}(\al,\be)^2=\log2$ when $\al$ and $\be$ have disjoint supports.

Answer

On the support of $\al$, $\frac{\al+\be}2=\frac\al2$, so $\frac{\dd\al}{\dd(\al+\be)/2}=2$ there and $\KL\big(\al\big|\frac{\al+\be}2\big)=\int\log2\,\dd\al=\log2$. Same for $\be$. Hence $\mathrm{JS}^2=\frac12\log2+\frac12\log2=\log2$ — independent of how far apart the supports are.

Why is total variation both a $\varphi$-divergence and a dual norm, and why does that not save it for GANs?

Answer

$\varphi_\TV(s)=\abs{s-1}$ gives $\int\abs{\rho_\al-\rho_\be}$, and the ball $\norm{f}_\infty\le1$ gives the same number (the witness is the sign of $\rho_\al-\rho_\be$). But its ball is too large (not compact): $\TV$ is the strong topology, equal to $2$ for any two disjoint supports, so it is as flat as JS for a generator far from the data.
