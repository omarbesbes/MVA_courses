8. Mixtures: likelihood, KL and transport 2019 notes §9.1; StatOT §6.1; OT4ML §13.2

Maximum likelihood minimises $\KL(\text{data}\,|\,\text{model})$ and needs overlapping densities; variational inference minimises the other KL and seeks a mode; transport fits positions. Gaussian mixtures can be compared by transporting their components: a small OT problem whose ground cost is the $W_2$ distance between Gaussians.

The opening question of the syllabus

The syllabus of this lesson starts with “variational inference, comparison with KL, mixture estimation, OT on mixture of Gaussians”. Two questions are behind these words.

  1. Fitting. To fit a model (a Gaussian, a mixture, a generator) to data or to a target law, which loss should we minimise — a KL divergence (in which direction?) or a transport cost? §6 compared them along translations; here we compare what they choose.
  2. Comparing mixtures. Two Gaussian mixtures can be compared as densities on $\R^d$ (with $W_2$), or as collections of meaningful components. The second view gives a cheap and interpretable distance.

Maximum likelihood is a KL projection

In the course 2019 notes §9.1, pp. 40–41

In the case where $\al_\theta$ has a density $\rho_\theta=\rho_{\al_\theta}$ with respect to the Lebesgue measure (or any other fixed reference measure), the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) is obtained by solving

$$\min_\theta\ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{MLE}}(\al_\theta,\be):=-\sum_i\log\big(\rho_\theta(x_i)\big).$$

This corresponds to using an empirical counterpart of a Kullback–Leibler loss since, assuming the $x_i$ are i.i.d. samples of some $\bar\be$, then $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{MLE}}(\al,\be)\xrightarrow{n\to+\infty}\KL(\al|\bar\be)$. This MLE approach is known to lead to optimal estimation procedures in many cases […]. However, it fails to work when estimating singular distributions, typically when the $\al_\theta$ does not have a density (so that $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{MLE}}(\al_\theta,\be)=+\infty$) […]. In order to cope with such difficult scenario, one has to use weak metrics in place of the MLE functional […]. The class of estimators obtained using $L=\Lc$, often called “Minimum Kantorovitch Estimators” (MKE), was initially introduced in [4].

$-\sum_i\log\rho_\theta(x_i)$
The negative log-likelihood of the sample $x_1,\dots,x_n$ under the model density $\rho_\theta$.
$\bar\be$
The unknown law of the data; $\be=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$ is its empirical version.
“fails for singular distributions”
A generator $\pf{(g_\theta)}{\zeta}$ that maps a low-dimensional latent space into a large one has no density: its likelihood is not defined (or $-\infty$). Hence the weak losses of §6.
MKE
$\min_\theta\Lc(\al_\theta,\be)$: fit the model by transport. Wasserstein GANs are MKEs with $c=d$, whose dual potential is approximated by a deep network (CompOT §9.4, pp. 159–161).

Let us redo the limit carefully, because the order of the arguments matters (see the note below).

  1. $$\frac1n\mathcal L_{\mathrm{MLE}}=-\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\log\rho_\theta(x_i)\ \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\ -\int\log\rho_\theta(x)\,\dd\bar\be(x).$$The law of large numbers, for i.i.d. $x_i\sim\bar\be$ (assuming the integral exists). Without the factor $\frac1n$ the sum grows like $n$.
  2. $$=\int\log\frac{\rho_{\bar\be}(x)}{\rho_\theta(x)}\,\dd\bar\be(x)-\int\log\rho_{\bar\be}(x)\,\dd\bar\be(x)=\KL(\bar\be\,|\,\al_\theta)+h(\bar\be).$$Add and subtract $\log\rho_{\bar\be}$ (the data density); $h(\bar\be)=-\int\rho_{\bar\be}\log\rho_{\bar\be}$ is the entropy of the data, which does not depend on $\theta$.

So maximum likelihood minimises $\KL(\text{data}\,|\,\text{model})$, the “forward” KL. It is finite only if the model puts mass wherever the data have mass.

Note on the sources — the limit of the likelihood

The 2019 notes (p. 40) and CompOT (§9.4, p. 159) write $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{MLE}}(\al,\be)\to\KL(\al|\bar\be)$ for $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{MLE}}=-\sum_i\log\rho_\theta(x_i)$. Two corrections: the sum must be divided by $n$, and the limit is $\KL(\bar\be|\al_\theta)+h(\bar\be)$ — arguments in the other order, plus a constant. Check with data $\N(0,1)$ and model $\N(0,4)$, $n=10^6$ samples: $\frac1n\mathcal L_{\mathrm{MLE}}=1.7373$, while $h+\KL(\text{data}|\text{model})=1.4189+0.3181=1.7371$ and $h+\KL(\text{model}|\text{data})=1.4189+0.8069=2.2258$; the unnormalised sum is $1.74\times10^6$.

Going further — estimating a mixture by maximum likelihood is an entropic transport problem

“Mixture estimation” in its simplest form: the data $X_1,\dots,X_n$ come from a location mixture $G_\rho=\int\N(y,\sigma^2\Id)\,\rho(\dd y)$ whose mixing law $\rho$ is unknown and may be any probability measure. The non-parametric maximum likelihood estimator (NPMLE) minimises $\ell_n(\rho)=-\frac1n\sum_i\log G_\rho(X_i)$ over all $\rho$. StatOT §6.4 (pp. 197–199) points out “a surprising connection with entropic optimal transport”: the NPMLE satisfies $\hat\rho=\argmin_\rho S_{2\sigma^2}(\mu_n,\rho)$, with $\mu_n=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{X_i}$, “where $S_{2\sigma^2}(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the entropic optimal transport cost with regularization parameter $\eps=2\sigma^2$. In other words, the NPMLE precisely minimizes the entropic OT cost to the data $\mu_n$.” (StatOT defines the components with the standard Gaussian density, then writes the formula for a general $\sigma$: read the components as $\N(y,\sigma^2\Id)$.)

Why: by the Gibbs variational principle, $-\log\int e^{-\norm{X_i-y}^2/(2\sigma^2)}\,\rho(\dd y)=\inf_{P_i}\int\frac{\norm{X_i-y}^2}{2\sigma^2}\,\dd P_i(y)+\KL(P_i|\rho)$. Each data point spreads itself over the components ($P_i$), paying the squared distance plus a KL penalty: together the $P_i$ form a transport plan from the data, with an entropic term, and the best $\rho$ is the second marginal of that plan. So here maximum likelihood (a KL) and transport are one problem. Entropic transport is the subject of Lesson 7; StatOT then solves this problem with Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao gradient flows (Lesson 8). (Checked in dimension 1 on a grid of atoms: the EM fixed point minimises $\rho\mapsto S_{2\sigma^2}(\mu_n,\rho)$, and $\ell_n(\hat\rho)=\frac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2)+\frac{S_{2\sigma^2}(\mu_n,\hat\rho)}{2\sigma^2}$.)

KL against $W_2$ on Gaussians

In the course CompOT Remark 8.2, (8.6)–(8.7), p. 122

It is interesting to contrast the geometry of the Kullback–Leibler divergence to that defined by quadratic optimal transport when comparing Gaussians. […] In the univariate case, $d=1$, if $\al=\N(m_\al,\sigma_\al^2)$ and $\be=\N(m_\be,\sigma_\be^2)$, one has

$$\KL(\al|\be)=\frac12\Big(\frac{\sigma_\al^2}{\sigma_\be^2}+\log\frac{\sigma_\be^2}{\sigma_\al^2}+\frac{\abs{m_\al-m_\be}}{\sigma_\be^2}-1\Big).\qquad(8.6)$$

This expression shows that the divergence between $\al$ and $\be$ diverges to infinity as $\sigma_\be$ diminishes to $0$ and $\be$ becomes a Dirac mass. In that sense, one can say that singular Gaussians are infinitely far from all other Gaussians in the KL geometry. […] The KL hyperbolic geometry over the space of Gaussian parameters $(m,\sigma)$ should be contrasted with the Euclidean geometry associated to OT […], since in the univariate case $W_2^2(\al,\be)=\abs{m_\al-m_\be}^2+\abs{\sigma_\al-\sigma_\be}^2$. (8.7)

Note on the sources — a missing square in (8.6)

The mean term of (8.6) must be $\frac{(m_\al-m_\be)^2}{\sigma_\be^2}$ (as in the refresher). Check: for $\N(0,1)$ against $\N(2,1)$, numerical integration gives $\KL=2.0000=\frac{2^2}2$, while the printed formula gives $1.0$. The second-order expansion printed just after (8.6) is correct.

KL geometry
Moving a mean by $\delta$ costs $\frac{\delta^2}{2\sigma^2}$: cheap for wide Gaussians, expensive for narrow ones. A narrow model ($\sigma_\be\to0$) is infinitely far from everything.
$W_2$ geometry
Euclidean in $(m,\sigma)$: moving a mean by $\delta$ costs $\delta^2$ whatever the widths, and a Dirac ($\sigma=0$) is at finite distance.

Numbers. $\KL\big(\N(0,1)\,|\,\N(0,0.1^2)\big)=47.20$ while $W_2^2=(1-0.1)^2=0.81$. And $\KL\big(\N(1,4)\,|\,\N(0,1)\big)=1.3069$ against $W_2^2=1+1=2$. A fitting procedure based on KL will never let the model collapse to a point (infinite penalty); one based on $W_2$ may, if that is cheaper.

Variational inference uses the other KL

In the course StatOT §6.1, pp. 173–174

In VI, we posit access to a probability measure $\pi$ over $\R^d$ via an expression for its density, and our goal is to perform inference. A typical example arises when $\pi$ is the posterior distribution from a Bayesian inference problem […]. The idea of VI is to approximate $\pi$ with an element of a simpler class $\mathcal Q$ of probability measures by solving the optimization problem

$$q^\star=\argmin_{q\in\mathcal Q}\ \KL(q\,\|\,\pi).\qquad(\mathrm{VI})$$

[…] typically we do not have access directly to $\pi$ but rather to an unnormalized density $\tilde\pi$, and the unknown normalization constant $Z=\int\tilde\pi$ does not affect the optimization objective in (VI). [Footnote: other objectives include] the KL divergence with the order of arguments swapped, which is closer to the statistician’s concept of maximum likelihood.

$\pi$
The target, e.g. a Bayesian posterior $\pi(\theta)\propto\text{prior}(\theta)\times\text{likelihood}(\text{data}|\theta)$, known up to its normalising constant $Z$.
$\mathcal Q$
A tractable family: Gaussians, products of independent factors (“mean field”), mixtures.
$\KL(q\|\pi)$
$=\E_q[\log q]-\E_q[\log\tilde\pi]+\log Z$: expectations under $q$, which we can sample; $\log Z$ is a constant. That is why VI uses this direction — the reverse KL, compared with maximum likelihood.

The two directions behave very differently on a multimodal target. Forward $\KL(\pi|q)=\int\pi\log\frac\pi q$ is huge where $\pi>0$ and $q\approx0$: $q$ must cover all the mass. Reverse $\KL(q|\pi)=\int q\log\frac q\pi$ is huge where $q>0$ and $\pi\approx0$: $q$ must avoid the valleys, so it settles on one mode.

Worked example — fitting one Gaussian to two bumps

Target $\pi=0.3\,\N(-2,0.5^2)+0.7\,\N(2,0.5^2)$ (mean $0.8$, standard deviation $1.9$); family $\mathcal Q$: all Gaussians $\N(m,s^2)$.

  • Forward KL (maximum likelihood with infinitely many samples of $\pi$): minimising $-\int\pi\log q$ over Gaussians matches the mean and the variance, $q=\N(0.8,\,1.9^2)$. It covers both bumps and puts mass in the valley: $\KL(\pi|q)=0.724$, but $\KL(q|\pi)=2.096$.
  • Reverse KL (VI): two local minima, $\N(-2.00,\,0.50^2)$ with $\KL(q|\pi)=1.204$ and $\N(2.00,\,0.50^2)$ with $\KL(q|\pi)=0.357$. Each sits on one bump and pays $\approx\log\frac1w$ for the weight $w$ it ignores ($-\log0.3=1.204$, $-\log0.7=0.357$). Their forward KL is huge ($21.7$ and $8.98$): they miss a whole mode. Gradient descent finds one or the other depending on its start.
  • $W_2$ (minimum Kantorovich estimator): on the line $W_2^2(\pi,\N(m,s^2))=\int_0^1\big(Q_\pi(u)-m-s\Phi^{-1}(u)\big)^2\dd u$, a least-squares fit of the quantile function. Since $\int_0^1\Phi^{-1}=0$ and $\int_0^1(\Phi^{-1})^2=1$, the solution is $m=\int Q_\pi=\E_\pi X=0.8$ and $s=\int_0^1Q_\pi\Phi^{-1}=1.704$: one broad Gaussian, narrower than moment matching ($W_2^2=0.708$ against $0.746$; the right bump alone gives $3.596$). By Cauchy–Schwarz, $s\le\mathrm{sd}(\pi)$, with equality only for Gaussian $\pi$.

(Computed by quadrature and Nelder–Mead from three starting points.)

In machine learning — variational inference and VAEs

Bayesian inference at scale. For a Bayesian neural network or a large hierarchical model, the posterior over parameters is known only up to $Z$; VI replaces sampling (MCMC) by optimisation: choose a Gaussian $q$ (often with diagonal covariance) and minimise $\KL(q\|\pi)$ by stochastic gradients, estimating $\E_q[\log q-\log\tilde\pi]$ with samples of $q$. The worked example explains a known behaviour: mode-seeking — reverse-KL fits underestimate the spread and can miss modes. Variational autoencoders (Kingma and Welling, 2014) do the same, amortised over data: an encoder outputs a Gaussian $q_\phi(z|x)$ approximating the posterior of the latent code, trained by maximising $\log p_\theta(x)-\KL\big(q_\phi(z|x)\,\|\,p_\theta(z|x)\big)$ (the evidence lower bound). StatOT §6.1 studies VI as a gradient flow in the Wasserstein space — the subject of Lesson 8.

OT between Gaussian mixtures

In the course OT4ML Def. 13.9, Prop. 13.10, pp. 293–294

Definition 13.9 (Wasserstein-over-Wasserstein distance). Let $1\le p<+\infty$ and let $\X$ be a Polish metric space. For $\mathfrak A,\mathfrak B\in\Pc_p(\Pc_p(\X))$, define $\ \mathrm W_p^p(\mathfrak A,\mathfrak B):=\inf_{\Pi\in\U(\mathfrak A,\mathfrak B)}\int W_p^p(\al,\be)\,\dd\Pi(\al,\be)$ (13.8).

For $p=2$, Gaussian mixtures provide a particularly explicit example with two levels of geometry. A mixture $\sum_ia_i\N(m_i,\Sigma_i)$ can either be viewed as the collapsed measure on $\X$, or as the component law $\mathfrak A=\sum_ia_i\delta_{\N(m_i,\Sigma_i)}$ on the Bures–Wasserstein space of Gaussian components. Given two component laws $\mathfrak A=\sum_ia_i\delta_{\N(m_i,\Sigma_i)}$, $\mathfrak B=\sum_jb_j\delta_{\N(n_j,\Lambda_j)}$, the discrete problem induced by (13.8) uses the component cost

$$C_{i,j}=\norm{m_i-n_j}^2+\B(\Sigma_i,\Lambda_j)^2 .$$

Let $P^\star$ be an optimal coupling between the weights $a$ and $b$. […] collapsing these component geodesics gives $\bar\al_t=\sum_{i,j}P^\star_{i,j}\N(m_{ij,t},\Sigma_{ij,t})$. […] It is generally not the same as the true $W_2$ interpolation between the collapsed mixture densities, which can split and recombine mass inside and across components.

Proposition 13.10 (Collapsing is non-expansive). […] For the collapsed mixtures, $W_p(\bar\al_{\mathfrak A},\bar\be_{\mathfrak B})\le\mathrm W_p(\mathfrak A,\mathfrak B)$.

$\mathfrak A$
The mixture seen as a histogram over Gaussians: weight $a_i$ on the component $\N(m_i,\Sigma_i)$, which is now a point of a metric space (the Gaussians with the $W_2$ distance).
$C_{ij}$
The ground cost between components: $W_2^2$ between two Gaussians, in closed form (Lesson 1: means + Bures). In 1D, $(m_i-n_j)^2+(\sigma_i-\tau_j)^2$.
$\mathrm W_2(\mathfrak A,\mathfrak B)$
Often written $\mathrm{MW}_2$ (“mixture Wasserstein”): a $K\times L$ linear program between the weight vectors — tiny compared with OT between two densities on $\R^d$.
collapsed mixture
The usual density $\sum_ia_i\N(m_i,\Sigma_i)$ on $\X$.
Prop. 13.10
Transporting component by component is one particular way of transporting the densities, so it costs at least as much: $W_2\le\mathrm{MW}_2$.
Idea of the proof (finite mixtures)

Glue: for each pair of components $(i,j)$ take an optimal coupling $\gamma_{ij}$ between $\N(m_i,\Sigma_i)$ and $\N(n_j,\Lambda_j)$, and mix them with the weights of an optimal component plan: $\pi=\sum_{ij}P^\star_{ij}\gamma_{ij}$. This is a coupling of the two collapsed mixtures, and its cost is $\sum_{ij}P^\star_{ij}W_2^2(\text{components})=\mathrm{MW}_2^2$. The minimum over all couplings can only be smaller.

Full proof for finite mixtures
  1. $\pi:=\sum_{i,j}P^\star_{ij}\gamma_{ij}$ is a probability on $\X\times\X$.A convex combination of probabilities ($P^\star\ge0$, $\sum_{ij}P^\star_{ij}=1$).
  2. Its first marginal is $\sum_{i,j}P^\star_{ij}\N(m_i,\Sigma_i)=\sum_ia_i\N(m_i,\Sigma_i)=\bar\al$; likewise the second is $\bar\be$.The first marginal of $\gamma_{ij}$ is the $i$-th component; then $\sum_jP^\star_{ij}=a_i$ (row sums of the component plan).
  3. $$W_2^2(\bar\al,\bar\be)\le\int\norm{x-y}^2\dd\pi=\sum_{i,j}P^\star_{ij}\int\norm{x-y}^2\dd\gamma_{ij}=\sum_{i,j}P^\star_{ij}C_{ij}=\mathrm{MW}_2^2 .$$$\pi$ is a coupling of the collapsed mixtures; linearity of the integral; $\gamma_{ij}$ optimal between Gaussians, whose cost is $C_{ij}$.

This is the gluing idea of Lesson 3, §2 in another guise: a plan between “parts” combined with plans inside the parts. The book’s proof (p. 294) does the same for general laws $\mathfrak A,\mathfrak B$ with a measurable selection of optimal plans.

Worked examples — how far apart are $\mathrm{MW}_2$ and $W_2$?
  • One component against two. $\bar\al=\N(0,1)$ and $\bar\be=\frac12\N(-1,0.5^2)+\frac12\N(1,0.5^2)$. The only component plan sends half of $\N(0,1)$ to each component: $\mathrm{MW}_2^2=\frac12\big[(0+1)^2+(1-0.5)^2\big]+\frac12\big[(0-1)^2+(1-0.5)^2\big]=1.25$. The true $W_2^2$ between the densities (quantile formula, by quadrature) is $0.0733$: 17 times smaller. The true plan splits the Gaussian at $0$ — left half to the left bump, right half to the right one — which a component-wise plan cannot do: it must move the whole $\N(0,1)$ by an affine map to each component.
  • Two against two. $\bar\al=\frac12\N(-2,0.5^2)+\frac12\N(2,0.5^2)$ and $\bar\be=\frac14\N(-1,0.5^2)+\frac34\N(1.5,1^2)$: $C=\begin{pmatrix}1&12.5\\9&0.5\end{pmatrix}$, optimal component plan $P^\star=\begin{pmatrix}0.25&0.25\\0&0.5\end{pmatrix}$, $\mathrm{MW}_2^2=0.25+3.125+0.25=3.625$, while $W_2^2=1.560$.

In the course StatOT §5.6, p. 159

[…] the representation of a Gaussian mixture by a mixing measure $\nu$ is “overparametrized”, i.e., $\nu$ is highly non-unique: consider the equality $\N(0,I)=\int\N(x,\tau I)\,\nu(\dd x)$ where $\nu=\N(0,(1-\tau)I)$, valid for any $\tau\in[0,1]$.

So $\mathrm{MW}_2$ is a distance between mixing laws (component lists), not between densities: the same density written with different components is at positive $\mathrm{MW}_2$ from itself. That is a feature when components have a meaning (clusters, objects), and a bug otherwise.

In machine learning — comparing and interpolating Gaussian mixture models

Gaussian mixture models are fitted everywhere (clustering, density models of colours or patches of images, speaker models in audio). Comparing two fitted GMMs with $K$ and $L$ components by $\mathrm{MW}_2$ costs a $K\times L$ transport problem with closed-form costs — instead of an OT problem between densities in $\R^d$, which has no closed form and is expensive to approximate. The optimal plan $P^\star$ tells which cluster corresponds to which (useful to match clusterings of two datasets or two time points), and the component geodesics give interpolations that stay GMMs with at most $K+L-1$ components (a vertex of the transport polytope). Delon and Desolneux (SIAM J. Imaging Sciences, 2020) use this distance for colour transfer between images modelled by GMMs and for GMM barycenters; Chen, Georgiou and Tannenbaum (IEEE Access, 2019) introduced it for GMMs (both cited by StatOT §5.6).

Reflexes
  • Maximum likelihood → $\KL(\text{data}|\text{model})$ + const: mass-covering, needs a density everywhere the data are.
  • Variational inference → $\KL(q|\pi)$: mode-seeking, needs $\pi$ only up to a constant.
  • $W_2$ fit on the line → least squares on quantile functions.
  • Two Gaussian mixtures → $\mathrm{MW}_2$: a small LP with cost $W_2^2$ between components; it is an upper bound on $W_2$ between the densities.

For a target law $p$ on $\R$ with a density, mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $s_p$, which Gaussian minimises $\KL(p|q)$ over all Gaussians $q$?

Answer

$\KL\big(p|\N(m,s^2)\big)=-h(p)-\E_p\log q=-h(p)+\frac12\log(2\pi s^2)+\frac{\E_p(X-m)^2}{2s^2}$. The minimum in $m$ is at $m=\mu$ (the mean minimises $\E_p(X-m)^2$); then $\frac12\log s^2+\frac{s_p^2}{2s^2}$ has derivative $\frac1s-\frac{s_p^2}{s^3}$, zero at $s=s_p$. Moment matching.

Why is $\mathrm{MW}_2(\mathfrak A,\mathfrak B)=0$ only if the two mixtures have the same components with the same weights, while $W_2(\bar\al,\bar\be)=0$ can happen with different components?

Answer

$\mathrm{MW}_2$ is a Wasserstein distance on the space of Gaussians, so it vanishes iff the two histograms over Gaussians are equal. $W_2$ only sees the collapsed density, and the same density has many mixture representations (StatOT’s example $\N(0,I)=\int\N(x,\tau I)\,\dd\N(0,(1-\tau)I)(x)$).
