Exercise 1 — A $c$-transform by hand ★

On $\X=\Y=\{0,1,2\}$ with $c(x,y)=\abs{x-y}$, let $f=(0,3,0)$. Compute $f^c$, $f^{c\bar c}$ and $f^{c\bar cc}$. Which values of $f$ were raised, and why?

Hint

Tabulate $c(x,y)-f(x)$ and take column minima; then tabulate $c(x,y)-f^c(y)$ and take row minima.

Solution

$f^c(0)=\min(0,\,1-3,\,2)=-2$, $f^c(1)=\min(1,\,-3,\,1)=-3$, $f^c(2)=\min(2,\,-2,\,0)=-2$: $f^c=(-2,-3,-2)$. Then $f^{c\bar c}(0)=\min(0+2,\,1+3,\,2+2)=2$, $f^{c\bar c}(1)=\min(1+2,\,0+3,\,1+2)=3$, $f^{c\bar c}(2)=2$: $f^{c\bar c}=(2,3,2)$, and $f^{c\bar cc}=(-2,-3,-2)=f^c$. The values at $0$ and $2$ were raised from $0$ to $2$: $(2,3,2)$ is the smallest 1-Lipschitz function above $(0,3,0)$ (a jump of $3$ over a distance $1$ is not allowed, §5), and $f^c=-f^{c\bar c}$ as Prop. 6.17 predicts.

Exercise 2 — $W_1$ three ways ★

On $\R$, $\al=\frac12\delta_0+\frac12\delta_2$ and $\be=\frac14\delta_0+\frac12\delta_1+\frac14\delta_2$. Compute $W_1(\al,\be)$ with the CDF formula, give an optimal 1-Lipschitz witness and an optimal plan.

Hint

$C_\al-C_\be$ is constant on $[0,1)$ and on $[1,2)$.

Solution

$C_\al-C_\be=\frac12-\frac14=\frac14$ on $[0,1)$ and $\frac12-\frac34=-\frac14$ on $[1,2)$: $W_1=\frac14+\frac14=\frac12$. Witness: slope $-1$ on $[0,1]$, $+1$ on $[1,2]$, e.g. $f(0)=0$, $f(1)=-1$, $f(2)=0$: $\int f\,\dd(\al-\be)=0-\frac12\cdot(-1)=\frac12$. ✓ Plan: keep $\frac14$ at $0$ and $\frac14$ at $2$, move $\frac14$ from $0$ to $1$ and $\frac14$ from $2$ to $1$: cost $\frac14+\frac14=\frac12$. ✓ The plan uses only contacts: $f(0)-f(1)=1=d(0,1)$ and $f(2)-f(1)=1=d(2,1)$.

Exercise 3 — Pinsker on two coins ★

Let $\al$ = Bernoulli$(0.5)$ and $\be$ = Bernoulli$(0.2)$ (as histograms on $\{0,1\}$). Compute $\norm{\al-\be}_\TV$ and $\KL(\al|\be)$, and check Pinsker’s inequality.

Solution

$\norm{\al-\be}_\TV=\abs{0.5-0.2}+\abs{0.5-0.8}=0.6$. $\KL(\al|\be)=0.5\log\frac{0.5}{0.2}+0.5\log\frac{0.5}{0.8}=0.4581-0.2350=0.2231$. Pinsker: $0.6^2=0.36\le2\cdot0.2231=0.4463$. ✓ (On two points the proof of §6 is exactly this binary computation.)

Exercise 5 — Certify or refute ★★

In the shipping problem of §1, a colleague proposes $P=\begin{pmatrix}0.4&0&0.1\\0&0.3&0\\0&0.1&0.1\end{pmatrix}$. Check that it is a coupling, try to certify it with complementary slackness, and improve it.

Hint

Solve $f_i+g_j=C_{ij}$ on the five used routes (with $f_1=0$), then compute the slacks of the four unused routes. A negative slack tells you which route to open; push mass around the cycle it creates.

Solution

Row sums $(0.5,0.3,0.2)$, column sums $(0.4,0.4,0.2)$: a coupling, of cost $1.6+0.2+2.1+0.4+0.3=4.6$. On its support: $f_1+g_1=4$, $f_1+g_3=2$, $f_2+g_2=7$, $f_3+g_2=4$, $f_3+g_3=3$; with $f_1=0$: $g_1=4$, $g_3=2$, $f_3=1$, $g_2=3$, $f_2=4$. Slacks of the unused routes: $(1,2)$: $8-3=5$; $(2,1)$: $6-4-4=-2$; $(2,3)$: $8-4-2=2$; $(3,1)$: $5-1-4=0$. The negative slack on W₂→F₁ refutes optimality (no admissible prices can be tight on this support). Open that route: the cycle W₂→F₁, W₁→F₁, W₁→F₃, W₃→F₃, W₃→F₂, W₂→F₂ gets $+\theta,-\theta,+\theta,-\theta,+\theta,-\theta$; the largest $\theta$ keeping $P\ge0$ is $\theta=0.1$ (entry $(3,3)$ vanishes). The cost changes by $\theta(6-4+2-3+4-7)=-2\theta=-0.2$, giving $4.4$ — the plan $P^\star$ of §1. The change rate $-2$ is the slack of the opened route: one step of the network simplex.

Exercise 6 — Quadratic $c$-transforms of parabolas ★★

On $\R$ with $c(x,y)=\frac12(x-y)^2$, let $f(x)=\frac a2x^2$. (a) For $a<1$, compute $f^c$ and $f^{c\bar c}$. (b) What happens for $a>1$? (c) Relate with §4: for which $a$ is $\varphi=\frac12x^2-f$ convex?

Hint

$\frac12(x-y)^2-\frac a2x^2=\frac{1-a}2x^2-xy+\frac12y^2$ is a parabola in $x$.

Solution

(a) For $a<1$ the parabola in $x$ is convex, minimal at $x=\frac y{1-a}$, with value $\frac12y^2-\frac{y^2}{2(1-a)}=-\frac a{1-a}\cdot\frac{y^2}2$. So $f^c(y)=\frac{a'}2y^2$ with $a'=-\frac a{1-a}<1$, and applying the same formula again gives $f^{c\bar c}(x)=\frac{a''}2x^2$ with $a''=-\frac{a'}{1-a'}=\frac{a/(1-a)}{1/(1-a)}=a$: $f^{c\bar c}=f$, so $f$ is $c$-concave. (Numerically, for $a=\frac12$: $f^c(1)=-0.5$ and $f^c(2)=-2$; for $a=-1$: $f^c(1)=0.25$, $f^c(2)=1$.) (b) For $a>1$ the parabola in $x$ is concave: $f^c\equiv-\infty$ — the transform is not defined (for $a=1$: $f^c(y)=-\infty$ for $y\ne0$ and $f^c(0)=0$). (c) $\varphi(x)=\frac{1-a}2x^2$ is convex iff $a\le1$, the $c$-concavity criterion of §4: $f^c=\frac12y^2-\varphi^*$, and $\varphi^*(y)=\frac{y^2}{2(1-a)}$ for $a<1$.

Exercise 7 — Potentials between two uniform laws ★★

$\al=\mathcal U[0,1]$, $\be=\mathcal U[0,2]$, $c=\abs{x-y}^2$ (with $\X=[0,1]$, $\Y=[0,2]$). Find optimal potentials with $f(0)=0$, check that the bill equals $W_2^2$, and compute the slack.

Hint

$T(x)=2x$, and on the line $f'(x)=2(x-T(x))$ (§2). Then $g=f^c$, an infimum over $x\in[0,1]$.

Solution

$f'(x)=2(x-2x)=-2x$, $f(x)=-x^2$. $g(y)=\min_{x\in[0,1]}\big((x-y)^2+x^2\big)=\min_x\big(2x^2-2xy\big)+y^2$, minimised at $x=\frac y2\in[0,1]$: $g(y)=\frac{y^2}2$. Bill: $\int_0^1-x^2\,\dd x+\int_0^2\frac{y^2}2\cdot\frac12\,\dd y=-\frac13+\frac23=\frac13$, and $W_2^2=\int_0^1(2x-x)^2\,\dd x=\frac13$. ✓ Slack: $(x-y)^2+x^2-\frac{y^2}2=2\big(x-\frac y2\big)^2\ge0$, zero exactly on $y=2x$. The convex potential is $\varphi=\frac{x^2}2-\frac f2=x^2$, with $\varphi'=2x=T$.

Exercise 8 — Jensen–Shannon between shifted uniform laws ★★

Compute $\mathrm{JS}(\al_t,\be)^2$ for $\al_t=\mathcal U[t,1+t]$ and $\be=\mathcal U[0,1]$, $t\ge0$. Compare its derivative in $t$ with that of $W_1(\al_t,\be)$.

Hint

Split the line into the overlap $[t,1]$ (when $t<1$) and the two parts covered by only one measure; on each part, compute $\log\frac{\dd\al}{\dd m}$ with $m=\frac{\al+\be}2$.

Solution

On the overlap both densities are $1$, so $m=\frac{\al_t+\be}2$ has density $1$ there and $\log\frac{\dd\al_t}{\dd m}=0$. On the part where only $\al_t$ lives (length $\min(t,1)$), $m$ has density $\frac12$ and $\frac{\dd\al_t}{\dd m}=2$. So $\KL(\al_t|m)=\int\log\frac{\dd\al_t}{\dd m}\,\dd\al_t=\min(t,1)\log2$, and the same for $\be$. Hence $\mathrm{JS}^2=\frac12\KL(\al_t|m)+\frac12\KL(\be|m)=\min(t,1)\log2$ ($0.3466$ at $t=\frac12$, checked by quadrature). Its derivative is $\log2$ for $t<1$ and $0$ for $t>1$; that of $W_1=t$ is $1$ for every $t$. A generator trained with JS stops receiving a signal once the supports separate (§6).

Exercise 9 — Semi-discrete transport in one step ★★

$\al=\mathcal U[0,1]$, $\be=\frac13\delta_0+\frac23\delta_1$, $c=\abs{x-y}^2$. Find the optimal weights $g^\star$ (gauge $g_1+g_2=0$), the cells, and $W_2^2$. Show that one gradient step with $\tau=1$ from $g=0$ lands exactly on $g^\star$.

Hint

The wall is at $x^\star(g)=\frac{1-g_2+g_1}2$ (§7, with $y_1=0$, $y_2=1$); the first cell must have mass $\frac13$.

Solution

The wall solves $x^2-g_1=(x-1)^2-g_2$: $x^\star=\frac{1-g_2+g_1}2$. Optimal: $x^\star=\frac13$ (the $\frac13$-quantile), so $g_2-g_1=\frac13$, $g^\star=(-\frac16,\frac16)$. Cells $[0,\frac13]\to0$ and $[\frac13,1]\to1$; $W_2^2=\int_0^{1/3}x^2\,\dd x+\int_{1/3}^1(1-x)^2\,\dd x=\frac1{81}+\frac8{81}=\frac19$. From $g=0$: masses $(\frac12,\frac12)$, gradient $b-\text{masses}=(-\frac16,\frac16)$, so $g\leftarrow(-\frac16,\frac16)=g^\star$, whose cell masses are $(\frac13,\frac23)$. The mass of cell 1 is affine in $g$ with slope $\frac12$ in $g_1-g_2$, and the step $\tau=1$ is exactly the inverse of the curvature here (in the example of §7 the slope is $\frac1{1.4}$ and the same step overshoots).

Exercise 10 — Lloyd can stop at a bad configuration ★★

Four points of mass $\frac14$ at the corners $(0,0),(2,0),(0,1),(2,1)$ of a rectangle; $m=2$ codepoints, $c=\norm{x-y}^2$. Compare the configurations $Y_A=\{(0,\frac12),(2,\frac12)\}$ and $Y_B=\{(1,0),(1,1)\}$: energies, and are they fixed points of Lloyd’s algorithm?

Solution

$Y_A$: each point is at distance $\frac12$ from its codepoint, energy $4\cdot\frac14\cdot\frac14=0.25$. $Y_B$: each point is at distance $1$, energy $1$. Both are fixed points: for $Y_B$ the Voronoi wall is the line $y=\frac12$, the cells are the bottom pair and the top pair, whose centroids are $(1,0)$ and $(1,1)$ again. Lloyd started near $Y_B$ (for instance from $(1,0.1)$, $(1,0.9)$) stays at an energy four times the optimum: the problem is non-convex in the codepoints, and the book’s warning about initialisation (p. 115) is not academic.

Exercise 11 — Two Gaussian mixtures, without computing the map ★★

(StatOT §1.8, Exercise 6, p. 50.) For $m>0$, $\mu=\frac12\N(-m,1)+\frac12\N(m,1)$ and $\nu=\frac14\N(-m,1)+\frac34\N(m,1)$. Prove that $\frac m2\le W_2(\mu,\nu)\le m$.

Hint

Lower bound: $W_2\ge W_1\ge\E_\nu f-\E_\mu f$ for a 1-Lipschitz $f$ — try $f(x)=x$. Upper bound: $\nu$ is $\mu$ with a quarter of the mass moved from the left bump to the right bump; build a coupling that translates exactly that quarter by $2m$.

Solution

Lower bound. $W_2\ge W_1$ (Jensen, Lesson 3), and with the 1-Lipschitz $f(x)=x$: $W_1\ge\E_\nu X-\E_\mu X=\big(-\frac m4+\frac{3m}4\big)-0=\frac m2$. (In fact $W_1=\frac m2$ exactly: $C_\mu-C_\nu=\frac14\big(\Phi(x+m)-\Phi(x-m)\big)\ge0$ never changes sign, so the witness $f(x)=x$ is optimal.) Upper bound. Draw $X\sim\mu$. If $X$ comes from the left bump, set $Y=X+2m$ with probability $\frac12$ and $Y=X$ otherwise; if it comes from the right bump, $Y=X$. Then $Y\sim\frac14\N(-m,1)+\frac34\N(m,1)=\nu$ and $\E\abs{X-Y}^2=\frac14(2m)^2=m^2$, so $W_2\le m$. Numbers (quantile formula): $W_2/m=0.505$, $0.526$, $0.626$, $0.782$ for $m=0.5,1,2,4$: the ratio moves from the lower bound towards the upper one as the bumps separate.

Exercise 12 — Prices are derivatives of the optimal cost ★★★

In the shipping problem of §1, move a quantity $t$ of supply from W₂ to W₁: $a(t)=(0.5+t,\,0.3-t,\,0.2)$. Show that $\mathrm L_C(a(t),b)=4.4-2t$ for $t\in[-0.3,0.1]$, and explain the slope with the prices.

Hint

Keep the support (tree) of $P^\star$ and solve for its entries as functions of $t$; the prices of §1 stay admissible and tight on that support. When does the support stop being feasible?

Solution

On the tree of $P^\star$ the marginals determine the entries: column 3 is served only by W₁, so $P_{13}=0.2$; row 1: $P_{11}=0.3+t$; column 1: $P_{21}=0.1-t$; row 2: $P_{22}=0.2$; column 2: $P_{32}=0.2$. This is a coupling of $a(t)$ and $b$ as long as $P_{11}\ge0$ and $P_{21}\ge0$, i.e. $t\in[-0.3,0.1]$. The prices $f=(1,3,0)$, $g=(3,4,1)$ do not depend on $a$; they stay admissible and tight on this support, so by complementary slackness the plan is optimal and $\mathrm L_C(a(t),b)=\ps{f}{a(t)}+\ps{g}{b}=4.4+t(f_1-f_2)=4.4-2t$. Directly: $4(0.3+t)+2\cdot0.2+6(0.1-t)+7\cdot0.2+4\cdot0.2=4.4-2t$. Outside the interval the basis changes and so does the slope (exact LP: $5.2$ at $t=-0.35$ instead of $5.1$, and $4.25$ at $t=0.15$ instead of $4.1$): $a\mapsto\mathrm L_C(a,b)$ is convex and piecewise linear, with the optimal prices as (super)gradients — the machine-learning box of §1.
