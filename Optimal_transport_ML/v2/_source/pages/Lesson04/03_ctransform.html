3. $c$-transforms: the best response to a price OT4ML §5.3, pp. 99–104

Fix the price $f$ on the sources: the best admissible price on the targets is the $c$-transform $f^c(y)=\inf_xc(x,y)-f(x)$, a lower envelope of shifted cost curves. It makes one potential enough (the semi-dual), gives regularity for free, and alternating transforms stops improving after one round.

Fix $f$, choose the best $g$

The dual problem has two unknown functions tied by infinitely many constraints $f(x)+g(y)\le c(x,y)$. The simplest way to make progress is to fix one of them and optimise the other — which turns out to be possible in closed form.

In the course OT4ML p. 99, Def. 5.8 and Prop. 5.9

Keeping a dual potential $f$ fixed, one can maximize in closed form over the second potential in the dual problem (5.6), which leads one to consider

$$\sup_{g\in\mathcal C(\Y)}\Big\{\int g\,\dd\be\ ;\ \forall(x,y),\ g(y)\le c(x,y)-f(x)\Big\}.$$

For each $y$, feasibility imposes the upper bound $g(y)\le\inf_x\{c(x,y)-f(x)\}$. Since $\be$ is nonnegative, an optimal $g$ must saturate this bound $\be$-almost everywhere. This pointwise best response leads to the following definition.

Definition 5.8 ($c$-transform). For a function $f:\X\to\R\cup\{-\infty\}$ that is not identically $-\infty$, its $c$-transform is

$$\forall y\in\Y,\qquad f^c(y):=\inf_{x\in\X}\ c(x,y)-f(x).\qquad(5.8)$$

[…] For a function $g:\Y\to\R\cup\{-\infty\}$ that is not identically $-\infty$, the $\bar c$-transform associated with $\bar c(y,x)=c(x,y)$ is $\ \forall x\in\X,\ g^{\bar c}(x):=\inf_{y\in\Y}c(x,y)-g(y)$.

Proposition 5.9 ($c$-transforms solve the semi-relaxed problems). Assume the compactness and continuity hypotheses of Proposition 5.5. For fixed $f\in\mathcal C(\X)$, the maximizers of the dual objective over all $g\in\mathcal C(\Y)$ such that $f\oplus g\le c$ are exactly the functions satisfying $g=f^c$ $\be$-almost everywhere. Symmetrically, for fixed $g$, the maximizers over $f$ are exactly the functions satisfying $f=g^{\bar c}$ $\al$-almost everywhere.

$\inf_x\ c(x,y)-f(x)$
For the target point $y$, try every source $x$: the price at $y$ may not exceed $c(x,y)-f(x)$ for any of them. The largest number below all these bounds is their infimum.
$\R\cup\{-\infty\}$
Potentials may take the value $-\infty$ (“this point is never used”); then $c(x,y)-f(x)=+\infty$ does not constrain anything. On compact spaces with continuous $f$ and $c$ everything is finite and the infima are minima.
$\bar c$
The cost read backwards, $\bar c(y,x)=c(x,y)$: the same operation from the target side. For a symmetric cost on $\X=\Y$ (like $\abs{x-y}^p$) the two transforms are the same operation.
“$\be$-almost everywhere”
Where $\be$ has no mass, $g$ does not enter the bill $\int g\,\dd\be$, so it can be anything below $f^c$. The transform is the canonical choice, defined everywhere (“canonical extensions of dual solutions beyond the active supports”, OT4ML p. 99).
Proof of Prop. 5.9
  1. $f\oplus g\le c\iff\forall y,\ g(y)\le\inf_x\big(c(x,y)-f(x)\big)=f^c(y)$.“$g(y)\le c(x,y)-f(x)$ for every $x$” means that $g(y)$ is a lower bound of these numbers, i.e. at most their infimum.
  2. So $f^c$ is admissible (with $f$) and dominates every admissible $g$ pointwise.Take $g=f^c$ in step 1. On compact spaces $f^c$ is continuous (Prop. 5.10 below).
  3. $\int g\,\dd\be\le\int f^c\,\dd\be$, with equality iff $\int(f^c-g)\,\dd\be=0$, i.e. iff $g=f^c$ $\be$-a.e.$\be\ge0$, and a non-negative function with zero integral vanishes $\be$-almost everywhere.
Worked example — a transform by hand

$\X=\Y=\{0,1,2\}\subset\R$, $c(x,y)=\abs{x-y}$, and $f=(f(0),f(1),f(2))=(0,2,1)$. Tabulate $c(x,y)-f(x)$ (rows $x$, columns $y$) and take the minimum of each column:

$c(x,y)-f(x)$$y=0$$y=1$$y=2$
$x=0$, $f=0$$0$$1$$2$
$x=1$, $f=2$$-1$$-2$$-1$
$x=2$, $f=1$$1$$0$$-1$
$f^c(y)$ = column min$-1$$-2$$-1$

So $f^c=(-1,-2,-1)$. Transform back, with row minima of $c(x,y)-f^c(y)$: $f^{c\bar c}(0)=\min(0+1,\,1+2,\,2+1)=1$, $f^{c\bar c}(1)=\min(1+1,\,0+2,\,1+1)=2$, $f^{c\bar c}(2)=\min(2+1,\,1+2,\,0+1)=1$. So $f^{c\bar c}=(1,2,1)\ge f=(0,2,1)$: the double transform raised $f(0)$ from $0$ to $1$. Why? With $c=\abs{x-y}$ the $c$-concave functions are exactly the 1-Lipschitz ones (§5), and $f^{c\bar c}$ is the smallest of them above $f$ (§9); here $f(1)-f(0)=2>1=\abs{1-0}$, so $f$ is not 1-Lipschitz. The value $f(0)=0$ was “wasted”: against $g=f^c=(-1,-2,-1)$ the vendor could charge $1$ at $x=0$ without breaking any check ($1+g(y)\le\abs{0-y}$ for $y=0,1,2$). Transforming once more gives back $f^c$: $f^{c\bar cc}=(-1,-2,-1)$ (try it).

Picture: a lower envelope

When $\X=\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}$ is finite, $f^c(y)=\min_i\big(c(x_i,y)-f_i\big)$: draw, for each source point $x_i$, the cost curve $y\mapsto c(x_i,y)$ lowered by $f_i$; $f^c$ is the lower envelope of these $n$ curves. For $c=\abs{x-y}^2$ the curves are parabolas with their lowest point at $(x_i,-f_i)$; for $c=\abs{x-y}$ they are V-shapes. Raising a price $f_i$ lowers its curve, so that source “wins” on a larger region of targets.

In the course OT4ML p. 100

With the present sign convention $f\oplus g\le c$, the discrete transform of $f$ is the vector $f^C\in\R^m$ defined by $(f^C)_j=\min_{1\le i\le n}C_{i,j}-f_i$. This is a minimum because feasibility imposes the upper bound $g_j\le C_{i,j}-f_i$ for every $i$. […] With $\bar C:=C\T$, the opposite transform of $g$ is $(g^{\bar C})_i=\min_{1\le j\le m}C_{i,j}-g_j$. Thus the continuous best-response operation reduces exactly to taking column minima for $f^C$, or row minima for $g^{\bar C}$, in shifted cost matrices.

On the shipping example of §1: with $f=(1,3,0)$, subtract $f_i$ from row $i$ of $C$ and take column minima: $C-f\ind\T=\begin{pmatrix}3&7&1\\3&4&5\\5&4&3\end{pmatrix}$, so $f^C=(3,4,1)$ — exactly the optimal $g$. With $f=0$, $f^C=(4,4,2)$, the “cheap price list” of §1.

One potential is enough: the semi-dual

In the course OT4ML Def. 6.1, p. 105

Under the compactness and continuity assumptions of Chapter 5, define $\mathcal D_0(f,g):=\int_\X f\,\dd\al+\int_\Y g\,\dd\be$ if $(f,g)\in\mathcal R(c)$, $-\infty$ otherwise, and

$$\mathcal E_0(g):=\mathcal D_0(g^{\bar c},g)=\int_\X g^{\bar c}\,\dd\al+\int_\Y g\,\dd\be.\qquad(6.2)$$

Thus the dual problem (5.6) is $\Lc(\al,\be)=\max_{f,g}\mathcal D_0(f,g)$. For fixed $g$, feasibility is equivalent to $f\le g^{\bar c}$. Since $\al$ is nonnegative, the largest admissible choice $f=g^{\bar c}$ maximizes the objective. Consequently, $\Lc(\al,\be)=\sup_{g\in\mathcal C(\Y)}\mathcal E_0(g)$ […] Partial maximization of a jointly concave objective preserves concavity, so $\mathcal E_0$ is concave. The semi-dual is invariant under $g\mapsto g+s$ […] The main computational advantage is that the semi-dual is unconstrained.

$\mathcal D_0$
The dual objective with the constraint built in: $-\infty$ for inadmissible prices (the subscript $0$: no entropic regularisation, which Lesson 7 adds).
$\mathcal E_0(g)$
The semi-dual: choose $g$ freely, and let the source side answer optimally with $g^{\bar c}$. One unknown function, no constraint.
concave
For each $x$, $g\mapsto g^{\bar c}(x)=\inf_y\big(c(x,y)-g(y)\big)$ is an infimum of affine functions of $g$, hence concave; integrating against $\al\ge0$ and adding the linear $\int g\,\dd\be$ keeps concavity. Maximising a concave function is a convex problem: no spurious local maxima.
$g\mapsto g+s$
$(g+s)^{\bar c}=g^{\bar c}-s$, and the two $s$ cancel because both measures have mass $1$: the gauge of §1.

Symmetrically $\Lc(\al,\be)=\sup_f\int f\,\dd\al+\int f^c\,\dd\be$ (CompOT (5.4), p. 90). When one of the measures is discrete, the semi-dual is a concave function of finitely many numbers even if the other measure is continuous: this is the semi-discrete method of §7.

Transforms inherit the regularity of the cost

In the course OT4ML Prop. 5.10, p. 99

Proposition 5.10 (Modulus stability of $c$-transforms). Suppose that $\omega$ is a modulus such that $\abs{c(x,y)-c(x,y')}\le\omega\big(d_\Y(y,y')\big)$ for all $x,y,y'$. Then every finite $f^c$ has modulus $\omega$. Symmetrically, $g^{\bar c}$ inherits any uniform modulus of $c$ in its first variable. In particular, a uniform $L$-Lipschitz bound on the cost yields an $L$-Lipschitz bound on the corresponding transform.

modulus $\omega$
A function $\omega:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ with $\omega(t)\to0$ as $t\to0$, controlling how much $c$ can change when $y$ moves (a modulus of continuity). $\omega(t)=Lt$ is the Lipschitz case.
“has modulus $\omega$”
$\abs{f^c(y)-f^c(y')}\le\omega(d(y,y'))$ — whatever $f$ is, even discontinuous or wild. The transform smooths $f$ out to the regularity of the cost.
  1. For each $x$, $F_x(y):=c(x,y)-f(x)$ has modulus $\omega$ in $y$.$f(x)$ is a constant in $y$, so $\abs{F_x(y)-F_x(y')}=\abs{c(x,y)-c(x,y')}\le\omega(d(y,y'))$.
  2. $$\abs{f^c(y)-f^c(y')}=\Big|\inf_xF_x(y)-\inf_xF_x(y')\Big|\le\sup_x\abs{F_x(y)-F_x(y')}\le\omega\big(d(y,y')\big).$$For two families of numbers, $\abs{\inf A-\inf B}\le\sup\abs{A-B}$: if $A_x\le B_x+\eps$ for all $x$, then $\inf A\le\inf B+\eps$, and symmetrically.

Examples. $c=\abs{x-y}$: $\abs{c(x,y)-c(x,y')}\le\abs{y-y'}$ (reverse triangle inequality), so every $f^c$ is 1-Lipschitz — the V-shapes of the figure. $c=\abs{x-y}^2$ on $[0,R]$: $\abs{(x-y)^2-(x-y')^2}=\abs{y-y'}\,\abs{y+y'-2x}\le2R\abs{y-y'}$, so $f^c$ is $2R$-Lipschitz. This is exactly the compactness used in §2 to prove that optimal potentials exist (OT4ML Remark 5.6).

The algebra of $c$-transforms, and why alternating them stalls

Since $f\mapsto f^c$ improves $g$ for a fixed $f$, and $g\mapsto g^{\bar c}$ improves $f$ for a fixed $g$, it is tempting to alternate: $f\to f^c\to f^{c\bar c}\to f^{c\bar cc}\to\cdots$, hoping to climb to the optimum. The algebra says that this stops after one round.

In the course OT4ML Prop. 5.14 and (5.9), p. 102

Proposition 5.14 (Algebra of $c$-transforms). Writing $f^{c\bar c}:=(f^c)^{\bar c}$, the following pointwise identities hold whenever the transforms are well defined:

$$\text{(i) } f\le f'\Rightarrow f^c\ge f'^c,\qquad\text{(ii) } f^{c\bar c}\ge f,\qquad\text{(iii) } g^{\bar cc}\ge g,\qquad\text{(iv) } f^{c\bar cc}=f^c.$$

Identity (iv) shows that the next best response leaves the pair obtained after one full cycle unchanged:

$$(f^{c\bar c},f^c)\longmapsto(f^{c\bar c},f^{c\bar cc})=(f^{c\bar c},f^c).\qquad(5.9)$$

Thus exact block maximization reaches a coordinatewise fixed point after one full cycle. The resulting pair is dual feasible, but it need not maximize the joint dual objective because its value still depends on the arbitrary initialization $f$.

This is Exercise 3 of the sheet; it is solved line by line, with every hypothesis made explicit, in §9. The same proposition is in the compact notes (Prop. 5.12, p. 24), the 2019 notes (Prop. 15, p. 25) and CompOT (Prop. 3.1, pp. 44–45, for matrices).

(i)
Order-reversing: raise the source prices, and the best target prices go down (there is less room under the cost).
(ii), (iii)
Going there and back can only raise a potential: $f^{c\bar c}$ is an admissible improvement of $f$ (it is the best answer to $f^c$, and $f$ was one possible answer).
(iv)
After one round nothing moves any more: $f^c$ is already the best response to $f^{c\bar c}$. Compare with the Legendre transform, $h^{***}=h^*$ (OT4ML p. 102).
Idea of the proof

(i) is the minus sign in the definition. (ii) is the definition read backwards: $f^c(y)\le c(x,y)-f(x)$ for every pair, i.e. $f(x)\le c(x,y)-f^c(y)$, and then take the infimum in $y$. (iv) is squeezed: (ii) and (i) give $f^{c\bar cc}\le f^c$, and (iii) applied to $g=f^c$ gives $f^{c\bar cc}\ge f^c$.

Short proof (the complete line-by-line version is the solution of Exercise 3, §9)
  1. (i) If $f\le f'$, then $c(x,y)-f(x)\ge c(x,y)-f'(x)$ for every $x$; take the infimum over $x$.The infimum is monotone: if $A_x\ge B_x$ for all $x$, then $\inf A\ge\inf B$.
  2. (ii) For all $x,y$: $f^c(y)\le c(x,y)-f(x)$, so $c(x,y)-f^c(y)\ge f(x)$; take the infimum over $y$: $f^{c\bar c}(x)\ge f(x)$.An infimum is below each of its terms (here the term $x$ of the infimum defining $f^c(y)$).
  3. (iii) The same with the roles of $\X,\Y$ (and $c,\bar c$) exchanged.The definition is symmetric.
  4. (iv) Apply (i) to $f\le f^{c\bar c}$ (from (ii)): $f^c\ge(f^{c\bar c})^c=f^{c\bar cc}$. Apply (iii) to $g=f^c$: $f^{c\bar cc}=(f^c)^{\bar cc}\ge f^c$. Hence equality.Two opposite inequalities.
Worked example — the alternation stalls below the optimum

On the shipping problem of §1 (optimal value $4.4$), start from $f=(0,0,0)$:

  1. $g=f^C=(4,4,2)$, bill $3.6$.Column minima of $C$.
  2. $f=g^{\bar C}=(0,2,0)$, bill $0.3\cdot2+3.6=4.2$.Row minima of $C-\ind g\T=\begin{pmatrix}0&4&0\\2&3&6\\1&0&1\end{pmatrix}$.
  3. $g=f^C=(4,4,2)$ again, bill $4.2$; and so on forever.Column minima of $C-f\ind\T=\begin{pmatrix}4&8&2\\4&5&6\\5&4&3\end{pmatrix}$: identity (iv) at work.

The pair $f=(0,2,0)$, $g=(4,4,2)$ is admissible and each potential is the best response to the other, yet the bill $4.2$ is below the optimum $4.4$. Starting instead from $f=(0,0,1)$ the alternation stops at $4.0$; from $f=(1,0,0)$ it happens to reach $4.4$. The limit depends on the start, as the book says. (All values computed exactly.)

Intuition — why the hard minimum stalls, and what Lesson 7 does

A best response only looks at the cheapest source of each target; it ignores how much mass each source has. At $f=(0,2,0)$, $g=(4,4,2)$ every column has a contact and every row has a contact, so no single-side move helps; yet the contacts cannot carry a coupling with the right marginals (the factory F₂ only touches W₃, which has $0.2<0.4$). To improve, both price lists must move together. Entropic regularisation (Lesson 7) replaces the minimum by a soft minimum (log-sum-exp), which weighs all sources; alternating soft transforms is Sinkhorn’s algorithm, and it keeps improving (OT4ML p. 102).

Pitfall — update one side at a time

The alternation must be sequential: $(f,g)\mapsto(f,f^c)\mapsto(f^{c\bar c},f^c)$. Replacing both at once by $(g^{\bar c},f^c)$ can break admissibility, because each was computed against the old other potential. The book’s example (p. 102): $c\equiv0$ and $f=g\equiv-1$ is admissible ($-2\le0$), but $g^{\bar c}=f^c\equiv1$ and $1+1>0$.

$c$-concave functions: what the transforms produce

In the course OT4ML Def. 5.15, p. 102, and p. 103

Definition 5.15 ($c$-concave functions). A function on $\Y$ is $c$-concave if it equals $f^c$ for some function $f$ on $\X$. A function on $\X$ is $\bar c$-concave if it equals $g^{\bar c}$ for some function $g$ on $\Y$.

[p. 103] Three model costs distinguish the possibilities. On $\X=\Y=\R^d$, the cost $c(x,y)=-\ps{x}{y}$ gives exactly the convex cone of finite concave functions. For $c_\tau(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2/(2\tau)$, one has $u\in\mathrm{Conc}_{c_\tau}(\R^d)\iff y\mapsto u(y)-\frac{\norm{y}^2}{2\tau}$ is concave […]. Finally, on any metric space $(\X,d)$, $\mathrm{Conc}_d(\X)=\{u:\X\to\R\ ;\ \Lip(u)\le1\}$.

$c$-concave
“Is a best response to something.” By (iv), $u$ is $c$-concave iff $u=u^{\bar cc}$: transforming twice gives it back. So one may always assume the potentials $c$-concave: replace $(f,g)$ by $(f^{c\bar c},f^c)$, which is admissible and has a larger bill.
$c=-\ps{x}{y}$
$f^c(y)=\inf_x\big(-\ps{x}{y}-f(x)\big)$ is an infimum of affine functions of $y$: concave. (The name “$c$-concave” comes from here.)
$c=\frac{\norm{x-y}^2}{2\tau}$
$u$ minus a parabola is concave: $u$ may curve upwards, but not more than $\frac{\norm{y}^2}{2\tau}$ (“semiconcave”). This is the case of Brenier’s theorem (§4).
$c=d$
The $c$-concave functions are exactly the 1-Lipschitz functions — the case of $W_1$ (§5).

Reflexes
  • One price is given and you need the other → take the $c$-transform: column minima for matrices, a lower envelope for curves.
  • You need regular (Lipschitz, equicontinuous) potentials → replace $(f,g)$ by $(f^{c\bar c},f^c)$; they inherit the modulus of $c$ and the bill does not decrease.
  • You see an alternating scheme with hard minima → expect it to stop after one round; smooth it (Lesson 7).

For $c(x,y)=\abs{x-y}^2$ on $\R$ and $f(x)=-x^2$, compute $f^c$. Same question for $f(x)=+x^2$.

Answer

$f(x)=-x^2$: $f^c(y)=\inf_x\big((x-y)^2+x^2\big)=\inf_x\big(2x^2-2xy\big)+y^2=-\frac{y^2}2+y^2=\frac{y^2}2$ (minimum at $x=\frac y2$). $f(x)=+x^2$: $(x-y)^2-x^2=-2xy+y^2$ is affine in $x$, unbounded below as soon as $y\ne0$: $f^c(y)=-\infty$ for $y\ne0$ and $f^c(0)=0$. A price that grows as fast as the cost leaves no room on the other side. (With $c=\frac12\abs{x-y}^2$, $f=\frac a2x^2$ has a finite transform iff $a<1$: Exercise 6 of §10.)
