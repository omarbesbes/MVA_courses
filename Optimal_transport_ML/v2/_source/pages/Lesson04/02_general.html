2. Kantorovich duality for general measures OT4ML §5.2, pp. 96–98

For arbitrary measures the price vectors become continuous functions, paired with the measures by integration; the statement is the same, weak duality is the same one line, and optimal plans live in the zero set of the slack $c(x,y)-f(x)-g(y)$.

From price vectors to price functions

Lesson 3 explained that a measure is known through the averages $\int\varphi\,\dd\al$ of continuous test functions. That is the right way to “price” a measure: a price list on a continuous space is a continuous function $f$ on $\X$, and the bill for collecting $\al$ is $\ps{f}{\al}:=\int f\,\dd\al$. For discrete measures this gives back §1, with $f_i=f(x_i)$ and $g_j=g(y_j)$: “the discrete case (5.2) corresponds to the dual vectors being samples of the continuous potentials” (OT4ML p. 97).

In the course OT4ML Def. 5.4 and Prop. 5.5, p. 96

Definition 5.4 (Continuous admissible potentials). For $c\in\mathcal C(\X\times\Y)$, define

$$\mathcal R(c):=\big\{(f,g)\in\mathcal C(\X)\times\mathcal C(\Y)\ ;\ f(x)+g(y)\le c(x,y)\ \text{for all }(x,y)\big\}.\qquad(5.5)$$

Its elements are admissible continuous potentials.

Proposition 5.5 (Kantorovich duality). Assume that $\X$ and $\Y$ are compact metric spaces and that $c\in\mathcal C(\X\times\Y)$. Then

$$\Lc(\al,\be)=\max_{(f,g)\in\mathcal R(c)}\ \int_\X f(x)\,\dd\al(x)+\int_\Y g(y)\,\dd\be(y),\qquad(5.6)$$

Here, $(f,g)$ is a pair of continuous functions, often called “Kantorovich potentials”. More general Polish-space versions require explicit lower-semicontinuity and integrability hypotheses; value duality and dual attainment are then separate statements. Proposition 5.12 below records the noncompact quadratic case used in Brenier’s theorem.

$\mathcal C(\X)$
Continuous real functions on $\X$. On a compact space they are bounded, so $\int f\,\dd\al$ is always defined.
$\mathcal R(c)$
Price functions that never bill a pair $(x,y)$ above its cost: $f\oplus g\le c$ everywhere — infinitely many constraints, one per pair.
$\Lc(\al,\be)$
The Kantorovich cost $\min_{\pi\in\U(\al,\be)}\int c\,\dd\pi$ (Lessons 2–3).
“max”
Two statements in one: no duality gap (the supremum of the bills equals the minimal cost) and attainment (an optimal pair of continuous potentials exists).
“Polish-space versions”
On $\R^d$ (not compact) the potentials may be unbounded and one asks them to be integrable; then “no gap” and “attained” must be proved separately. The quadratic case on $\R^d$, the one needed for Brenier, is Prop. 5.12 (§4).

The same result is in the compact notes (Prop. 5.5, p. 22), the 2019 notes (Prop. 13, p. 23), CompOT (Prop. 2.5, p. 30) and StatOT (Thm. 1.20, p. 46, for $\norm{x-y}^p$ on $\R^d$ with $f\in L^1(\al)$, $g\in L^1(\be)$).

Weak duality in one line, and the two hard parts

  1. $$\int_\X f\,\dd\al+\int_\Y g\,\dd\be=\int_{\X\times\Y}\big(f(x)+g(y)\big)\,\dd\pi(x,y)$$For any coupling $\pi\in\U(\al,\be)$: its first marginal is $\al$, so $\int f(x)\,\dd\pi(x,y)=\int f\,\dd\al$; likewise for $g$ and $\be$. (This is the continuous version of “rows sum to $a$, columns to $b$”.)
  2. $$\le\int_{\X\times\Y}c(x,y)\,\dd\pi(x,y).$$$f(x)+g(y)\le c(x,y)$ at every point, and $\pi\ge0$.
  3. $$\sup_{(f,g)\in\mathcal R(c)}\int f\,\dd\al+\int g\,\dd\be\ \le\ \inf_{\pi\in\U(\al,\be)}\int c\,\dd\pi=\Lc(\al,\be).$$Line 2 holds for every admissible pair and every coupling; take the supremum on the left and the infimum on the right.

The two hard parts are the reverse inequality and the existence of a maximiser.

Idea of the proof (the hard parts)
  1. No gap. Suppose a number $t<\Lc(\al,\be)$. Then “$(\al,\be,t)$ is achievable” is false: no non-negative measure $\pi$ has marginals $(\al,\be)$ and cost $\le t$. The set of achievable triples is a closed convex cone; a point outside it can be separated from it by a continuous linear functional — that is, by two continuous functions and a number. Rescaled, the two functions are admissible prices with a bill above $t$.
  2. Attainment. Improve a maximising sequence by $c$-transforms (§3): the improved prices inherit the modulus of continuity of $c$, so after fixing the gauge they form an equicontinuous, bounded family; Arzelà–Ascoli extracts a uniformly convergent subsequence, whose limit is admissible and optimal.
Proof of the hard parts (OT4ML pp. 96–97, in steps)

No gap. Write $V=\Lc(\al,\be)$ and consider, in $\mathcal M(\X)\times\mathcal M(\Y)\times\R$, the set

$$\mathcal K=\Big\{\Big(\pi_1,\ \pi_2,\ \int c\,\dd\pi+r\Big)\ :\ \pi\in\mathcal M_+(\X\times\Y),\ r\ge0\Big\},$$

where $\pi_1,\pi_2$ are the two marginals of a non-negative measure $\pi$ (not necessarily of mass 1).

  1. $\mathcal K$ is a convex cone.Marginals and $\int c\,\dd\pi$ are linear in $\pi$, and non-negative combinations of non-negative measures (and of $r\ge0$) stay admissible.
  2. $\mathcal K$ is closed (for weak convergence on the measure factors).If the first marginals converge, the masses $\pi(\X\times\Y)=\pi_1(\X)$ are bounded; on the compact $\X\times\Y$ a bounded sequence of measures has a weakly convergent subsequence (Prokhorov / Banach–Alaoglu), and marginals and $\int c\,\dd\pi$ ($c$ continuous) pass to the limit.
  3. For $tIf it were, some $\pi\ge0$ with marginals $\al,\be$ (a coupling) would satisfy $\int c\,\dd\pi=t-r\le t
  4. There are $F\in\mathcal C(\X)$, $G\in\mathcal C(\Y)$, $\lambda\in\R$ with $$\int F\,\dd\al+\int G\,\dd\be+\lambda t<0\le\int F\,\dd\eta+\int G\,\dd\zeta+\lambda s\quad\text{for all }(\eta,\zeta,s)\in\mathcal K.$$Hahn–Banach strict separation of a point from a closed convex set (refresher below): the continuous linear functionals for the weak topology on measures are integrations against continuous functions. The right side can be taken $\ge0$ because $\mathcal K$ is a cone containing $0$.
  5. $\lambda\ge0$, and $F(x)+G(y)+\lambda c(x,y)\ge0$ for all $(x,y)$.Test the right inequality with $(0,0,r)\in\mathcal K$ ($r\to\infty$) for the first claim, and with $\pi=\delta_{(x,y)}$, $r=0$ (marginals $\delta_x,\delta_y$, cost $c(x,y)$) for the second.
  6. $\lambda>0$.If $\lambda=0$, integrating $F(x)+G(y)\ge0$ against $\al\otimes\be$ gives $\int F\,\dd\al+\int G\,\dd\be\ge0$, contradicting the left inequality of step 4 (which then reads $\int F\,\dd\al+\int G\,\dd\be<0$).
  7. $f:=-F/\lambda$ and $g:=-G/\lambda$ satisfy $f\oplus g\le c$ and $\int f\,\dd\al+\int g\,\dd\be>t$.Divide step 5 and the left inequality of step 4 by $\lambda>0$.
  8. So $\sup_{\mathcal R(c)}\big(\int f\,\dd\al+\int g\,\dd\be\big)>t$ for every $tLet $t\uparrow V$.

Attainment. Take a maximising sequence $(f_k,g_k)$.

  1. Replace $g_k$ by $\tilde g_k(y)=\min_x c(x,y)-f_k(x)$, then $f_k$ by $\tilde f_k(x)=\min_y c(x,y)-\tilde g_k(y)$.These are the $c$- and $\bar c$-transforms of §3. Each replacement keeps admissibility and does not decrease the objective (the new function is the largest admissible one, Prop. 5.9).
  2. Shift $(\tilde f_k,\tilde g_k)\to(\tilde f_k-s_k,\tilde g_k+s_k)$ so that $\tilde f_k(x_0)=0$ for a fixed $x_0$.The gauge does not change the objective (both measures have mass 1) nor admissibility.
  3. The $\tilde f_k$ share the modulus of continuity of $c$ in $x$, the $\tilde g_k$ that of $c$ in $y$; and they are uniformly bounded.Transforms inherit the modulus of the cost (Prop. 5.10, §3), which is uniform because $c$ is continuous on a compact set. With $\tilde f_k(x_0)=0$ and a common modulus $\omega$, $\abs{\tilde f_k(x)}\le\omega\big(d(x,x_0)\big)\le\omega(\diam\X)$; then $\tilde g_k=\min_x(c-\tilde f_k)$ is bounded too.
  4. By Arzelà–Ascoli, a subsequence converges uniformly to some $(f,g)$, which is admissible and attains the supremum.Uniform limits keep $f\oplus g\le c$, and $\int f_k\,\dd\al\to\int f\,\dd\al$ under uniform convergence.
Refresher — separating a point from a closed convex set (Hahn–Banach)

In $\R^N$: if $K$ is closed and convex and $z\notin K$, there is a vector $w$ and a number $\gamma$ with $\ps{w}{z}<\gamma\le\ps{w}{k}$ for every $k\in K$ — a hyperplane strictly separates $z$ from $K$ (take $w=k^\star-z$, where $k^\star$ is the projection of $z$ on $K$). If $K$ is a cone containing $0$, one may take $\gamma=0$. The Hahn–Banach theorem extends this to locally convex spaces such as measures with the weak topology, where the continuous linear functionals are $\mu\mapsto\int F\,\dd\mu$ with $F$ continuous. This is the only place where infinite dimension costs something: the proof of the discrete case just quotes LP duality, whose proof is the same separation in $\R^N$.

Complementary slackness and the slack landscape

In the course OT4ML Prop. 5.7, p. 97, and p. 98

Proposition 5.7 (Continuous complementary slackness). For $\pi\in\U(\al,\be)$ and $(f,g)\in\mathcal R(c)$,

$$\int c\,\dd\pi-\int f\,\dd\al-\int g\,\dd\be=\int\big(c(x,y)-f(x)-g(y)\big)\,\dd\pi(x,y)\ \ge\ 0.$$

The coupling and potentials are respectively primal and dual optimal if and only if this gap vanishes. In that case,

$$\supp(\pi)\subset\big\{(x,y)\in\X\times\Y\ ;\ f(x)+g(y)=c(x,y)\big\}.\qquad(5.7)$$

[p. 98] The same optimality condition is even more explicit if one plots the slack $s(x,y):=c(x,y)-f(x)-g(y)$. Dual feasibility is the statement $s\ge0$, while complementary slackness says that an optimal coupling can only live where this slack vanishes.

$s(x,y)$
The slack: a non-negative landscape over $\X\times\Y$ for admissible prices.
the gap
The average slack under the plan $\pi$. It is zero iff $\pi$ only charges the zero set of $s$.
$\supp(\pi)$
The support of $\pi$: the smallest closed set carrying all its mass (the points every neighbourhood of which has positive mass).
(5.7)
“Optimal plans live in the valley $\{s=0\}$ of optimal potentials.” The potentials do not say how much mass goes where — the marginals do — but they say where it may go.
Full proof
  1. The identity and the sign of the gap.Same computation as the weak duality steps above (marginals of $\pi$), and $s\ge0$, $\pi\ge0$.
  2. Zero gap $\iff$ both optimal.A zero gap means $\int c\,\dd\pi$ equals a lower bound on all plans and $\int f\,\dd\al+\int g\,\dd\be$ equals an upper bound on all bills (weak duality): both are optimal. Conversely, if both are optimal, the two values are equal by Prop. 5.5.
  3. If the gap is zero, $s=0$ on $\supp(\pi)$.$s$ is continuous and $\ge0$. If $s(x_0,y_0)>0$ at a point of the support, then $s>s(x_0,y_0)/2$ on a neighbourhood $U$, and $\pi(U)>0$ (definition of the support), so $\int s\,\dd\pi\ge\pi(U)\,s(x_0,y_0)/2>0$: contradiction.

A complete example: two Gaussians on the line

In the course OT4ML pp. 97–98

For the one-dimensional quadratic cost $c(x,y)=\abs{x-y}^2$, let $\al,\be\in\Pc_2(\R)$ and assume that $\al$ is atomless. Theorem 2.41 gives the monotone optimal map $T=C_\be^{-1}\circ C_\al$ […]. Applying Proposition 5.12 below to $c/2$ and multiplying its potentials by two supplies closed optimal potentials $(f,g)$ for $c$, with $g=f^c$, and the convex potential $\varphi(x)=\abs{x}^2/2-f(x)/2$. Its contact conclusion gives $T(x)\in\partial\varphi(x)$ for $\al$-almost every $x$. […] Atomlessness of $\al$ therefore gives $f'(x)=2(x-T(x))$ for $\al$-almost every $x$.

$f'(x)=2(x-T(x))$
On the line, the optimal potential is obtained by integrating the displacement: its slope at $x$ is twice how far $x$ is from its destination. Know the map, get the potential (and conversely).
$\varphi=\frac{x^2}2-\frac f2$
A convex function whose derivative is the map: $\varphi'(x)=x-\frac{f'(x)}2=T(x)$. This is Brenier’s theorem in 1D, read from the dual (proved in general in §4).
$g=f^c$
The target potential is the $c$-transform of the source potential (§3): $g(y)=\inf_x\abs{x-y}^2-f(x)$.

Let us do it completely for $\al=\N(0,1)$ and $\be=\N(m,\sigma^2)$ with $\sigma>0$, where (Lesson 1) $T(x)=m+\sigma x$ and $W_2^2(\al,\be)=m^2+(\sigma-1)^2$.

  1. $$f'(x)=2\big(x-m-\sigma x\big)=2(1-\sigma)x-2m,\qquad f(x)=(1-\sigma)x^2-2mx.$$The formula of the book, integrated with the gauge $f(0)=0$.
  2. $$g(y)=\inf_x\ \big[(x-y)^2-(1-\sigma)x^2+2mx\big]=\inf_x\ \big[\sigma x^2-2x(y-m)\big]+y^2 .$$Definition of $f^c$; expand $(x-y)^2=x^2-2xy+y^2$ and collect the terms in $x$.
  3. $$g(y)=y^2-\frac{(y-m)^2}{\sigma}.$$$\sigma x^2-2x(y-m)$ is a convex parabola in $x$ (because $\sigma>0$), minimal at $x=\frac{y-m}{\sigma}=T^{-1}(y)$, with minimum $-\frac{(y-m)^2}{\sigma}$.
  4. $$\int f\,\dd\al+\int g\,\dd\be=(1-\sigma)\cdot1-0+\big(m^2+\sigma^2\big)-\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma}=m^2+(1-\sigma)^2=W_2^2(\al,\be).$$$\E[X^2]=1$, $\E[X]=0$ for $X\sim\N(0,1)$; $\E[Y^2]=m^2+\sigma^2$ and $\E[(Y-m)^2]=\sigma^2$ for $Y\sim\N(m,\sigma^2)$. The bill equals the cost: the potentials are optimal (weak duality makes it a certificate).
  5. $$s(x,y)=(x-y)^2-f(x)-g(y)=\frac{\big(y-m-\sigma x\big)^2}{\sigma}=\frac{\big(y-T(x)\big)^2}{\sigma}.$$Expand: $\sigma x^2-2xy+2mx+\frac{(y-m)^2}{\sigma}=\frac1\sigma\big(\sigma^2x^2-2\sigma x(y-m)+(y-m)^2\big)$, a perfect square.

The slack is a squared distance to the graph of $T$: it is $\ge0$ everywhere (the prices are admissible) and vanishes exactly on the graph $y=T(x)$, where the optimal plan $\pf{(\Id,T)}{\al}$ lives.

Worked example — $m=1$, $\sigma=2$

$f(x)=-x^2-2x$, $g(y)=y^2-\frac{(y-1)^2}2$, $T(x)=1+2x$. Then $\int f\,\dd\al=-1$, $\int g\,\dd\be=5-2=3$, and the bill $2$ equals $W_2^2=1^2+(2-1)^2=2$. The slack is $s(x,y)=\frac{(y-1-2x)^2}2$: at $(0,1)$ and $(0.5,2)$ it is $0$ (points of the graph), at $(1,0)$ and $(-1,2)$ it is $4.5$. The convex potential is $\varphi(x)=\frac{x^2}2-\frac{f(x)}2=x^2+x$, and $\varphi'(x)=2x+1=T(x)$. (All values checked by quadrature and by minimising on a grid.)

Intuition — the potentials do not move mass, they forbid

The book insists: “The potentials are not transport maps themselves; rather, their contact set with the cost matrix is where an optimal coupling is allowed to put mass” (OT4ML p. 96). In the example, $f$ and $g$ are two parabolas; neither looks like $T$. Their combination $c-f\oplus g$ is the landscape whose valley is the graph of $T$.

Pitfall — a valley can be wider than the plan

The contact set can contain pairs that carry no mass (degenerate plans, as in §1), and when the optimal plan is not unique the valley contains all of them. Complementary slackness says “mass ⇒ contact”, never “contact ⇒ mass”.

Let $\al=\delta_{x_0}$. Show that $g(y)=c(x_0,y)$ and $f=g^{\bar c}$ (that is, $f(x)=\inf_y c(x,y)-c(x_0,y)$) are optimal potentials, and compute $f(x_0)$.

Answer

$f(x)+g(y)\le c(x,y)$ by construction of $f$ (it is the largest such function). $f(x_0)=\inf_y\big(c(x_0,y)-c(x_0,y)\big)=0$. The bill is $f(x_0)+\int c(x_0,y)\,\dd\be(y)=\int c(x_0,y)\,\dd\be(y)$, which is the cost of the only coupling $\delta_{x_0}\otimes\be$ (Lesson 3, §1). Bill = cost: both are optimal.

In the worked example ($m=1$, $\sigma=2$), for which $y$ is the slack at $x=0$ equal to $2$? What does the slack measure?

Answer

$s(0,y)=\frac{(y-1)^2}2=2$ for $y=-1$ and $y=3$. It measures, in units of $\frac1\sigma$, the squared distance between $y$ and the destination $T(0)=1$ of $x=0$: sending mass from $0$ to $y\ne1$ “wastes” $s(0,y)$ per unit compared with what the prices allow.
