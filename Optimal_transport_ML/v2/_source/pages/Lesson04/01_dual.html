1. The discrete dual: prices that certify a plan OT4ML §5.1, pp. 95–96

Put a price on each source and on each target, never more than the cost of a route: the total price is a lower bound on the cost of every plan; the best prices reach exactly the optimal cost; and an optimal plan only uses the routes whose price equals their cost.

The problem: how do you know that a plan is optimal?

Lesson 2 solved the transport linear program and found sparse optimal plans. Suppose now that a colleague hands you a plan $P$ of cost $4.4$ and claims it is optimal. Checking it against every other plan is hopeless (the polytope $\U(a,b)$ has infinitely many points, and exponentially many vertices). What you want is a certificate: a short piece of evidence, checkable in a few operations, proving that no plan costs less than $4.4$. For a minimisation problem such evidence is a lower bound equal to the cost of the plan — and lower bounds are exactly what duality produces.

You have already met such a certificate for the assignment problem (Lesson 1, Hungarian algorithm: numbers $u_i,v_j$ with $u_i+v_j\le C_{ij}$, see the first companion). This section shows that it exists for every transport problem, and what it means.

A shipping company sets prices

Peyré and Cuturi tell the dual as a story. An operator must move goods from warehouses (the sources, with supplies $a_i$) to factories (the targets, with demands $b_j$); shipping one unit from warehouse $i$ to factory $j$ costs $C_{ij}$. Instead of solving the linear program, the operator outsources the job.

In the course CompOT Remark 2.21, pp. 28–30

Outsourcing logistics. […] The vendor splits the logistic task into that of collecting and then delivering the goods and will apply a collection price $f_i$ to collect a unit of resource at each warehouse $i$ (no matter where that unit is sent to) and a price $g_j$ to deliver a unit of resource to factory $j$ (no matter from which warehouse that unit comes from). On aggregate, since there are exactly $a_i$ units at warehouse $i$ and $b_j$ needed at factory $j$, the vendor asks as a consequence of that pricing scheme a price of $\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}$ to solve the operator’s logistic problem.

Checking prices. […] if for any pair $i,j$ the aggregate price $f_i+g_j$ is strictly larger than $C_{i,j}$, the vendor is charging more than the fair price charged by the transportation company for that task, and the operator should refuse the vendor’s offer. […] for any transport plan $P$ (including the optimal one $P^\star$), the marginal constraints imply

$$\sum_{i,j}P_{i,j}C_{i,j}\ \ge\ \sum_{i,j}P_{i,j}\big(f_i+g_j\big)=\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}.$$
$f_i$, $g_j$
The vendor’s price list: $f_i$ per unit collected at warehouse $i$, $g_j$ per unit delivered at factory $j$. A route $i\to j$ is billed $f_i+g_j$ per unit, whatever the route actually costs.
$\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}$
The vendor’s total bill: every unit is collected once and delivered once, and the amounts collected ($a$) and delivered ($b$) are fixed. The bill does not depend on the plan.
$f_i+g_j\le C_{ij}$
“No route is billed more than it costs”: otherwise the operator would ship along that route himself. The vendor wants the largest bill that passes this check.
the inequality
Whatever plan the operator might run himself, it costs at least the bill. So the bill is a lower bound on the optimal cost $\mathrm L_C(a,b)$.

Let us check the inequality line by line; it is the whole of “weak duality”.

  1. $$\sum_{i,j}P_{ij}C_{ij}\ \ge\ \sum_{i,j}P_{ij}\big(f_i+g_j\big)$$Term by term: $C_{ij}\ge f_i+g_j$ (the prices pass the check) and $P_{ij}\ge0$.
  2. $$=\sum_if_i\Big(\sum_jP_{ij}\Big)+\sum_jg_j\Big(\sum_iP_{ij}\Big)$$Split the sum and group the terms of $f_i$ by row, those of $g_j$ by column.
  3. $$=\sum_if_ia_i+\sum_jg_jb_j=\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}.$$The rows of a coupling sum to $a$, its columns to $b$. Only here do we use that $P$ is a coupling.
Worked example — three warehouses, three factories

Costs in euros per ton (rows: warehouses W₁, W₂, W₃; columns: factories F₁, F₂, F₃), supplies $a$ and demands $b$ in hundreds of tons:

F₁ ($b_1=0.4$)F₂ ($b_2=0.4$)F₃ ($b_3=0.2$)
W₁ ($a_1=0.5$)482
W₂ ($a_2=0.3$)678
W₃ ($a_3=0.2$)543

A cheap price list. Charge nothing for collecting, $f=(0,0,0)$, and for delivering to F$_j$ the cheapest cost of reaching it, $g_j=\min_iC_{ij}$: $g=(4,4,2)$. Every route passes the check ($f_i+g_j=g_j\le C_{ij}$), and the bill is $0.4\cdot4+0.4\cdot4+0.2\cdot2=3.6$. Hence every plan costs at least $3.6$.

A better price list. $f=(1,3,0)$ and $g=(3,4,1)$. The billed prices $f_i+g_j$ are

$$\big(f_i+g_j\big)_{ij}=\begin{pmatrix}4&5&2\\6&7&4\\3&4&1\end{pmatrix}\ \le\ C=\begin{pmatrix}4&8&2\\6&7&8\\5&4&3\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{entrywise},$$

and the bill is $\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}=(0.5+0.9+0)+(1.2+1.6+0.2)=4.4$. Every plan costs at least $4.4$.

A plan. $P^\star=\begin{pmatrix}0.3&0&0.2\\0.1&0.2&0\\0&0.2&0\end{pmatrix}$ has row sums $(0.5,0.3,0.2)=a$, column sums $(0.4,0.4,0.2)=b$, and costs $0.3\cdot4+0.2\cdot2+0.1\cdot6+0.2\cdot7+0.2\cdot4=4.4$.

The plan costs $4.4$ and nothing can cost less: the plan is optimal, and $\mathrm L_C(a,b)=4.4$. A few additions, multiplications and comparisons checked it; no other plan was examined. (Checked with an exact LP solver.)

The dual linear program

The vendor’s best strategy is a linear program: maximise the bill under the checks. The theorem says that the vendor can always charge exactly the optimal cost — never less, and of course never more.

In the course OT4ML Def. 5.1 and Prop. 5.2, p. 95

Definition 5.1 (Admissible potentials). For a discrete problem with marginal sizes $n,m$ and cost matrix $C\in\R^{n\times m}$, a pair $(f,g)\in\R^n\times\R^m$ is admissible if it lies below the cost:

$$\mathrm R(C):=\big\{(f,g)\in\R^n\times\R^m\ ;\ \forall(i,j)\in[\![n]\!]\times[\![m]\!],\ f_i+g_j\le C_{i,j}\big\}.\qquad(5.1)$$

Equivalently, $f\oplus g\le C$ entrywise. In particular, the feasible set depends on the cost matrix, not on the marginal weights.

Proposition 5.2 (Discrete Kantorovich duality). One has

$$\mathrm L_C(a,b)=\max_{(f,g)\in\mathrm R(C)}\ \ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}.\qquad(5.2)$$
$[\![n]\!]$
The set of indices $\{1,\dots,n\}$.
$f\oplus g$
The $n\times m$ matrix $(f_i+g_j)_{ij}$: “$f$ down the rows, $g$ across the columns”.
$\mathrm R(C)$
The admissible price lists: an intersection of $nm$ half-spaces, a convex polyhedron in $\R^{n+m}$. It is never empty ($f=0$, $g_j=\min_iC_{ij}$ belongs to it) and never bounded (you can always lower prices).
$\mathrm L_C(a,b)$
The optimal transport cost $\min_{P\in\U(a,b)}\ps{C}{P}$.
“max”
The supremum is attained: there are optimal potentials $(f^\star,g^\star)$, also called Kantorovich potentials.
primal / dual sizes
The primal has $nm$ unknowns and $n+m$ equality constraints; the dual has $n+m$ unknowns and $nm$ inequality constraints. Same value.
Reminder — Lagrangian duality for linear programs

For $\min\{\ps{c}{p}:\ Ap=q,\ p\ge0\}$, the dual function $H(h)=\min_{p\ge0}\ps{c}{p}+\ps{h}{q-Ap}$ is always a lower bound (weak duality), and for a feasible linear program with finite value $\max_hH=\min_p$ (strong duality), both attained. Key computation: $\min_{p\ge0}\ps{v}{p}$ is $0$ if $v\ge0$, $-\infty$ otherwise. Details in the refresher above and on the Reminders page.

Idea of the proof

Put a multiplier on each marginal constraint: $f_i$ on “row $i$ sums to $a_i$”, $g_j$ on “column $j$ sums to $b_j$”. Minimising the Lagrangian over $P\ge0$ gives $\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}$ if $C-f\oplus g\ge0$ and $-\infty$ otherwise: the dual function is the vendor’s bill, restricted to admissible prices. Weak duality is the one-line inequality above; equality is strong duality of linear programs.

Full proof (Lagrangian duality)
  1. $$\mathcal L(P,f,g)=\ps{C}{P}+\ps{a-P\ind_m}{f}+\ps{b-P\T\ind_n}{g}.$$The Lagrangian of $\min\{\ps{C}{P}:\ P\ind_m=a,\ P\T\ind_n=b,\ P\ge0\}$, with multipliers $f\in\R^n$ for the row constraints and $g\in\R^m$ for the column constraints (OT4ML (5.3)). $\ind_m$ is the vector of ones, so $P\ind_m$ is the vector of row sums.
  2. $$\mathcal L(P,f,g)=\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}+\ps{C-f\oplus g}{P}.$$Expand: $\ps{P\ind_m}{f}=\sum_{ij}P_{ij}f_i$ and $\ps{P\T\ind_n}{g}=\sum_{ij}P_{ij}g_j$, so the two terms in $P$ combine into $-\sum_{ij}P_{ij}(f_i+g_j)=-\ps{f\oplus g}{P}$.
  3. $$\min_{P\ge0}\mathcal L(P,f,g)=\begin{cases}\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}&\text{if }C-f\oplus g\ge0,\\-\infty&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$$\min_{P\ge0}\ps{Q}{P}$ is $0$ when $Q\ge0$ (take $P=0$; nothing is negative) and $-\infty$ when some $Q_{ij}<0$ (put a huge mass on $(i,j)$).
  4. $$\max_{f,g}\ \min_{P\ge0}\mathcal L=\max_{(f,g)\in\mathrm R(C)}\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}\ \le\ \mathrm L_C(a,b).$$Maximising the dual function only makes sense on $\mathrm R(C)$, where it is the bill. The inequality is weak duality (the three-line computation above).
  5. The primal polytope $\U(a,b)$ is non-empty (it contains $ab\T$) and compact, so the primal value is finite and attained. Strong duality for linear programs then gives equality in line 4, and a maximiser $(f^\star,g^\star)$ exists.Finite-dimensional LP duality (e.g. Bertsimas–Tsitsiklis, Thm. 4.4, cited by CompOT p. 27). This is the only non-elementary step.

Complementary slackness: where an optimal plan may move mass

In the course OT4ML Prop. 5.3, pp. 95–96

Proposition 5.3 (Discrete complementary slackness). For every $P\in\U(a,b)$ and $(f,g)\in\mathrm R(C)$,

$$\ps{C}{P}-\ps{f}{a}-\ps{g}{b}=\sum_{i,j}P_{i,j}\big(C_{i,j}-f_i-g_j\big)\ \ge\ 0.$$

The plan and potentials are respectively primal and dual optimal if and only if

$$\mathrm{Supp}(P)\subset\big\{(i,j)\in[\![n]\!]\times[\![m]\!]\ ;\ f_i+g_j=C_{i,j}\big\}.\qquad(5.4)$$
$C_{ij}-f_i-g_j$
The slack of route $(i,j)$: how much cheaper the vendor bills it than it costs. Admissible prices have all slacks $\ge0$.
left-hand side
The duality gap: cost of the plan minus the bill. It is the sum of the slacks, weighted by the mass the plan sends along each route.
$\mathrm{Supp}(P)$
The support: the routes with $P_{ij}>0$.
$\{f_i+g_j=C_{ij}\}$
The contact set: routes billed exactly at cost. (5.4) says: an optimal plan moves mass only along contacts of optimal prices.
Idea of the proof

The gap is a weighted sum of non-negative numbers. Strong duality says that at the optimum the gap is $0$, so each term vanishes: wherever $P_{ij}>0$, the slack is $0$. Conversely, a plan and prices with zero gap are both optimal, because each one is a bound for the other.

Full proof
  1. $$\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}=\sum_{i,j}P_{ij}(f_i+g_j),\quad\text{so}\quad\ps{C}{P}-\ps{f}{a}-\ps{g}{b}=\sum_{i,j}P_{ij}\big(C_{ij}-f_i-g_j\big).$$The computation of the weak-duality steps (rows of $P$ sum to $a$, columns to $b$).
  2. Each term $P_{ij}(C_{ij}-f_i-g_j)$ is $\ge0$, hence so is the gap.$P\ge0$, and $(f,g)\in\mathrm R(C)$ means every slack is $\ge0$.
  3. If $P$ and $(f,g)$ are optimal, then $\ps{C}{P}=\mathrm L_C=\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}$, so the gap is $0$; a sum of non-negative terms is $0$ only if every term is $0$; so $P_{ij}>0\Rightarrow C_{ij}=f_i+g_j$.Strong duality (Prop. 5.2) makes the two optimal values equal.
  4. Conversely, if $P$ lives on contacts, the gap is $0$: $\ps{C}{P}=\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}$. But every plan costs $\ge\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}$ (weak duality), so $P$ is optimal; and every admissible bill is $\le\ps{C}{P}$, so $(f,g)$ is optimal.Only weak duality is needed for this direction: a feasible plan and admissible prices with equal values certify each other.
Worked example — the slacks of the shipping problem

With $f=(1,3,0)$ and $g=(3,4,1)$, the slack matrix is

$$C-f\oplus g=\begin{pmatrix}0&3&0\\0&0&4\\2&0&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad P^\star=\begin{pmatrix}0.3&0&0.2\\0.1&0.2&0\\0&0.2&0\end{pmatrix}.$$

The five routes used by $P^\star$ are exactly the five zeros of the slack matrix, so the gap $\sum_{ij}P^\star_{ij}(C_{ij}-f_i-g_j)$ is $0$: this is the certificate. The four other routes are billed below their cost (by $3$, $4$, $2$, $2$ euros per ton): an optimal plan must not use them. Five is $n+m-1$: the support of $P^\star$ is a spanning tree of the bipartite graph warehouses–factories (Lesson 2: vertices of $\U(a,b)$), drawn below.

The gauge, uniqueness, and what prices measure

The gauge. Adding $s$ to every $f_i$ and subtracting $s$ from every $g_j$ changes neither the routes’ prices $f_i+g_j$ nor the bill (since $\sum_ia_i=\sum_jb_j=1$). So prices are only defined up to this shift — CompOT notes that a vendor can pay you to collect and recover it on delivery. One fixes it by a gauge, for instance $f_1=0$ or $\ps{f}{a}=0$ (OT4ML Fig. 5.1). An exact LP solver normalised with $f_1=0$ returns $f=(0,2,-1)$, $g=(4,5,2)$ for the example: the same prices shifted by $s=-1$.

Finding the prices from the plan. When the optimal plan has $n+m-1$ positive entries forming a tree (a non-degenerate vertex), the equations $f_i+g_j=C_{ij}$ on its support determine the prices up to the gauge. Walk along the tree:

  1. $f_1=1$.The gauge: any value would do.
  2. $g_1=C_{11}-f_1=3$ and $g_3=C_{13}-f_1=1$.Routes W₁→F₁ and W₁→F₃ are used, hence contacts.
  3. $f_2=C_{21}-g_1=3$.Route W₂→F₁ is used.
  4. $g_2=C_{22}-f_2=4$, then $f_3=C_{32}-g_2=0$.Routes W₂→F₂ and W₃→F₂ are used.
  5. Check the four unused routes: slacks $8-5=3$, $8-4=4$, $5-3=2$, $3-1=2$, all $\ge0$. The prices are admissible, hence optimal.Complementary slackness (Prop. 5.3), converse direction.

This is exactly what the network simplex of Lesson 2 does at every vertex; when step 5 finds a negative slack, that route enters the tree (a pivot). When the optimal plan is degenerate (fewer than $n+m-1$ entries), the equations leave freedom and the optimal prices are not unique (exercise 1 of §10).

In the course OT4ML p. 96

The formulation (5.2) shows that $(a,b)\mapsto\mathrm L_C(a,b)$ is a convex function (as a supremum of linear functions). From the primal problem (3.2), one also sees that $C\mapsto\mathrm L_C(a,b)$ is concave.

Decoding: in (5.2) the admissible set $\mathrm R(C)$ does not depend on $(a,b)$ (Def. 5.1 insists on it), and for fixed prices the bill $\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}$ is linear in $(a,b)$; a supremum of linear functions is convex. Symmetrically, $\U(a,b)$ does not depend on $C$ and $\ps{C}{P}$ is linear in $C$; an infimum of linear functions is concave.

In machine learning — the prices are the gradient of an OT loss

When OT is used as a loss between histograms — a predicted histogram $a_\theta$ (the softmax output of a network over $n$ labels, or the weights of a mixture) against a target histogram $b$ — training needs the derivative of $a\mapsto\mathrm L_C(a,b)$. Duality gives it for free: $\mathrm L_C(a,b)=\max_{(f,g)\in\mathrm R(C)}\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}$ is a maximum of functions linear in $a$, and when the optimal prices are unique (up to the gauge), its derivative is the optimal $f^\star$ (Danskin’s theorem; along directions that keep $\sum_ia_i=1$ the gauge does not matter). On the shipping example, moving a little supply from W$_i$ to W$_j$ changes the cost at rate $f^\star_i-f^\star_j$: finite differences give $-2.000$, $1.000$, $3.000$ for $(i,j)=(1,2),(1,3),(2,3)$, exactly $1-3$, $1-0$, $3-0$. Backpropagation then only needs $\partial a_\theta/\partial\theta$. This is how Wasserstein losses for multi-label prediction (Frogner et al., NeurIPS 2015, with the entropic potentials of Lesson 7) and Wasserstein dictionary learning (2019 notes §9.1, p. 40) are trained; the convexity in $a$ recalled above is what makes those losses well behaved.

Pitfall — reading prices in absolute terms

Only differences of prices mean something: $f_i-f_{i'}$ (how much more expensive it is to ship from W$_i$ than from W$_{i'}$) and $f_i+g_j$. A negative price is not an error, and two solvers can return prices that differ by a constant.

Reflexes
  • To prove a lower bound on a transport cost → exhibit admissible prices ($f_i+g_j\le C_{ij}$) and compute their bill. (Upper bound → exhibit a plan.)
  • To certify an optimal plan → find prices that are admissible and equal to the cost on the plan’s support (walk along the tree).
  • To refute optimality → solve $f_i+g_j=C_{ij}$ on the support; if some unused route gets a negative slack, the plan is not optimal (and that route should enter).

Without solving anything, give a lower bound on $\mathrm L_C(a,b)$ for any problem, using $f=0$.

Answer

With $f=0$ the best admissible $g$ is $g_j=\min_iC_{ij}$ (each factory is billed the cheapest way to reach it), so $\mathrm L_C(a,b)\ge\sum_jb_j\min_iC_{ij}$. Symmetrically $\mathrm L_C(a,b)\ge\sum_ia_i\min_jC_{ij}$. On the example: $3.6$ and $0.5\cdot2+0.3\cdot6+0.2\cdot3=3.4$, both below $4.4$.

The vendor proposes $f=(2,3,0)$, $g=(3,4,1)$. Accept?

Answer

No: route W₁→F₁ is billed $2+3=5>C_{11}=4$, and route W₁→F₃ is billed $2+1=3>C_{13}=2$. The operator would ship along these routes himself. (The bill would be $4.9>4.4$: an inadmissible price list can “beat” the optimal cost, which is why the check matters.)
