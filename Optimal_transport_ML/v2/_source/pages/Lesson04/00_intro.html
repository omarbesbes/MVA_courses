What you will be able to do after this page
  1. Write the dual of a discrete transport problem, read it as a price list (the “shipping company”), prove weak duality in one line, and use complementary slackness to certify — or refute — that a transport plan is optimal.
  2. State Kantorovich duality for general measures, and find optimal plans in the zero set of the slack $c(x,y)-f(x)-g(y)$; compute the potentials between two Gaussians on the line.
  3. Compute $c$-transforms (by hand, and as lower envelopes), prove their algebra (Exercise 3 of the sheet), and explain why alternating them stops improving after one cycle.
  4. Prove Brenier’s theorem from duality: quadratic cost $\to$ Legendre transforms $\to$ a convex potential $\to$ its gradient is the optimal map.
  5. Use the Kantorovich–Rubinstein formula $W_1=\max_{\Lip(f)\le1}\int f\,\dd(\al-\be)$: on the line (area between CDFs), on graphs (Beckmann flows), to finish the proof that $W_p$ metrizes weak convergence, and to understand Wasserstein GANs.
  6. Compare transport with dual norms (MMD) and $\varphi$-divergences (KL, Jensen–Shannon, total variation): which ones see how far mass moves, which ones saturate when supports are disjoint.
  7. Solve semi-discrete transport by gradient ascent on cell weights (Laguerre cells), recognise k-means as optimal quantization, and compare likelihood (KL) with transport for mixtures, including the distance between Gaussian mixtures.

The story of the lesson in seven steps

Every transport problem has a twin problem about prices; the prices certify optimality, reveal the shape of optimal maps, and become the objects that machine learning trains (critics of GANs, weights of cells).

  1. Prices (§1). The transport linear program has a dual: a price $f_i$ at each source and $g_j$ at each target, with $f_i+g_j\le C_{ij}$. Any such prices give a lower bound on the cost of every plan; the best prices give exactly the optimal cost, and they touch the cost exactly where an optimal plan moves mass.
  2. Functions (§2). For general measures the prices become continuous functions $f,g$, the Kantorovich potentials. Optimal plans live in the zero set of the slack $c(x,y)-f(x)-g(y)$.
  3. Best responses (§3). Given $f$, the best $g$ is the $c$-transform $f^c$. One potential is enough (the semi-dual); transforms inherit the regularity of the cost; alternating them stops improving after one cycle (which is why Lesson 7 will smooth them).
  4. Brenier (§4). For the quadratic cost, $c$-transforms are Legendre transforms: the optimal potential is, up to $\frac12\norm{x}^2$, a convex function, and the optimal map is its gradient.
  5. $W_1$ (§5). When the cost is a distance, one 1-Lipschitz price is enough. $W_1$ becomes a norm, the area between two CDFs on the line, a minimal flow on a graph, and it completes the proof (started in Lesson 3) that $W_p$ metrizes weak convergence.
  6. Losses for learning (§6). Dual norms ($W_1$, MMD) compare measures through test functions and stay informative when supports are disjoint; $\varphi$-divergences (KL, Jensen–Shannon) compare densities and saturate. A GAN learns the test function.
  7. Cells and mixtures (§7–§8). When one measure is discrete, the dual is a concave function of $m$ weights whose gradient is a mass mismatch (Laguerre cells); free weights give k-means. Finally: maximum likelihood is a KL, and Gaussian mixtures can be compared by transporting their components.

Refresher — the Kantorovich problem (Lessons 2–3)

Discrete. Histograms $a\in\R_+^n$, $b\in\R_+^m$ with $\sum_ia_i=\sum_jb_j=1$, a cost matrix $C\in\R^{n\times m}$. A coupling (transport plan) is a matrix $P\ge0$ with row sums $a$ and column sums $b$; the set of couplings is $\U(a,b)$, and

$$\mathrm L_C(a,b)=\min_{P\in\U(a,b)}\ps{C}{P}=\min_{P\in\U(a,b)}\sum_{i,j}C_{ij}P_{ij}.$$

It is a linear program: linear objective, linear constraints. $\U(a,b)$ is a non-empty (it contains $ab\T$) compact polytope, so a minimum exists, and it is attained at a vertex, which has at most $n+m-1$ non-zero entries (Lesson 2).

General. For probability measures $\al$ on $\X$ and $\be$ on $\Y$, $\Lc(\al,\be)=\min_{\pi\in\U(\al,\be)}\int c\,\dd\pi$, the minimum over joint laws with marginals $\al$ and $\be$; an optimal plan exists for continuous costs on compact spaces (and l.s.c. costs on $\R^d$). Used in every section. More: Lesson 3, §1, Reminders.

Refresher — Lagrangian duality and linear programs

To minimise $\ps{c}{p}$ under constraints $Ap=q$ and $p\ge0$, put a price (multiplier) $h$ on the equality constraints and form the Lagrangian $\mathcal L(p,h)=\ps{c}{p}+\ps{h}{q-Ap}$. Two facts:

  • Weak duality (always true). For every $h$, the dual function $H(h)=\min_{p\ge0}\mathcal L(p,h)$ is a lower bound on the primal value: take $p^\star$ feasible, then $H(h)\le\mathcal L(p^\star,h)=\ps{c}{p^\star}$ because $Ap^\star=q$. So $\max_hH(h)\le\min_p\ps{c}{p}$.
  • Strong duality for linear programs. If the primal is feasible and its value is finite, then $\max_hH(h)=\min_p\ps{c}{p}$, and both are attained. (For general convex problems one needs a qualification condition such as Slater’s; linear programs need nothing more.)

The trick used again and again: $\min_{p\ge0}\ps{v}{p}=0$ if $v\ge0$ entrywise, and $-\infty$ otherwise (if some $v_k<0$, push $p_k\to+\infty$). Used in §1 (the dual of the transport LP) and §5 (Beckmann). More: Lagrangian duality, linear programming.

Refresher — the convex conjugate (Legendre–Fenchel transform)

For $h:\R^d\to\R\cup\{+\infty\}$, its conjugate is $h^*(y)=\sup_x\ \ps{x}{y}-h(x)$. It is a supremum of affine functions of $y$, hence always convex and lower semi-continuous. Three facts used in §4:

  • Fenchel–Young. $h(x)+h^*(y)\ge\ps{x}{y}$ for all $x,y$ (take this $x$ in the supremum), with equality iff $y\in\partial h(x)$, the subdifferential (the slopes of the affine functions that touch $h$ from below at $x$; it is $\{\nabla h(x)\}$ where $h$ is convex and differentiable).
  • Biconjugate. $h^{**}$ is the largest convex l.s.c. function below $h$ (Fenchel–Moreau), and $h^{***}=h^*$.
  • Examples in 1D. $h(x)=\frac a2x^2$ ($a>0$) gives $h^*(y)=\frac{y^2}{2a}$; in particular $\frac12x^2$ is its own conjugate. $h(x)=\abs{x}$ gives $h^*=0$ on $[-1,1]$ and $+\infty$ outside.

More: Legendre–Fenchel, convex functions and subgradients.

Refresher — Lipschitz functions and the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem

$f$ is $L$-Lipschitz if $\abs{f(x)-f(y)}\le L\,d(x,y)$; $\Lip(f)$ is the smallest such $L$. On $\R^d$, a differentiable $f$ has $\Lip(f)=\sup_x\norm{\nabla f(x)}$. Two facts used repeatedly:

  • An infimum of $L$-Lipschitz functions is $L$-Lipschitz (when finite): $\abs{\inf_kF_k(y)-\inf_kF_k(y')}\le\sup_k\abs{F_k(y)-F_k(y')}$. This is why $c$-transforms are regular (§3).
  • Arzelà–Ascoli. On a compact metric space, a family of functions that is uniformly bounded and equicontinuous (for instance: all 1-Lipschitz functions with $f(x_0)=0$) is relatively compact for the sup norm: every sequence has a uniformly convergent subsequence. Used for the existence of optimal potentials (§2) and in the metrization of weak convergence (§5).

More: Reminders, and the proof of Lesson 3, §4.

Refresher — measures on the line (CDF, quantiles, the monotone map)

$C_\al(x)=\al((-\infty,x])$ is the CDF, $C_\al^{-1}(t)=\inf\{x:\ C_\al(x)\ge t\}$ the quantile function. For $p\ge1$ the optimal plan on $\R$ is monotone and

$$W_p^p(\al,\be)=\int_0^1\abs{C_\al^{-1}(t)-C_\be^{-1}(t)}^p\,\dd t,\qquad W_1(\al,\be)=\int_{\R}\abs{C_\al(x)-C_\be(x)}\,\dd x .$$

When $\al$ has no atoms the optimal map is $T=C_\be^{-1}\circ C_\al$ (non-decreasing). Used in §2 (potentials on the line), §5 ($W_1$ as an area), §7 (semi-discrete and quantization on the line), §8 (fitting Gaussians). More: Lesson 3, Reminders.

Refresher — Brenier’s theorem and Gaussians (Lesson 1)

Brenier. On $\R^d$ with $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2$, if $\al$ has a density (and both measures have finite second moments), there is a unique optimal plan; it is $\pi=\pf{(\Id,T)}{\al}$ with $T=\nabla\varphi$ and $\varphi$ convex. In Lessons 1–3 it was stated and used; §4 proves it.

Gaussians on the line. From $\N(m_\al,\sigma_\al^2)$ to $\N(m_\be,\sigma_\be^2)$ the optimal map is $T(x)=m_\be+\frac{\sigma_\be}{\sigma_\al}(x-m_\al)$ and $W_2^2=(m_\al-m_\be)^2+(\sigma_\al-\sigma_\be)^2$. In $\R^d$: $W_2^2=\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+\B(\Sigma_\al,\Sigma_\be)^2$ with the Bures distance $\B$. More: Lesson 3, Reminders.

Refresher — entropy and the Kullback–Leibler divergence

For probability densities $p,q$, $\KL(p|q)=\int p\log\frac pq$ (for histograms $\sum_ia_i\log\frac{a_i}{b_i}$). It is $\ge0$, with equality iff $p=q$ (Jensen’s inequality applied to the concave $\log$: $-\KL=\int p\log\frac qp\le\log\int q=0$). It is not symmetric, and it is $+\infty$ as soon as $p$ puts mass where $q$ has none. Between Gaussians on the line,

$$\KL\big(\N(m_a,\sigma_a^2)\,\big|\,\N(m_b,\sigma_b^2)\big)=\frac12\Big(\frac{\sigma_a^2}{\sigma_b^2}+\log\frac{\sigma_b^2}{\sigma_a^2}+\frac{(m_a-m_b)^2}{\sigma_b^2}-1\Big).$$

The (differential) entropy is $h(p)=-\int p\log p$; for $\N(m,\sigma^2)$ it is $\frac12\log(2\pi e\sigma^2)$. Used in §6 and §8. More: entropy and KL, $f$-divergences.

$f\oplus g$“$f$ plus $g$”The function $(x,y)\mapsto f(x)+g(y)$, or the matrix $(f_i+g_j)_{ij}$.
$\mathrm R(C)$, $\mathcal R(c)$“admissible potentials”Pairs with $f\oplus g\le C$ (resp. $\le c$): prices never above the cost.
$f^c$, $g^{\bar c}$; $f^C$, $g^{\bar C}$“$c$-transform”, “$\bar c$-transform”$f^c(y)=\inf_x c(x,y)-f(x)$, $g^{\bar c}(x)=\inf_yc(x,y)-g(y)$; for matrices, $f^C$ is the vector of column minima of $C-f\ind\T$ and $g^{\bar C}$ the vector of row minima of $C-\ind g\T$ (§3).
$f^{c\bar c}$“$f$ $c$ $c$-bar”$(f^c)^{\bar c}$: first the $c$-transform, then the $\bar c$-transform. The 2019 notes sometimes write $f^{cc}$.
$\mathcal E_0(g)$“semi-dual”$\int g^{\bar c}\,\dd\al+\int g\,\dd\be$: the dual after eliminating $f$ (§3, §7). The 2019 notes and CompOT write $\mathcal E(g)$.
$\varphi$, $\varphi^*$, $\partial\varphi$“convex potential”, “conjugate”, “subdifferential”For the quadratic cost, $\varphi=\frac12\norm{x}^2-f$; the map is $T=\nabla\varphi$ (§4).
$\Lip(f)$“Lipschitz constant”$\sup_{x\ne y}\abs{f(x)-f(y)}/d(x,y)$ (§5).
$\mathrm L_j(g)$; $\mathrm V_j(Y)$“Laguerre cell”; “Voronoi cell”The points that $y_j$ captures, with weights $g$ (power cells), or with $g=0$ (nearest site) (§7).
$\norm{\xi}_B$“dual norm for the ball $B$”$\sup_{f\in B}\int f\,\dd\xi$: total variation, $W_1$, MMD… (§6).
$\mathcal D_\varphi(\al|\be)$; $\KL$, $\mathrm{JS}$, $\TV$“$\varphi$-divergence”$\int\varphi\big(\frac{\dd\al}{\dd\be}\big)\dd\be$ (+ a term for singular mass) (§6).
$\norm{\al-\be}_\TV$“total variation”$\abs{\al-\be}(\X)$, values in $[0,2]$ — as in Lesson 3. Some books use half of it.
$\al_\theta=\pf{(g_\theta)}{\zeta}$“generator push-forward”A model: draw $z\sim\zeta$ (latent), output $g_\theta(z)$ (§6). Do not confuse the generator $g_\theta$ with a potential $g$.
$\mathfrak A=\sum_ia_i\delta_{\N(m_i,\Sigma_i)}$; $\mathrm{MW}_2$“law of components”A Gaussian mixture seen as a histogram over Gaussians; transport between such laws (§8).
Pitfall — two normalizations of the quadratic cost

Lessons 1–3 use $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2$; for the Euclidean case the book’s duality chapter switches to $c(x,y)=\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$ (“It is convenient to normalize the quadratic cost as $c(x,y)=\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$”, OT4ML p. 100). Halving the cost halves the potentials and the optimal value, and does not change the optimal plan or map. The formulas differ by factors of 2: with $\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$ the convex potential is $\varphi=\frac12\norm{x}^2-f$; with $\norm{x-y}^2$ it is $\varphi=\frac12\norm{x}^2-\frac f2$. This page says which one it uses each time.
