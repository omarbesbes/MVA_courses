4. Brenier’s theorem, proved by duality OT4ML pp. 100–102

For the quadratic cost a $c$-transform is a Legendre transform in disguise: optimal potentials are $\frac12\norm{x}^2$ minus a convex function $\varphi$, optimal plans live on the graph of the subdifferential $\partial\varphi$, and when $\al$ has a density that graph is the graph of a map — the gradient $\nabla\varphi$.

Reminder — Brenier’s theorem, as stated in Lesson 1

OT4ML Thm. 2.23, p. 29 Let $\al,\be\in\M(\R^d)$ have finite second moments, and assume that $\al$ is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure. For the quadratic cost $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2$, there exists a convex function $\varphi:\R^d\to\R\cup\{+\infty\}$ such that $T=\nabla\varphi$, $\pf{T}{\al}=\be$, and $T$ is the unique optimal Monge map $\al$-almost everywhere. The optimal Kantorovich plan is $\pf{(\Id,T)}{\al}$.

The book adds: “The proof uses the Kantorovich relaxation and duality developed later in Chapter 5.” This section is that proof. Lessons 1–3 used the theorem (displacement interpolation, barycenter maps); see the refresher.

What has to be proved, and the plan

The theorem makes three claims: (a) an optimal plan does not split mass — it is carried by the graph of a map; (b) this map is the gradient of a convex function; (c) the optimal plan is unique. Duality proves all three at once, in three moves:

  1. For the quadratic cost, admissible potentials are “$\frac12\norm{x}^2$ minus a convex function”, and $c$-transforms are Legendre transforms.
  2. Optimal potentials exist (Prop. 5.12), and complementary slackness says that optimal plans live on the contact set $\{y\in\partial\varphi(x)\}$.
  3. A convex function is differentiable almost everywhere; if $\al$ has a density, $\partial\varphi(x)=\{\nabla\varphi(x)\}$ for $\al$-almost every $x$, so the contact set is the graph of the map $\nabla\varphi$.

Move 1: for the quadratic cost, $c$-transforms are Legendre transforms

In the course OT4ML pp. 100–101, Def. 5.11

It is convenient to normalize the quadratic cost as $c(x,y)=\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$. For any $\pi\in\U(\al,\be)$,

$$\int c(x,y)\,\dd\pi(x,y)=\frac12\int\norm{x}^2\dd\al(x)+\frac12\int\norm{y}^2\dd\be(y)-\int\ps{x}{y}\,\dd\pi(x,y).$$

Definition 5.11 (Legendre–Fenchel transform and biconjugate). For $h:\R^d\to\R\cup\{+\infty\}$, define $h^*(y):=\sup_{x\in\R^d}\{\ps{x}{y}-h(x)\}$, $h^{**}:=(h^*)^*$. […] The first two terms depend only on the marginals, so the nonseparable part of quadratic transport is the bilinear term $-\ps{x}{y}$. More precisely, if $\varphi(x)=\frac12\norm{x}^2-f(x)$, then

$$f^c(y)=\frac12\norm{y}^2-\varphi^*(y),\qquad f^{c\bar c}(x)=\frac12\norm{x}^2-\varphi^{**}(x).$$
the decomposition
$\frac12\norm{x-y}^2=\frac12\norm{x}^2+\frac12\norm{y}^2-\ps{x}{y}$. Integrated against a coupling, the first two terms give numbers that are the same for every coupling (they only depend on $\al$ and $\be$). So minimising the quadratic cost is the same as maximising the correlation $\int\ps{x}{y}\,\dd\pi$.
$\varphi=\frac12\norm{x}^2-f$
The potential $f$ “minus its quadratic part”, with a sign flip. The claim is that the $c$-transform of $f$ is described by the convex conjugate of $\varphi$.
$\varphi^{**}$
The largest convex l.s.c. function below $\varphi$ (Fenchel–Moreau). So $f^{c\bar c}\ge f$ — identity (ii) of §3 — is the inequality $\varphi^{**}\le\varphi$.
Reminder — Legendre transform and Fenchel–Young

$h^*(y)=\sup_x\ps{x}{y}-h(x)$ is convex and l.s.c.; $h(x)+h^*(y)\ge\ps{x}{y}$ with equality iff $y\in\partial h(x)$; $h^{**}$ is the convex l.s.c. envelope of $h$. Example: $h(x)=\frac a2x^2\Rightarrow h^*(y)=\frac{y^2}{2a}$. Details in the refresher and the Reminders.

  1. $$f^c(y)=\inf_x\Big(\frac12\norm{x}^2-\ps{x}{y}+\frac12\norm{y}^2-f(x)\Big)$$Definition of $f^c$ with $c=\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$, expanded.
  2. $$=\frac12\norm{y}^2+\inf_x\big(\varphi(x)-\ps{x}{y}\big)$$$\frac12\norm{y}^2$ does not depend on $x$; and $\frac12\norm{x}^2-f(x)=\varphi(x)$.
  3. $$=\frac12\norm{y}^2-\sup_x\big(\ps{x}{y}-\varphi(x)\big)=\frac12\norm{y}^2-\varphi^*(y).$$$\inf(-A)=-\sup A$, and the definition of the conjugate.
  4. $$f^{c\bar c}(x)=\frac12\norm{x}^2-\big(\varphi^*\big)^*(x)=\frac12\norm{x}^2-\varphi^{**}(x).$$Apply lines 1–3 to $g=f^c$ (the cost is symmetric): the role of $\varphi$ is played by $\frac12\norm{y}^2-g(y)=\varphi^*(y)$.

Consequence. A potential obtained as a transform ($f=g^{\bar c}$) has $\varphi=\frac12\norm{x}^2-f=\psi^*$ for $\psi=\frac12\norm{y}^2-g$: a conjugate, hence convex. This is where the convexity in Brenier’s theorem comes from. In one line: “on the full Euclidean space, a nontrivial extended-valued function $f$ is $c$-closed precisely when $x\mapsto\frac12\norm{x}^2-f(x)$ is proper, lower-semicontinuous and convex” (OT4ML p. 101).

Move 2: optimal potentials exist, and plans live on $\partial\varphi$

On $\R^d$ (not compact) the duality theorem of §2 does not apply as such. The book records the exact statement needed.

In the course OT4ML Prop. 5.12, p. 101

Proposition 5.12 (Quadratic dual attainment on $\R^d$). Let $\al,\be\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$ and $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2/2$. The dual supremum over pointwise feasible pairs in $L^1(\al)\times L^1(\be)$ is attained by Borel potentials $f,g:\R^d\to\R\cup\{-\infty\}$ that form a closed pair in the pointwise sense

$$f=g^{\bar c},\qquad g=f^c,\qquad\Lc(\al,\be)=\int f\,\dd\al+\int g\,\dd\be .$$

They can be represented by a proper lower-semicontinuous convex function $\varphi:\R^d\to\R\cup\{+\infty\}$ through

$$f(x)=\frac12\norm{x}^2-\varphi(x),\qquad g(y)=\frac12\norm{y}^2-\varphi^*(y),$$

with the convention that a finite number minus $+\infty$ is $-\infty$. For every optimal coupling $\pi$, the Fenchel contact relation

$$\varphi(x)+\varphi^*(y)=\ps{x}{y},\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad y\in\partial\varphi(x),$$

holds $\pi$-almost everywhere.

$\Pc_2(\R^d)$
Probability measures with finite second moment, so that the quadratic cost is integrable.
$L^1(\al)\times L^1(\be)$
Integrable potentials: on $\R^d$ they need not be bounded (in the Gaussian example of §2 they are parabolas).
closed pair
Each potential is the transform of the other: $(f,g)=(g^{\bar c},f^c)$, the fixed points of §3.
proper l.s.c. convex $\varphi$
Convex, not identically $+\infty$, lower semi-continuous; it may be $+\infty$ outside a convex set (its domain).
$y\in\partial\varphi(x)$
The contact set of §2, rewritten: for the quadratic cost the slack is $c-f\oplus g=\varphi(x)+\varphi^*(y)-\ps{x}{y}$, which is $\ge0$ by Fenchel–Young and vanishes iff $y$ is a subgradient of $\varphi$ at $x$.
Idea of the proof

Start from some optimal integrable pair $(f_0,g_0)$ (this is the one input the course cites, from Villani’s book). Improve it by convexification: replace $\varphi_0=\frac12\norm{x}^2-f_0$ by its convex envelope $\varphi=\varphi_0^{**}$; this can only raise both potentials, keeps them admissible, so the pair stays optimal and becomes a closed pair. Then complementary slackness: the slack $\varphi(x)+\varphi^*(y)-\ps{x}{y}$ is $\ge0$ and integrates to $0$ against any optimal plan, so it vanishes $\pi$-almost everywhere.

Proof of Prop. 5.12 (OT4ML p. 101, in steps)

Move 3: the proof of Brenier’s theorem

In the course OT4ML Remark 5.13, p. 102

Remark 5.13 (Proof of Brenier’s theorem). Let $\al,\be\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$ and assume that $\al$ has a density. Proposition 5.12 supplies a proper lower-semicontinuous convex function $\varphi$ and shows that every optimal coupling satisfies $y\in\partial\varphi(x)$ almost everywhere. The function $\varphi$ is finite $\al$-almost everywhere; its convex effective domain therefore has nonempty interior, its boundary is Lebesgue-null, and $\varphi$ is differentiable Lebesgue-almost everywhere in its interior. Hence $\partial\varphi(x)$ is a singleton for $\al$-almost every $x$. Every optimal coupling is consequently concentrated on the graph of $T=\nabla\varphi$, which proves the map structure and uniqueness in Brenier’s theorem. Multiplying the cost by two rescales the dual potentials but leaves this map unchanged, recovering the convention $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2$ used elsewhere in the book.

effective domain
$\mathrm{dom}\,\varphi=\{\varphi<+\infty\}$, a convex set.
“nonempty interior”
A convex set with empty interior lies in an affine hyperplane, which has Lebesgue measure $0$; but $\al(\mathrm{dom}\,\varphi)=1$ and $\al$ has a density, so the domain has positive volume.
“differentiable almost everywhere”
A convex function is locally Lipschitz in the interior of its domain, hence differentiable Lebesgue-almost everywhere there (Rademacher’s theorem; for convex functions there is also an elementary proof).
$\partial\varphi(x)=\{\nabla\varphi(x)\}$
At a point where a convex function is differentiable, the only supporting slope is the gradient.

Worked examples — three convex potentials
  • Gaussians on the line (§2). For $\N(0,1)\to\N(1,4)$ with $c=\abs{x-y}^2$ we found $f(x)=-x^2-2x$. Then $\varphi(x)=\frac{x^2}2-\frac{f(x)}2=x^2+x$: convex, and $\varphi'(x)=2x+1=T(x)$. ✓
  • Gaussians in $\R^d$. The map of Lesson 1, $T(x)=m_\be+A(x-m_\al)$ with $A$ symmetric positive semi-definite, is the gradient of $\varphi(x)=\frac12\ps{Ax}{x}+\ps{m_\be-Am_\al}{x}$, convex because $A\succeq0$. Brenier’s theorem says this is the only optimal map — which is how Lesson 1 proved the Gaussian formula.
  • A discrete target (semi-discrete, §7). If $\be=\sum_jb_j\delta_{y_j}$ and $g_j=g(y_j)$, then (for $c=\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$) $f(x)=g^{\bar c}(x)=\min_j\frac12\norm{x-y_j}^2-g_j$ and $\varphi(x)=\frac12\norm{x}^2-f(x)=\max_j\big(\ps{x}{y_j}-\frac12\norm{y_j}^2+g_j\big)$, a maximum of affine functions: convex, piecewise affine, with gradient $y_j$ on the region where the $j$-th piece wins. The optimal map is piecewise constant, $T(x)=y_j$ on a cell (with $c=\norm{x-y}^2$ the same formula holds with $g_j/2$).
Pitfall — the normalisation of the cost

With $c=\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$: $\varphi=\frac12\norm{x}^2-f$. With $c=\norm{x-y}^2$: $\varphi=\frac12\norm{x}^2-\frac f2$. Mixing the two gives a wrong map: on the Gaussian example, $\frac12x^2-f(x)=\frac32x^2+2x$ has derivative $3x+2\ne T(x)$. Peyré–Cuturi’s statement of the theorem (CompOT Thm. 2.1, p. 31) writes $\varphi(x)=\frac{\norm{x}^2}2-f(x)$ for the cost $\norm{x-y}^2$, which is this slip (see the notes on the sources, §11).

Pitfall — no density, no map

The density of $\al$ is used once, in step 2, and it is needed: from $\al=\delta_0$ to $\be=\frac12\delta_{-1}+\frac12\delta_1$ the only plan splits the atom; here $\varphi(x)=\abs{x}$ is not differentiable at $0$ and $\partial\varphi(0)=[-1,1]$ contains both destinations. The book notes that “does not charge hypersurfaces” is enough (OT4ML Remark 2.26, p. 29).

Other routes, and what each resource proves

  • OT4ML (this section): duality, with one cited ingredient (the integrable dual attainment of Villani’s Thm. 5.10). The proof of Thm. 2.23 (p. 29) is the same argument in four sentences.
  • StatOT (Thm. 1.8, p. 29, proof in §1.4.3): cyclical monotonicity. The support of an optimal plan is cyclically monotone (Lesson 2: no cycle of exchanges lowers the cost), Rockafellar’s theorem puts a cyclically monotone set inside the subdifferential of a convex function, and differentiability does the rest. No duality needed, but Rockafellar’s theorem plays the role of the cited attainment.
  • CompOT (Thm. 2.1, pp. 31–33): a sketch along the duality route (maximise correlation, replace $\psi$ by $\varphi^*$, then by $\varphi^{**}$, contact $y\in\partial\varphi(x)$), with the normalisation slip above.
  • Compact notes and 2019 notes: the compact notes keep the quadratic dual attainment (Prop. 5.11, p. 23) but not the remark that draws the conclusion; the 2019 notes announce the proof “detailed in Section 5.3” (p. 14), but their §5.3 (pp. 24–25) only contains the $c$-transform calculus.
In machine learning — learning Brenier maps with input-convex neural networks

Problem. Given two point clouds in high dimension — cells before and after a drug treatment, images of two domains, latent codes — one wants an optimal map $T$ that can be applied to new points. Brenier says: look for $T=\nabla\varphi$ with $\varphi$ convex. Model. An input-convex neural network (ICNN, Amos, Xu and Kolter 2017) is a network $\varphi_\theta(x)$ that is convex in $x$ by construction: non-negative weights on the hidden-to-hidden connections and convex non-decreasing activations (ReLU, softplus). Loss. The quadratic dual with $\varphi$ convex reads $\min_\varphi\int\varphi\,\dd\al+\int\varphi^*\,\dd\be$ (CompOT (2.31), p. 32); Makkuva, Taghvaei, Oh and Lee (ICML 2020) parametrise $\varphi$ and a second potential by ICNNs and solve the resulting min–max problem with stochastic gradients on samples, then read the map as $\nabla\varphi_\theta$ (automatic differentiation). This is OT4ML Example 5.18 (p. 104): “convex stability can be enforced by the architecture rather than by a penalty”. In single-cell biology this is used to predict how each cell responds to a perturbation (for instance the CellOT method of Bunne et al., 2023).

Why does the proof need $\al$ to have a density, but not $\be$?

Answer

The map goes from $\al$: we need $\varphi$ differentiable at $\al$-almost every point, i.e. the non-differentiability set (Lebesgue-null) must be $\al$-null. Nothing is required of $\be$: it can even be discrete (the semi-discrete case, §7), and then $T$ is piecewise constant. If instead $\be$ has a density, the same argument applied to $\varphi^*$ gives the inverse map $\nabla\varphi^*$ from $\be$ to $\al$.

For $c=\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$, check that $f\equiv0$, $g\equiv0$ are optimal potentials between $\al$ and itself, and find $\varphi$ and $T$.

Answer

Admissible: $0\le\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$. The bill is $0=\Lc(\al,\al)$. Then $\varphi(x)=\frac12\norm{x}^2$, $\varphi^*(y)=\frac12\norm{y}^2$ (self-conjugate), and $T=\nabla\varphi=\Id$: the identity map, as it should.
