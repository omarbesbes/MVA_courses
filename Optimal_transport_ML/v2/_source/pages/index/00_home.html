Optimal transport asks for the cheapest way to move one distribution of mass onto another. This simple question gives a distance between probability distributions that sees the geometry of the space, maps and correspondences between data sets, interpolations and averages of distributions, and the dynamics behind several modern generative models. This guide walks through the eight lessons of the course, one page each.

1. How to use this guide

What a lesson page contains

Every lesson page has the same structure, so that you always know where to look.

  1. Objectives and “Before you start”. What you will be able to do, the story of the lesson in a few steps, what you need from earlier lessons (with folded refreshers), and a notation decoder.
  2. The sections of the lecture, in order. Each one starts with the problem it solves, quotes the course, decodes the quote symbol by symbol, tries it on a small example with numbers, gives the idea of the proof, and then the full proof (folded).
  3. The exercise sheet of the lesson, fully solved: each exercise is quoted, then a hint, then a step-by-step solution. Then more exercises, graded from ★ to ★★★.
  4. The wrap-up: the lesson on one screen, a formula sheet, the reflexes to acquire, the machine-learning applications in one table, what comes next, a glossary English–French, and notes on the sources.

The boxes

Coloured boxes tell you what kind of text you are reading. The most frequent ones:

“When you see … → think …”.

Proofs, hints and solutions are folded: click to open them, or use the button “Open all proofs” at the top of each page. Interactive figures have sliders, buttons or points to drag, and the text below each one says what to look at. The button “Theme” switches between light and dark. The pages work offline: formulas, fonts and figures are all inside each file.

A study routine that works

  • Before the lecture: read “Before you start” and the story of the lesson (ten minutes). You will recognize the ideas when they come.
  • After the lecture: go through the sections in order. Do the quick checks (“Quick check”) without opening the answer first; open a proof only after reading its idea.
  • Every week: do the exercise sheet with the hints, and compare your solution with the one on the page.
  • When a prerequisite is unclear: follow the “Reminder” link. The Reminders page recalls each tool from scratch, with small examples.
  • Before the exam: the wrap-up of each lesson (formula sheet and reflexes), then the extra exercises.

2. The course in eight lessons

From maps to plans, from plans to a distance, from the distance to its dual, its cheap one-dimensional version, its dynamics, its fast regularized version, and finally to evolutions of distributions that minimize an energy.

Lesson 1 — Monge: transport maps

Match points at minimal cost (the assignment problem); on the line, sorting is optimal; Monge’s problem with maps $T$ such that $\pf{T}{\al}=\be$, and why it can fail; Brenier’s theorem: the optimal map is the gradient of a convex function; Gaussians and the Bures distance.

Open Lesson 1 →

Lesson 2 — Kantorovich: transport plans

Let mass split: couplings, the transport polytope and the linear program; the north-west corner rule on the line; Birkhoff–von Neumann (for equal weights, a permutation is optimal); when plans and maps give the same value.

Open Lesson 2 →

Lesson 3 — Wasserstein distances

$W_p$ is a distance (gluing); weak convergence and total variation; displacement interpolation and geodesics; Wasserstein barycenters (Gaussians, the line, a fixed-point algorithm); the continuous Kantorovich problem.

Open Lesson 3 →

Lesson 4 — Duality

The dual linear program and its potentials; complementary slackness; $c$-transforms; Brenier’s theorem from duality; $W_1$ and Lipschitz functions (Kantorovich–Rubinstein), the critic of Wasserstein GANs.

Open Lesson 4 →

Lesson 5 — Sliced optimal transport

Project on directions, solve 1D problems by sorting, average: the sliced Wasserstein distance; its properties, its statistics (no curse of dimensionality) and its computation by Monte Carlo; its uses as a loss.

Open Lesson 5 →

Lesson 6 — Dynamic OT and flow matching

Transport as a motion: the continuity equation and the Benamou–Brenier formula; velocity fields and flows; flow matching and rectified flows, the bridge to generative modelling.

Open Lesson 6 →

Lesson 7 — Entropic OT and Sinkhorn

Add entropy to the linear program: a smooth, strictly convex problem solved by Sinkhorn’s matrix scaling; convergence, the effect of $\eps$, the Sinkhorn divergence, and differentiable transport losses.

Open Lesson 7 →

Lesson 8 — Wasserstein gradient flows

Steepest descent of an energy in the space of distributions: the JKO scheme, the heat and Fokker–Planck equations as gradient flows, diffusion models, and the training dynamics of wide two-layer networks.

Open Lesson 8 →

3. Optimal transport in machine learning: a map

Optimal transport gives machine learning three things: a way to compare distributions that sees geometry (a loss), correspondences between data sets (a plan or a map), and ways to move samples (interpolations, flows).

Each lesson has “In machine learning” boxes, and its wrap-up lists its applications in one table. Here is the overall map.

Task in MLWhat optimal transport providesLessons
Generative modelling: train a generatora loss between generated and real samples that has gradients even when the supports are disjoint ($W_1$ and its dual critic, sliced and Sinkhorn losses)3, 4, 5, 7
Generative modelling: flows and diffusionpaths of distributions from noise to data (displacement interpolation, Benamou–Brenier, flow matching, gradient flows)3, 6, 8
Evaluating generators (FID)$W_2$ between Gaussians fitted to network features1, 3
Domain adaptation, colour transfera plan or a map that moves one data set onto another while moving it as little as possible1, 2, 3, 7
Matching and correspondences (point clouds, graphs, object detection)optimal assignments and permutations (Hungarian algorithm, linear programming)1, 2, 3
Comparing documents, histograms, point cloudsWasserstein distances with a meaningful ground cost (word mover’s distance), fast versions on the line, on trees and by slicing2, 3, 5
Averaging distributions (images, shapes, fair scores)Wasserstein barycenters, exact or entropic3, 7
Clustering and quantization ($k$-means)the best approximation of a distribution by a few points, Lloyd’s algorithm, semi-discrete transport3, 4
Learning with many distances, large dataSinkhorn’s algorithm on GPUs, differentiable transport losses, the sample complexity of each variant3, 5, 7

5. Notation used on every page

SymbolMeaning
$\al,\be$; $\ga$probability measures: the source and the target; a third one
$\X,\Y$; $c(x,y)$; $d(x,y)$the spaces where the mass lives; the cost of moving a unit of mass from $x$ to $y$; a distance
$\delta_x$; $\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i}$a Dirac mass at $x$; a discrete measure with weights $a_i$ (a histogram)
$\pf{T}{\al}$the push-forward of $\al$ by the map $T$: the law of $T(X)$ when $X\sim\al$
$\U(\al,\be)$; $\pi$ or $P$the couplings (transport plans) of $\al$ and $\be$; a plan
$\Lc(\al,\be)$; $W_p(\al,\be)$the optimal transport cost for the cost $c$; the Wasserstein distance of order $p$
$C$; $\ps{P}{C}$a cost matrix; the cost $\sum_{ij}P_{ij}C_{ij}$ of the plan $P$
$C_\al$, $C_\al^{-1}$the CDF and the quantile function of a measure on the line
$\N(m,\Sigma)$; $\B(\Sigma,\Lambda)$a Gaussian; the Bures distance between covariance matrices
$f,g$; $\eps$dual potentials (Lessons 4 and 7); the entropic regularization (Lesson 7)

When a lesson’s sources use another notation, the notation decoder at the top of that lesson says so.
