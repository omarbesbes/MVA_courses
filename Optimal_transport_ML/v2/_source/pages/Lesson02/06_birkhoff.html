6. Birkhoff–von Neumann: Monge = Kantorovich for uniform weights

The corners of the set of bistochastic matrices are exactly the permutation matrices; so for two data sets of the same size with equal weights, the linear program has a permutation among its solutions — the relaxation loses nothing, and the assignment problem is solved by linear programming.

The theorem

In the course OT4ML Thm. 3.17, p. 57

Also in the compact notes, Thm. 3.15, p. 9, and in StatOT §1.1.3, p. 20.

Theorem 3.17 (Birkhoff–von Neumann). The extreme points of $\mathcal B_n$ are exactly the permutation matrices.

$\mathcal B_n$
Non-negative $n\times n$ matrices whose rows and columns all sum to $1$ (§3).
“exactly”
Two statements: every permutation matrix is a corner of $\mathcal B_n$; and every other bistochastic matrix is the midpoint of two different bistochastic matrices.
Idea of the proof

A permutation matrix is a corner because its entries are $0$ or $1$, the corners of $[0,1]$. Conversely, a bistochastic matrix with a fractional entry has a whole cycle of fractional entries (a row that contains one fractional entry must contain another one to reach the sum $1$), and moving $\pm\frac\eps2$ alternately around the cycle splits it into two bistochastic matrices — the same mechanism as in §5.

Full proof (OT4ML pp. 57–58)

Permutation matrices are extreme.

  1. Let $P_\sigma=\frac12(Q+R)$ with $Q,R\in\mathcal B_n$. Every entry of a bistochastic matrix lies in $[0,1]$.Non-negative entries in a row summing to $1$.
  2. If $(P_\sigma)_{ij}=0$ then $Q_{ij}=R_{ij}=0$; if $(P_\sigma)_{ij}=1$ then $Q_{ij}=R_{ij}=1$. So $Q=R=P_\sigma$.The only way to write $0$ (resp. $1$) as the average of two numbers of $[0,1]$ is $0=\frac{0+0}2$ (resp. $1=\frac{1+1}2$): $0$ and $1$ are the extreme points of $[0,1]$.

Other bistochastic matrices are not extreme. Let $P\in\mathcal B_n$ be a non-permutation matrix. A bistochastic matrix with entries in $\{0,1\}$ is a permutation matrix, so $P$ has a fractional entry $0

  1. Consider the bipartite graph whose edges are the fractional entries. Every vertex touched by a fractional edge touches at least two.If row $i$ contains $P_{ij_1}\in(0,1)$, the rest $1-P_{ij_1}>0$ of the row sum is carried by other entries, none of which can be $1$ (that would exceed the sum); so another fractional entry exists in row $i$. Same for columns.
  2. Walk along fractional edges without immediately going back: you never get stuck, and since the graph is finite you eventually revisit a vertex. Take a shortest cycle $(i_1,j_1,i_2,j_2,\dots,i_p,j_p)$, $i_{p+1}=i_1$: its $i$’s are distinct, its $j$’s are distinct, and the edges $(i_s,j_s)$, $(i_{s+1},j_s)$ are all fractional.Every vertex has degree $\ge2$ in this graph (step 1).
  3. Let $\eps=\min_s\min\big(P_{i_s,j_s},P_{i_{s+1},j_s},1-P_{i_s,j_s},1-P_{i_{s+1},j_s}\big)>0$, $A=\{(i_s,j_s)\}$, $B=\{(i_{s+1},j_s)\}$. Define $Q=P+\frac\eps2$ on $A$, $-\frac\eps2$ on $B$; $R=P-\frac\eps2$ on $A$, $+\frac\eps2$ on $B$; both equal to $P$ elsewhere.All the numbers in the minimum are positive because the entries are fractional.
  4. $Q,R\in\mathcal B_n$, $Q\ne R$ and $P=\frac12(Q+R)$: $P$ is not extreme.Modified entries stay in $[0,1]$ by the choice of $\eps$; each row $i_s$ and each column $j_s$ of the cycle has one edge in $A$ and one in $B$, so its sum is unchanged.
Worked example — splitting a $3\times3$ bistochastic matrix

$P=\begin{pmatrix}0.6&0.4&0\\0.4&0.3&0.3\\0&0.3&0.7\end{pmatrix}$ (rows and columns sum to $1$). All its non-zero entries are fractional. A shortest cycle: row 1 → column 1 → row 2 → column 2 → row 1, through the entries $0.6,0.4,0.3,0.4$. Then $\eps=\min(0.6,0.4,0.3,0.4,\,0.4,0.6,0.7,0.6)=0.3$, $A=\{(1,1),(2,2)\}$, $B=\{(2,1),(1,2)\}$, and

$$Q=\begin{pmatrix}0.75&0.25&0\\0.25&0.45&0.3\\0&0.3&0.7\end{pmatrix},\qquad R=\begin{pmatrix}0.45&0.55&0\\0.55&0.15&0.3\\0&0.3&0.7\end{pmatrix},\qquad P=\tfrac12(Q+R).$$

Both are bistochastic (checked), so $P$ is not a corner of $\mathcal B_3$.

Monge = Kantorovich for uniform weights

In the course OT4ML Cor. 3.18, Rem. 3.19, p. 58

Also CompOT Prop. 2.1, p. 19.

Corollary 3.18 (Kantorovich for matching). If $m=n$ and $a=b=\ind_n/n$, then the discrete Kantorovich problem (3.2) admits an optimal solution of the form $P_\sigma/n$. The associated permutation $\sigma$ solves the assignment problem of Section 1.1.

Equivalently, for uniform empirical measures, one can always choose a permutation matrix among the minimizers of the relaxed Kantorovich problem: the relaxation is tight for assignment problems.

Remark 3.19 (General discrete case). For general input measures, one does not have equivalence between Monge and Kantorovich problems, since the Monge constraint can be empty. In finite dimension, however, the support of an optimal coupling still enjoys strong sparsity: one can choose an optimal basic feasible plan whose bipartite support is cycle-free, hence with at most $n+m-1$ nonzero entries.

  1. The feasible set is $\U(\ind_n/n,\ind_n/n)=\mathcal B_n/n$, a non-empty compact convex set.Definition 3.12.
  2. The linear cost $P\mapsto\ps{C}{P}$ has an extreme minimiser on it.Prop. 3.16.
  3. The extreme points of $\mathcal B_n/n$ are the $P_\sigma/n$, so this minimiser is $P_{\sigma^\star}/n$ for some $\sigma^\star$.Scaling by $\frac1n$ maps extreme points to extreme points; Theorem 3.17.
  4. Its cost is $\frac1n\sum_iC_{i,\sigma^\star(i)}\ge\min_\sigma\frac1n\sum_iC_{i,\sigma(i)}$; and conversely every $P_\sigma/n$ is feasible. So $\mathrm L_C=\min_\sigma\frac1n\sum_iC_{i,\sigma(i)}$ and $\sigma^\star$ is an optimal assignment.The relaxed inequality of §3 gives the other direction.

On the $3\times3$ example of §3, the LP value $\frac53$ is the best assignment $[2,1,3]$ divided by $3$ — now we know why. Note what Cor. 3.18 does not say: other optimal plans may be fractional when several permutations tie (any convex combination of optimal permutations is optimal), and nothing like it holds for non-uniform weights, where the Monge set can be empty (Remark 3.19).

In machine learning — matching predictions to targets in object detection (DETR)

DETR (Carion, Massa, Synnaeve, Usunier, Kirillov, Zagoruyko, End-to-end object detection with transformers, ECCV 2020) predicts a fixed set of $N$ boxes with class probabilities ($N=100$ in the paper). An image contains $k\le N$ objects; the ground truth is padded with $N-k$ “no object” labels. To compute the training loss one must decide which prediction answers for which object: DETR solves the assignment problem $\min_\sigma\sum_i\mathcal L_{\text{match}}(y_i,\hat y_{\sigma(i)})$, where the matching cost rewards a high predicted probability of the right class and penalises box disagreement (an $\ell^1$ term and a generalized-IoU term), with the Hungarian algorithm; the loss is then computed on the matched pairs. This removes duplicate detections without hand-made post-processing. By Cor. 3.18 the matching is exactly the uniform Kantorovich problem between the two sets — an LP whose optimum is a permutation; the Hungarian method of Lesson 1 is a specialised LP solver (a dual method, CompOT §3.6).

Decomposing a bistochastic matrix into permutations

In the course OT4ML Cor. 3.20 and Alg. 3.2, pp. 58–59

Corollary 3.20 (Birkhoff–von Neumann decomposition). Every $P\in\mathcal B_n$ admits a representation $P=\sum_{r=1}^L\lambda_rP_{\sigma_r}$, $\lambda_r>0$, $\sum_{r=1}^L\lambda_r=1$, $L\le(n-1)^2+1$, where each $P_{\sigma_r}$ is a permutation matrix.

Hall’s theorem states that a finite bipartite graph admits a matching covering all row vertices if and only if every row subset $I$ has at least $\abs I$ neighbors. To apply it, let $R\ge0$ have every row and column sum equal to the same number $s>0$, and let $G_R$ be its support graph. If $N(I)$ denotes the neighbor set of $I$, then $s\abs I=\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{j\in N(I)}R_{i,j}\le\sum_{j\in N(I)}\sum_iR_{i,j}=s\abs{N(I)}$. Thus $\abs{N(I)}\ge\abs I$, so $G_R$ contains a perfect matching.

Algorithm 3.2 (Birkhoff–von Neumann decomposition). Input: $P\in\mathcal B_n$. Initialize $R\leftarrow P$, $s\leftarrow1$, an empty list $\mathcal D$. While $s>0$: form the support graph $G_R$; [find a perfect matching by successive augmenting paths — the book details this loop]; set $\sigma(i)=j$ for every matched $(i,j)$ and $\lambda\leftarrow\min_iR_{i,\sigma(i)}$; append $(\lambda,\sigma)$ to $\mathcal D$, set $R\leftarrow R-\lambda P_\sigma$ and $s\leftarrow s-\lambda$. Return $\mathcal D$, so that $P=\sum_{(\lambda,\sigma)\in\mathcal D}\lambda P_\sigma$.

convex combination
“$P$ is a mixture of permutations”: with probability $\lambda_r$, use the permutation $\sigma_r$. Equivalently $\mathcal B_n$ is the convex hull of the $n!$ permutation matrices.
$(n-1)^2+1$
$\mathcal B_n$ has dimension $n^2-(2n-1)=(n-1)^2$ (§5 count with $n=m$), and Carathéodory’s theorem (reminder below) needs at most dimension $+1$ points. Much smaller than $n!$: $5$ terms for $n=3$, $82$ for $n=10$.
$N(I)$
The columns in which the rows of $I$ have a positive entry.
Hall’s condition
“No group of rows is confined to fewer columns than its size.” The computation shows it holds for any matrix with equal row and column sums: the mass $s\abs I$ of the rows of $I$ must fit in the columns of $N(I)$, which can hold at most $s\abs{N(I)}$.
$\lambda=\min_iR_{i,\sigma(i)}$
The largest multiple of $P_\sigma$ that can be removed without creating negative entries; at least one entry of $R$ becomes $0$, so the loop ends.
Reminder — Carathéodory’s theorem

In an affine space of dimension $d$, every point of the convex hull of a set $S$ is a convex combination of at most $d+1$ points of $S$. (If more points are used, their differences are linearly dependent, and moving along a dependence removes one of them while keeping all weights $\ge0$ — the same trick as the cycles of §5.) More.

Worked example — Algorithm 3.2 on the $3\times3$ matrix
  1. The support of $P$ contains the perfect matching $\sigma=[2,1,3]$ (entries $0.4,0.4,0.7$): $\lambda=0.4$, and $R=P-0.4P_\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}0.6&0&0\\0&0.3&0.3\\0&0.3&0.3\end{pmatrix}$, row sums $0.6$.This choice takes the matching with the largest minimum; any perfect matching would do.
  2. Next matching $[1,2,3]$ (entries $0.6,0.3,0.3$): $\lambda=0.3$, $R=\begin{pmatrix}0.3&0&0\\0&0&0.3\\0&0.3&0\end{pmatrix}$.$\min(0.6,0.3,0.3)$.
  3. Last matching $[1,3,2]$: $\lambda=0.3$, $R=0$. Weights $0.4+0.3+0.3=1$.$R/0.3$ is itself a permutation matrix.

Three terms, below the bound $(3-1)^2+1=5$. Starting with the identity instead gives the same three permutations in another order. For a generic bistochastic matrix the bound is attained: in our tests with random dense matrices, the greedy algorithm always used exactly $5$ permutations for $n=3$ and $10$ for $n=4$ (Exercise 13.12 explains why).

Rational weights: back to an assignment

In the course OT4ML Prop. 3.21, p. 60

Also in the compact notes, Prop. 3.18, p. 10.

Proposition 3.21 (Rational weights as duplicated uniform matching). Let $\al=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{k_i}N\delta_{x_i}$, $\be=\sum_{j=1}^m\frac{\ell_j}N\delta_{y_j}$, $\sum_ik_i=\sum_j\ell_j=N$, with positive integers $k_i,\ell_j$. Replace each $x_i$ by $k_i$ identical copies and each $y_j$ by $\ell_j$ identical copies, producing two uniform $N$-point clouds. Then the duplicated assignment problem and the discrete Kantorovich problem between $\al$ and $\be$ have the same optimal value. Moreover, there exists an optimal coupling of the form $P_{i,j}=\frac{n_{i,j}}N$, $n_{i,j}\in\N$, $\sum_jn_{i,j}=k_i$, $\sum_in_{i,j}=\ell_j$ […]. Fractional optimal couplings may nevertheless coexist when the optimum is degenerate.

Why. An assignment between the copies collapses to integer counts $n_{ij}$ (how many copies of $x_i$ are matched to copies of $y_j$), which form a coupling $n_{ij}/N$ with the same cost: so Kantorovich $\le$ assignment. Conversely, the polytope $\{Q\ge0:\ Q\ind=k,\ Q\T\ind=\ell\}$ has integer vertices (its constraint matrix is the incidence matrix of a bipartite graph, which is totally unimodular: every square submatrix has determinant $0$, $1$ or $-1$, so by Cramer’s rule a vertex, which solves a square system with integer right-hand side, has integer entries), so an integer optimal $Q=NP$ exists — it is a collapsed assignment. Birkhoff–von Neumann is the special case $k=\ell=\ind$. This is how the widgets of this page compute exact optima: they duplicate the atoms and run the Hungarian method.

Worked example

$\al=\frac23\delta_0+\frac13\delta_3$ and $\be=\frac13(\delta_0+\delta_1+\delta_2)$, cost $\abs{x-y}^2$, $N=3$. Duplicate: sources $\{0,0,3\}$, targets $\{0,1,2\}$, sort and match: $0\to0$, $0\to1$, $3\to2$, average cost $\frac{0+1+1}3=\frac23$. Collapsed: $3P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$ — the atom at $0$ splits its mass $\frac23$ into $\frac13+\frac13$. An LP solver gives the same value $\frac23$.

In machine learning — matching point clouds and shapes

A 3D shape is often represented by a point cloud (say $2048$ points sampled on its surface). To train an autoencoder or a generative model of shapes, one needs a loss between the input cloud and the reconstructed cloud that does not depend on the order of the points. Achlioptas, Diamanti, Mitliagkas and Guibas (Learning representations and generative models for 3D point clouds, ICML 2018) use, among others, the Earth Mover’s distance $\min_\phi\sum_x\norm{x-\phi(x)}$ over bijections $\phi$ between the two clouds — an assignment problem, i.e. by Cor. 3.18 the Kantorovich problem with uniform weights; because the exact $O(n^3)$ solution is too slow inside a training loop, they rely on a fast approximation. With non-uniform weights (points weighted by the area they represent on a mesh), the same comparison is a genuine Kantorovich problem, which sparse plans (§5) turn into a near one-to-one correspondence between the two shapes.

Pitfall — “bistochastic” versus “coupling of uniform weights”

$P\in\mathcal B_n$ has row sums $1$; the coupling is $P/n$, with row sums $\frac1n$. Forgetting the factor $\frac1n$ multiplies all costs by $n$. (The sheet’s Exercise 5 uses exactly this rescaling: $Q:=nP$.)

Reflexes
  • Two data sets of the same size, equal weights → the optimal plan can be taken to be a permutation (Birkhoff–von Neumann); solve with the Hungarian method or any LP solver.
  • A bistochastic matrix → a mixture of at most $(n-1)^2+1$ permutations; peel them off one perfect matching at a time.
  • Rational weights $k_i/N$ → duplicate the atoms and solve an $N\times N$ assignment.

Find all optimal plans for $n=m=2$, uniform weights, $C=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$; then for $C=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$.

Answer

Couplings: $P(s)=\begin{pmatrix}s&\frac12-s\\\frac12-s&s\end{pmatrix}$, $s\in[0,\frac12]$. First cost: $\ps{C}{P(s)}=1-2s$, unique optimum $s=\frac12$, the identity permutation (divided by $2$). Second cost: $\ps{C}{P(s)}=1$ for all $s$: every coupling is optimal, including the fractional ones — Birkhoff–von Neumann guarantees a permutation among the optima, not that all optima are permutations.
