9. When the relaxation loses nothing: Monge = Kantorovich

Kantorovich’s value is never larger than Monge’s; they are equal — and an optimal plan is a map — for uniform discrete weights (Birkhoff–von Neumann), for the quadratic cost from a density (Brenier), on the line from an atomless source; with atoms, the two problems genuinely differ.

Quadratic cost, source with a density

In the course OT4ML Cor. 3.36, Rem. 3.37–3.38, pp. 67–68

Also in the compact notes, Cor. 3.27, p. 12.

The proof of Brenier’s theorem 2.23 relies on Kantorovich relaxation and duality. It proves that, under its hypotheses, the relaxation is tight: it has the same cost as the Monge problem and its optimal coupling is induced by a map.

Corollary 3.36 (Monge–Kantorovich equivalence under Brenier). Let $\al,\be\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$, assume that $\al$ is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure, and let $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2$. If $T$ is the Brenier map solving Monge’s problem, then $\pi=\pf{(\Id,T)}{\al}$ is the unique optimal coupling solving the Kantorovich problem. In particular, Monge and Kantorovich costs are the same.

Remark 3.37 (Nonsmooth potentials and splitting). If $\al$ does not have a density, then $\varphi$ may be non-smooth on a set charged by $\al$, and non-smooth points can lead to mass splitting. For instance, moving $\delta_0$ to $(\delta_{-1}+\delta_{+1})/2$ can be represented by a plan concentrated on the set-valued subdifferential of $\varphi(x)=\abs x$, but not by a deterministic map. […]

Remark 3.38 (Probabilistic form of tightness). If $(X,Y)$ has the optimal Kantorovich law under the assumptions of Corollary 3.36, then $Y=T(X)$ almost surely with $X\sim\al$ and $T(X)\sim\be$. This is analogous to the Birkhoff–von Neumann result in the fully discrete uniform case […]. The hypotheses are quite different, however: Birkhoff–von Neumann is finite-dimensional and need not give uniqueness, whereas Brenier’s theorem uses absolute continuity of the source and gives uniqueness of the optimal map almost everywhere.

$\Pc_2(\R^d)$
Probability measures with a finite second moment $\int\norm x^2\dd\al<\infty$ (so that all the costs are finite).
“the unique optimal coupling”
Not only is there an optimal plan that is a map: every optimal plan is this one.
subdifferential $\partial\varphi(x)$
The set of slopes of the supporting lines of the convex function $\varphi$ at $x$. Where $\varphi$ is differentiable it is $\{\nabla\varphi(x)\}$; at a kink it is a whole set ($\partial\abs\cdot(0)=[-1,1]$), and mass may split among it.
“tight relaxation”
Here “tight” means “loses nothing”: same value, solution of the original form.
Idea of the proof (completed in Lesson 4)

Kantorovich duality (Lesson 4) shows that every optimal plan is concentrated on the graph of the subdifferential $\partial\varphi$ of one convex function $\varphi$. A convex function is differentiable almost everywhere (for Lebesgue measure), hence $\al$-almost everywhere when $\al$ has a density: there $\partial\varphi(x)=\{\nabla\varphi(x)\}$, so the plan sends $x$ to the single point $\nabla\varphi(x)$, i.e. $\pi=\pf{(\Id,\nabla\varphi)}{\al}$. Without a density, $\al$ can sit on kinks of $\varphi$, where the subdifferential is a set: that is where mass splits ($\delta_0$ sits on the kink of $\abs x$).

Atomless sources: the same value, even without an optimal map

In the course OT4ML Prop. 4.15, p. 79; Example 2.18, p. 26

Proposition 4.15 (Kantorovich as the plan-space relaxation of Monge). Let $(\X,d)$ be a compact metric space, let $p\ge1$, and let $\al,\be\in\Pc(\X)$ with $\al$ atomless. Then $\tilde{\mathcal W}_p(\al,\be)=\mathcal W_p(\al,\be)$. This is an equality of infimal values: the Kantorovich minimum is attained, whereas the infimum defining $\tilde{\mathcal W}_p$ in (2.6) need not be attained by a transport map.

Example 2.18 (A splitting obstruction). Let $\al$ be uniform on $\{0\}\times[0,1]$ and let $\be$ be the average of the uniform laws on $\{-1\}\times[0,1]$ and $\{1\}\times[0,1]$. For the squared Euclidean cost, the relaxed plan that sends $(0,t)$ equally to $(-1,t)$ and $(1,t)$ has cost $1$, which is the smallest possible value […]. No deterministic map can attain this value. […] Feasible maps nevertheless approach cost $1$: partition $[0,1]$ into $n$ equal cells, send the lower half of each cell affinely onto the corresponding full cell on the left segment, and send the upper half similarly to the right segment. The vertical displacement is at most $1/(2n)$, so the costs converge to $1$. Thus the Monge infimum equals the relaxed value but is not attained.

$\tilde{\mathcal W}_p$
The Monge (map) version of the transport cost, $\big(\inf_{\pf{T}{\al}=\be}\int d(x,T(x))^p\,\dd\al\big)^{1/p}$; $\mathcal W_p$ is the Kantorovich version (Lesson 3).
atomless
$\al(\{x\})=0$ for every point: no Diracs (densities, uniform laws on segments…).
“equality of infimal values”
Couplings can be approximated by maps (the proof cuts space into small cells and fills them with maps, which is possible without atoms), so the two infima agree — but the limit coupling need not be a map.
Worked example — the cost of the strip maps of Example 2.18

In the $n$-strip map, a point $(0,t)$ of the lower half of a strip moves horizontally by $1$ and vertically by $u/(2n)$ with $u$ uniform on $[0,1]$ (and similarly in the upper half). Its cost is $1+\int_0^1\big(\frac{u}{2n}\big)^2\dd u=1+\frac1{12n^2}$: $1.0833$ for $n=1$, $1.0208$ for $n=2$, $1.0008$ for $n=10$ (we also checked $n=2$ by numerical integration). The infimum $1$ is the Kantorovich value, attained only by the splitting plan.

With atoms, the two problems differ

Without atoms, the values agree; with atoms, maps may exist and still be much worse than plans.

Worked example — all the maps, and the best coupling
  1. A map sends each of the three atoms to $-1$ or $+1$, and the masses arriving at $\pm1$ must be $\frac12$ each. The middle atom (mass $\frac12$) fills one side alone, so the two outer atoms (mass $\frac14$ each) must go together to the other side.The only subsets of $\{\frac14,\frac12,\frac14\}$ summing to $\frac12$ are $\{\frac12\}$ and $\{\frac14,\frac14\}$.
  2. Two feasible maps: $0\mapsto1$ and $\pm1\mapsto-1$, cost $\frac12\cdot1+\frac14\cdot0+\frac14\cdot4=1.5$; or the mirror image, same cost. So $\mathcal M_c(\al,\be)=1.5$.Brute force over the $2^3=8$ maps.
  3. The coupling $P=\begin{pmatrix}\frac14&0\\\frac14&\frac14\\0&\frac14\end{pmatrix}$ (rows $-1,0,1$; columns $-1,1$) costs $\frac14\cdot1+\frac14\cdot1=0.5$, and an exact LP confirms it is optimal: $\mathrm L_C(a,b)=0.5$.Lower bound: the mass $\frac12$ at $0$ must move by at least $1$ wherever it goes.

Monge’s value is three times Kantorovich’s. Now spread the middle atom into a small uniform piece, $\frac12\mathcal U[-\eps,\eps]$ instead of $\frac12\delta_0$. A map can split that piece: send its left half to $-1$ and its right half to $+1$. This map is optimal (it is the quantile coupling of Thm. 3.39 below), and both values equal $\frac12-\frac\eps2+\frac{\eps^2}6$, which tends to $0.5$ (checked with an exact solver for $\eps=0.2,\,0.05,\,0.01$). So the Monge value tends to $0.5$ as $\eps\to0$ but equals $1.5$ at $\eps=0$: it is not a continuous function of the measures, whereas the Kantorovich value is.

The line, with atoms allowed

In the course OT4ML Thm. 3.39, p. 68

Also in the compact notes, Thm. 3.28, p. 12.

The atomless assumption in the Monge statement of Section 2.6 is a limitation of maps, not of one-dimensional optimality. Once couplings are allowed, atoms can be split by assigning subintervals of quantile levels to different target points.

Theorem 3.39 (One-dimensional Kantorovich solution). Let $\al,\be\in\M(\R)$ and let $h:\R\to[0,+\infty)$ be convex. Consider the cost $c(x,y)=h(x-y)$. Write $q_\al:=C_\al^{-1}$ and $q_\be:=C_\be^{-1}$, and assume that $h(q_\al-q_\be)\in L^1(0,1)$. Then the quantile coupling

$$\pi^\star=\pf{(q_\al,q_\be)}{\mathrm{Leb}_{[0,1]}}=\pf{(C_\al^{-1},C_\be^{-1})}{\mathrm{Leb}_{[0,1]}}$$

solves the Kantorovich problem (3.7) for $c(x,y)=h(x-y)$. In particular, $h(t)=\abs t^p$ gives the usual one-dimensional optimal coupling for every $p\ge1$.

$\pf{(q_\al,q_\be)}{\mathrm{Leb}_{[0,1]}}$
Draw a level $U$ uniformly in $[0,1]$ and output the pair $(C_\al^{-1}(U),C_\be^{-1}(U))$: equal quantile levels are coupled.
atoms
An atom of mass $m$ of $\al$ occupies an interval of levels of length $m$; its levels are sent to all the points of $\be$ at those levels — the atom is split. This is the continuous version of the north-west rule (§4, remark on quantile intervals).
value
$\Lc(\al,\be)=\int_0^1h\big(C_\al^{-1}(r)-C_\be^{-1}(r)\big)\dd r$, now for all measures on the line.
Idea of the proof

On two sorted finite grids this is Prop. 3.10: uncross until the plan is the north-west one. For general measures, approximate the quantile functions by step functions (finite grids), apply the discrete result, and pass to the limit; the hypothesis $h(q_\al-q_\be)\in L^1$ makes dominated convergence work. (Full proof: OT4ML p. 68.)

Worked example

With $c=\abs{x-y}^2$: $\Lc(\al,\be)=\int_0^{1/2}(0-2r)^2\,\dd r+\int_{1/2}^1(2r-2r)^2\,\dd r=\frac{4}{3}\cdot\frac18=\frac16$. Discretising both measures ($200$ cells each, then $400$) and solving exactly with POT gives $0.166666$ both times. No map from $\al$ achieves this (the atom would go to one point); since $\al$ has an atom, Thm. 2.41 does not apply but Thm. 3.39 does.

Pitfall — “a map exists” is not “Monge = Kantorovich”

In the atoms example, feasible maps exist and the best one costs three times the optimal plan. Equality of the values needs hypotheses on the source (atomless, or a density for uniqueness), not just a non-empty set of maps (see the notes on the sources about the 2019 notes).

Between $\al=\frac12(\delta_0+\delta_2)$ and $\be=\mathcal U[0,2]$ (cost $\abs{x-y}^2$), is there an optimal map? What is the optimal plan, and its cost?

Answer

No map: $\pf{T}{\al}$ is carried by at most two points, never uniform. Quantile coupling: levels $r\le\frac12$ go from $0$ to $2r\in[0,1]$, levels $r>\frac12$ from $2$ to $2r\in[1,2]$: each atom is spread uniformly on the half-interval next to it. Cost $\int_0^{1/2}(2r)^2\dd r+\int_{1/2}^1(2-2r)^2\dd r=\frac16+\frac16=\frac13$. (In the other direction, from $\be$ to $\al$, the map $y\mapsto0$ on $[0,1)$, $y\mapsto2$ on $[1,2]$ is optimal, with the same cost: the source $\be$ has a density.)
