1. Recap of Lesson 1: Monge’s problem, the line, Gaussians, Monge–Ampère

Monge moves each point to one point; this works beautifully on the line (sort), for Gaussians (an affine map) and for densities with the quadratic cost (a convex gradient, solution of the Monge–Ampère equation) — but a map can never split an atom, which is what Kantorovich will fix.

The lecture started with a recap of Lesson 1. It is short here: the statements, what they mean, one example each, and the point of the recap — what Monge’s formulation cannot do.

Monge’s problem and its weak points

In the course OT4ML Def. 2.14, p. 25

Definition 2.14 (Monge problem and Monge map). For $\al\in\M(\X)$, $\be\in\M(\Y)$ and measurable $c:\X\times\Y\to[0,+\infty]$, define

$$\mathcal M_c(\al,\be):=\inf_{T:\X\to\Y\text{ measurable}}\Big\{\int_\X c(x,T(x))\,\dd\al(x)\ ;\ \pf{T}{\al}=\be\Big\}.$$

A feasible $T$ is a Monge map; a minimizer is an optimal Monge map. The value is $+\infty$ if no feasible map exists.

$\M(\X)$
Probability measures on $\X$ (positive, total mass $1$).
$T:\X\to\Y$
A deterministic rule: the mass at $x$ goes, all of it, to the single point $T(x)$.
$\pf{T}{\al}=\be$
The constraint: after moving, the mass is distributed exactly as $\be$.
$\int c(x,T(x))\,\dd\al(x)$
The total cost: each unit of mass at $x$ pays $c(x,T(x))$.
“$+\infty$ if no feasible map”
The constraint set can be empty — the first weak point below.

For $n$ points against $n$ points with weights $\frac1n$ (two data sets of the same size), a feasible map is a permutation $\sigma$ (OT4ML Prop. 2.15, p. 26), and Monge’s problem is the assignment problem $\min_\sigma\frac1n\sum_ic(x_i,y_{\sigma(i)})$, solved in Lesson 1 by the Hungarian method.

In the course OT4ML Remark 2.17, p. 26

Remark 2.17 (Feasibility versus optimality). An optimal map solving (2.5) might fail to exist for two distinct reasons. First, the constraint set can be empty, for instance if $\al=\delta_x$ and $\be$ is not a single Dirac mass. […] Second, even when feasible maps exist, the infimum may fail to be attained because the class of maps is not closed under weak limits.

Three weak points of Monge’s formulation, all visible on small discrete examples (OT4ML §3.1, p. 51):

  • It can be empty. $\delta_0\to\frac12\delta_{-1}+\frac12\delta_1$ has no feasible map; nor has a data set of $n$ points (weights $\frac1n$) sent to a data set of $m>n$ points (weights $\frac1m$): the image of $n$ atoms has at most $n$ atoms.
  • It is not symmetric. Merging is allowed, splitting is not.
  • It is not convex. The set of maps with $\pf{T}{\al}=\be$ is not convex (the average of two permutations is not a permutation), so the problem is combinatorial; and even when maps exist the infimum may not be attained (OT4ML Example 2.18, p. 26, revisited in §9).

The line: match equal quantile levels

In the course OT4ML Thm. 2.41 and Prop. 2.44, p. 38

Theorem 2.41 (One-dimensional Monge solution). Let $\al,\be\in\M(\R)$, assume that $\al$ has no atoms, and let $h:\R\to[0,+\infty)$ be convex. Consider the cost $c(x,y)=h(x-y)$. […] Then $T=C_\be^{-1}\circ C_\al$ satisfies $\pf{T}{\al}=\be$ and minimizes $\min_{\pf{S}{\al}=\be}\int h(x-S(x))\,\dd\al(x)$.

Proposition 2.44 (One-dimensional Wasserstein formulas). Let $1\le p<+\infty$ and let $\al,\be\in\M(\R)$ have finite $p$-th moments. Then

$$\mathcal W_p^p(\al,\be)=\int_0^1\abs{C_\al^{-1}(r)-C_\be^{-1}(r)}^p\,\dd r=\norm{C_\al^{-1}-C_\be^{-1}}^p_{L^p([0,1])}.$$
$C_\al(x)=\al((-\infty,x])$
The cumulative distribution function (CDF): the mass to the left of $x$.
$C_\al^{-1}(r)$
The quantile function: the point below which a fraction $r$ of the mass lies.
$T=C_\be^{-1}\circ C_\al$
“Send the point at quantile level $r$ of $\al$ to the point at the same level $r$ of $\be$”: the increasing rearrangement. For two clouds of $n$ points, this is sorting both clouds and matching in order.
“$\al$ has no atoms”
Needed for a map: an atom occupies a whole interval of levels, which may have to go to several places. Section 9 shows that with couplings the atoms are no longer a problem.
Worked example — two uniform laws

$\al=\mathcal U[0,1]$, $\be=\mathcal U[2,4]$: $C_\al^{-1}(r)=r$, $C_\be^{-1}(r)=2+2r$, so $T(x)=2+2x$ (stretch by $2$, shift by $2$) and

$$W_2^2(\al,\be)=\int_0^1(2+2r-r)^2\,\dd r=\int_0^1(2+r)^2\,\dd r=\frac{27-8}{3}=\frac{19}3\approx6.33,\qquad W_1=\int_0^1(2+r)\,\dd r=2.5 .$$

Gaussians in any dimension

In the course OT4ML Def. 2.55 and Prop. 2.56, p. 46

Definition 2.55 (Bures metric). For positive semidefinite covariance matrices $\Sigma$ and $\Lambda$, $\B(\Sigma,\Lambda)^2:=\tr\big(\Sigma+\Lambda-2(\Sigma^{1/2}\Lambda\Sigma^{1/2})^{1/2}\big)$.

Proposition 2.56 (Gaussian $\mathcal W_2$ formula and Bures covariance term). Assume that $\Sigma_\al$ and $\Sigma_\be$ are positive definite. The covariance equation and its unique symmetric positive-definite solution are

$$A\Sigma_\al A=\Sigma_\be,\ A\succ0\quad\iff\quad A=\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}\big(\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}.$$

The affine map $T(x)=m_\be+A(x-m_\al)$ is the optimal quadratic-cost transport from $\N(m_\al,\Sigma_\al)$ to $\N(m_\be,\Sigma_\be)$, and $\mathcal W_2(\al,\be)^2=\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+\B(\Sigma_\al,\Sigma_\be)^2$.

$M^{1/2}$
Matrix square root: diagonalise $M=UDU\T$ ($D\ge0$) and take $UD^{1/2}U\T$. More.
$A\Sigma_\al A=\Sigma_\be$
“$T$ pushes $\N(m_\al,\Sigma_\al)$ to $\N(m_\be,\Sigma_\be)$”: an affine image of a Gaussian is Gaussian, with covariance $A\Sigma_\al A\T$.
$A\succ0$ symmetric
Then $T$ is the gradient of the convex quadratic $\varphi(x)=\ps{m_\be}{x}+\frac12\ps{A(x-m_\al)}{x-m_\al}$, hence optimal by Brenier (below).
two terms
The cost splits into “move the mean” and “reshape the covariance”.
Worked example — a 2D Gaussian

$\al=\N(0,I_2)$ and $\be=\N\big((1,1),\Sigma\big)$ with $\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ (eigenvalues $3$ and $1$, eigenvectors $(1,1)/\sqrt2$ and $(1,-1)/\sqrt2$).

  1. $$A=\Sigma^{1/2}=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt3+1&\sqrt3-1\\\sqrt3-1&\sqrt3+1\end{pmatrix}\approx\begin{pmatrix}1.366&0.366\\0.366&1.366\end{pmatrix}.$$With $\Sigma_\al=I$ the formula reduces to $A=\Sigma_\be^{1/2}$; square roots of the eigenvalues: $\sqrt3$ and $1$.
  2. $$\B(I,\Sigma)^2=\tr\big(I+\Sigma-2\Sigma^{1/2}\big)=2+4-2(\sqrt3+1)=4-2\sqrt3\approx0.536 .$$$\tr I=2$, $\tr\Sigma=4$, $\tr\Sigma^{1/2}=\sqrt3+1$ (sum of the eigenvalues). Note $4-2\sqrt3=(\sqrt3-1)^2$.
  3. $$W_2^2(\al,\be)=\norm{(1,1)}^2+4-2\sqrt3=6-2\sqrt3\approx2.536,\qquad W_2\approx1.592 .$$Mean term $1^2+1^2=2$. (A Monte-Carlo estimate of $\E\norm{X-T(X)}^2$ with $4\times10^5$ samples gives $2.538$.)

Brenier’s theorem and the Monge–Ampère equation

In the course OT4ML Thm. 2.23, p. 29; eq. (2.7), p. 33

Theorem 2.23 (Brenier). Let $\al,\be\in\M(\R^d)$ have finite second moments, and assume that $\al$ is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure. For the quadratic cost $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2$, there exists a convex function $\varphi:\R^d\to\R\cup\{+\infty\}$ such that $T=\nabla\varphi$, $\pf{T}{\al}=\be$, and $T$ is the unique optimal Monge map $\al$-almost everywhere. The optimal Kantorovich plan is $\pf{(\Id,T)}{\al}$.

For sufficiently smooth positive densities and a classical Brenier map, the change-of-variables formula (2.4) gives the Monge–Ampère equation

$$\det\big(\nabla^2\varphi(x)\big)\,\rho_\be\big(\nabla\varphi(x)\big)=\rho_\al(x).\qquad(2.7)$$
“absolutely continuous”
$\al$ has a density $\rho_\al$ (no atoms, no mass on curves or surfaces).
$T=\nabla\varphi$, $\varphi$ convex
The multi-dimensional version of “increasing”: in 1D the derivative of a convex function is non-decreasing, i.e. the sorting map.
“the optimal Kantorovich plan is $\pf{(\Id,T)}{\al}$”
A forward reference to this lesson: the relaxed problem of §8 has the same solution (§9).
$\det(\nabla^2\varphi)$
The Jacobian determinant of $T=\nabla\varphi$: how much $T$ dilates volumes near $x$.
(2.7)
Mass conservation, point by point: the mass $\rho_\al(x)\dd x$ of a small cell around $x$ equals the mass $\rho_\be(T(x))\times\det(\nabla^2\varphi(x))\dd x$ of its image. A fully non-linear PDE for $\varphi$.
Reminder — change of variables

If $T$ is a diffeomorphism and $Y=T(X)$ with $X$ of density $\rho_\al$, then $Y$ has a density $\rho_\be$ with $\rho_\al(x)=\rho_\be(T(x))\,\abs{\det\nabla T(x)}$. This is the identity behind (2.7), with $\nabla T=\nabla^2\varphi$ (symmetric, $\ge0$ by convexity, so the absolute value can be dropped). More.

Worked example — checking Monge–Ampère on the two examples above
  • Line. $T(x)=2+2x$ from $\mathcal U[0,1]$ to $\mathcal U[2,4]$: $T'(x)\,\rho_\be(T(x))=2\times\frac12=1=\rho_\al(x)$. ✓
  • Gaussians. $\varphi(x)=\ps{m_\be}{x}+\frac12\ps{Ax}{x}$, $\nabla^2\varphi=A$, $\det A=\sqrt3$. Since $A\Sigma_\be^{-1}A=I$ (from $A^2=\Sigma_\be$), $\det A\cdot\rho_\be(Ax+m_\be)=\sqrt3\cdot\frac{e^{-\frac12\ps{x}{A\Sigma_\be^{-1}Ax}}}{2\pi\sqrt{\det\Sigma_\be}}=\frac{\sqrt3}{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{e^{-\norm{x}^2/2}}{2\pi}=\rho_\al(x).$ ✓ (Checked numerically at three points.)
The point of the recap

Everything above produces a map, and needs hypotheses for it: no atoms on the line, a density for Brenier. For discrete data — histograms, point clouds with weights, data sets of different sizes — maps may not exist at all. Kantorovich’s idea is to stop asking where each point goes and to ask how much mass goes from where to where. Then (§9) we will see that under exactly the hypotheses of this section, nothing is lost.

Between $\al=\frac13(\delta_0+\delta_1+\delta_2)$ and $\be=\frac12(\delta_0+\delta_2)$, is there a Monge map? And from $\be$ to $\al$?

Answer

From $\al$ to $\be$: a map sends each atom (mass $\frac13$) whole to $0$ or $2$; the mass arriving at $0$ is a multiple of $\frac13$, never $\frac12$. No map. From $\be$ to $\al$: each atom of mass $\frac12$ lands on one point, so $\pf{T}{\be}$ has atoms of mass $\frac12$ or $1$, never $\frac13$. No map either. Both directions need splitting.
