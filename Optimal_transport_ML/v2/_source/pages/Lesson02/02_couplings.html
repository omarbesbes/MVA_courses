2. Kantorovich’s relaxation: couplings and a linear program

Instead of a map, record a matrix $P\ge0$ whose entry $P_{ij}$ is the mass sent from $x_i$ to $y_j$; the only constraints are mass conservation (row sums $a$, column sums $b$), and the cost $\ps{C}{P}$ is linear: transport becomes a linear program.

Couplings: a table of shipments

The problem. We want to compare (and transport) two weighted point clouds $\al=\sum_{i=1}^na_i\delta_{x_i}$ and $\be=\sum_{j=1}^mb_j\delta_{y_j}$ with arbitrary weights and arbitrary sizes $n$, $m$ — two colour histograms, two data sets, two documents. A map cannot do it in general (§1). What can?

In the course OT4ML §3.1 and Def. 3.1, p. 51

Kantorovich’s key idea is to relax the deterministic nature of transportation. Instead of requiring each source point $x_i$ to be sent to exactly one target, the mass at $x_i$ may be dispatched across several locations. […] The relaxation is encoded, in place of a permutation $\sigma$ or a map $T$, by a coupling matrix $P\in\R_+^{n\times m}$, where $P_{i,j}$ describes the amount of mass flowing from $x_i$ to $y_j$.

Definition 3.1 (Discrete couplings and mass conservation). Admissible couplings are only constrained to satisfy conservation of mass:

$$\U(a,b):=\big\{P\in\R_+^{n\times m}\ ;\ P\ind_m=a\ \text{ and }\ P\T\ind_n=b\big\}.\qquad(3.1)$$

Equivalently, $P\ind_m=\big(\sum_jP_{i,j}\big)_i\in\R^n$, $P\T\ind_n=\big(\sum_iP_{i,j}\big)_j\in\R^m$.

$P\in\R_+^{n\times m}$
A table with one row per source and one column per target, with non-negative entries: you cannot ship a negative amount.
$P_{i,j}$
The mass that leaves $x_i$ and arrives at $y_j$. Not a probability of going from $i$ to $j$: that would be $P_{i,j}/a_i$.
$P\ind_m=a$
“Row $i$ sums to $a_i$”: everything at $x_i$ leaves, and no more.
$P\T\ind_n=b$
“Column $j$ sums to $b_j$”: $y_j$ receives exactly its mass.
“only constrained”
Nothing says that row $i$ has a single non-zero entry: mass splitting is allowed. Merging (several non-zero entries in a column) is allowed too.
Intuition — warehouses and factories

Peyré–Cuturi illustrate the problem with an operator who runs $n$ warehouses holding $a_i$ units of raw material and $m$ factories needing $b_j$ units; shipping one unit from $i$ to $j$ costs $C_{i,j}$, linearly in the amount (CompOT Remark 2.10, p. 18 — this is Hitchcock’s 1941 transportation problem). A coupling is a shipping schedule; the row and column sums are the stocks and the demands. Note what the constraint forces: $\sum_ia_i=\sum_i\sum_jP_{ij}=\sum_jb_j$. Both sides must have the same total mass (the sheet’s Exercise 3(c)).

Two remarks of the book make the gain concrete. The product $a\otimes b$ below is always a coupling, so $\U(a,b)$ is never empty. And the formulation is symmetric: $P\in\U(a,b)$ if and only if $P\T\in\U(b,a)$ — swapping roles transposes the table (OT4ML p. 53). Monge’s problem had neither property.

The smallest cases, and the product coupling

In the course OT4ML Remark 3.2, p. 51

Remark 3.2 (Small transportation polytopes). […] If $n=m=1$, mass conservation fixes the only entry, so the feasible set is a singleton. The same happens for $(n,m)=(2,1)$, and by symmetry for $(n,m)=(1,2)$ […]. The first nontrivial case is $(n,m)=(2,2)$. Let $a=(p,1-p)$ and $b=(q,1-q)$ with $p,q\in[0,1]$. Once $s:=P_{1,1}$ is chosen, the marginal constraints force all other entries, hence every coupling has the form

$$P(s)=\begin{pmatrix}s&p-s\\q-s&1-p-q+s\end{pmatrix}.$$

The nonnegativity constraints are exactly $s\in[\max(0,p+q-1),\min(p,q)]$, so $\U(a,b)$ is a segment, possibly reduced to a point at the boundary. In general, when all marginal entries are positive, the transportation polytope has affine dimension $(n-1)(m-1)$ before the nonnegativity inequalities cut out its faces.

Why $P(s)$ has this form. Row 1 sums to $p$, so $P_{1,2}=p-s$; column 1 sums to $q$, so $P_{2,1}=q-s$; then row 2 forces $P_{2,2}=1-p-(q-s)$. Each entry must be $\ge0$: $s\ge0$, $s\le p$, $s\le q$ and $s\ge p+q-1$. Why $(n-1)(m-1)$ in general: fill the first $n-1$ rows and $m-1$ columns freely; the last column and the last row are then forced by the marginals, and the last corner entry is forced twice — consistently, because both $a$ and $b$ sum to $1$. So $nm$ entries, $n+m$ equations, but only $n+m-1$ independent ones: $(n-1)(m-1)$ free parameters (proved in §5). For $2\times3$ that is a polygon (the widget of §5), for $10\times10$ already $81$ dimensions.

Worked example — a $2\times2$ problem, solved by drawing a segment

Sources $x=(0,2)$ with $a=(0.5,0.5)$; targets $y=(1,3)$ with $b=(0.3,0.7)$; cost $c(x,y)=\abs{x-y}^2$, so $C=\begin{pmatrix}1&9\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ (row $i$, column $j$).

  1. $$P(s)=\begin{pmatrix}s&0.5-s\\0.3-s&0.2+s\end{pmatrix},\qquad s\in[\max(0,\,0.5+0.3-1),\ \min(0.5,0.3)]=[0,0.3].$$Remark 3.2 with $p=0.5$, $q=0.3$.
  2. $$\ps{C}{P(s)}=1\cdot s+9(0.5-s)+1\cdot(0.3-s)+1\cdot(0.2+s)=5-8s .$$Expand $\sum_{ij}C_{ij}P_{ij}$; the cost is linear in $s$.
  3. The minimum over $[0,0.3]$ is at the endpoint $s=0.3$: $P^\star=\begin{pmatrix}0.3&0.2\\0&0.5\end{pmatrix}$, cost $2.6$.A linear function on a segment is minimised at an endpoint (slope $-8<0$: the right one).

Reading: the source at $0$ fills the near target $y=1$ (mass $0.3$) and sends its remaining $0.2$ to $y=3$; the source at $2$ sends everything to $y=3$. The source at $0$ splits its mass. The worst coupling ($s=0$) costs $5$, the product coupling ($s=0.15$) costs $3.8$. Checked with an exact LP solver: $2.6$.

In the course OT4ML Def. 3.3 and Prop. 3.4, p. 52

Definition 3.3 (Discrete product coupling). Given weights $a\in\Sigma_n$ and $b\in\Sigma_m$, the discrete product, or trivial, coupling is $(a\otimes b)_{i,j}:=a_ib_j$. It belongs to $\U(a,b)$ and corresponds to choosing the source and target labels independently.

Proposition 3.4 (Discrete product optimality is degenerate). Assume that the zero-mass rows and columns have been removed, so that $a_i>0$ and $b_j>0$, and let $C$ be a finite cost matrix. The product plan $a\otimes b$ minimizes $P\mapsto\ps{C}{P}$ over $\U(a,b)$ if and only if every coupling $P\in\U(a,b)$ minimizes it.

$a\otimes b$
Row sums $\sum_ja_ib_j=a_i$, column sums $b_j$: always feasible. In probability: $X$ and $Y$ independent.
“degenerate”
The product plan is optimal only in the dull case where all couplings cost the same. It is a feasibility witness, not a candidate solution.
Idea of the proof of Prop. 3.4

All entries of $a\otimes b$ are positive: it lies inside the polytope. From an interior point you can move a little in the direction of any coupling $Q$ and also a little in the opposite direction. A linear function minimised at an interior point must therefore be constant: going one way cannot increase it if going the other way does not decrease it.

Full proof (OT4ML p. 52)

If every coupling is optimal, so is $a\otimes b$. Conversely, assume $a\otimes b$ optimal and let $Q\in\U(a,b)$.

  1. For $t>0$ small enough, $R:=(1+t)(a\otimes b)-tQ$ has non-negative entries.$a_ib_j>0$ for all $(i,j)$ and $Q_{ij}\le1$; take $t\le\min_{ij}a_ib_j$.
  2. $R\ind_m=(1+t)a-ta=a$ and $R\T\ind_n=b$, so $R\in\U(a,b)$.Both $a\otimes b$ and $Q$ have marginals $(a,b)$, and the coefficients $(1+t)$ and $-t$ sum to $1$.
  3. $$a\otimes b=\tfrac1{1+t}R+\tfrac t{1+t}Q,\qquad\text{so}\qquad\ps{C}{a\otimes b}=\tfrac1{1+t}\ps{C}{R}+\tfrac t{1+t}\ps{C}{Q}.$$Solve step 1 for $a\otimes b$; then linearity of $P\mapsto\ps{C}{P}$.
  4. Both $\ps{C}{R}$ and $\ps{C}{Q}$ are $\ge\ps{C}{a\otimes b}$ (optimality); a convex average of two numbers $\ge v$ equals $v$ only if both equal $v$. So $\ps{C}{Q}=\ps{C}{a\otimes b}$: $Q$ is optimal.If one of them were $>v$, the average would be $>v$ (both weights are positive).

On the numbers of the $2\times2$ example, with $Q=P^\star$ and $t=0.5$: $R=1.5\,(a\otimes b)-0.5\,P^\star=\begin{pmatrix}0.075&0.425\\0.225&0.275\end{pmatrix}\ge0$, and $a\otimes b=\frac23R+\frac13P^\star$. Costs: $\ps{C}{R}=5-8(0.075)=4.4$, $\ps{C}{P^\star}=2.6$, and $\frac23\cdot4.4+\frac13\cdot2.6=3.8=\ps{C}{a\otimes b}$. Here the two costs differ, so $a\otimes b$ is not optimal — as expected, since this cost is not “flat”.

The discrete Kantorovich problem

In the course OT4ML Def. 3.5, p. 53

Kantorovich, aiming for economic planning, made a strong simplifying assumption: the cost of transportation should be linear in the amount of transported mass.

Definition 3.5 (Discrete Kantorovich problem). For $a\in\Sigma_n$, $b\in\Sigma_m$, and $C\in\R^{n\times m}$, define

$$\mathrm L_C(a,b):=\min_{P\in\U(a,b)}\ps{C}{P}=\min_{P\in\U(a,b)}\sum_{i,j}C_{i,j}P_{i,j}.\qquad(3.2)$$

A minimizer $P$ is an optimal coupling, or optimal transport plan. Here $C_{i,j}$ is the cost of moving one unit of mass from $x_i$ to $y_j$. This is a linear program, and its solutions need not be unique.

$\Sigma_n$
The probability simplex: $a\ge0$, $\sum_ia_i=1$.
$\ps{C}{P}$
Total bill of the schedule $P$: “mass times unit price”, summed over all pairs.
“linear program”
Unknowns $P_{ij}$ ($nm$ numbers), a linear objective, linear equality constraints ($n+m$ marginals) and sign constraints $P_{ij}\ge0$.
“min”, not “inf”
The minimum is attained: a continuous (linear) function on a non-empty compact set ($\U(a,b)$ is closed and bounded, entries in $[0,1]$).
“need not be unique”
Ties happen: a whole edge of the polytope can be optimal (§5, and Exercise 13.3).

For measures, CompOT writes $\Lc(\al,\be):=\mathrm L_C(a,b)$ with $C_{ij}=c(x_i,y_j)$ (Remark 2.11, p. 20): the points only enter through the cost matrix; the weights only through the marginals.

Monge is a special case: maps are couplings

If a map $T$ satisfies $\pf{T}{\al}=\be$ (with distinct $y_j$), define $P_{ij}=a_i$ if $T(x_i)=y_j$ and $0$ otherwise.

  1. Row $i$: exactly one $j$ has $T(x_i)=y_j$, so $\sum_jP_{ij}=a_i$.$T(x_i)$ is one of the targets, because $\pf{T}{\al}$ only charges the support of $\be$.
  2. Column $j$: $\sum_iP_{ij}=\sum_{i:\,T(x_i)=y_j}a_i=\pf{T}{\al}(\{y_j\})=b_j$.Definition of the push-forward: the mass arriving at $y_j$ is the total mass of the atoms sent there.
  3. $\ps{C}{P}=\sum_ia_ic(x_i,T(x_i))=\int c(x,T(x))\,\dd\al(x)$.Only the entries $P_{i,j}$ with $y_j=T(x_i)$ are non-zero.

So every Monge competitor is a Kantorovich competitor with the same cost, and

$$\mathrm L_C(a,b)\ \le\ \mathcal M_c(\al,\be)\qquad(\text{with }\mathcal M_c=+\infty\text{ when no map exists}).$$

Kantorovich minimises over a bigger set: its value can only be smaller. When are they equal? That is the question of §6 and §9.

In machine learning — the Earth Mover’s Distance for image retrieval

Problem. Find, in a database, the images whose colours look like those of a query image. Representation (Rubner, Tomasi, Guibas, The Earth Mover’s Distance as a metric for image retrieval, IJCV 2000): cluster the pixel colours of each image (in the perceptual CIE-Lab space); the image becomes a signature, i.e. a discrete measure $\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i}$ where $x_i$ is a cluster colour and $a_i$ the fraction of pixels in the cluster. Ground distance: $C_{ij}=\norm{x_i-y_j}$ in Lab. Distance between images: the transport cost $\mathrm L_C(a,b)$ (Rubner et al. also allow unequal total weights by partial matching). Retrieval ranks the database by this distance.

Why transport. Bin-by-bin comparisons ignore colour proximity. On three lightness levels $1,2,3$ with $d(i,j)=\abs{i-j}$: an all-dark image $\delta_1$ against $\delta_2$ or against $\delta_3$ gives the same $\ell^1$ distance $2$, but transport costs $1$ and $2$ — the slightly lighter image is closer. The course book lists this application (OT4ML Example 3.23, p. 61); its practical point: “moving a small amount of mass to a nearby color or pixel is cheaper than moving it far away”.

Convex in the marginals, concave in the cost

In the course OT4ML Prop. 3.6, p. 53

Proposition 3.6 (Convexity in the marginals and concavity in the cost). For every fixed cost matrix $C\in\R^{n\times m}$, the map $(a,b)\mapsto\mathrm L_C(a,b)$ is jointly convex on $\Sigma_n\times\Sigma_m$. For every fixed $(a,b)\in\Sigma_n\times\Sigma_m$, the map $C\mapsto\mathrm L_C(a,b)$ is concave on $\R^{n\times m}$.

Idea

Convexity: mixing two optimal plans gives a plan for the mixed marginals — maybe not optimal, so the value at the mix is at most the mix of the values. Concavity: $\mathrm L_C$ is a minimum of the linear functions $C\mapsto\ps{C}{P}$, and a minimum of linear functions is concave.

Full proof (OT4ML p. 53)
  1. Let $P_r$ be optimal between $a_r$ and $b_r$ ($r=0,1$) and $P_t=(1-t)P_0+tP_1$. Then $P_t\in\U\big((1-t)a_0+ta_1,(1-t)b_0+tb_1\big)$.$P_t\ge0$, and $P_t\ind=(1-t)a_0+ta_1$, $P_t\T\ind=(1-t)b_0+tb_1$ by linearity.
  2. $$\mathrm L_C\big((1-t)a_0+ta_1,(1-t)b_0+tb_1\big)\le\ps{C}{P_t}=(1-t)\mathrm L_C(a_0,b_0)+t\,\mathrm L_C(a_1,b_1).$$$\mathrm L_C$ is a minimum over couplings, $P_t$ is one of them; then linearity of $\ps{C}{\cdot}$ and optimality of $P_0,P_1$.
  3. For fixed $(a,b)$ and any $P\in\U(a,b)$: $\ps{(1-t)C_0+tC_1}{P}=(1-t)\ps{C_0}{P}+t\ps{C_1}{P}\ge(1-t)\mathrm L_{C_0}(a,b)+t\,\mathrm L_{C_1}(a,b)$. Minimising over $P$ gives $\mathrm L_{(1-t)C_0+tC_1}(a,b)\ge(1-t)\mathrm L_{C_0}(a,b)+t\,\mathrm L_{C_1}(a,b)$: concavity.Each $\ps{C_r}{P}$ is at least its minimum $\mathrm L_{C_r}(a,b)$.
Reminder — convex and concave functions

$F$ is convex if $F((1-t)u+tv)\le(1-t)F(u)+tF(v)$ for $t\in[0,1]$; concave if the reverse holds. A supremum of linear (or convex) functions is convex; an infimum of linear functions is concave. More.

In machine learning — a transport loss between label histograms

Frogner, Zhang, Mobahi, Araya-Polo and Poggio (Learning with a Wasserstein loss, NeurIPS 2015) train multi-label classifiers (for instance image tagging on Flickr) whose output is a histogram $h_\theta(x)\in\Sigma_K$ over $K$ labels. The loss compares it with the histogram $y$ of the true labels through $\mathrm L_C(h_\theta(x),y)$, where the ground cost $C_{kl}$ is a distance between labels $k$ and $l$ — for instance between word embeddings of the tag names. Predicting “kitten” when the truth is “cat” then costs little, predicting “truck” a lot; a bin-by-bin loss such as cross-entropy cannot tell the two mistakes apart. Proposition 3.6 says that this loss is convex in the prediction $h$ (not in the network weights $\theta$); its gradient with respect to $h$ is given by the optimal dual potential (Lesson 4), and in practice the entropic version of Lesson 7 is used to compute it fast.

Pitfall — couplings need equal masses

$\U(a,b)$ is empty if $\sum_ia_i\ne\sum_jb_j$ (sum all the entries of $P$ in two ways). Before calling a solver, normalise both histograms — or use unbalanced transport (Lesson 7), which is designed for unequal masses.

Pitfall — $P_{ij}$ is a mass, not a transition probability

The fraction of the mass of $x_i$ that goes to $y_j$ is $P_{ij}/a_i$ (the rows of $P$ divided by their sums). Mixing up the two gives wrong barycentric projections (§11) and wrong transition matrices in single-cell analysis.

Reflexes
  • To write a discrete transport problem → list the points, their weights $a,b$, the cost matrix $C$; the unknown is $P\ge0$ with row sums $a$, column sums $b$.
  • To bound $\mathrm L_C(a,b)$ from above → exhibit one coupling: the product $a\otimes b$, the north-west corner plan (§4), a map if there is one.
  • When you see a $2\times2$ problem → one parameter $s=P_{11}$; the cost is affine in $s$; look at the endpoints.

What is $\U(a,b)$ when $n=1$? Compute $\mathrm L_C(a,b)$.

Answer

$a=(1)$ and $P$ is a single row with sums $b$: $P=b\T$, the only coupling (all the mass of the single source is split according to $b$). So $\mathrm L_C(a,b)=\sum_jC_{1j}b_j=\int c(x_1,y)\,\dd\be(y)$.

In the $2\times2$ example, replace the cost by $c(x,y)=\abs{x-y}$. Is the optimum still unique?

Answer

$C=\begin{pmatrix}1&3\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\ps{C}{P(s)}=s+3(0.5-s)+(0.3-s)+(0.2+s)=2-2s$: still minimised only at $s=0.3$, value $1.4$. (Ties appear when the slope vanishes, i.e. when $C_{11}+C_{22}=C_{12}+C_{21}$: see Exercise 13.3.)
