14. Summary of the lecture

The lecture on one screen
  1. Recap of Monge (§1): $\min\{\int c(x,T(x))\,\dd\al(x):\ \pf{T}{\al}=\be\}$. On the line, $T=C_\be^{-1}\circ C_\al$ and $W_p^p=\int_0^1\abs{C_\al^{-1}-C_\be^{-1}}^p$; between Gaussians, an affine map and $W_2^2=\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+\B(\Sigma_\al,\Sigma_\be)^2$; with a density and $\norm{x-y}^2$, $T=\nabla\varphi$ with $\varphi$ convex (Brenier), a solution of the Monge–Ampère equation. But a map cannot split an atom: the problem can be empty, it is not symmetric, and it is not convex.
  2. Couplings (§2): $\U(a,b)=\{P\ge0:\ P\ind_m=a,\ P\T\ind_n=b\}$ is never empty (it contains $a\otimes b$) and is a convex polytope. The discrete Kantorovich problem $\mathrm L_C(a,b)=\min_{P\in\U(a,b)}\ps{C}{P}$ is a linear program, below Monge’s value (maps are couplings), jointly convex in $(a,b)$ and concave in $C$.
  3. Permutations (§3): $P_\sigma/n$ is a coupling of uniform weights, and the assignment problem is the same linear objective restricted to these matrices; relaxing to all of $\U(\ind_n/n,\ind_n/n)=\mathcal B_n/n$ is Kantorovich’s problem.
  4. The line (§4): the north-west corner rule builds a coupling with at most $n+m-1$ entries; on sorted points with a convex cost $h(x-y)$ it is optimal (uncross any two crossing shipments); the mass sent from $x_i$ to $y_j$ is the overlap of their quantile intervals. For a concave cost, sorting fails.
  5. Vertices (§5): a linear objective on a polytope attains its minimum at a vertex; the vertices of $\U(a,b)$ are the couplings whose support has no cycle; hence an optimal plan with at most $n+m-1$ non-zero entries. The polytope has dimension $(n-1)(m-1)$.
  6. Birkhoff–von Neumann (§6): the vertices of $\mathcal B_n$ are the permutation matrices, so for $n=m$ and uniform weights Monge = Kantorovich; every bistochastic matrix is a mixture of at most $(n-1)^2+1$ permutations; rational weights reduce to an assignment by duplicating atoms.
  7. Solving (§7): the network simplex moves from vertex to vertex along cycles, guided by reduced costs; interior-point methods cross the inside. Prices with $f_i+g_j\le C_{ij}$, tight wherever $P_{ij}>0$, certify optimality (complementary slackness).
  8. General measures (§8): $\U(\al,\be)$ = joint laws with marginals $\al$ and $\be$; $\Lc(\al,\be)=\min_\pi\int c\,\dd\pi=\min_{X\sim\al,\,Y\sim\be}\E[c(X,Y)]$; a minimiser exists for every lower semicontinuous cost $c\ge0$ on Polish spaces (tightness, Prokhorov).
  9. When nothing is lost (§9): with a density and the quadratic cost the optimal plan is $\pf{(\Id,\nabla\varphi)}{\al}$ (Brenier); for atomless sources on a compact space, the values agree (Prop. 4.15); on the line, the quantile coupling is optimal for all measures. With atoms the gap can be large ($1.5$ against $0.5$).
  10. $c$-cyclical monotonicity (§10): no reshuffling of the destinations of finitely many arrows of an optimal plan lowers the cost; for $\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$ optimal maps are monotone, on the line optimal arrows do not cross.
  11. Using a plan (§11): interpolate, $\al_t=\sum_{ij}P_{ij}\delta_{(1-t)x_i+ty_j}$ (sheet, Exercise 4); project, $\bar T(x_i)=\frac1{a_i}\sum_jP_{ij}y_j$; transfer labels — domain adaptation, cell trajectories.

The reflexes to acquire

  • Discrete transport → write $a$, $b$ and $C$; the unknown is $P\ge0$ with row sums $a$ and column sums $b$. Check the total masses first.
  • Upper bound on $\mathrm L_C$ → exhibit one coupling: the product, the north-west plan, a map if there is one.
  • Optimality of a plan → exhibit prices $f,g$ with $f_i+g_j\le C_{ij}$ everywhere and $=$ on the support.
  • A $2\times2$ problem → one parameter $s=P_{11}$; the cost is affine in $s$: compare the two endpoints.
  • On the line with a convex cost $h(x-y)$ → sort both supports, sweep from the north-west corner; no solver needed.
  • Concave cost, or unsorted points → the north-west plan is only a vertex, not an optimum.
  • Linear objective on a polytope → look at the vertices; vertex of $\U(a,b)$ ⇔ no cycle in the support ⇒ at most $n+m-1$ non-zeros.
  • To show a plan is not a vertex → find a cycle in its support and move $\pm\eps$ alternately along it.
  • Same number of points, uniform weights → a permutation is optimal (Birkhoff–von Neumann): Hungarian method or any LP solver.
  • Rational weights → duplicate atoms. Bistochastic matrix → peel off permutations, at most $(n-1)^2+1$.
  • Atoms in the source → expect maps to fail or cost more; use a coupling. Density (quadratic cost) or the line → nothing is lost.
  • To show a plan is not optimal → find two (or $k$) arrows whose destinations can be exchanged more cheaply.
  • Need a map from a plan → barycentric projection, knowing it shrinks the target when mass splits.
  • Interpolate two point clouds → move each entry of the plan along its segment; never mix the weights.
  • Large problems → exact LPs handle a few thousand points; beyond, sort along directions (Lesson 5) or regularise (Lesson 7).

Notes on the sources (errors found, each with its check)
  • CompOT (2.12), p. 19. Permutation matrices are defined with entries $\frac1n$, and the same page states $P_\sigma\ind=\ind_n$, $P_\sigma\T\ind=\ind_n$. With entries $\frac1n$ the row and column sums are $\frac1n$ ($1$ for the $0/1$ matrices of the course book). Check: $\frac13P_{[2,1,3]}$ has row sums $(\frac13,\frac13,\frac13)$ (§3).
  • CompOT §3.1, p. 42: the constraint matrix. The printed $A=\begin{pmatrix}\ind_n\T\otimes I_m\\ I_n\otimes\ind_m\T\end{pmatrix}$ is used with $P$ stacked column by column ($p_{i+n(j-1)}=P_{ij}$) and $Ap=(a,b)$. Check (script, random $2\times3$ and $3\times5$ matrices): applied to the column-wise vector it returns neither $(a,b)$ nor $(b,a)$; applied to the row-wise vector it returns $(b,a)$. For column-wise stacking the operator is $\begin{pmatrix}\ind_m\T\otimes I_n\\ I_m\otimes\ind_n\T\end{pmatrix}$, which returns $(a,b)$.
