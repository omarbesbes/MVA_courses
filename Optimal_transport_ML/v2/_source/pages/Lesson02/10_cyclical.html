10. $c$-cyclical monotonicity: what optimal plans look like

Take finitely many pairs $(x_i,y_i)$ that an optimal plan connects; no reshuffling of their targets can be cheaper — otherwise moving a little mass around that cycle would lower the cost. This finite “exchange test” describes the support of every optimal plan.

The exchange test

The problem. The constraints of the LP are global (marginals), but optimality has a local face: in §4, crossing arrows on the line could be uncrossed; in §7, a negative reduced cost meant a cheaper cycle. Is there a test that only looks at a few pairs at a time?

In the course OT4ML §3.5, Def. 3.45, pp. 71–72

Also StatOT Def. 1.9, p. 31 (for the quadratic cost).

Optimal transport plans behave well when one looks at any finite sub-collection of points in their support: the restriction is still an optimal matching for those points alone. For finitely supported marginals this is immediate, and it leads to the notion of $c$-cyclical monotonicity.

Definition 3.45 ($c$-cyclical monotonicity). A set $\Gamma\subset\X\times\Y$ is $c$-cyclically monotone if, for every $k\ge2$, every finite family $(x_i,y_i)_{i=1}^k\subset\Gamma$ and every permutation $\sigma$ of $\{1,\dots,k\}$,

$$\sum_{i=1}^kc(x_i,y_i)\ \le\ \sum_{i=1}^kc(x_i,y_{\sigma(i)}).$$

Any permutation is a product of cycles, so it suffices to verify the inequality for cyclic permutations, $\sum_{i=1}^kc(x_i,y_i)\le\sum_{i=1}^kc(x_i,y_{i+1})$, $y_{k+1}=y_1$.

$\Gamma$
A set of pairs: think of the support of a plan, the set of “arrows” $x\to y$ along which mass travels.
the inequality
Keep the sources $x_1,\dots,x_k$, reshuffle the destinations among them: the total cost cannot go down. The pairs of $\Gamma$ are already optimally matched among themselves.
$k=2$
The two-point (“uncrossing”) test: $c(x_1,y_1)+c(x_2,y_2)\le c(x_1,y_2)+c(x_2,y_1)$.
cycles suffice
A permutation decomposes into disjoint cycles, and the inequality for a permutation is the sum of the inequalities for its cycles.

Optimal plans pass the test

In the course OT4ML Thm. 3.46, p. 72

Also StatOT Prop. 1.11, p. 33 (quadratic cost).

Let the marginals be uniform on $n$ points […]. By Corollary 3.18, there exists an optimal plan induced by a permutation. Its support $\Gamma=\{(x_i,y_{\sigma(i)})\}_i$ is $c$-cyclically monotone: otherwise exchanging the finitely many targets along a violating cycle would lower the matching cost. The following theorem, in the spirit of Rockafellar’s cyclic-monotonicity theorem, says that the same finite-exchange condition holds for any optimal coupling, not only for finite uniform matchings.

Theorem 3.46 (Optimal plans are $c$-cyclically monotone). Assume that $c:\X\times\Y\to[0,+\infty)$ is continuous and that the Kantorovich value is finite. For any optimal plan $\pi$ solving (3.7), $\mathrm{supp}(\pi)$ is $c$-cyclically monotone.

$\mathrm{supp}(\pi)$
The points $(x,y)$ every neighbourhood of which has positive $\pi$-mass. For a discrete plan, the pairs $(x_i,y_j)$ with $P_{ij}>0$.
continuous cost
Used to pass from points to small neighbourhoods carrying positive mass.
Idea of the proof

If some pairs $(x_i,y_i)$ of the support could be reshuffled more cheaply, take a small mass $\eps$ from each pair $(x_i,y_i)$ and give it to $(x_i,y_{i+1})$: every $x_i$ still sends the same total, every $y_i$ still receives the same total (it loses $\eps$ from $x_i$ and gains $\eps$ from $x_{i-1}$), and the cost goes down. For a general (non-discrete) plan, do the same with small neighbourhoods of the points, which carry positive mass by definition of the support.

Full proof for discrete plans, and the continuous case

Let $P$ be optimal and $(x_1,y_1),\dots,(x_k,y_k)$ pairs of its support (each carries a mass $P(x_i,y_i)>0$), with $\sum_ic(x_i,y_i)>\sum_ic(x_i,y_{i+1})$.

  1. Let $0<\eps\le\frac1k\min_iP(x_i,y_i)$. Define $P'$ from $P$ by removing $\eps$ from each $(x_i,y_i)$ and adding $\eps$ to each $(x_i,y_{i+1})$ ($y_{k+1}=y_1$).Entries stay $\ge0$ even if a pair appears several times in the family (at most $k$ times).
  2. $P'$ has the same marginals as $P$.Each source $x_i$ loses $\eps$ (to $y_i$) and gains $\eps$ (to $y_{i+1}$); each target $y_i$ loses $\eps$ (from $x_i$) and gains $\eps$ (from $x_{i-1}$).
  3. $\ps{C}{P'}-\ps{C}{P}=\eps\Big(\sum_ic(x_i,y_{i+1})-\sum_ic(x_i,y_i)\Big)<0$, contradicting the optimality of $P$.Only the $2k$ modified entries contribute.

General plans (OT4ML p. 72). By continuity of $c$, the strict inequality persists for all points in small neighbourhoods $U_i\ni x_i$, $V_i\ni y_i$. Since $(x_i,y_i)\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi)$, $m_i=\pi(U_i\times V_i)>0$. Remove from $\pi$ a small multiple of its restriction to each $U_i\times V_i$, and reinsert the same amounts as products “first marginal of piece $i$ $\otimes$ second marginal of piece $i+1$”. The marginals are unchanged (the same pieces are removed and reinserted, with the targets permuted), and integrating the uniform strict inequality shows that the cost decreases: a contradiction.

Going further — for discrete problems the test is also sufficient

For finitely supported measures, a coupling whose support is $c$-cyclically monotone is optimal. Sketch: suppose $P$ is not optimal and let $P'$ be cheaper. The difference $D=P'-P$ has zero row and column sums, and its negative entries sit where $P>0$. Such a “circulation” decomposes into alternating cycles $x_{i_1}\to y_{j_1}\to x_{i_2}\to\dots$ with positive weights, where the edges with $D<0$ are support edges of $P$. Since $\ps{C}{D}<0$, one of these cycles has negative total cost — and that cycle is exactly a reshuffling of targets among support pairs of $P$ that lowers the cost: a violation of $c$-cyclical monotonicity. Lesson 4 gives the general statement through duality (and StatOT, p. 42, mentions the converse for general measures).

Worked example — detecting a non-optimal plan with the test

On the line with $c=\abs{x-y}^2$, the uniform plan matching $0\to2$, $1\to0$, $2\to1$ costs $4+1+1=6$ (times $\frac13$). Two-point test on the pairs $(0,2),(1,0)$: $c(0,2)+c(1,0)=4+1=5$, swapped $c(0,0)+c(1,2)=0+1=1<5$: violated. Of the eight reshufflings of these three pairs, five lower the cost; the best one, the cycle that sends each source to the target of the next pair, costs $0$ (it is the identity $0\to0$, $1\to1$, $2\to2$). The plan is far from optimal, and the test says it by looking at two pairs only.

Worked example — monotone is not cyclically monotone

In the plane, let $x_1=(1,0)$, $x_2=(0,1)$, $x_3=(-1,0)$, $x_4=(0,-1)$ and let $T$ be the rotation by $90°$, so that $T(x_i)=x_{i+1}$. With $c=\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$:

  • Two-point test: for $x_1,x_2$, $c(x_1,x_2)+c(x_2,x_3)=1+1=2$ and swapped $c(x_1,x_3)+c(x_2,x_2)=2+0=2$: passed, with equality (the rotation is monotone: $\ps{Tx-Ty}{x-y}=\cos(90°)\norm{x-y}^2=0\ge0$).
  • Four-cycle: the four pairs cost $4\times1=4$; sending each $x_i$ to the target of the previous pair, i.e. to $x_i$ itself, costs $0$: violated.

So the two-point test is not enough in dimension $d\ge2$; the full cyclical condition is what characterises gradients of convex functions (Rockafellar’s theorem, StatOT Thm. 1.10) and leads to Brenier’s theorem (Lesson 4). OT4ML (p. 72) makes the same point: “a small rotation is monotone yet not a gradient”.

What it says about maps

In the course OT4ML pp. 72–73

Assume the optimal plan is induced by a measurable map $T$ […]. For $c(x,y)=\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$, taking $k=2$ gives $\ps{T(x)-T(y)}{x-y}\ge0$ for every $x,y\in G$, so the optimal representative of $T$ is monotone on $G$. […] In one space dimension, with cost $c(x,y)=\abs{x-y}^p$, the two-point inequality becomes $\abs{x-T(x)}^p+\abs{y-T(y)}^p\le\abs{x-T(y)}^p+\abs{y-T(x)}^p$. For $p>1$, strict convexity implies that a reversed pair $xT(y)$ violates this inequality. Hence every optimal map is nondecreasing on the full-measure transport set, recovering the classical monotone rearrangement. For $p=1$, uncrossing is non-strict: the monotone rearrangement remains optimal, but other optimal maps and non-deterministic plans can occur, as in the book-shifting example of Remark 3.30.

Why the two-point test gives monotonicity. Expand $\frac12\norm{x-T(x)}^2+\frac12\norm{y-T(y)}^2\le\frac12\norm{x-T(y)}^2+\frac12\norm{y-T(x)}^2$: the squared norms $\norm x^2,\norm y^2,\norm{T(x)}^2,\norm{T(y)}^2$ appear on both sides and cancel, leaving $-\ps{x}{T(x)}-\ps{y}{T(y)}\le-\ps{x}{T(y)}-\ps{y}{T(x)}$, i.e. $\ps{T(x)-T(y)}{x-y}\ge0$. Optimal maps for the quadratic cost never “turn back”.

In machine learning — checking or enforcing optimality of a learned map

Neural networks are increasingly trained to output transport maps (to push a source data set onto a target one, for instance for domain translation or for generative modelling). A learned map $T_\theta$ may push the source onto the target and still be far from optimal. The exchange test gives a cheap diagnostic: sample pairs $(x,T_\theta(x))$, $(x',T_\theta(x'))$ and check $\ps{T_\theta(x)-T_\theta(x')}{x-x'}\ge0$; violations reveal non-optimal regions. The course book develops a convex penalty built on the same idea — the Monge gap (OT4ML §3.4, beyond this lesson), which on the line reduces to a sum of hinges $(t_i-t_j)_+$ over reversed pairs — and uses it to regularise regression towards monotone maps.

Reflexes
  • To show a plan is not optimal → find two (or $k$) of its arrows whose targets can be exchanged more cheaply.
  • Quadratic cost → the two-point test reads $\ps{T(x)-T(x')}{x-x'}\ge0$; on the line, arrows of an optimal plan do not cross ($p>1$).
  • In dimension $\ge2$, monotone is not enough: check cycles, or look for a convex potential.

On the line with $c=\abs{x-y}$ ($p=1$), sources $\{0,1\}$ and targets $\{1,2\}$ with weights $\frac12$: does the crossed plan $0\to2$, $1\to1$ pass the two-point test? Is it optimal?

Answer

Its cost is $\frac12(2+0)=1$; the uncrossed plan $0\to1$, $1\to2$ costs $\frac12(1+1)=1$ too. The test $c(0,2)+c(1,1)\le c(0,1)+c(1,2)$ reads $2\le2$: passed (with equality). Both plans are optimal — in fact every coupling of this pair is optimal, because here $c(x,y)=y-x$ on all four pairs (Exercise 13.3). For $p=1$ crossing is allowed; for $p>1$ it is not.
