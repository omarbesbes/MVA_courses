12. The exercise sheet, solved

The sheet Discrete Kantorovich Formulation — Exercises (exo-kanto-discr-uncorrected.pdf, 2 pages) has four exercises and an optional fifth, which comes with the teachers’ solution. Each exercise is quoted, then a hint, then a full solution. Try before opening.

Exercise 1 — the line, by the north-west corner

In the course sheet, Exercise 1, p. 1

Exercise 1 (1D discrete $\Rightarrow$ optimal Kantorovich plan). Let $n=m=3$ and consider two 1D discrete measures with sorted supports and weights $a=(1.1,0.8,1.1)$ and $b=(1,1,1)$. For the quadratic cost $c(x,y)=\abs{x-y}^2$, compute the optimal coupling matrix $P\in\R_+^{3\times3}$.

Hint

The positions are not given — and not needed. Which result gives an optimal plan from the order of the points alone? (§4.)

Solution

The weights are not probability vectors, but both total $3$, which is all that $\U(a,b)$ needs. The supports are sorted and $h(t)=t^2$ is convex, so by Prop. 3.10 the north-west corner plan is optimal, whatever the positions.

  1. Cell $(1,1)$: $\min(1.1,1)=1$. Column 1 exhausted; row 1 keeps $0.1$: go right.$r=(0.1,0.8,1.1)$, $s=(0,1,1)$.
  2. Cell $(1,2)$: $\min(0.1,1)=0.1$. Row 1 exhausted: go down.$s=(0,0.9,1)$.
  3. Cell $(2,2)$: $\min(0.8,0.9)=0.8$. Row 2 exhausted: go down.$s=(0,0.1,1)$.
  4. Cell $(3,2)$: $\min(1.1,0.1)=0.1$. Column 2 exhausted: go right.$r_3=1$.
  5. Cell $(3,3)$: $\min(1,1)=1$. Done.Last row and last column together.
$$P=\begin{pmatrix}1&0.1&0\\0&0.8&0\\0&0.1&1\end{pmatrix}:\quad\text{row sums }(1.1,0.8,1.1),\ \text{column sums }(1,1,1),\ 5=n+m-1\text{ non-zero entries.}$$

Reading: the heavy outer atoms fill their neighbours and spill their excess $0.1$ into the middle target, which receives from all three sources. If the points are distinct, $\abs\cdot^2$ is strictly convex and this plan is the unique optimum (Exercise 13.9). Check: with $x=(0,1,2)$, $y=(0.5,1.5,2.5)$ its cost is $0.95$, equal to the exact LP value; with two other sorted supports, e.g. $y=(-3,1.2,10)$, the LP returns the same matrix.

Exercise 2 — parametric cases

In the course sheet, Exercise 2, p. 1

Exercise 2 (Parametric 1D cases). (1) Two against two: $a=(1+\eps,1-\eps)$ and $b=(1,1)$ with $\abs\eps\le1$. Compute the optimal $P\in\R_+^{2\times2}$ as a function of the sign of $\eps$.

(2) Three against three: $a=(1+\eps,1+\eta,1-\eps-\eta)$ and $b=(1,1,1)$ with feasibility $1+\eps\ge0$, $1+\eta\ge0$, $1-\eps-\eta\ge0$. Compute the optimal monotone $P\in\R_+^{3\times3}$ in the two regimes (i) $\eps>0$, $\eta>0$ and (ii) $\eps<0$, $\eta>0$.

Hint

Same tool as Exercise 1 (supports implicitly sorted, convex cost): the answer is the north-west plan; only the order in which rows and columns are exhausted depends on the signs. In (2)(ii), after the first column, row 2 still holds $1+\eta+\eps$: compare it with the capacity $1$ of column 2.

Solution

(1) If $\eps\ge0$, row 1 overflows column 1: $P_{11}=1$, $P_{12}=\eps$, $P_{22}=1-\eps$. If $\eps\le0$, row 1 is exhausted first and row 2 completes column 1:

$$\eps\ge0:\ P=\begin{pmatrix}1&\eps\\0&1-\eps\end{pmatrix},\qquad\eps\le0:\ P=\begin{pmatrix}1+\eps&0\\-\eps&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

Both give the identity at $\eps=0$; all entries are $\ge0$ because $\abs\eps\le1$. (For $\eps=0.3$: $\begin{pmatrix}1&0.3\\0&0.7\end{pmatrix}$; for $\eps=-0.3$: $\begin{pmatrix}0.7&0\\0.3&1\end{pmatrix}$.)

(2)(i) $\eps,\eta>0$. $P_{11}=\min(1+\eps,1)=1$, row 1 keeps $\eps$: $P_{12}=\eps$. Column 2 still needs $1-\eps$: $P_{22}=\min(1+\eta,1-\eps)=1-\eps$. Row 2 keeps $\eps+\eta$: $P_{23}=\eps+\eta$. Row 3 completes: $P_{33}=1-\eps-\eta$.

$$P=\begin{pmatrix}1&\eps&0\\0&1-\eps&\eps+\eta\\0&0&1-\eps-\eta\end{pmatrix}\qquad(\eps=0.2,\ \eta=0.3:\ \begin{pmatrix}1&0.2&0\\0&0.8&0.5\\0&0&0.5\end{pmatrix}).$$

Feasibility: $1-\eps\ge0$ follows from $1-\eps-\eta\ge0$ and $\eta>0$.

(2)(ii) $\eps<0<\eta$. $P_{11}=1+\eps$ (row 1 exhausted), $P_{21}=-\eps$ (column 1 completed). Row 2 still holds $1+\eta+\eps$, and the answer depends on the sign of $\eps+\eta$, which the sheet does not separate:

$$\eps+\eta\ge0:\ P=\begin{pmatrix}1+\eps&0&0\\-\eps&1&\eps+\eta\\0&0&1-\eps-\eta\end{pmatrix},\qquad\eps+\eta<0:\ P=\begin{pmatrix}1+\eps&0&0\\-\eps&1+\eps+\eta&0\\0&-(\eps+\eta)&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

In the first case row 2 overflows column 2 into column 3; in the second, row 2 cannot fill column 2 and row 3 completes it. At $\eps+\eta=0$ both formulas agree. Examples: $(\eps,\eta)=(-0.2,0.5)$ gives $\begin{pmatrix}0.8&0&0\\0.2&1&0.3\\0&0&0.7\end{pmatrix}$; $(\eps,\eta)=(-0.5,0.2)$ gives $\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0&0\\0.5&0.7&0\\0&0.3&1\end{pmatrix}$.

Check: on $600$ random draws of $(\eps,\eta)$ (211 feasible ones in the three regimes) with random sorted supports, the formulas coincide with the north-west plan and with the exact LP optimum (largest excess $3.6\times10^{-15}$).

Note on the sources — Exercise 2(2)(ii) has two sub-cases

The sheet announces “the two regimes (i) $\eps>0,\eta>0$ and (ii) $\eps<0,\eta>0$”, but regime (ii) splits further according to the sign of $\eps+\eta$ (example above: $(-0.2,0.5)$ and $(-0.5,0.2)$ give different supports). A single formula for regime (ii) is necessarily wrong in one of the two sub-cases.

Exercise 3 — couplings in basic cases

In the course sheet, Exercise 3, p. 1

Exercise 3 (Couplings in basic cases). (a) What are the couplings between $\delta_x$ and $\delta_y$? (b) What are the couplings between $\delta_x$ and $\frac12(\delta_y+\delta_z)$, with $y\ne z$? (c) Let $\al,\be$ be finite positive measures with different total masses. What is $\Pi(\al,\be)$, the set of couplings?

Hint

Use the set form of the marginal constraints, $\pi(A\times\Y)=\al(A)$ and $\pi(\X\times B)=\be(B)$, with well-chosen sets: $A=\X\setminus\{x\}$; and, for (c), $A=\X$, $B=\Y$.

Solution
  1. (a) $\pi\big((\X\setminus\{x\})\times\Y\big)=\delta_x(\X\setminus\{x\})=0$ and $\pi\big(\X\times(\Y\setminus\{y\})\big)=0$, so $\pi$ gives mass only to $\{x\}\times\{y\}$, with total mass $1$: $\Pi(\delta_x,\delta_y)=\{\delta_{(x,y)}\}$.The complement of $\{(x,y)\}$ is contained in the union of the two null sets.
  2. (b) As in (a), $\pi$ lives on $\{x\}\times\Y$; its second marginal is $\frac12(\delta_y+\delta_z)$, so $\pi(\{(x,y)\})=\pi(\X\times\{y\})=\frac12$ and likewise for $z$: $\Pi=\{\frac12\delta_{(x,y)}+\frac12\delta_{(x,z)}\}$, a single coupling, which splits the atom at $x$.More generally the only coupling of $\delta_x$ and $\be$ is $\delta_x\otimes\be$. No map achieves (b): this is Monge’s first failure (§1), repaired.
  3. (c) If $\pi$ were a coupling, $\pi(\X\times\Y)=\al(\X)$ (first marginal with $A=\X$) and $\pi(\X\times\Y)=\be(\Y)$ (second marginal with $B=\Y$), so $\al(\X)=\be(\Y)$ — false. Hence $\Pi(\al,\be)=\emptyset$.Balanced transport needs equal masses; unbalanced transport (Lesson 7) relaxes the marginal constraints.

Exercise 4 — the interpolation $\al_t$ of a discrete plan

In the course sheet, Exercise 4, p. 1

Exercise 4 (Discrete $\al_t$ from a coupling). Let $\al_0=\sum_{i=1}^na_i\delta_{x_i}$ and $\al_1=\sum_{j=1}^mb_j\delta_{y_j}$ be probability measures on $\R^d$ ($a_i,b_j>0$, $\sum_ia_i=\sum_jb_j=1$). For $t\in[0,1]$ define $P_t(x,y)=(1-t)x+ty$ and set $\al_t:=\pf{(P_t)}{\pi}$.

(a) Show that $\al_t$ interpolates between $\al_0$ and $\al_1$, i.e. $\al_0=\pf{(P_0)}{\pi}$ and $\al_1=\pf{(P_1)}{\pi}$. (b) Prove that $\al_t$ is discrete for every $t\in[0,1]$. Give an upper bound on the number of Dirac masses of $\al_t$ in terms of $P$. Deduce the crude bound $\#\mathrm{supp}(\al_t)\le nm$. (Bonus: explain why one can choose an optimal coupling with at most $n+m-1$ nonzeros, hence an interpolation with at most $n+m-1$ atoms.) (c) Give the explicit expression of $\al_t$ in terms of the matrix $P$. (d) Assume $n=m$. Solution

(a) $P_0(x,y)=x$ and $P_1(x,y)=y$ are the two coordinate projections, so $\pf{(P_0)}{\pi}$ and $\pf{(P_1)}{\pi}$ are the two marginals of $\pi$, namely $\al_0$ and $\al_1$. Explicitly, $\pf{(P_0)}{\pi}=\sum_{i,j}P_{ij}\delta_{x_i}=\sum_i\big(\sum_jP_{ij}\big)\delta_{x_i}=\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i}$.

(b)–(c) By the hint,

$$\al_t=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^mP_{ij}\,\delta_{(1-t)x_i+ty_j},$$

a discrete measure whose atoms are among the points $(1-t)x_i+ty_j$ with $P_{ij}>0$. Hence $\#\mathrm{supp}(\al_t)\le\#\{(i,j):P_{ij}>0\}\le nm$ (fewer if some of these points coincide). Bonus: the LP attains its minimum at a vertex of $\U(a,b)$ (Prop. 3.16), and a vertex has a cycle-free support, hence at most $n+m-1$ non-zero entries (§5); with such an optimal plan, $\al_t$ has at most $n+m-1$ atoms.

(d) The sheet does not fix the cost; take the quadratic cost $\norm{x-y}^2$ (the one for which $\al_t$ is the displacement interpolation) and distinct points. Condition: some optimal coupling is a permutation plan, $P_{i,\sigma(i)}=a_i=b_{\sigma(i)}$ (so $b$ must be a rearrangement of $a$, and that matching must be optimal).

  • Sufficient: such a plan gives $\al_t=\sum_ia_i\delta_{(1-t)x_i+ty_{\sigma(i)}}$: $n$ distinct atoms at $t=0$ and $t=1$ (distinct points), and also for $0
  • Necessary: for $0
  • Always true for uniform weights $a=b=\ind_n/n$: Birkhoff–von Neumann provides an optimal permutation (Cor. 3.18).

Example. In $\R^2$, $x_1=(0,0)$, $x_2=(1,0)$, $x_3=(0,1)$ and $y_i=x_i+(2,1)$, weights $\frac13$. The optimal plan is the identity matching (cost $\norm{(2,1)}^2=5$, checked with an LP), and $\al_t=\frac13\sum_i\delta_{x_i+t(2,1)}$: three atoms translated at constant speed; at $t=\frac12$ they sit at $(1,0.5)$, $(2,0.5)$, $(1,1.5)$.

Remark. For a non-quadratic cost, collisions can happen. On the line with $c=\abs{x-y}$, sources $\{0,1\}$, targets $\{1,2\}$, weights $\frac12$: the crossed plan $0\to2$, $1\to1$ is optimal (§10, quiz), and its two pieces meet at the point $1$ at $t=\frac12$, so $\al_{1/2}$ has a single atom.

Exercise 5 — when does a finite-cost coupling exist? (optional)

In the course sheet, Exercise 5, p. 2

Exercise 5 (When does a finite-cost coupling exist?). Let $n\in\N$ and consider the uniform discrete measures $\al=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{x_i}$, $\be=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n\delta_{y_j}$. Let $C=(C_{ij})\in(\R\cup\{+\infty\})^{n\times n}$ be a cost matrix; $+\infty$ means the pair $(x_i,y_j)$ is forbidden. A coupling $\pi$ has finite cost if it assigns zero mass to all $+\infty$ entries.

(i) $2\times2$ by hand. For $C=\begin{pmatrix}C_{11}&C_{12}\\C_{21}&C_{22}\end{pmatrix}$, determine exactly when a finite-cost coupling exists in terms of the positions of $+\infty$. When it exists, write one explicit coupling matrix $P\in\R_+^{2\times2}$ with row/column sums $1/2$ supported only on finite entries.

(ii) General uniform case. Let $E=\{(i,j):C_{ij}<+\infty\}$ be the set of allowed edges. Prove: there exists a finite-cost coupling $\iff$ there exists a permutation $\sigma$ with $(i,\sigma(i))\in E$ $\forall i$. (Hint: If $P$ is a coupling, then $Q:=nP$ is doubly stochastic. Recall Birkhoff–von Neumann: every doubly stochastic matrix is a convex combination of permutation matrices.)

Solution

(i) The couplings are $P(s)=\begin{pmatrix}s&\frac12-s\\\frac12-s&s\end{pmatrix}$, $s\in[0,\frac12]$. For $0

  • a finite-cost coupling exists $\iff$ ($C_{11},C_{22}<\infty$) or ($C_{12},C_{21}<\infty$);

(ii) ($\Leftarrow$) If $\sigma$ uses only allowed cells, $P_{i,\sigma(i)}=\frac1n$ (and $0$ elsewhere) is a coupling of finite cost.

  2. For a forbidden cell $(i,j)$: $0=Q_{ij}=\sum_k\lambda_k(P_{\sigma_k})_{ij}$, a sum of non-negative terms, so $(P_{\sigma_k})_{ij}=0$ for every $k$.$\lambda_k>0$ and $(P_{\sigma_k})_{ij}\in\{0,1\}$.

Check: on $400$ random forbidden patterns ($2\le n\le6$), “an allowed permutation exists” and “the LP with cost $1$ on forbidden cells and $0$ elsewhere has value $0$” agreed $400$ times out of $400$ ($195$ patterns were feasible).

Note on the sources — the two directions are swapped in the sheet’s solution of Exercise 5(ii)

The statement is “finite-cost coupling $\iff$ permutation”. The sheet’s solution labels “($\Rightarrow$) If there is a permutation $\sigma$ … then $P$ … is a coupling with finite cost” and “($\Leftarrow$) Suppose a finite-cost coupling exists …”: with the statement written in this order, these are the directions ($\Leftarrow$) and ($\Rightarrow$) respectively. The arguments themselves are correct. In (i), the obstruction “exactly one of $\{(1,1),(2,2)\}$ and exactly one of $\{(1,2),(2,1)\}$ is $+\infty$” is given as an example (“e.g.”): the exact condition is “at least one forbidden cell in each of the two supports”.
