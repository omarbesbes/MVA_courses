11. Using a plan: interpolation, barycentric projection, transfer

A plan says which pieces of mass travel together: move each piece along a straight line to interpolate between the two distributions, average its destinations to get a map, or carry labels and other information across the plan from one data set to the other.

Moving along a plan

In the course exercise sheet, Exercise 4 (exo-kanto-discr, p. 1)

Let $\al_0=\sum_{i=1}^na_i\delta_{x_i}$ and $\al_1=\sum_{j=1}^mb_j\delta_{y_j}$ be probability measures on $\R^d$ […]. Let $\pi$ be a (Kantorovich) optimal coupling between $\al_0$ and $\al_1$ and write it in discrete form $\pi=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^mP_{ij}\delta_{(x_i,y_j)}$, $P\in\R_+^{n\times m}$, $\sum_jP_{ij}=a_i$, $\sum_iP_{ij}=b_j$. For $t\in[0,1]$ define $P_t:\R^d\times\R^d\to\R^d$ by $P_t(x,y)=(1-t)x+ty$ and set $\al_t:=\pf{(P_t)}{\pi}$.

$P_t(x,y)=(1-t)x+ty$
The point at fraction $t$ of the segment from $x$ to $y$ (not to be confused with the plan $P$).
$\al_t=\pf{(P_t)}{\pi}$
Every piece of mass $P_{ij}$ travels from $x_i$ to $y_j$ in a straight line at constant speed; $\al_t$ is the distribution of all the pieces at time $t$.

Pushing a discrete measure forward moves its atoms (refresher), so

$$\al_t=\sum_{i,j}P_{ij}\,\delta_{(1-t)x_i+ty_j},\qquad\al_0=\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i},\qquad\al_1=\sum_jb_j\delta_{y_j}.$$

At $t=0$ the pieces leaving the same $x_i$ sit together and add up to $a_i$; at $t=1$ the pieces arriving at the same $y_j$ add up to $b_j$. In between there is one atom per non-zero entry of $P$ — at most $n+m-1$ for a vertex (§5). For the quadratic cost and an optimal plan, different pieces never collide for $0 $$\norm{(1-t)(x-x')+t(y-y')}^2\ \ge\ (1-t)^2\norm{x-x'}^2+t^2\norm{y-y'}^2\ >\ 0$$

unless the two arrows are the same. With the quadratic cost and an optimal plan, $\al_t$ is the displacement interpolation of Lesson 1 extended to plans that split mass — a geodesic for $W_2$ (Lesson 3, §6).

From a plan to a map: the barycentric projection

In the course OT4ML Def. 12.32, p. 281

Definition 12.32 (Barycentric projection of a coupling). Let $\al,\be\in\Pc_1(\R^d)$ and let $\pi\in\U(\al,\be)$. Disintegrate $\pi$ with respect to its first marginal as $\pi(\dd x,\dd y)=\pi_x(\dd y)\al(\dd x)$. […] the barycentric projection of $\pi$ is the map $\bar T_\pi(x):=\int_{\R^d}y\,\dd\pi_x(y)$, $\bar\be_\pi:=\pf{(\bar T_\pi)}{\al}$.

The projected target $\bar\be_\pi$ records the distribution of conditional means, not the full second marginal. […] if $\pi=\pf{(\Id,T)}{\al}$ is induced by a map, then $\bar T_\pi=T$ and $\bar\be_\pi=\be$. For discrete measures […] $\pi_{x_i}=\sum_{j=1}^m\frac{P_{i,j}}{a_i}\delta_{y_j}$ and $\bar T_\pi(x_i)=\frac1{a_i}\sum_{j=1}^mP_{i,j}\,y_j$.

$\pi_x$
The conditional law of the destination given the source $x$: “where does the mass at $x$ go?”. Discretely, row $i$ of $P$ divided by its sum $a_i$.
$\bar T_\pi(x)$
The mean destination of $x$: one point per source, i.e. a map. In probability, $\bar T_\pi(X)=\E[Y\mid X]$.
$\bar\be_\pi$
Where the sources land if each goes to its mean destination. It is $\be$ when no mass splits; otherwise it is a “shrunk” version of $\be$, with the same mean.
Worked example — interpolation, projection and transfer on the plan of §5

$P=\begin{pmatrix}0.3&0.3&0\\0&0.15&0.25\end{pmatrix}$, sources $x=(0,1)$ with $a=(0.6,0.4)$, targets $y=(0,1,2)$ with $b=(0.3,0.45,0.25)$.

  1. $$\al_{1/2}=0.3\,\delta_0+0.3\,\delta_{0.5}+0.15\,\delta_{1}+0.25\,\delta_{1.5}.$$Midpoints of the four arrows $0\to0$, $0\to1$, $1\to1$, $1\to2$, with their masses.
  2. $$\bar T(x_1)=\frac{0.3\cdot0+0.3\cdot1}{0.6}=0.5,\qquad\bar T(x_2)=\frac{0.15\cdot1+0.25\cdot2}{0.4}=1.625 .$$Definition 12.32 with $a_1=0.6$, $a_2=0.4$.
  3. $\bar\be=0.6\,\delta_{0.5}+0.4\,\delta_{1.625}\ne\be$, but both have mean $0.95$.$0.6\cdot0.5+0.4\cdot1.625=0.95=0.3\cdot0+0.45\cdot1+0.25\cdot2$: averaging destinations preserves the overall mean.
  4. Rows normalised: $K=P/a=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.5&0\\0&0.375&0.625\end{pmatrix}$ — the probabilities that a unit of mass at $x_i$ ends at $y_j$.Each row of $K$ sums to $1$.
  5. Columns normalised (“backward” projection): if the sources carry labels $\ell=(0,1)$, the targets receive $\tilde\ell_j=\frac1{b_j}\sum_iP_{ij}\ell_i=(0,\ \tfrac{0.15}{0.45},\ 1)=(0,\ 0.333,\ 1)$.Target $y_2$ receives $0.3$ from the source labelled $0$ and $0.15$ from the one labelled $1$.

What to look at: orange sources and blue targets, sized by their masses; the green atoms are $\al_t$. Start with one point to two points: no map can do it, the plan splits the atom, and the barycentric projection sends everything to the midpoint — a map, but onto the wrong target. Move $t$ and count the atoms (the readout): one per non-zero entry of the plan, at most $n+m-1$; with the product plan there are $nm$ of them. With the optimal plan the atoms never collide before $t=1$; drag the points to try to make them collide. With 8 to 8, uniform weights, the plan is a permutation (Birkhoff–von Neumann) and the barycentric projection is the matching itself.

In machine learning — domain adaptation by transporting the training set

Problem. A classifier is trained on labelled source data (e.g. images from one camera, or simulated data) and must work on unlabelled target data whose distribution has shifted (another camera, real data). OT domain adaptation (Courty, Flamary, Tuia, Rakotomamonjy, Optimal transport for domain adaptation, IEEE TPAMI 2017): compute a plan $P$ between the source samples $x_i^s$ and the target samples $x_j^t$ (empirical measures, usually with an entropic and a class-based regularisation), map each source sample by the barycentric projection $\hat x_i^s=\frac1{a_i}\sum_jP_{ij}x_j^t$, and train the classifier on the transported, still labelled, samples $(\hat x_i^s,y_i^s)$. The course book describes the label-side version (OT4ML Example 3.22, p. 61): the backward barycentric rule $\tilde y_j=b_j^{-1}\sum_iP_{i,j}y_i^s$ gives soft labels to the target samples (step 5 of the example above), and JDOT optimises the plan and the classifier jointly.

In machine learning — single-cell developmental trajectories (Waddington-OT)

Single-cell RNA sequencing measures the gene expression of thousands of individual cells, but destroys them: cells observed at day $k$ are not the same as at day $k+1$. Each snapshot is an empirical measure $\al_{t_k}=\sum_ia_i^{[k]}\delta_{x_i^{[k]}}$ in gene-expression space. A coupling $P^{[k]}$ between consecutive snapshots gives “soft ancestor–descendant masses”, and the row normalisation $K^{[k]}_{i,j}=P^{[k]}_{i,j}/\sum_{j'}P^{[k]}_{i,j'}$ is a transition probability from day $k$ to day $k+1$ (step 4 of the example). Multiplying $K^{[1]}K^{[2]}\cdots$ gives the probable fates of a cell, or the probable ancestors of a population — for instance, which early cells will become stem-like in a reprogramming experiment (OT4ML Example 3.24, pp. 61–62; Schiebinger et al., Cell 2019). Waddington-OT replaces the balanced coupling by unbalanced, entropic transport to account for cell growth and death (Lessons 7–8).

Pitfall — the barycentric projection is not a transport map onto $\be$

When mass splits, $\pf{\bar T}{\al}\ne\be$: averaging destinations shrinks the target (“one point to two points” collapses onto the midpoint). It is the right object for regression-like uses (labels, features), not a substitute for the plan when you need $\be$ itself.

Reflexes
  • Interpolate between two point clouds → move each entry of the plan along its segment: $\al_t=\sum_{ij}P_{ij}\delta_{(1-t)x_i+ty_j}$.
  • Need a map from a plan → barycentric projection $\bar T(x_i)=\frac1{a_i}\sum_jP_{ij}y_j$ (rows normalised).
  • Transfer information from sources to targets → normalise the columns: $\tilde\ell_j=\frac1{b_j}\sum_iP_{ij}\ell_i$.

For $\al=\delta_0$ and $\be=\frac12(\delta_{-1}+\delta_1)$ on $\R$: what is $\al_t$? What is the barycentric projection, and $\bar\be$?

Answer

The only coupling sends $\frac12$ to each target: $\al_t=\frac12\delta_{-t}+\frac12\delta_t$ (the atom splits at once and both halves move at speed $1$). $\bar T(0)=\frac12(-1)+\frac12(1)=0$, so $\bar\be=\delta_0\ne\be$: the projection keeps everything in place.
