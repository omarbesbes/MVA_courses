What you will be able to do after this page
  1. Explain why Monge’s problem (move each point to one point) can fail, and how Kantorovich’s couplings repair it: mass may split, the feasible set is never empty and becomes convex.
  2. Write the discrete transport problem as a linear program $\min_{P\in\U(a,b)}\ps{C}{P}$, describe its feasible set (a polytope of dimension $(n-1)(m-1)$) and prove that it has an optimal solution with at most $n+m-1$ non-zero entries.
  3. Run the north-west corner rule by hand, and prove that on the line, after sorting, it gives an optimal plan for every convex cost $h(x-y)$ (and why it fails for concave costs).
  4. State and prove the Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem, deduce that Monge = Kantorovich for uniform weights (the assignment problem is a linear program), and decompose a bistochastic matrix into permutations.
  5. Certify that a plan is optimal with dual prices (complementary slackness), and follow the network simplex on a small example.
  6. Define the general Kantorovich problem between arbitrary measures, prove that an optimal plan exists, and say exactly when the relaxation loses nothing (uniform weights, densities, the line) and when it does (atoms).
  7. Check $c$-cyclical monotonicity, and use a plan: interpolate along it, project it to a map, transfer labels — as in domain adaptation, single-cell trajectories, image and document retrieval.

The story of the lecture in seven steps

Let mass split: the transport problem becomes a linear program over “couplings”, always solvable, with sparse solutions — and in many cases (uniform weights, densities, the line) no worse than moving points by a map.

  1. Recap (§1). Monge looks for a map $T$ moving $\al$ onto $\be$ at least cost. On the line, sort; for Gaussians, an affine map; in general, Brenier’s map $\nabla\varphi$, solution of the Monge–Ampère equation. But a map cannot split a Dirac: the problem can be empty, and it is not convex.
  2. Couplings (§2). Record how much mass goes from $x_i$ to $y_j$: a matrix $P\ge0$ with row sums $a$ and column sums $b$. Minimising the linear cost $\ps{C}{P}$ over these matrices is a linear program.
  3. Permutations (§3). For $n$ points against $n$ points with equal weights, Monge’s problem is an assignment problem over permutation matrices; Kantorovich’s relaxation replaces them by bistochastic matrices. Is anything lost?
  4. The line (§4). Fill the table from its top-left corner: the north-west corner rule builds a plan in $n+m-1$ steps; on sorted points and for convex costs it is optimal.
  5. Vertices and Birkhoff–von Neumann (§5–§6). A linear program is minimised at a vertex; vertices of the transport polytope are sparse (at most $n+m-1$ entries); for uniform weights they are permutations — so Monge = Kantorovich.
  6. Solving, and the general problem (§7–§9). Network simplex and dual prices; couplings between arbitrary measures; an optimal plan always exists; it is a map for densities with the quadratic cost (Brenier), on the line, or for uniform discrete weights — not with atoms.
  7. Structure and use (§10–§11). Optimal plans cannot be improved by exchanging targets along cycles ($c$-cyclical monotonicity). A plan can be used to interpolate, to build a map (barycentric projection) and to transfer information between data sets.

Refresher — discrete measures, histograms and push-forward

A histogram is a vector of weights $a=(a_1,\dots,a_n)$ with $a_i\ge0$ and $\sum_ia_i=1$; the set of histograms is the probability simplex $\Sigma_n$. Attached to points $x_1,\dots,x_n$ it defines the discrete measure $\al=\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i}$: mass $a_i$ at $x_i$, and $\int f\,\dd\al=\sum_ia_if(x_i)$. An empirical measure is the case $a_i=\frac1n$ (a data set of $n$ points).

The push-forward of $\al$ by a map $T$ moves each bit of mass from $x$ to $T(x)$: $\pf{T}{\al}(B)=\al(T^{-1}(B))$, and for a discrete measure $\pf{T}{\Big(\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i}\Big)}=\sum_ia_i\delta_{T(x_i)}$: the weights travel with their points, and two atoms sent to the same point merge. In the language of random variables, if $X\sim\al$ then $T(X)\sim\pf{T}{\al}$. Used in §1 (Monge), §8 (marginals are push-forwards by projections) and §11 (interpolation). More.

Refresher — convex sets, polytopes, extreme points, linear programs

A set $\mathcal C$ is convex if it contains the segment between any two of its points. A polytope is a bounded set defined by finitely many linear equalities and inequalities; it is convex and compact. An extreme point (or vertex) of a convex set is a point that is not the midpoint of two other points of the set: the corners of a polygon, the endpoints of a segment; a disk has all the points of its circle as extreme points, an open half-plane none.

A linear program (LP) minimises a linear function $\ps{c}{p}$ under linear equalities and inequalities. Two facts are used: (i) a linear function on a non-empty polytope attains its minimum, and among the minimisers there is a vertex; (ii) every LP has a dual LP whose value is the same (strong duality), and an optimal primal–dual pair satisfies complementary slackness. Both are proved on this page in the case we need (§5 and §7). More.

Refresher — bipartite graphs, cycles, trees and forests

A bipartite graph has two families of vertices (here: the $n$ sources and the $m$ targets) and edges only between the families. A cycle is a closed path that uses each vertex at most once; in a bipartite graph it alternates source, target, source, target… and has an even number of edges. A graph without cycles is a forest; a connected forest is a tree.

The counting fact used in §5: a forest with $k$ vertices has at most $k-1$ edges. Proof: a tree with $k_c$ vertices has exactly $k_c-1$ edges (remove a leaf and its edge, and induct); a forest is a disjoint union of trees, so its number of edges is $\sum_c(k_c-1)=k-(\text{number of trees})\le k-1$.

Refresher — permutations and permutation matrices

A permutation $\sigma$ of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ is a bijection; there are $n!$ of them ($3!=6$, $10!\approx3.6\times10^6$, $20!\approx2.4\times10^{18}$). Its permutation matrix $P_\sigma$ has a $1$ in position $(i,\sigma(i))$ and $0$ elsewhere: exactly one $1$ in each row and each column. In the figures we write a permutation in one line, $\sigma=[\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(n)]$. Used in §3 and §6. More.

Refresher — what Lesson 1 established (summary of §1)

Monge’s problem: $\min\{\int c(x,T(x))\,\dd\al(x):\ \pf{T}{\al}=\be\}$. Between $n$ points and $n$ points with equal weights it is the assignment problem $\min_\sigma\frac1n\sum_iC_{i,\sigma(i)}$. On the line with a convex cost, sorting is optimal; between Gaussians, the optimal map is affine; on $\R^d$ with $\norm{x-y}^2$ and a source with a density, the optimal map is $\nabla\varphi$ with $\varphi$ convex (Brenier). Section 1 recalls these statements with their references. More; the full treatment is in Lesson 1.

Notation decoder

The page uses the notation of the course book (OT4ML). The other resources use other letters; the table says how to translate.

SymbolReadMeaning
$a\in\Sigma_n$, $b\in\Sigma_m$“histograms”Weights of the source points $x_1,\dots,x_n$ and of the target points $y_1,\dots,y_m$.
$\ind_n$“the all-ones vector”$(1,\dots,1)\in\R^n$. So $P\ind_m$ is the vector of row sums of $P$, $P\T\ind_n$ the vector of column sums.
$\U(a,b)$“couplings of $a$ and $b$”Matrices $P\ge0$ with row sums $a$ and column sums $b$ (§2). The sheet writes $\Pi(\al,\be)$, StatOT $\Gamma_{\mu,\nu}$.
$P_{ij}$“mass from $i$ to $j$”How much of the mass at $x_i$ is sent to $y_j$.
$C$, $C_{ij}=c(x_i,y_j)$“cost matrix”Price of moving one unit of mass from $x_i$ to $y_j$.
$\ps{C}{P}$“total cost of $P$”$\sum_{i,j}C_{ij}P_{ij}$.
$\mathrm L_C(a,b)$“Kantorovich value”$\min_{P\in\U(a,b)}\ps{C}{P}$ (discrete problem, §2).
$a\otimes b$“product coupling”$(a\otimes b)_{ij}=a_ib_j$: source and target chosen independently.
$P_\sigma$; $\mathcal B_n$“permutation matrix”; “Birkhoff polytope”§3: the $0/1$ matrix of $\sigma$; the set of bistochastic matrices (row and column sums $1$). Peyré–Cuturi put $\frac1n$ instead of $1$ in $P_\sigma$.
$\mathrm{Extr}(\mathcal C)$“extreme points”Points of $\mathcal C$ that are not midpoints of two other points of $\mathcal C$ (§5).
$\pi\in\U(\al,\be)$“coupling of measures”A probability on pairs $(x,y)$ with marginals $\al$ and $\be$ (§8).
$P_\X$, $P_\Y$“projections”$P_\X(x,y)=x$, $P_\Y(x,y)=y$; the marginals of $\pi$ are $\pf{(P_\X)}{\pi}$ and $\pf{(P_\Y)}{\pi}$.
$\Lc(\al,\be)$“OT cost for $c$”$\inf_{\pi\in\U(\al,\be)}\int c\,\dd\pi$ (general problem, §8).
$(\Id,T)_\#\al$“graph coupling of $T$”The coupling of the map $T$: “$Y=T(X)$”.
$C_\al$, $C_\al^{-1}$“CDF”, “quantile function”$C_\al(x)=\al((-\infty,x])$ and its generalized inverse (§1, §9). The book writes $\mathcal C_\al$.
$f,g$ or $u,v$“dual potentials, prices”Numbers attached to sources and targets with $f_i+g_j\le C_{ij}$ (§7; Lesson 4).
