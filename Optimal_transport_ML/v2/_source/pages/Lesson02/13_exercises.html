13. More exercises

Graded from ★ (direct application) to ★★★ (needs an idea). Try before opening the hint; open the hint before the solution. Every number was checked with an exact solver.

Exercise 13.1 — A $2\times2$ problem from scratch ★

$a=(\frac12,\frac12)$ at $x=(0,1)$, $b=(\frac13,\frac23)$ at $y=(0,1)$, cost $\abs{x-y}^2$. Describe $\U(a,b)$, find the optimal plan and its cost, and the cost of the product plan.

Hint

Remark 3.2 with $p=\frac12$, $q=\frac13$; here $C=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$.

Solution

$P(s)=\begin{pmatrix}s&\frac12-s\\\frac13-s&\frac16+s\end{pmatrix}$ with $s\in[\max(0,\frac12+\frac13-1),\min(\frac12,\frac13)]=[0,\frac13]$. Cost $(\frac12-s)+(\frac13-s)=\frac56-2s$, minimal at $s=\frac13$: $P^\star=\begin{pmatrix}\frac13&\frac16\\0&\frac12\end{pmatrix}$, cost $\frac16$ (the source at $0$ fills the target at $0$ and sends its extra $\frac16$ to $1$). The product plan is $s=\frac16$, cost $\frac12$.

Exercise 13.2 — Maps are couplings ★

$\al=\frac14(\delta_0+\delta_1+\delta_2+\delta_3)$, $\be=\frac12(\delta_0+\delta_1)$, cost $\abs{x-y}^2$. (a) Write the coupling of the map $T(0)=T(1)=0$, $T(2)=T(3)=1$, check its marginals and compute its cost. (b) Same for $T(0)=T(3)=0$, $T(1)=T(2)=1$. (c) Is there a coupling cheaper than (a)?

Hint

$P_{ij}=a_i$ if $T(x_i)=y_j$. For (c), the points are sorted on the line.

Solution

(a) $P=\frac14\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&0\\0&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$: row sums $\frac14$, column sums $\frac12$. Cost $\frac14(0+1+1+4)=1.5$. (b) $P=\frac14\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\\0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, cost $\frac14(0+0+1+9)=2.5$. (c) No: plan (a) is the north-west plan on sorted points, optimal for a convex cost (Prop. 3.10); the LP value is $1.5$. Here Monge and Kantorovich agree (every source atom has mass $\frac14$, and each target needs exactly two of them).

Exercise 13.3 — When every coupling is optimal ★★

(a) Show that if $C_{ij}=u_i+v_j$ for all $(i,j)$, every coupling of $(a,b)$ has the same cost $\ps{u}{a}+\ps{v}{b}$. (b) For a $2\times2$ problem with positive weights, show that all couplings are optimal if and only if $C_{11}+C_{22}=C_{12}+C_{21}$. (c) Apply to $x=(0,1)$, $y=(1,2)$, $c=\abs{x-y}$, uniform weights.

Hint

(a) Use the marginals as in the proof of weak duality (§7). (b) Along the segment $P(s)$ of Remark 3.2, the cost is affine in $s$: compute its slope.

Solution

(a) $\sum_{ij}P_{ij}(u_i+v_j)=\sum_iu_i\sum_jP_{ij}+\sum_jv_j\sum_iP_{ij}=\ps{u}{a}+\ps{v}{b}$, independent of $P$. (b) $\ps{C}{P(s)}=sC_{11}+(p-s)C_{12}+(q-s)C_{21}+(1-p-q+s)C_{22}=\text{const}+s\,(C_{11}+C_{22}-C_{12}-C_{21})$. With positive weights the segment is non-degenerate, so the cost is constant on it iff the slope vanishes. (Conversely, $C_{11}+C_{22}=C_{12}+C_{21}$ means exactly that $C$ is of the form $u_i+v_j$.) (c) $C=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$: $1+1=2+0$, so every coupling costs $1$ — the sorted plan, the crossed one and everything in between. It is $c(x,y)=y-x$ on these four pairs ($u=-x$, $v=y$): the discrete book-shifting phenomenon (§8), and the reason why Prop. 3.4 allows the product plan to be optimal.

Exercise 13.4 — The dimension of the transport polytope ★★

Let $\mathcal A(P)=(P\ind_m,P\T\ind_n)\in\R^{n+m}$. (a) Show that $\mathcal A$ has rank $n+m-1$. (b) Deduce that, when all $a_i,b_j>0$, $\U(a,b)$ has dimension $(n-1)(m-1)$.

Hint

(a) A vector $(u,v)$ orthogonal to the image satisfies $\ps{u}{P\ind}+\ps{v}{P\T\ind}=0$ for all $P$; test on the elementary matrices $e_ie_j\T$. (b) The product plan has all entries positive.

Solution

(a) The image is contained in $\{(r,c):\sum_ir_i=\sum_jc_j\}$, a hyperplane, so the rank is $\le n+m-1$. If $(u,v)$ is orthogonal to the image, testing on $P=e_ie_j\T$ gives $u_i+v_j=0$ for all $i,j$, so $u=\lambda\ind_n$, $v=-\lambda\ind_m$: the orthogonal of the image is one-dimensional and the rank is exactly $n+m-1$ (OT4ML proof of Prop. 3.8). (b) The affine space $\{P:\mathcal A(P)=(a,b)\}$ has dimension $nm-(n+m-1)=(n-1)(m-1)$, and it contains the point $a\otimes b$ with all entries positive; a small ball around it in the affine space stays in $\R_+^{n\times m}$, so $\U(a,b)$ has full dimension $(n-1)(m-1)$ in it.

Exercise 13.5 — The north-west rule in the wrong order ★★

$a=(0.2,0.5,0.3)$ at $x=(0,1,2)$, $b=(0.5,0.1,0.4)$ at $y=(0.5,1.5,2.5)$, cost $\abs{x-y}^2$. Run the north-west rule with the targets processed in the order $y_3,y_2,y_1$. Give the plan (columns in the original order), its cost, and compare with the optimum.

Hint

Run the rule on $a$ and $b'=(0.4,0.1,0.5)$, then put the columns back.

Solution

Steps on $(a,b')$: $0.2$ in (1, $y_3$); $0.2$ in (2, $y_3$); $0.1$ in (2, $y_2$); $0.2$ in (2, $y_1$); $0.3$ in (3, $y_1$). In the original column order: $P=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0.2\\0.2&0.1&0.2\\0.3&0&0\end{pmatrix}$, still a vertex with $5$ entries. Cost $0.2\cdot6.25+0.2\cdot0.25+0.1\cdot0.25+0.2\cdot2.25+0.3\cdot2.25=2.45$, against the optimum $0.45$ (the north-west plan on sorted targets, §4). The rule produces a vertex whatever the order; only the sorted order makes it optimal.

Exercise 13.6 — A Birkhoff–von Neumann decomposition ★★

Decompose $P=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.5&0&0\\0.25&0.25&0.5&0\\0.25&0&0.25&0.5\\0&0.25&0.25&0.5\end{pmatrix}$ into permutation matrices. How many terms does Carathéodory allow?

Hint

All entries are multiples of $\frac14$: look for four permutations with weight $\frac14$ each. Row 1 says that two of them start with $1$ and two with $2$.

Solution

$P=\frac14\big(P_{[1,2,3,4]}+P_{[1,3,4,2]}+P_{[2,1,4,3]}+P_{[2,3,1,4]}\big)$ (one-line notation). Check row by row: row 1 receives columns $1,1,2,2$ ✓; row 2 columns $2,3,1,3$ ✓ ($\frac14,\frac14,\frac12$); row 3 columns $3,4,4,1$ ✓; row 4 columns $4,2,3,4$ ✓. The greedy algorithm of §6 (widget, “largest $\lambda$ first”) finds these four. Carathéodory allows up to $(4-1)^2+1=10$ terms.

Exercise 13.7 — Rational weights by duplication ★★

$\al=\frac12(\delta_0+\delta_1)$, $\be=\frac14\delta_0+\frac14\delta_2+\frac12\delta_3$, cost $\abs{x-y}^2$. Solve by duplicating the atoms into two uniform clouds of $N=4$ points, then collapse.

Hint

Sources $\{0,0,1,1\}$, targets $\{0,2,3,3\}$, all of mass $\frac14$; on the line, sort and match.

Solution

Sorted matching: $0\to0$, $0\to2$, $1\to3$, $1\to3$; average cost $\frac14(0+4+4+4)=3$. Collapsing the counts: $4P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix}$, i.e. $P=\begin{pmatrix}\frac14&\frac14&0\\0&0&\frac12\end{pmatrix}$ — the source at $0$ splits between $0$ and $2$. The LP gives the same value $3$ (and the north-west plan is this one, as it should on the line).

Exercise 13.8 — A certificate of optimality ★★

For the $2\times2$ example of §2 ($a=(0.5,0.5)$, $b=(0.3,0.7)$, $C=\begin{pmatrix}1&9\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$), find all pairs $(f,g)$ that certify the optimality of $P^\star=\begin{pmatrix}0.3&0.2\\0&0.5\end{pmatrix}$, and check that their revenue is $2.6$.

Hint

Three equations on the support, one inequality on the remaining cell; the system has one degree of freedom (adding $\lambda$ to $f$ and subtracting it from $g$).

Solution

Tightness: $f_1+g_1=1$, $f_1+g_2=9$, $f_2+g_2=1$. With $f_1=\lambda$: $g_1=1-\lambda$, $g_2=9-\lambda$, $f_2=-8+\lambda$. The last cell needs $f_2+g_1=-7\le1$: always true. So the certificates are $(f,g)=(\lambda,-8+\lambda;\ 1-\lambda,9-\lambda)$, $\lambda\in\R$ (e.g. $f=(0,-8)$, $g=(1,9)$). Revenue: $0.5\lambda+0.5(\lambda-8)+0.3(1-\lambda)+0.7(9-\lambda)=-4+0.3+6.3=2.6$ for every $\lambda$, equal to $\ps{C}{P^\star}$. The reduced cost of the empty cell is $C_{21}-f_2-g_1=8$: pushing mass into it would cost $8$ per unit.

Exercise 13.9 — Uniqueness on the line ★★

Let $x_1<\dotsunique optimal coupling.

Hint

Show that an optimal plan has no “crossed pair” $P_{ij}>0$, $P_{i'j'}>0$ with $ij'$ (strict Monge inequality), then that the only coupling without crossed pairs is the north-west plan.

Solution

No crossed pair. If $P_{ij},P_{i'j'}>0$ with $ij'$, move $\eta=\min(P_{ij},P_{i'j'})$ to the cells $(i,j')$, $(i',j)$: marginals unchanged, and the cost changes by $\eta[h(x_i-y_{j'})+h(x_{i'}-y_j)-h(x_i-y_j)-h(x_{i'}-y_{j'})]<0$ — the Monge inequality of §4 is strict when $h$ is strictly convex and $\delta,\eta>0$ (strictly increasing increments). So an optimal plan has no crossed pair.

Only the north-west plan has no crossed pair. Let $P$ have no crossed pair. If $P_{11}<\min(a_1,b_1)$, there are $P_{1j}>0$ with $j>1$ and $P_{i1}>0$ with $i>1$: a crossed pair. So $P_{11}=\min(a_1,b_1)$, the first north-west assignment; remove the exhausted row or column and induct. Hence the unique optimum is the north-west plan. (For $h=\abs\cdot$, uniqueness fails: Exercise 13.3(c).)

Hint

(a) Keep the outer atoms in place. Lower bound: the middle mass must move by at least $1$. (b) A map sends each atom whole to $\pm1$; which subsets of $\{w,1-2w,w\}$ sum to $\frac12$?

Solution

Exercise 13.11 — Lower bounds for the Word Mover’s Distance ★★★

Let $\mathrm{WMD}=\mathrm L_C(a,b)$ with $C_{ij}=\norm{x_i-y_j}$ (§7). (a) Show $\norm{\sum_ia_ix_i-\sum_jb_jy_j}\le\mathrm{WMD}$ (word centroid distance). (b) Show that dropping the column constraints gives $\sum_ia_i\min_j\norm{x_i-y_j}\le\mathrm{WMD}$, and similarly with rows; the relaxed WMD is the maximum of the two. (c) Compute everything for $x_1=(0,0)$, $x_2=(4,0)$, $y_1=(0,3)$, $y_2=(1,0)$, all weights $\frac12$.

Hint

(a) For any coupling, $\sum_{ij}P_{ij}(x_i-y_j)=\sum_ia_ix_i-\sum_jb_jy_j$; use the triangle inequality for sums. (b) Without the column constraints each row can put all its mass on its cheapest column.

Solution

(a) For an optimal $P$: $\norm{\sum_ia_ix_i-\sum_jb_jy_j}=\norm{\sum_{ij}P_{ij}(x_i-y_j)}\le\sum_{ij}P_{ij}\norm{x_i-y_j}=\mathrm{WMD}$ (the marginals give the first equality, the triangle inequality the second step). (b) The problem $\min\{\ps{C}{P}:P\ge0,\ P\ind=a\}$ has a larger feasible set, hence a smaller value; it decouples row by row, and row $i$ puts its mass $a_i$ on $\arg\min_jC_{ij}$: value $\sum_ia_i\min_jC_{ij}$. Same with columns. (c) $C=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\5&3\end{pmatrix}$. WMD: the two couplings that are maps both cost $\frac12(3+3)=\frac12(1+5)=3$, and the LP gives $3$. Centroids $(2,0)$ and $(0.5,1.5)$: WCD $=\sqrt{4.5}\approx2.121$. Relaxed: rows $\frac12(1+3)=2$, columns $\frac12(3+1)=2$, so RWMD $=2$. Both bounds are below $3$; on this toy the centroid bound happens to be the tighter one, whereas on real documents Kusner et al. found the relaxed WMD much closer to the WMD — which is why they use it to prune nearest-neighbour searches.

Exercise 13.12 — The Carathéodory bound is attained ★★★

Show that a bistochastic matrix chosen “at random” (with a density on the affine space of $\mathcal B_n$) needs exactly $(n-1)^2+1$ permutation matrices in any Birkhoff–von Neumann decomposition.

Hint

What is the dimension of the set of convex combinations of $k$ given matrices? How many such sets are there?

Solution

A convex combination of $k$ fixed permutation matrices lies in their convex hull, a set of dimension at most $k-1$. There are finitely many sets of at most $(n-1)^2$ permutation matrices, and each hull has dimension at most $(n-1)^2-1$, i.e. it is a null set in the $(n-1)^2$-dimensional affine space of $\mathcal B_n$. A finite union of null sets is null, so a matrix drawn with a density avoids it almost surely: it needs at least $(n-1)^2+1$ terms, and Carathéodory says that many suffice. In our tests the greedy algorithm used exactly $5$ terms for random $3\times3$ matrices and $10$ for $4\times4$ ones; the widget of §6 shows $17$ for $5\times5$. So the bound is not pessimism: it is the typical case.
