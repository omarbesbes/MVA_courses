8. The general Kantorovich problem

For arbitrary distributions, a coupling is a probability on pairs $(x,y)$ whose two marginals are $\al$ and $\be$ — the joint law of a random pair $(X,Y)$ with $X\sim\al$, $Y\sim\be$; the Kantorovich problem chooses the cheapest dependence, and a cheapest one always exists.

Couplings of arbitrary measures

The problem. Data are discrete, but models are not: a generative model produces a continuous law, a Gaussian is not a histogram. We need couplings between any two probability measures — discrete, with densities, or mixtures of both.

In the course OT4ML Def. 3.25–Rem. 3.27, pp. 63–64

Definition 3.25 (Marginals of a joint measure). Let $\pi\in\M(\X\times\Y)$ and let $P_\X(x,y)=x$, $P_\Y(x,y)=y$ be the coordinate projections. The marginals of $\pi$ are $\pi_1:=\pf{(P_\X)}{\pi}\in\M(\X)$, $\pi_2:=\pf{(P_\Y)}{\pi}\in\M(\Y)$. Equivalently, for all bounded continuous test functions $f$ on $\X$ and $g$ on $\Y$, $\int_{\X\times\Y}f(x)\,\dd\pi(x,y)=\int_\X f\,\dd\pi_1$ and $\int_{\X\times\Y}g(y)\,\dd\pi(x,y)=\int_\Y g\,\dd\pi_2$.

[…] or equivalently by the identities $\pi(A\times\Y)=\al(A)$ and $\pi(\X\times B)=\be(B)$ for measurable sets $A\subset\X$ and $B\subset\Y$.

Definition 3.26 (Couplings). Given $\al\in\M(\X)$ and $\be\in\M(\Y)$, the set of couplings between $\al$ and $\be$ is $\U(\al,\be):=\{\pi\in\M(\X\times\Y)\ ;\ \pi_1=\al\text{ and }\pi_2=\be\}$. This is the continuous analogue of the transportation polytope (3.1).

Remark 3.27 (Probabilistic interpretation of couplings). If $X\sim\al$ and $Y\sim\be$, then $\pi\in\U(\al,\be)$ means that $\pi$ is the law of a pair $(X,Y)$ whose coordinates have laws $\al$ and $\be$. The coupling encodes the dependence between $X$ and $Y$. The tensor product $\al\otimes\be$ corresponds to independence, whereas a graph coupling $\pf{(\Id,T)}{\al}$ corresponds to the deterministic relation $Y=T(X)$. In the discrete case, when $\al=\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i}$ and $\be=\sum_jb_j\delta_{y_j}$, the constraint $\pi_1=\al$ and $\pi_2=\be$ forces every coupling to have the form $\pi=\sum_{i,j}P_{i,j}\delta_{(x_i,y_j)}$ with $P\in\U(a,b)$. The discrete formulation is therefore a special case of the continuous one, not merely an approximation.

$\pi(A\times B)$
The mass that starts in $A$ and ends in $B$.
$\pf{(P_\X)}{\pi}=\al$
“Forget where the mass goes, keep where it comes from: you get $\al$.” The continuous version of “row sums $=a$”: $\pi(A\times\Y)=\al(A)$, i.e. $\int_\Y\dd\pi(x,y)=\dd\al(x)$ (the book’s mnemonic).
$\pf{(P_\Y)}{\pi}=\be$
“Column sums $=b$”: $\pi(\X\times B)=\be(B)$.
with test functions
$\int f(x)\,\dd\pi(x,y)=\int f\,\dd\al$: an expectation that only involves $X$ is computed with $\al$. This form is the one that passes to limits (existence, below).
$(X,Y)\sim\pi$
The laws of $X$ and $Y$ are fixed; the coupling is the dependence between them.

Three kinds of couplings come back all the time (OT4ML Def. 3.28 and p. 65):

  • The product $\al\otimes\be$: $\int h\,\dd(\al\otimes\be)=\int\big(\int h(x,y)\,\dd\be(y)\big)\dd\al(x)$; $X$ and $Y$ independent. Always a coupling, so $\U(\al,\be)$ is never empty.
  • Graph couplings $\pi=\pf{(\Id,T)}{\al}$ for a map with $\pf{T}{\al}=\be$, characterised by $\int h(x,y)\,\dd\pi=\int h(x,T(x))\,\dd\al(x)$. Taking $h(x,y)=f(x)$ and $h(x,y)=g(y)$ gives the two marginals $\al$ and $\pf{T}{\al}=\be$. “Graph couplings are precisely the Kantorovich representation of deterministic Monge maps.”
  • Semi-discrete couplings: $\al$ with a density, $\be=\sum_jb_j\delta_{y_j}$ discrete. Every coupling lives on the slices $\X\times\{y_j\}$: $\pi=\sum_j\al_j\otimes\delta_{y_j}$, where the pieces $\al_j$ (the part of $\al$ sent to $y_j$) have masses $b_j$ and add up to $\al$. When the optimal coupling is a map, the pieces are the cells of a partition of $\X$ (Laguerre cells, Lesson 4).
Worked example — choosing the dependence
  • Two uniform laws on $[0,1]$, cost $\abs{x-y}^2$, with $X\sim\mathcal U[0,1]$. Three couplings: $Y=X$ (cost $0$), $Y=1-X$ (cost $\E(2X-1)^2=\frac13$), $Y$ independent of $X$ (cost $2\,\mathrm{Var}(X)=\frac16$). Same marginals, three different costs; the identity coupling is optimal.
  • Two standard Gaussians (StatOT eq. (1.1), p. 19): take $(X,Y)$ jointly Gaussian with correlation $\rho\in[-1,1]$. Then $\E(X-Y)^2=2-2\rho$: $4$ for $\rho=-1$, $2$ for independence, $1$ for $\rho=\frac12$, $0$ for $\rho=1$ ($Y=X$). Kantorovich picks the most correlated coupling.
In machine learning — choosing the coupling in flow matching

Flow matching (Lesson 6) trains a neural velocity field $v_\theta(t,x)$ that transports a noise law $\al=\N(0,I)$ to the data law $\be$. Training needs pairs $(X_0,X_1)$ with $X_0\sim\al$ and $X_1\sim\be$ — that is, a coupling — and regresses $v_\theta(t,(1-t)X_0+tX_1)$ onto $X_1-X_0$. The simplest choice is the independent coupling $\al\otimes\be$: draw noise and data independently. Its straight paths cross each other a lot, and the learned flow is curved. Replacing it by an optimal coupling computed on each minibatch (an exact discrete transport problem between, say, $256$ noise samples and $256$ data samples — by Birkhoff–von Neumann, a permutation) gives straighter, non-crossing paths and flows that need fewer integration steps (“minibatch OT” couplings, Tong et al. 2023; Pooladian et al. 2023).

The general problem

In the course OT4ML Def. 3.31–Rem. 3.33, p. 66

Also StatOT, problem (KOT), p. 17.

Definition 3.31 (Continuous Kantorovich problem). For $\al\in\M(\X)$, $\be\in\M(\Y)$, and a nonnegative Borel cost $c:\X\times\Y\to[0,+\infty]$, define

$$\Lc(\al,\be):=\inf_{\pi\in\U(\al,\be)}\int_{\X\times\Y}c(x,y)\,\dd\pi(x,y).\qquad(3.7)$$

A minimizer is an optimal coupling, or optimal transport plan. The value is understood in $[0,+\infty]$. This is an infinite-dimensional linear program over a space of measures.

Proposition 3.32. […] the extended-valued map $(\al,\be)\mapsto\Lc(\al,\be)$ is jointly convex on pairs of probability measures. Conversely, for fixed marginals $(\al,\be)$, the map $c\mapsto\Lc(\al,\be)$ is concave on the cone of nonnegative Borel costs.

Remark 3.33 (Probabilistic interpretation of Kantorovich’s problem). $\Lc(\al,\be)=\inf_{X\sim\al,\,Y\sim\be}\E\big(c(X,Y)\big)$. The minimization is not over the marginal laws, which are fixed, but over all possible dependences between the two random variables.

Borel cost, values in $[0,+\infty]$
$c$ may take the value $+\infty$ to forbid pairs (as in the sheet’s Exercise 5).
“infinite-dimensional linear program”
The unknown $\pi$ ranges over a convex set, the constraints $\pi_1=\al$, $\pi_2=\be$ are linear, and $\pi\mapsto\int c\,\dd\pi$ is linear.
Prop. 3.32
The same two-line proof as Prop. 3.6, with measures instead of matrices.
“inf”
Written $\inf$ because existence is not part of the definition — it is the next proposition.

Two consequences, as in the discrete case. Since a map gives a graph coupling with the same cost, $\Lc(\al,\be)\le\mathcal M_c(\al,\be)$. And since $\al\otimes\be$ is a coupling, $\Lc(\al,\be)\le\iint c\,\dd\al\,\dd\be$, which is finite as soon as, for instance, $c$ is bounded, or $c=\norm{x-y}^p$ and both measures have finite $p$-th moments.

Worked example — a whole face of optimal couplings (OT4ML Remark 3.30, p. 65)

$\al=\mathcal U[0,2]$, $\be=\mathcal U[1,3]$, $c(x,y)=\abs{y-x}$. For every coupling,

$$\int\abs{y-x}\,\dd\pi\ \ge\ \int(y-x)\,\dd\pi=\int y\,\dd\be-\int x\,\dd\al=2-1=1,$$

with equality exactly for the couplings carried by the half-plane $\{y\ge x\}$. The translation $T(x)=x+1$ and the “book-shifting” map ($x\mapsto x+2$ on $[0,1]$, identity on $[1,2]$) both cost $1$, and so do infinitely many couplings that split mass: the optimal set is a whole face of $\U(\al,\be)$. With the quadratic cost the translation still costs $1$ but the book-shifting map costs $2$: strictly convex costs select a unique answer (§9).

An optimal plan always exists

In the course OT4ML Prop. 3.34, p. 66

Compactness of the set of couplings: StatOT Prop. 1.1, p. 17.

Proposition 3.34 (Existence for lower-semicontinuous costs). Assume that $\X$ and $\Y$ are Polish spaces and that $c:\X\times\Y\to[0,+\infty]$ is lower semicontinuous. Then the Kantorovich problem (3.7) admits at least one minimizer. In particular, this applies to every continuous nonnegative cost on compact metric spaces.

Polish space
A complete separable metric space: $\R^d$, closed subsets of $\R^d$, compact metric spaces.
lower semicontinuous (l.s.c.)
$c(x,y)\le\liminf c(x_k,y_k)$ when $(x_k,y_k)\to(x,y)$: at the limit point the cost may be lower than nearby, never higher. Continuous costs are l.s.c.; so is a cost that is continuous on a closed set of allowed pairs and equal to $+\infty$ on the (open) set of forbidden pairs.
“admits a minimizer”
The infimum is a minimum: an optimal plan exists. Monge’s problem has no such general result (§1, §9).
Reminder — weak convergence, tightness, Prokhorov

Measures $\pi_k$ converge weakly to $\pi$ if $\int\varphi\,\dd\pi_k\to\int\varphi\,\dd\pi$ for every bounded continuous $\varphi$ (Lesson 3 studies it in detail). A family of probability measures is tight if for every $\eps>0$ one compact set carries mass $\ge1-\eps$ under all of them. Prokhorov’s theorem: on a Polish space, a tight family is relatively compact for weak convergence (every sequence has a weakly convergent subsequence). Portmanteau: if $c\ge0$ is l.s.c. and $\pi_k\wto\pi$, then $\int c\,\dd\pi\le\liminf\int c\,\dd\pi_k$. More.

Idea of the proof — the direct method

Take a minimising sequence of couplings. They all have the same marginals, so they cannot let mass escape to infinity: the sequence is tight, hence has a weakly convergent subsequence. The limit is still a coupling (marginals pass to the limit), and the cost can only drop in the limit (lower semicontinuity). So the limit is a minimiser. On a compact space with a continuous cost it is even simpler: $\U(\al,\be)$ is weakly compact and the cost is weakly continuous.

Full proof (OT4ML p. 66; StatOT p. 17)
  1. $\U(\al,\be)\ne\emptyset$, and if $\Lc(\al,\be)=+\infty$ every coupling is a minimiser. Otherwise take $\pi_k\in\U(\al,\be)$ with $\int c\,\dd\pi_k\to\Lc(\al,\be)$.$\al\otimes\be\in\U(\al,\be)$; a minimising sequence exists by definition of the infimum.
  2. $\U(\al,\be)$ is tight: given $\eps>0$ choose compacts $K_\X$, $K_\Y$ with $\al(K_\X)\ge1-\frac\eps2$, $\be(K_\Y)\ge1-\frac\eps2$; then every coupling has $\pi(K_\X\times K_\Y)\ge1-\eps$.A single probability on a Polish space is tight; and $\pi\big((K_\X\times K_\Y)^c\big)\le\pi(K_\X^c\times\Y)+\pi(\X\times K_\Y^c)=\al(K_\X^c)+\be(K_\Y^c)\le\eps$ by the marginal constraints.
  3. By Prokhorov, a subsequence $\pi_{k'}$ converges weakly to some probability $\pi$ on $\X\times\Y$.Tightness (step 2); $\X\times\Y$ is Polish.
  4. $\pi\in\U(\al,\be)$.For bounded continuous $f$ on $\X$, $(x,y)\mapsto f(x)$ is bounded continuous on $\X\times\Y$, so $\int f(x)\,\dd\pi=\lim\int f(x)\,\dd\pi_{k'}=\int f\,\dd\al$; same for $g(y)$. These identities characterise the marginals (Def. 3.25).
  5. $\int c\,\dd\pi\le\liminf\int c\,\dd\pi_{k'}=\Lc(\al,\be)$, so $\pi$ is a minimiser.Portmanteau for the non-negative l.s.c. function $c$; the right-hand side is the limit of a minimising sequence.
Why this exact relaxation? (going further)

OT4ML Theorem 3.35 (p. 67) shows that, on compact spaces with a continuous cost, $\Lc$ is the largest functional of $(\al,\be)$ that is jointly convex, weakly lower semicontinuous, and below $c(x,y)$ on pairs of Diracs $(\delta_x,\delta_y)$. Kantorovich’s problem is not one relaxation among many: it is the convex, stable extension of the ground cost to probability measures.

Pitfall — existence of a plan is not existence of a map

Prop. 3.34 gives an optimal coupling. Whether it is a map is a different question, answered in §9: yes for the quadratic cost from a density (Brenier), on the line from an atomless source, for uniform discrete weights (Birkhoff–von Neumann); not in general (atoms).

What are the couplings of $\delta_x$ and an arbitrary $\be$? (Sheet, Exercise 3.)

Answer

Only one: $\delta_x\otimes\be$. Indeed $\pi\big((\X\setminus\{x\})\times\Y\big)=\delta_x(\X\setminus\{x\})=0$, so $\pi$ lives on $\{x\}\times\Y$, and its second marginal is $\be$. So $\Lc(\delta_x,\be)=\int c(x,y)\,\dd\be(y)$. Full solution in §12.
