7. Solving the linear program

Exact solvers walk from vertex to vertex of the transport polytope (network simplex) or approach it from inside (interior points); optimality is certified by “prices” $f_i,g_j$ with $f_i+g_j\le C_{ij}$, equality wherever mass moves — a first look at the duality of Lesson 4.

The algorithms

In the course OT4ML §3.2, pp. 62–63

The discrete Kantorovich problem is a linear program with much more structure than a generic dense LP. Its variables are the arcs of a complete bipartite network, its equality constraints are flow-conservation constraints, and its extreme points are sparse tree-like couplings. […]

Transportation simplex and network simplex. The transportation simplex goes back to Dantzig’s formulation of the transportation problem. It works on basic feasible couplings, whose positive support is completed into a spanning tree of the bipartite supply-demand graph. Starting from a basis, for instance one produced by the north-west corner rule, reduced costs identify whether an unused arc can decrease the objective. Adding such an arc creates a unique cycle in the tree; one then pushes as much mass as possible around this cycle and removes the exhausted arc. […] Like the ordinary simplex method, its worst-case number of pivots can be exponential for adversarial instances, but the per-pivot operations exploit sparsity and are very efficient in practice. For theoretical polynomial guarantees, one can instead use strongly polynomial minimum-cost-flow algorithms, such as Orlin’s algorithm.

Interior-point methods. Generic interior-point methods approach the same LP through a smooth central path. […] The logarithmic-barrier problem on the resulting transport polytope is $P_\eps:=\argmin_{P\ind_m=a,\ P\T\ind_n=b,\ P_{i,j}>0}\ \ps{C}{P}-\eps\sum_{i,j}\log P_{i,j}$ (3.5), where $\eps>0$ is decreased along the algorithm. […] The comparison with Sinkhorn is therefore subtle. Both methods keep iterates positive, but […] Sinkhorn fixes an entropic temperature $\eps$ and solves a different, KL-regularized OT problem.

basis, basic feasible coupling
A vertex of $\U(a,b)$ together with a spanning tree of $n+m-1$ cells containing its support (zero cells are added when the vertex is degenerate, as in the quiz of §4).
reduced cost
$r_{ij}=C_{ij}-f_i-g_j$, where the prices $f,g$ are fitted so that $r_{ij}=0$ on the tree. It is the change of cost per unit of mass pushed into cell $(i,j)$ around the cycle it closes.
pivot
Enter a cell with $r_{ij}<0$, push the largest amount $\theta$ around the cycle, remove the cell that drops to $0$: the plan moves to a neighbouring vertex, and the cost changes by $\theta\,r_{ij}\le0$.
central path, barrier
$-\eps\sum\log P_{ij}$ keeps every entry positive, so interior-point iterates are dense; they reach a vertex only in the limit $\eps\to0$.

Prices that certify optimality

The problem. A solver returns a plan. How can you be sure it is optimal without trusting the solver? Lesson 1 certified an assignment with numbers $u_i,v_j$; the same works for couplings.

In the course OT4ML Def. 5.1, Props. 5.2–5.3, p. 95

From the duality chapter (Lesson 4); the same facts in CompOT Props. 3.2–3.3, pp. 45–46.

Definition 5.1 (Admissible potentials). […] a pair $(f,g)\in\R^n\times\R^m$ is admissible if it lies below the cost: $\mathcal R(C):=\{(f,g):\ \forall(i,j),\ f_i+g_j\le C_{i,j}\}$.

Proposition 5.2 (Discrete Kantorovich duality). One has $\mathrm L_C(a,b)=\max_{(f,g)\in\mathcal R(C)}\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}$.

Proposition 5.3 (Discrete complementary slackness). For every $P\in\U(a,b)$ and $(f,g)\in\mathcal R(C)$, $\ps{C}{P}-\ps{f}{a}-\ps{g}{b}=\sum_{i,j}P_{i,j}\big(C_{i,j}-f_i-g_j\big)\ge0$. The plan and potentials are respectively primal and dual optimal if and only if $\mathrm{Supp}(P)\subset\{(i,j):\ f_i+g_j=C_{i,j}\}$.

$f_i,g_j$
Prices: in CompOT’s economic reading, a carrier buys the goods at $x_i$ for $f_i$ and sells them at $y_j$ for $g_j$ (signs aside); admissibility means that no route is priced above its transport cost.
$\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}$
The total revenue of the prices, a lower bound on every transport cost.
complementary slackness
Mass may move only along routes where the price is “tight”, $f_i+g_j=C_{ij}$. Then cost and revenue are equal, and both are optimal.
Idea

Use the marginals to rewrite the revenue as a sum over the plan: $\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}=\sum_{ij}P_{ij}(f_i+g_j)$. Subtract from the cost: each term $P_{ij}(C_{ij}-f_i-g_j)$ is $\ge0$. So every revenue is below every cost (weak duality), and they are equal exactly when all the terms vanish.

Full proof of weak duality and of the certificate
  1. $$\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}=\sum_if_i\sum_jP_{ij}+\sum_jg_j\sum_iP_{ij}=\sum_{i,j}P_{ij}(f_i+g_j).$$$a_i=\sum_jP_{ij}$ and $b_j=\sum_iP_{ij}$ (marginals of $P$).
  2. $$\ps{C}{P}-\ps{f}{a}-\ps{g}{b}=\sum_{i,j}P_{ij}(C_{ij}-f_i-g_j)\ \ge\ 0 .$$Each term is a product of $P_{ij}\ge0$ and $C_{ij}-f_i-g_j\ge0$ (admissibility).
  3. If moreover $f_i+g_j=C_{ij}$ wherever $P_{ij}>0$, the sum is $0$: $\ps{C}{P}=\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}$. Then for any other coupling $Q$: $\ps{C}{Q}\ge\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}=\ps{C}{P}$. So $P$ is optimal (and $(f,g)$ maximises the revenue).Step 2 applied to $Q$.

The converse — an optimal plan always comes with such prices, and the two values coincide (strong duality, Prop. 5.2) — is the duality theorem of linear programming; Lesson 4 proves it and studies the potentials.

Worked example — certifying the $3\times3$ assignment of §3

$C=\begin{pmatrix}4&1&3\\2&0&5\\3&2&2\end{pmatrix}$, uniform weights $\frac13$, candidate plan $P=\frac13P_{[2,1,3]}$ (mass on $(1,2),(2,1),(3,3)$). Look for prices tight on these three cells: $f_1+g_2=1$, $f_2+g_1=2$, $f_3+g_3=2$. Take $f=(0,-1,0)$ and $g=(3,1,2)$:

$$C-f\oplus g=\begin{pmatrix}4-3&1-1&3-2\\2+1-3&0+1-1&5+1-2\\3-3&2-1&2-2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\0&0&4\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}\ \ge0 .$$

Admissible, and zero on the support of $P$: certificate. Revenue $\frac13(0-1+0)+\frac13(3+1+2)=\frac53=\ps{C}{P}$. So the assignment $[2,1,3]$ is optimal, without enumerating permutations. (Two further cells, $(2,2)$ and $(3,1)$, are also tight: this problem has several optimal dual solutions.)

The network simplex on an example

Take the $2\times3$ problem of §5, $a=(0.6,0.4)$, $b=(0.3,0.45,0.25)$, with the cost matrix $C=\begin{pmatrix}3&1&2\\1&2&4\end{pmatrix}$ (an arbitrary one: here the north-west corner is not optimal). On the pentagon, the cost is $1+4s+t$.

  1. Start at the north-west vertex $P=\begin{pmatrix}0.3&0.3&0\\0&0.15&0.25\end{pmatrix}$, cost $2.5$. Its support $(1,1),(1,2),(2,2),(2,3)$ is a spanning tree.§4: the rule gives a vertex with $n+m-1=4$ cells.
  2. Prices on the tree: $f_1=0$; then $g_1=C_{11}-f_1=3$, $g_2=1$, $f_2=C_{22}-g_2=1$, $g_3=C_{23}-f_2=3$. Reduced costs off the tree: $r_{13}=2-0-3=-1$, $r_{21}=1-1-3=-3$.Solve $f_i+g_j=C_{ij}$ along the tree, starting from an arbitrary $f_1=0$ (the system has one degree of freedom).
  3. Pivot 1. Enter $(2,1)$ ($r=-3$, the most negative). The cycle is $(2,1)^+,(2,2)^-,(1,2)^+,(1,1)^-$; push $\theta=\min(P_{22},P_{11})=0.15$. New plan $\begin{pmatrix}0.15&0.45&0\\0.15&0&0.25\end{pmatrix}$, cost $2.5-0.15\times3=2.05$; cell $(2,2)$ leaves.Cells marked $-$ lose $\theta$, cells marked $+$ gain it: row and column sums are unchanged; the cost changes by $\theta\,r_{21}$.
  4. Pivot 2. New prices $f=(0,-2)$, $g=(3,1,6)$; $r_{13}=-4$, $r_{22}=3$. Enter $(1,3)$, $\theta=0.15$ (cell $(1,1)$ leaves): plan $\begin{pmatrix}0&0.45&0.15\\0.3&0&0.1\end{pmatrix}$, cost $2.05-0.6=1.45$.Same recipe.
  5. Pivot 3. Prices $f=(0,2)$, $g=(-1,1,2)$; $r_{11}=4$, $r_{22}=-1$. Enter $(2,2)$, $\theta=0.1$ (cell $(2,3)$ leaves): plan $P^\star=\begin{pmatrix}0&0.35&0.25\\0.3&0.1&0\end{pmatrix}$, cost $1.35$.Cost change $0.1\times(-1)$.
  6. Stop. Prices $f=(0,1)$, $g=(0,1,2)$: reduced costs $r_{11}=3$, $r_{23}=1$, both $\ge0$. The prices are admissible and tight on the support: $P^\star$ is optimal, and the revenue $0\cdot0.6+1\cdot0.4+0\cdot0.3+1\cdot0.45+2\cdot0.25=1.35$ equals the cost.Prop. 5.3. An exact LP solver confirms $1.35$.
Degenerate pivots

When a vertex has fewer than $n+m-1$ positive entries, the tree contains zero cells, and a pivot may push $\theta=0$: the tree changes, the plan does not. Such degenerate pivots are the rule for assignment problems (permutations have only $n$ entries out of $2n-1$), and a careless choice of entering cells can cycle forever; anti-cycling rules (e.g. Bland’s) prevent it. In the widget, the weights $a=(\frac12,\frac12)$, $b=(\frac14,\frac12,\frac14)$ produce degenerate vertices.

In practice: the numerics session and POT

The numerics session of this lecture (notebook 1-linprog) solves the transport LP with CVXPY, a modelling language that hands the problem to a generic solver. Written in CVXPY, the problem is literally its definition (our own transcription, in the notation of this page):

import cvxpy as cp
P = cp.Variable((n, m))
u, v = np.ones((m, 1)), np.ones((n, 1))
constraints = [0 <= P, P @ u == a, P.T @ v == b]
objective = cp.Minimize(cp.sum(cp.multiply(P, C)))
cp.Problem(objective, constraints).solve()

Interior-point solutions are dense up to rounding: to count the non-zero entries one thresholds them (say $P_{ij}>10^{-5}$), and the count depends on the threshold. A network-simplex solver returns an exact vertex. The library POT (Python Optimal Transport) provides one, ot.emd; with log=True it also returns dual potentials, so the certificate of Prop. 5.3 can be checked:

import numpy as np, ot
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, m = 6, 8
xs, ys = rng.random((n, 2)), rng.random((m, 2))          # two point clouds in the plane
a = rng.random(n); a /= a.sum(); b = rng.random(m); b /= b.sum()
M = ot.dist(xs, ys)                                       # squared Euclidean cost matrix
P, log = ot.emd(a, b, M, log=True)                        # exact optimal plan (network simplex)
print(ot.emd2(a, b, M))                                   # 0.07575528019451169
print((P > 1e-12).sum(), n + m - 1)                       # 13 13   (a vertex: n + m - 1 entries)
f, g = log["u"], log["v"]                                 # dual potentials
print((f[:, None] + g[None, :] - M).max())                # 3.1e-15  (admissible, up to rounding)
print(f @ a + g @ b)                                      # 0.07575528019451207  (= the cost)

The outputs above are those of our run (POT 0.9.7); SciPy’s HiGHS LP solver gives the same value. With random weights the problem is non-degenerate and the optimal vertex has exactly $n+m-1=13$ entries.

In machine learning — the Word Mover’s Distance between documents

Kusner, Sun, Kolkin and Weinberger (From word embeddings to document distances, ICML 2015) represent a document by the normalised frequencies $a_i$ of its words (stop words removed) and each word by its word2vec embedding $x_i\in\R^{300}$. The Word Mover’s Distance between two documents is $\mathrm L_C(a,b)$ with $C_{ij}=\norm{x_i-y_j}$: how far the words of one document must “travel” in embedding space to become the words of the other. Two sentences with no word in common — “Obama speaks to the media in Illinois” and “The President greets the press in Chicago”, the example of the paper — are close, because each word has a nearby partner. On eight document-classification data sets, the paper reports lower $k$-nearest-neighbour error rates with this distance than with seven competing document representations. Its weakness is cost: an exact LP per pair of documents. The paper therefore prunes with two cheap lower bounds: the distance between the mean embeddings, $\norm{\sum_ia_ix_i-\sum_jb_jy_j}$, and the “relaxed” WMD obtained by dropping one of the two marginal constraints, so that every word simply goes to its nearest word in the other document (Exercise 13.11).

Pitfall — exact transport does not scale

A dense $n\times n$ problem has $n^2$ variables; network simplex codes handle a few thousand points, not millions. For large problems, use the structure (the line: §4; sliced transport: Lesson 5) or change the problem (entropic regularization and Sinkhorn: Lesson 7) — which also gives smooth, differentiable losses.

Reflexes
  • To prove a plan optimal → find prices $f,g$ with $f_i+g_j\le C_{ij}$ everywhere and $=$ on its support.
  • To compute prices for a vertex → set one price to $0$ and solve $f_i+g_j=C_{ij}$ along the support tree.
  • Negative reduced cost $r_{ij}$ → pushing mass into cell $(i,j)$ around its cycle lowers the cost by $\abs{r_{ij}}$ per unit.

For the $2\times2$ example of §2 ($C=\begin{pmatrix}1&9\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$, optimal plan $\begin{pmatrix}0.3&0.2\\0&0.5\end{pmatrix}$), find prices certifying optimality.

Answer

Tight on the support: $f_1+g_1=1$, $f_1+g_2=9$, $f_2+g_2=1$. With $f_1=0$: $g=(1,9)$, $f_2=-8$. Check the last cell: $f_2+g_1=-7\le C_{21}=1$ ✓ (reduced cost $8$). Revenue $0.5\cdot0+0.5\cdot(-8)+0.3\cdot1+0.7\cdot9=2.6=$ the cost. Optimal.
