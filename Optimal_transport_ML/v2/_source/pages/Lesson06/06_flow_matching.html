6. Flow matching: learn the velocity by regression OT4ML §16.1, pp. 407–410

Choose a path between noise and data by interpolating random pairs $(X_0,X_1)$; the velocity field that moves the distribution along this path is the conditional expectation $\E[X_1-X_0\mid X_t=x]$, so it can be learned by plain least squares on samples — and integrating it from noise produces data.

The idea: fix the path first

Training a continuous normalizing flow by maximum likelihood (§1) needs to simulate the ODE and its Jacobian traces at every step. Flow matching avoids this in two moves:

  1. Choose the path $(\al_t)$ in advance, in a way that is trivial to sample: draw a pair $(X_0,X_1)$ (noise, data) and put $X_t$ on the segment between them.
  2. Find a velocity that moves this path. By §1 such a velocity exists (many, in fact); flow matching takes a specific one, an average of the velocities of the segments, which can be fitted by regression.
In the course OT4ML Def. 16.1, p. 407, and Example 16.2, p. 408

Definition 16.1 (Deterministic interpolant). Let $\pi\in\U(\al_0,\al_1)$. A deterministic interpolant is a measurable family $I_t:\R^d\times\R^d\to\R^d$, differentiable in $t$, satisfying $I_0(x,y)=x$, $I_1(x,y)=y$. It induces $\al_t=\pf{(I_t)}{\pi}$ and has pathwise velocity $\partial_tI_t(x,y)$.

Example 16.2 (Linear two-endpoint deterministic interpolants). […] The simplest choices are the independent, or trivial, coupling $\pi=\al_0\otimes\al_1$ and the straight path $I_t(x,y)=(1-t)x+ty$. With this linear path and an arbitrary coupling $\pi$, the regression below is the common core of flow matching and rectified flow: Lipman et al. emphasize conditional probability paths and simulation-free training of continuous normalizing flows, while rectified flow emphasizes straight couplings, reflow, and the possibility of reducing transport costs and discretization error. […] If $\pi=\al\otimes\be$ and $\al=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$, $\be=\frac1m\sum_j\delta_{y_j}$, then $\al_t$ consists of $n\times m$ Dirac masses, $\al_t=\frac1{nm}\sum_{i,j}\delta_{I_t(x_i,y_j)}$. If $\pi=\pf{(\Id,T)}{\al}$ is a Brenier-type coupling, then $\al_t=\pf{((1-t)\Id+tT)}{\al}$ is the so-called McCann OT interpolation.

$\pi\in\U(\al_0,\al_1)$
A coupling of noise and data: how the pairs $(X_0,X_1)$ are drawn. The default is independent: draw a noise vector and, separately, a data point.
$I_t(x,y)$
A path from $x$ (at $t=0$) to $y$ (at $t=1$) for each pair. The linear one is the segment $(1-t)x+ty$.
$\al_t=\pf{(I_t)}{\pi}$
The law of $X_t=I_t(X_0,X_1)$: “where the pairs are at time $t$”. It starts at $\al_0$ and ends at $\al_1$, whatever the coupling.
$\partial_tI_t(x,y)$
The velocity of the pair’s own path; for the segment, the constant $y-x$.
$n\times m$ Diracs
With independent pairs, every source point is joined to every target point: $nm$ moving atoms of mass $\frac1{nm}$, whose paths cross. With the optimal coupling one gets McCann’s interpolation (Lesson 3), whose paths do not cross.

The independent coupling leaves a problem: the segments cross, so at a point $x$ and time $t$ several pairs pass, with different velocities $X_1-X_0$. A particle at $x$ cannot follow all of them. The natural answer — average them — is the content of the next proposition.

The velocity is a conditional expectation

In the course OT4ML Prop. 16.3, pp. 408–409

Proposition 16.3 (Flow matching vector field). Assume that $t\mapsto I_t(u)$ is absolutely continuous for $\pi$-almost every $u$, that its derivative $(t,u)\mapsto\partial_tI_t(u)$ is jointly measurable, and that $\int_0^1\!\int\norm{\partial_tI_t(u)}^2\dd\pi(u)\,\dd t<+\infty$. For almost every $t$, consider the flow-matching problem over measurable fields $v_t:\R^d\to\R^d$

$$\min_{v_t}\int\norm{v_t(I_t(u))-[\partial_tI_t](u)}^2\,\dd\pi(u).\qquad(16.2)$$

Its minimizer is characterized $\al_t$-almost everywhere by the conditional expectation

$$v_t(z)=\E_{u\sim\pi}\big([\partial_tI_t](u)\ \big|\ z=I_t(u)\big).\qquad(16.3)$$

Then $v_t\in L^2(\al_t;\R^d)$ for almost every $t$, and the pair $(\al_t,v_t)$ satisfies the continuity equation (14.2) in the distributional sense on $(0,1)\times\R^d$.

$u$
The latent pair $(x,y)$, drawn from $\pi$ (OT4ML allows more general latent variables).
(16.2)
A regression: predict the pathwise velocity $\partial_tI_t$ (for segments, $X_1-X_0$) from the position $I_t(u)=X_t$ only. No ODE, no density, no constraint: an ordinary least-squares problem.
(16.3)
The best predictor is the average velocity of all the pairs passing through $z$ at time $t$. OT4ML p. 410: “intuitively, $v_t(z)$ is the average velocity of all trajectories passing through $z$”.
last sentence
The averaged field moves the distribution exactly along the chosen path $(\al_t)$: it is a valid velocity of the curve in the sense of §1.

For the linear interpolant $X_t=tX_1+(1-t)X_0$, the exercise sheet writes the field in three equivalent ways:

In the course exercise sheet, Exercise 1, (1)
$$v_t(x)=\E[X_1-X_0\mid X_t=x]=\frac1{1-t}\E[X_1-X_t\mid X_t=x]=\frac1{1-t}\big(\E[X_1\mid X_t=x]-x\big).\qquad(1)$$

The second form uses $X_1-X_t=(1-t)(X_1-X_0)$; the third uses $\E[X_t\mid X_t=x]=x$. Reading: the particle at $x$ heads for its expected destination $\E[X_1\mid X_t=x]$, at the speed needed to arrive at $t=1$.

Reminder — conditional expectation = best least-squares predictor

$\E[Y\mid X]$ minimizes $\E\norm{Y-g(X)}^2$ over measurable $g$, and $\E[\ps{Y-\E[Y\mid X]}{h(X)}]=0$ for every $h$ (orthogonality). Hence Pythagoras and conditional Jensen, $\norm{\E[Y\mid X]}^2\le\E[\norm Y^2\mid X]$. See the refresher and Reminders — conditional laws.

Idea of the proof

(16.2) is a least-squares problem on the functions of $X_t$, so its solution is the projection: the conditional expectation. To check the continuity equation, differentiate $\E[\varphi(X_t)]$: the chain rule gives $\E[\ps{\nabla\varphi(X_t)}{\partial_tI_t}]$, and since $\nabla\varphi(X_t)$ is a function of $X_t$, the pathwise velocity can be replaced by its conditional mean.

Full proof (linear interpolant, random-variable notation)

Let $(X_0,X_1)\sim\pi$, $X_t=(1-t)X_0+tX_1$, $D=X_1-X_0$ (with $\E\norm D^2<\infty$), and $v_t(x)=\E[D\mid X_t=x]$.

  1. For every measurable $g$: $\E\norm{g(X_t)-D}^2\ge\E\norm{v_t(X_t)-D}^2$, with equality iff $g=v_t$ $\al_t$-a.e.The conditional expectation is the orthogonal projection of $D$ onto the closed subspace of $L^2$ of the functions of $X_t$ (reminder above). This is (16.3).
  2. $$\int\norm{v_t}^2\dd\al_t=\E\norm{\E[D\mid X_t]}^2\le\E\norm{D}^2<\infty .$$Conditional Jensen. So $v_t\in L^2(\al_t)$.
  3. For a smooth compactly supported $\varphi$: $\ \dfrac{\dd}{\dd t}\E[\varphi(X_t)]=\E\big[\ps{\nabla\varphi(X_t)}{D}\big]$.Chain rule, $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}X_t=X_1-X_0=D$, and differentiation under $\E$ (bounded $\nabla\varphi$, integrable $D$).
  4. $$\E\big[\ps{\nabla\varphi(X_t)}{D}\big]=\E\big[\ps{\nabla\varphi(X_t)}{\E[D\mid X_t]}\big]=\int\ps{\nabla\varphi}{v_t}\,\dd\al_t .$$Orthogonality with $h=\nabla\varphi$ (the “tower property”), then the definition of $v_t$ and $X_t\sim\al_t$.
  5. $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\int\varphi\,\dd\al_t=\int\ps{\nabla\varphi}{v_t}\,\dd\al_t$: the weak continuity equation for $(\al_t,v_t)$. $\square$Steps 3–4; this is the probabilistic form of (14.3) (§1).
Worked example — noise to two atoms on the line

$X_0\sim\N(0,1)$ and $X_1=\pm1$ with probability $\frac12$ each, independent; $X_t=(1-t)X_0+tX_1$.

  1. Given $X_1=y$, $X_t\sim\N\big(ty,(1-t)^2\big)$.$X_t=ty+(1-t)X_0$ with $X_0$ standard normal.
  2. $$\P(X_1=1\mid X_t=x)=\frac{e^{-(x-t)^2/2(1-t)^2}}{e^{-(x-t)^2/2(1-t)^2}+e^{-(x+t)^2/2(1-t)^2}}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-2tx/(1-t)^2}}.$$Bayes’ rule with equal priors; expand the squares: $(x+t)^2-(x-t)^2=4tx$.
  3. $$\E[X_1\mid X_t=x]=2\P(X_1=1\mid X_t=x)-1=\tanh\Big(\frac{tx}{(1-t)^2}\Big),\qquad v_t(x)=\frac{\tanh\big(tx/(1-t)^2\big)-x}{1-t}.$$$\frac{2}{1+e^{-2s}}-1=\tanh s$; then formula (1).

Numbers: $v_{1/2}(0.2)=\frac{\tanh(0.4)-0.2}{0.5}=0.3599$. Monte Carlo check: among $2\times10^8$ simulated pairs, the $1.9\times10^6$ with $X_{1/2}\in[0.19,0.21]$ have average $X_1-X_0=0.3615$ (standard error $0.0013$). At $t=0$: $v_0(x)=\E[X_1]-x=-x$ — every particle first heads to the mean of the data. Near $t=1$, $\tanh$ saturates: the particle has “decided” its atom and rushes to it at speed $\approx\frac{\pm1-x}{1-t}$ ($v_{0.9}(0.5)=5.000$).

Training: the loss, and why it never reaches zero

In practice the regression is done for all times at once with a network $v_\theta(t,x)$:

$$\mathcal L(\theta)=\E_{t\sim\mathcal U[0,1]}\ \E_{(X_0,X_1)\sim\pi}\big\lVert v_\theta(t,X_t)-(X_1-X_0)\big\rVert^2,\qquad X_t=(1-t)X_0+tX_1 .$$

This is often called conditional flow matching: the target $X_1-X_0$ is the velocity of the path conditioned on the pair, which is known, while the “true” target $v_t(X_t)$ is not. The two give the same minimizer:

Proposition (conditional and marginal losses differ by a constant) — derived here, as in Lipman et al. (2023)

$\mathcal L(\theta)=\E_t\E\norm{v_\theta(t,X_t)-v_t(X_t)}^2+C$, where $C=\E_t\E\norm{X_1-X_0-v_t(X_t)}^2$ does not depend on $\theta$.

Proof (Pythagoras)
  1. $v_\theta(t,X_t)-D=\big(v_\theta(t,X_t)-v_t(X_t)\big)+\big(v_t(X_t)-D\big)$, with $D=X_1-X_0$.Add and subtract $v_t(X_t)$.
  2. $\E\big[\ps{v_\theta(t,X_t)-v_t(X_t)}{v_t(X_t)-D}\big]=0$.The first factor is a function of $X_t$; orthogonality of $D-\E[D\mid X_t]$ to such functions.
  3. Expand the square, average over $t$: $\mathcal L(\theta)=\E_t\E\norm{v_\theta-v_t}^2+\E_t\E\norm{v_t(X_t)-D}^2$. $\square$Steps 1–2.

So stochastic gradient descent on the tractable loss $\mathcal L$ is stochastic gradient descent on the distance to the true field. But the minimum value $C$ is not zero: it is the average conditional variance of $X_1-X_0$ given $X_t$. In the two-atom example, $C(t)=\E\norm{D}^2-\E\norm{v_t(X_t)}^2$ equals $1$ at $t=0$, $1.80$ at $t=\frac12$ and is $\approx0$ for $t\ge0.9$ (the atom is then known from $X_t$); averaged over $t$, $C=1.018$. A training loss that plateaus at a positive value is normal.

Algorithm — flow matching in practice

Training. Repeat: draw a batch of noise $x_0^{(b)}\sim\N(0,\Id)$, a batch of data $x_1^{(b)}$, times $t^{(b)}\sim\mathcal U[0,1]$; form $x_t^{(b)}=(1-t^{(b)})x_0^{(b)}+t^{(b)}x_1^{(b)}$; take a gradient step on $\frac1B\sum_b\norm{v_\theta(t^{(b)},x_t^{(b)})-(x_1^{(b)}-x_0^{(b)})}^2$.

Sampling. Draw $x\sim\N(0,\Id)$; for $k=0,\dots,K-1$: $x\leftarrow x+\frac1Kv_\theta(\frac kK,x)$ (Euler; higher-order ODE solvers are common). Output $x$. The cost of a sample is $K$ evaluations of the network.

Reminder — stochastic gradient descent

To minimize an expectation $\E_\xi[\ell(\theta,\xi)]$, step along $-\nabla_\theta\ell(\theta,\xi)$ for random draws $\xi$ (here $\xi=(t,X_0,X_1)$): an unbiased estimate of the gradient. The flow-matching loss is an expectation of a squared error, so SGD applies directly — unlike OT losses computed on minibatches, whose gradients are biased (OT4ML p. 416). More: Reminders — gradient descent and SGD.

What the regression guarantees

In the course OT4ML p. 410

For the exact field $v_t$, integrating the ODE $\dot x=v_t(x)$ defines a transport map $T_t$. If $v_t$ is regular enough, or more generally if the continuity equation has a unique solution for this velocity, then $\pf{(T_t)}{\al_0}=\al_t$. […] The sampling procedure first draws $X_0\sim\al$ and then integrates the ODE $\dot X_t=v_t(X_t)$ starting with $X_{t=0}=X_0$. In the ideal exact-field limit, the resulting $X_{t=1}$ is distributed according to $\al_1=\be$.

So the flow map $T_1$ is a valid transport from noise to data. How good is it? Combining §2 with the conditional Jensen inequality gives a chain (derived here), for any coupling $\pi$ and the linear interpolant, when the flow is well defined:

$$W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1)\ \le\ \int\norm{T_1(x)-x}^2\dd\al_0\ \le\ \int_0^1\!\!\int\norm{v_t}^2\dd\al_t\,\dd t\ \le\ \E_\pi\norm{X_1-X_0}^2 .$$
  1. First inequality: $(X_0,T_1(X_0))$ is a coupling of $\al_0$ and $\al_1$.$\pf{(T_1)}{\al_0}=\al_1$ (OT4ML p. 410).
  2. Second: Jensen in time along each trajectory, then the push-forward relation.Part 1 of the proof of Benamou–Brenier (§2).
  3. Third: $\int\norm{v_t}^2\dd\al_t=\E\norm{\E[X_1-X_0\mid X_t]}^2\le\E\norm{X_1-X_0}^2$ for each $t$.Conditional Jensen.

The flow never costs more than the coupling it was trained on, but it may cost more than $W_2^2$. In the two-atom example: $W_2^2=2-2\sqrt{2/\pi}=0.4042$ (the optimal map is $x\mapsto\mathrm{sign}(x)$); the flow map is also $\mathrm{sign}(x)$ (numerically, $T_1(-0.01)=-1$, $T_1(0.01)=1$: on the line the flow is monotone, Exercise 3), so the first inequality is an equality, $0.4042$; the action of the path is $0.9824$; the independent coupling costs $\E\abs{X_1-X_0}^2=2$. The particles arrive at the optimal places, but along curved paths at varying speed.

Worked example — few Euler steps

Same example, Euler with $K$ steps from the points $-1.5,-0.3,0.4,2.0$ (exact field): $K=1$ sends all of them to $0$ ($=\E[X_1]$, since $v_0(x)=-x$); $K=2$ gives $(-0.905,-0.291,0.380,0.964)$; $K=4$ gives $(-1.000,-0.997,1.000,1.000)$; $K=10$ lands on $\pm1$. Curved paths need several steps; §8 shows how better couplings straighten them.

In machine learning — flow matching for image generation

Data: $10^6$ images (often compressed to latent codes by an autoencoder). Model: a U-Net or a transformer $v_\theta(t,x)$ (with a text embedding $c$ as extra input for text-to-image). Loss: the regression above, with independent pairs (noise, image) and the straight interpolant. Sampling: an ODE solver from Gaussian noise. This is the recipe of Lipman, Chen, Ben-Hamu, Nickel and Le (Flow matching for generative modeling, ICLR 2023), of Liu, Gong and Liu (Flow straight and fast, rectified flow, ICLR 2023), and of Albergo, Boffi and Vanden-Eijnden (stochastic interpolants), all cited in OT4ML p. 407; it is used at scale in Stable Diffusion 3 (§5). The appeal: a simple, stable regression loss (no adversarial game, no ODE and no likelihood during training), and a transport from noise to data that is exact in the ideal limit.

Note on the sources — “the least-squares formula (14.8)”

OT4ML p. 407 says that in flow matching the vector field “is obtained by constructing an interpolation $\al_t$ and then finding $v_t$ using the least-squares formula (14.8)”. Proposition 16.3 shows that the conditional expectation (16.3) moves the path, but it is the least-energy field of Prop. 14.3 only when it is a gradient. That holds when one endpoint is a standard Gaussian and the coupling is independent (OT4ML Prop. 16.7 and p. 411, see §7), not in general. Check: between independent $\N(0,\mathrm{diag}(4,1))$ and $\N\big(0,\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\big)$ the flow-matching field is linear with a non-symmetric matrix (§8); its action is $2.6394$, while the least-energy field of the same Gaussian path (obtained from the Lyapunov equation $S\Sigma_t+\Sigma_tS=\dot\Sigma_t$) has action $2.5414$.

Pitfall — the trajectories of the flow are not the training segments

Training uses straight segments between pairs; sampling follows the averaged field, whose trajectories are curved and never cross (an ODE with a regular field has non-crossing trajectories). A sample does not travel to “its” training partner: the flow builds its own pairing (middle panel of the figure above).

Reflexes
  • Velocity of an interpolant path → $v_t(x)=\E[\partial_tI_t\mid X_t=x]$; for segments $=\frac{\E[X_1\mid X_t=x]-x}{1-t}$.
  • Conditional expectation in a Gaussian or mixture model → Bayes’ rule, posterior weights, softmax.
  • To compare losses with unknown targets → Pythagoras with the conditional expectation.
  • Cost of the generated map → between $W_2^2$ and the cost of the training coupling.

If the data distribution is a single atom, $X_1=y$ almost surely, what is the flow-matching velocity, and where does each particle go?

Answer

$\E[X_1\mid X_t=x]=y$, so $v_t(x)=\frac{y-x}{1-t}$. The solution of $\dot x=\frac{y-x}{1-t}$ is $x(t)=(1-t)x_0+ty$: every particle goes straight to $y$ at constant speed. All the trajectories meet at $t=1$ — allowed, since the field blows up at $t=1$.

With the independent coupling, why does a single Euler step from $t=0$ send every sample to $\E[X_1]$?

Answer

At $t=0$, $X_t=X_0$ is independent of $X_1$, so $v_0(x)=\E[X_1-X_0\mid X_0=x]=\E[X_1]-x$ and $x+v_0(x)=\E[X_1]$. One Euler step averages all the data: a single blurry image.
