2. The Benamou–Brenier formula: $W_2^2$ is the least kinetic energy OT4ML §14.2, pp. 330–331

Fix only the start $\al_0$ and the end $\al_1$, and look for the motion that costs the least total kinetic energy: its cost is exactly $W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1)$, and the optimal motion moves every particle along a straight line at constant speed — the displacement interpolation of Lesson 3.

The problem, first for one particle

In §1 the curve $(\al_t)$ was given. Now only its endpoints are prescribed. Which motion from $\al_0$ to $\al_1$ is the cheapest, and what does it cost? Start with a single particle going from $x$ to $y$ in time $1$ along a path $\ga(t)$. Its total kinetic energy (the action) is $\int_0^1\norm{\dot\ga(t)}^2\dd t$, and

  1. $$\norm{y-x}^2=\Big\lVert\int_0^1\dot\ga(t)\,\dd t\Big\rVert^2\le\int_0^1\norm{\dot\ga(t)}^2\,\dd t.$$Fundamental theorem of calculus, then Jensen’s inequality for the convex function $\norm{\cdot}^2$ and the uniform probability $\dd t$ on $[0,1]$ (reminder below).
  2. Equality holds iff $\dot\ga$ is constant, i.e. $\ga(t)=(1-t)x+ty$: the straight line at constant speed.Equality case of Jensen for a strictly convex function.

So for points, “least action” gives back the squared distance, and the optimal path is the segment run at constant speed. Benamou and Brenier proved that the same holds for distributions, with $W_2^2$ in place of $\norm{x-y}^2$.

Reminder — Jensen’s inequality in time

For an integrable $u:[0,1]\to\R^d$, $\big\lVert\int_0^1u(t)\,\dd t\big\rVert^2\le\int_0^1\norm{u(t)}^2\dd t$, with equality iff $u$ is constant almost everywhere. It is Jensen’s inequality $f(\E U)\le\E f(U)$ for $f=\norm{\cdot}^2$ and $U=u(\tau)$ with $\tau$ uniform on $[0,1]$; equivalently Cauchy–Schwarz. The gap is exactly the variance: $\int\norm u^2-\norm{\int u}^2=\int\norm{u-\bar u}^2$. More: Reminders — Jensen, Cauchy–Schwarz.

The theorem

In the course OT4ML Def. 14.4 and Thm. 14.5, p. 330

Definition 14.4 (Benamou–Brenier action). For an admissible pair $(\al_t,v_t)$, define $\ \mathrm{Act}_2(\al,v):=\int_0^1\!\int_{\R^d}\norm{v_t(x)}^2\,\dd\al_t(x)\,\dd t$.

Theorem 14.5 (Benamou–Brenier). For probability measures $\al_0,\al_1\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$,

$$\mathcal W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1)=\inf_{(\al_t,v_t)}\int_0^1\!\!\int_{\R^d}\norm{v_t(x)}^2\,\dd\al_t(x)\,\dd t,\qquad(14.9)$$

where the infimum is over $(\al_t,v_t)$ solving $\partial_t\al_t+\nabla\cdot(\al_tv_t)=0$ with $\al_{t=0}=\al_0$ and $\al_{t=1}=\al_1$. If $\al_0$ has a density and $T$ is the optimal Monge map $\pf{T}{\al_0}=\al_1$, a minimizing curve is

$$\al_t=\pf{\big((1-t)\Id+tT\big)}{\al_0},\qquad v_t\big((1-t)x+tT(x)\big)=T(x)-x\quad\text{for $\al_0$-a.e. $x$ and a.e. }t\in(0,1).\qquad(14.10)$$
$\Pc_2(\R^d)$
Probability measures with a finite second moment, so that $W_2$ is finite (Lesson 3 §2).
$\mathrm{Act}_2(\al,v)$
The total kinetic energy of the motion: at each time, $\int\norm{v_t}^2\dd\al_t$ adds up “mass × speed²” over all the mass; then integrate over $t\in[0,1]$. No factor $\frac12$ here.
$\inf_{(\al_t,v_t)}$
Over all motions with the right endpoints: the curve and its velocity are both unknowns. The continuity equation ties them together (mass is conserved, it only moves).
$T$
Brenier’s optimal map from $\al_0$ to $\al_1$ (Lesson 1): $T=\nabla\varphi$ with $\varphi$ convex. It exists as soon as $\al_0$ has a density.
(14.10)
The minimizer is McCann’s displacement interpolation: the particle starting at $x$ is at $(1-t)x+tT(x)$ at time $t$, with the constant velocity $T(x)-x$. The formula defines $v_t$ at the place where that particle is.
In the course StatOT Thm. 5.7, p. 150

Theorem 5.7 (Benamou–Brenier). Let $\mu_0,\mu_1\in\Pc_{2,ac}(\R^d)$. Then $W_2^2(\mu_0,\mu_1)=\inf\big\{\int_0^1\norm{v_t}^2_{\mu_t}\dd t\ \big|\ (\mu_t,v_t)_{t\in[0,1]}\text{ solves (5.2)}\big\}$. The optimal curve $(\mu_t)_{t\in[0,1]}$ is unique and is described by $X_t\sim\mu_t$, where $X_t=(1-t)X_0+tX_1$ and $(X_0,X_1)\sim\bar\ga\in\Gamma_{\mu_0,\mu_1}$ with $\bar\ga$ being an optimal coupling.

StatOT adds two things: the optimal curve is unique (for absolutely continuous endpoints, $\Pc_{2,ac}$), and it reads in random variables: take an optimal coupling $(X_0,X_1)$ and interpolate linearly, $X_t=(1-t)X_0+tX_1$. ($\norm{v_t}^2_{\mu_t}$ is StatOT’s notation for $\int\norm{v_t}^2\dd\mu_t$, and (5.2) is the continuity equation.)

Reminder — the two results of earlier lessons that meet here
  • Brenier (Lesson 1). If $\al_0$ has a density, the optimal plan for $\norm{x-y}^2$ is a map $T=\nabla\varphi$, $\varphi$ convex, and $W_2^2=\int\norm{T(x)-x}^2\dd\al_0$. See refresher and Lesson 1.
  • Displacement interpolation (Lesson 3). $\al_t=\pf{P_t}{\pi}$, $P_t(x,y)=(1-t)x+ty$, is a constant-speed geodesic: $W_2(\al_s,\al_t)=\abs{t-s}W_2(\al_0,\al_1)$. See Lesson 3 §6.

Benamou–Brenier says that this geodesic is also the curve of least kinetic energy: the static problem (Kantorovich, Brenier) and the dynamic one (least action) have the same value and the same solution.

Worked example — paths between two Gaussians on the line

Go from $\al_0=\N(0,1)$ to $\al_1=\N(4,2^2)$, so $W_2^2=(4-0)^2+(2-1)^2=17$.

Kinetic energy of a Gaussian path. Let $\al_t=\N(m_t,\sigma_t^2)$ with smooth $m_t$, $\sigma_t>0$. The particle at $x=m_t+\sigma_tz$ (a fixed “quantile” $z$) moves with velocity $\dot m_t+\dot\sigma_tz$, so

$$v_t(x)=\dot m_t+\frac{\dot\sigma_t}{\sigma_t}(x-m_t),\qquad\int v_t^2\,\dd\al_t=\dot m_t^2+\dot\sigma_t^2\,\E[Z^2]=\dot m_t^2+\dot\sigma_t^2 .$$

(On the line this is the velocity of the curve, §1.) The energy is the squared Euclidean speed of the point $(m_t,\sigma_t)$ in the half-plane: for 1D Gaussians, $W_2$ is the Euclidean distance between the points $(m,\sigma)$, and the paths are curves in that plane.

Idea of the proof

Dynamic $\le$ static: the McCann motion is admissible and costs exactly $\int\norm{T(x)-x}^2\dd\al_0=W_2^2$, because each particle moves at the constant speed $T(x)-x$. Static $\le$ dynamic: any motion carries each particle from $x$ to some $T_1(x)$, which defines a coupling; by Jensen in time, the kinetic energy of each trajectory is at least the squared length of its chord. It is the one-particle argument above, averaged over the mass.

Full proof (for flows given by smooth velocity fields)

Part 1: the action of any motion is at least $W_2^2$. Let $(\al_t,v_t)$ be admissible with a smooth velocity, and let $T_t$ be its flow ($\dot T_t=v_t\circ T_t$, $T_0=\Id$), so that $\al_t=\pf{(T_t)}{\al_0}$ (§1) and in particular $\pf{(T_1)}{\al_0}=\al_1$.

  1. $$W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1)\le\int\norm{T_1(x)-x}^2\,\dd\al_0(x).$$With $X_0\sim\al_0$, the pair $(X_0,T_1(X_0))$ is a coupling of $\al_0$ and $\al_1$; any coupling gives an upper bound on the minimum.
  2. $$\norm{T_1(x)-x}^2=\Big\lVert\int_0^1v_t(T_t(x))\,\dd t\Big\rVert^2\le\int_0^1\norm{v_t(T_t(x))}^2\,\dd t.$$$T_1(x)-T_0(x)=\int_0^1\partial_tT_t(x)\,\dd t$ and $\partial_tT_t=v_t\circ T_t$; then Jensen in time (reminder above).
  3. $$\int\!\!\int_0^1\norm{v_t(T_t(x))}^2\,\dd t\,\dd\al_0(x)=\int_0^1\!\!\int\norm{v_t(y)}^2\,\dd\al_t(y)\,\dd t=\mathrm{Act}_2(\al,v).$$Fubini, then the push-forward relation $\int g(T_t(x))\,\dd\al_0(x)=\int g\,\dd\al_t$ with $g=\norm{v_t}^2$.
  4. $$W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1)\le\mathrm{Act}_2(\al,v).$$Steps 1–3. Taking the infimum over motions: static $\le$ dynamic.

Part 2: McCann’s motion attains $W_2^2$. Assume $\al_0$ has a density, let $T=\nabla\varphi$ be the Brenier map and $S_t=(1-t)\Id+tT$.

  1. $S_t=\nabla\big(\frac{1-t}2\norm{x}^2+t\varphi\big)$ is the gradient of a strictly convex function for $t<1$, hence injective.A sum of a strictly convex quadratic and a convex function; the gradient of a strictly convex function is injective ($\ps{\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(x')}{x-x'}>0$ if $x\ne x'$).
  2. So $v_t(S_t(x)):=T(x)-x$ defines a velocity field on the support of $\al_t=\pf{(S_t)}{\al_0}$, and $\partial_tS_t(x)=T(x)-x=v_t(S_t(x))$: the particles of the motion $S_t$ follow $v_t$.Injectivity: each place is occupied by one particle, so the velocity at that place is well defined. By Prop. 14.2 (§1), $(\al_t,v_t)$ solves the continuity equation, with $\al_{t=0}=\al_0$ and $\al_{t=1}=\pf{T}{\al_0}=\al_1$.
  3. $$\mathrm{Act}_2(\al,v)=\int_0^1\!\!\int\norm{v_t(S_t(x))}^2\dd\al_0(x)\,\dd t=\int_0^1\!\!\int\norm{T(x)-x}^2\dd\al_0(x)\,\dd t=W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1).$$Push-forward relation; the integrand does not depend on $t$; Brenier: $T$ is optimal.

Parts 1 and 2 give (14.9), and (14.10) is a minimizer. $\square$

Fine print — what the short proof assumes

Part 1 used a flow $T_t$, which exists when $v$ is regular (Lipschitz). StatOT’s proof of Thm. 5.7 starts the same way (“let $\dot X_t=v_t(X_t)$ with $X_0\sim\mu_0$”), which is why its statement restricts to absolutely continuous measures. For general finite-energy solutions of the continuity equation, OT4ML’s proof (p. 331) invokes the superposition principle: the curve is lifted to a probability measure on absolutely continuous paths with the same time marginals, and Jensen is applied path by path. Without a Monge map (Part 2), OT4ML moves each pair of an optimal coupling along its segment and replaces the velocities of the paths crossing a point by their conditional mean, which by Jensen does not increase the action — exactly the mechanism of flow matching in §6.

What the theorem says

  • Equality cases. The two inequalities of Part 1 are equalities iff (i) the coupling $(X_0,T_1(X_0))$ is optimal and (ii) each particle moves at constant speed on a straight line. That is why the optimal motion is unique when $\al_0$ has a density (Brenier makes the optimal coupling unique).
  • Action and length. By Cauchy–Schwarz in time, $\Big(\int_0^1\norm{v_t}_{L^2(\al_t)}\dd t\Big)^2\le\int_0^1\norm{v_t}^2_{L^2(\al_t)}\dd t$, with equality at constant speed. Minimizing the action gives the shortest path and constant speed; $W_2(\al_0,\al_1)$ is the length of the shortest path — the Riemannian picture of the Wasserstein space used in Lesson 8.
  • Path space. OT4ML p. 334: $W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1)=\inf\big\{\int\!\int_0^1\norm{\dot\ga(t)}^2\dd t\,\dd M(\ga)\ ;\ \pf{(e_0)}{M}=\al_0,\ \pf{(e_1)}{M}=\al_1\big\}$, an infimum over laws $M$ of random curves ($e_t(\ga)=\ga(t)$). If $\al_0$ has a density, the optimal $M^*$ is unique and “concentrates on straight-line geodesics”. This is the starting point of Schrödinger bridges (§9).

The mixture path, computed

Lesson 3 showed that the mixture $(1-t)\al_0+t\al_1$ is not a geodesic. Its kinetic energy can be computed on the line, where the velocity is unique (§1): with $\rho_t=(1-t)\rho_0+t\rho_1$ and CDFs $F_0,F_1$,

  1. $$\rho_tv_t=-\partial_tF_t=F_0-F_1,\qquad\text{so}\qquad\int v_t^2\,\dd\al_t=\int\frac{(F_0(x)-F_1(x))^2}{(1-t)\rho_0(x)+t\rho_1(x)}\,\dd x.$$$F_t=(1-t)F_0+tF_1$, so $\partial_tF_t=F_1-F_0$; then $v_t=-\partial_tF_t/\rho_t$ (§1).
  2. $$\mathrm{Act}_2=\int(F_0-F_1)^2\int_0^1\frac{\dd t}{(1-t)\rho_0+t\rho_1}\,\dd x=\int\frac{(F_0(x)-F_1(x))^2}{\mathrm{LM}(\rho_0(x),\rho_1(x))}\,\dd x,\qquad\mathrm{LM}(a,b)=\frac{a-b}{\log a-\log b}.$$Fubini, and $\int_0^1\frac{\dd t}{(1-t)a+tb}=\frac{\log b-\log a}{b-a}$ (checked: $1.23900$ both ways for $a=0.3$, $b=1.7$). $\mathrm{LM}$ is the logarithmic mean.

Numbers (quadrature on $2\times10^5$ points, confirmed by integrating the energy in time): from $\N(0,1)$ to $\N(4,2^2)$ the mixture costs $46.07$, i.e. $2.71$ times $W_2^2=17$; its energy is $92.7$ at $t=0.05$, $22.3$ at $t=\frac12$ and $25.1$ at $t=0.95$: near the endpoints mass must appear where the other density is tiny, which needs huge velocities. From $\N(0,1)$ to $\N(1,1)$: $1.086$ against $W_2^2=1$; to $\N(2,1)$: $5.52$ against $4$. For two uniform laws with disjoint supports the action is infinite: on $(0,1)$, $\rho_1=0$ and $F_0-F_1\ne0$, and $\int_0^1\frac{\dd t}{1-t}=\infty$.

In machine learning — kinetic regularization of flows, and trajectories of cells

Faster neural ODEs. A continuous normalizing flow (§1) trained only by maximum likelihood may learn twisted trajectories that need many solver steps. RNODE (Finlay, Jacobsen, Nurbekyan, Oberman, How to train your neural ODE, ICML 2020) adds the penalty $\lambda\int_0^1\E\norm{v_\theta(t,X_t)}^2\dd t$ — the Benamou–Brenier action — to the loss; OT-Flow (Onken, Wu Fung, Li, Ruthotto, AAAI 2021) writes $v_\theta=-\nabla\Phi_\theta$ (a gradient, as in §1) and penalizes the same action plus a Hamilton–Jacobi residual (§3). By Benamou–Brenier the penalty pushes the flow towards the optimal one, whose trajectories are straight and cheap to integrate.

Single-cell trajectory inference. Single-cell sequencing destroys the cells it measures: one observes population snapshots $\al_{t_1},\al_{t_2},\dots$ of gene-expression vectors, never the trajectory of a cell (OT4ML Example 3.24, p. 61). To guess how cells move between snapshots, TrajectoryNet (Tong, Huang, Wolf, van Dijk, Krishnaswamy, ICML 2020, cited in OT4ML Example 3.24) fits a continuous normalizing flow through the snapshots with a kinetic-energy (dynamic OT) penalty: among the motions that explain the data, it picks the one of least action, as Benamou–Brenier suggests. Waddington-OT (Schiebinger et al., Cell 2019) couples consecutive snapshots by (entropic, unbalanced) OT instead.

Pitfall — action is not length

The action $\int_0^1\norm{v_t}^2_{L^2(\al_t)}\dd t$ depends on the speed at which a curve is run; the length $\int_0^1\norm{v_t}_{L^2(\al_t)}\dd t$ does not. Minimizing the length has infinitely many solutions (all reparametrizations of the geodesic); minimizing the action selects the constant-speed one. And always check the convention: with a factor $\frac12$ in front of the action, the minimum is $\frac12W_2^2$.

Reflexes
  • Lower bound on the action of a motion → couple $X_0$ with its endpoint $T_1(X_0)$, then Jensen in time.
  • Upper bound on $W_2^2$ → exhibit one motion and compute its action.
  • 1D Gaussians → work in the $(m,\sigma)$ plane: energy $\dot m^2+\dot\sigma^2$, geodesics are straight segments.
  • “The cheapest motion” → straight lines at constant speed along the optimal map (McCann).

What is the action of the geodesic from $\al_0$ to $\al_1$ run with the clock $t^p$, $p\ge1$ (i.e. $\al_{t^p}$)?

Answer

The velocity at time $t$ is $pt^{p-1}$ times the geodesic velocity, so the energy is $p^2t^{2p-2}W_2^2$ and the action is $\frac{p^2}{2p-1}W_2^2$: $W_2^2$ for $p=1$, $\frac43W_2^2$ for $p=2$, and it grows like $p/2$. Only constant speed attains $W_2^2$.

Between $\N(0,1)$ and $\N(0,4)$ on the line, what is the optimal velocity field and its energy at each time?

Answer

$m_t=0$, $\sigma_t=1+t$: $v_t(x)=\frac{x}{1+t}$ (the dilation of §1), energy $\dot\sigma_t^2=1=W_2^2$ at every time.
