4. Computing dynamic OT on a grid (in brief) CompOT §7.2–7.3, pp. 109–112; OT4ML pp. 333–334

Discretize density and momentum on a staggered space-time grid, so that the discrete continuity equation is exact; then minimize the convex, non-smooth action under this linear constraint by Douglas–Rachford splitting, which alternates a pointwise cubic equation and a Poisson solve.

Why a grid solver

On the line, quantile functions give the optimal path for free (Lesson 3 §8). In dimension 2 or 3 — interpolating two images, moving a density inside a domain with obstacles — there is no closed form. The convex formulation (14.13) suggests a direct method: sample $\rho$ and $m$ on a grid in space and time and solve a finite convex problem. Its size is the number of space-time grid points, $T\times n_1\times n_2$, instead of the $n^2$ unknowns of a transport plan between two images with $n$ pixels. The price: the objective $J$ is not smooth (it jumps to $+\infty$ when $\rho=0$, $m\ne0$), so plain gradient descent does not apply. CompOT (p. 106) also notes the limit: this approach needs a grid, so it works in low dimension only.

The staggered grid

In the course CompOT §7.2, pp. 109–110

We discretize time as $t_k=k/T\in[0,1]$ and assume the space is uniformly discretized at points $x_i=(i_1/n_1,i_2/n_2)\in\X=[0,1]^2$. We use a staggered grid representation, so that $\al_t$ is represented using $\mathbf a\in\R^{(T+1)\times n_1\times n_2}$ associated to half grid points in time, whereas $J$ is represented using $J=(J_1,J_2)$, where $J_1\in\R^{T\times(n_1+1)\times n_2}$ and $J_1\in\R^{T\times n_1\times(n_2+1)}$ are stored at half grid points in each space direction. Using this representation, […] the time derivative is computed as $(\partial_t\mathbf a)_{k,i}:=\mathbf a_{k+1,i}-\mathbf a_{k,i}$ and spatial divergence as

$$\dv(J)_{k,i}:=J^1_{k,i_1+1,i_2}-J^1_{k,i_1,i_2}+J^2_{k,i_1,i_2+1}-J^2_{k,i_1,i_2},\qquad(7.10)$$

which are both defined at grid points […]. In order to evaluate the functional to be optimized, one needs interpolation operators from midgrid points to grid points […] The simplest choice is to use a linear operator $I(r,s)=\frac{r+s}2$ […]. The discrete counterpart to (7.3) reads

$$\min_{(\mathbf a,J)\in\mathcal C(\mathbf a_0,\mathbf a_1)}\Theta\big(I_a(\mathbf a),I_J(J)\big),\qquad\Theta(\tilde{\mathbf a},\tilde J):=\sum_{k=1}^T\sum_{i_1=1}^{n_1}\sum_{i_2=1}^{n_2}\theta(\tilde{\mathbf a}_{k,i},\tilde J_{k,i}),\qquad(7.11)$$

where the constraint now reads $\mathcal C(\mathbf a_0,\mathbf a_1):=\{(\mathbf a,J):\partial_t\mathbf a+\dv(J)=0,\ (\mathbf a_{0,\cdot},\mathbf a_{T,\cdot})=(\mathbf a_0,\mathbf a_1)\}$.

$\mathbf a$
The densities, one per cell and per time $t_k$, $k=0,\dots,T$ (so $T+1$ time layers). The first and last layers are the data $\mathbf a_0,\mathbf a_1$.
$J=(J_1,J_2)$
The momentum, one component per space direction, stored on the faces between cells and at mid-times: $J_1$ has $n_1+1$ faces in the first direction. (The second array should read $J_2$; see the note below.)
(7.10)
With this placement, “mass in a cell at $t_{k+1}$ minus mass at $t_k$” and “flux in minus flux out through its faces” are both natural differences: the discrete continuity equation $\partial_t\mathbf a+\dv J=0$ is an exact balance, cell by cell (CompOT absorbs the grid steps into the units).
$I_a,I_J$
Averages that bring $\mathbf a$ and $J$ to common points (cell centres, mid-times), where $\theta=J$ of §3 can be evaluated.
(7.11)
A finite convex problem: a sum of perspective functions of averaged values, under linear equality constraints.

Douglas–Rachford splitting

In the course OT4ML Alg. 14.1, p. 334, and CompOT p. 112

Algorithm 14.1: Douglas–Rachford for dynamic Benamou–Brenier. Input: functionals $\mathcal F$, $\mathcal G=\iota_{\mathcal C}$, proximal parameter $\tau>0$, initial field $Z^{(0)}$ […]. For $\ell=0,\dots,L-1$ do: $\ U^{(\ell+1)}=\mathrm{prox}_{\tau\mathcal F}(Z^{(\ell)})$. Project reflected point: $\ \tilde U^{(\ell+1)}=\mathrm{prox}_{\tau\mathcal G}(2U^{(\ell+1)}-Z^{(\ell)})=\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathcal C}(2U^{(\ell+1)}-Z^{(\ell)})$. Update $\ Z^{(\ell+1)}=Z^{(\ell)}+\tilde U^{(\ell+1)}-U^{(\ell+1)}$. […]

(CompOT, p. 112:) The proximal operator $\mathrm{Prox}_{\tau\Theta}$ is computed by solving a cubic polynomial equation at each grid position. The orthogonal projection on the affine constraint $\mathcal C(\mathbf a_0,\mathbf a_1)$ involves the resolution of a Poisson equation, which can be achieved in $O(N\log(N))$ operations using the fast Fourier transform, where $N=Tn_1n_2$ is the number of grid points. Lastly, the proximal operator $\mathrm{Prox}_{\tau G}$ is a linear projector, which requires the inversion of a small linear system.

$\mathrm{prox}_{\tau\mathcal F}(Z)$
The proximal operator: $\argmin_U\frac12\norm{U-Z}^2+\tau\mathcal F(U)$ — a step that decreases $\mathcal F$ without going far from $Z$. For an indicator function $\iota_{\mathcal C}$ (0 on $\mathcal C$, $+\infty$ outside) it is the orthogonal projection on $\mathcal C$.
$\mathcal F$, $\mathcal G$
The problem is split as $\min\mathcal F+\mathcal G$ with $\mathcal F$ the action (local, non-smooth) and $\mathcal G$ the indicator of the continuity equation (global, linear). Each is easy alone; together they are not.
the iteration
Douglas–Rachford never needs a gradient: it alternates the two proximal steps on a “reflected” point. At convergence $U^{(\ell)}$ and $\tilde U^{(\ell)}$ coincide and solve the problem. Papadakis, Peyré and Oudet (2014) showed that the original ALG2 solver of Benamou and Brenier is of this type (ADMM on the dual).
“small linear system”
When the averages are kept as separate variables (as in CompOT), a third projection, onto “averages = average of the staggered values”, is needed: one small tridiagonal system per line of the grid.
Reminder — proximal operators

For a convex l.s.c. function $f$, $\mathrm{prox}_{\tau f}(z)=\argmin_u\frac12\norm{u-z}^2+\tau f(u)$ exists and is unique; its fixed points are the minimizers of $f$. Examples: $f=\iota_{\mathcal C}$ gives the projection on $\mathcal C$; $f=\frac12\norm{u}^2$ gives $z/(1+\tau)$. Splitting methods (forward–backward, Douglas–Rachford, ADMM) minimize sums $f+g$ using only $\mathrm{prox}_f$ and $\mathrm{prox}_g$. More: Reminders — convex functions, gradient methods.

The pointwise step: a cubic equation

At one grid point, with $\tau>0$ and data $(\tilde a,\tilde m)$, we need $\argmin_{(a,m)}\frac12(a-\tilde a)^2+\frac12\norm{m-\tilde m}^2+\tau\frac{\norm m^2}a$.

  1. $$\partial_m:\quad m-\tilde m+\frac{2\tau}{a}m=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad m=\frac{a}{a+2\tau}\,\tilde m.$$Gradient in $m$ of a strictly convex function of $m$ (for fixed $a>0$).
  2. $$\partial_a:\quad a-\tilde a-\tau\frac{\norm m^2}{a^2}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad(a-\tilde a)(a+2\tau)^2=\tau\norm{\tilde m}^2.$$Substitute step 1: $\norm m^2/a^2=\norm{\tilde m}^2/(a+2\tau)^2$.
  3. Take the largest real root $a^\star$ of this cubic; if it is $\le0$ (which happens iff $-4\tau\tilde a\ge\norm{\tilde m}^2$), the prox is $(0,0)$.The function is convex in $(a,m)$; the root with $a>0$ is the minimizer when it exists, otherwise the minimum is on the boundary, where $J$ forces $m=0$ (and then $a=0$ is the closest point with $a\ge0$, $\tilde a<0$).
Worked example — one proximal step

$(\tilde a,\tilde m)=(1,2)$, $\tau=\frac12$: solve $(a-1)(a+1)^2=2$. The root is $a^\star=1.35930$, and $m^\star=\frac{1.35930}{2.35930}\cdot2=1.15229$. A direct numerical minimization of the objective gives the same point (objective $0.91226$). The step reduced the momentum (from $2$ to $1.15$) and increased the density (from $1$ to $1.36$): both lower $m^2/a$. With $(\tilde a,\tilde m)=(-1,\frac12)$: $-4\tau\tilde a=2\ge\frac14$, so the prox is $(0,0)$ (objective $0.625$).

The global step: a Poisson equation

Projecting $(\rho,m)$ orthogonally onto $\{\partial_t\rho+\dv m=0$, endpoints fixed$\}$ is a least-squares problem with linear constraints $AU=b$: $\mathrm{Proj}(U)=U-A\T(AA\T)^{-1}(AU-b)$. The matrix $AA\T$ is a discrete Laplacian in space-time (the constraint is a space-time divergence, and $A\T$ a space-time gradient), with Neumann-type boundary conditions: hence a Poisson equation, solved by fast cosine transforms.

Worked example — the solver on a two-bump to one-bump problem

We implemented this scheme in one space dimension (64 cells on $[-4,4]$, $T=32$ time steps, $\tau$ scaled as $10\,\Delta t\Delta x$, started from the mixture path with zero momentum), from $\al_0=\frac12\N(-2,0.4^2)+\frac12\N(0,0.4^2)$ to $\al_1=\N(2,0.6^2)$. The reference is $W_2^2=9.2784$ between the two discretized (piecewise constant) densities, computed from their quantile functions (the continuous value is $9.2839$).

Practical notes
  • The limit is the discrete problem, not $W_2^2$. Run longer and the action settles at $9.2794$ (3000 iterations) and $9.2795$ (8000), $0.012\%$ above the reference $9.2784$: the action is evaluated on averages over a staggered grid, so the discrete problem (7.11) is close to, but not exactly, the transport between the two piecewise-constant densities. The value $9.2791$ at 1000 iterations is below this limit because it is computed at the proximal output, which does not yet satisfy the continuity equation exactly.
  • Why bother in 1D? It is a test bed with a known answer. The same code runs in 2D and 3D, inside domains with obstacles (CompOT Fig. 7.3), and with extra terms (congestion, mean-field games, OT4ML §14.7), where no closed form exists.
  • Other solvers. Interior-point methods for second-order cone programs (CompOT pp. 110–111: $\Theta$ becomes a linear function under rotated Lorentz cone constraints) are precise but each iteration solves a large linear system; first-order splitting methods scale better.

Why does the staggered placement make the discrete continuity equation exact, while a co-located grid (all unknowns at the same points) would not?

Answer

With densities in cells and fluxes on the faces between cells, the change of mass in a cell is exactly “flux in minus flux out” through its two faces, and every flux leaving one cell enters its neighbour: total mass is conserved exactly. With co-located values the divergence must be approximated by centred differences, which do not telescope cell by cell and admit spurious oscillating solutions.
