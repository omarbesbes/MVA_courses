1. Moving mass: particles, densities and the continuity equation OT4ML §14.1, pp. 328–330

A distribution in motion can be followed particle by particle (each one obeys an ODE $\dot x=v_t(x)$) or watched at fixed places (the density obeys $\partial_t\rho_t+\dv(\rho_tv_t)=0$); the two descriptions are equivalent, but a given motion of the density has many velocity fields, and the most economical one is a gradient.

The problem: transport as a motion

Until now a transport plan was a static object: a map $T$ or a coupling $\pi$ that says where each unit of mass ends up. Lesson 3 already looked at the motion in between (the displacement interpolation $\al_t$, Lesson 3 §6). This lesson makes the motion the main object, for two reasons.

  • Geometry. In $\R^d$ the squared distance $\norm{x-y}^2$ is the least kinetic energy $\int_0^1\norm{\dot x(t)}^2\dd t$ of a path from $x$ to $y$. We will see that $W_2^2$ is the same thing for distributions (§2). This turns transport into a (convex) optimization over motions, which can be discretized on a grid (§3–§4).
  • Machine learning. Modern generative models (flow matching, diffusion models) produce samples by moving noise along a learned velocity field (§5–§8). To understand them we need the language of moving distributions.

Two descriptions of the same motion

In the course OT4ML §14.1, (14.1)–(14.3), p. 328

Lagrangian and Eulerian descriptions. We consider the evolution $t\mapsto\al_t\in\Pc(\R^d)$. Such an evolution can be described in a “Lagrangian” way as the advection of particles along a (time-dependent) vector field $v_t(x)$ in $\R^d$. At the particle level, this advection is governed by

$$\frac{\dd x(t)}{\dd t}=v_t(x(t)),\qquad(14.1)$$

and we write $T_t$ for the associated flow map, so that $T_t(x(0))=x(t)$. The advected measure is then $\al_t=\pf{(T_t)}{\al_0}$. For discrete measures, $\al_t=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{x_i(t)}$, meaning each $x_i(t)$ solves (14.1).

In the Eulerian description, the same motion is written directly on the evolving measure: the particle ODE becomes the PDE

$$\frac{\partial\al_t}{\partial t}+\dv(v_t\al_t)=0.\qquad(14.2)$$

[…] It is only a classical PDE when $\al_t$ has a smooth density. For general measures, and in particular for empirical measures, it is understood in the distributional sense: for every $\varphi\in C_c^1((0,1)\times\R^d)$,

$$\int_0^1\!\!\int_{\R^d}\big(\partial_t\varphi(t,x)+\ps{v_t(x)}{\nabla_x\varphi(t,x)}\big)\,\dd\al_t(x)\,\dd t=0.\qquad(14.3)$$
$v_t(x)$
A velocity field: at time $t$, the mass located at $x$ moves with velocity $v_t(x)\in\R^d$. It is attached to places, not to particles.
(14.1)
A particle sitting at $x(t)$ moves with the velocity of the place where it is. Given $x(0)$, this ODE determines the whole trajectory (if $v$ is regular, refresher).
$T_t$
The flow map: $T_t(x)$ is the position at time $t$ of the particle that started at $x$. $T_0=\Id$.
$\al_t=\pf{(T_t)}{\al_0}$
“Move every bit of the initial mass along its trajectory”: the distribution at time $t$ is the image of $\al_0$ by $T_t$. With random variables: $X_0\sim\al_0$ and $X_t=T_t(X_0)$, then $X_t\sim\al_t$.
(14.2)
The continuity equation. With a density, $\partial_t\rho_t+\dv(\rho_tv_t)=0$: the density at a fixed place increases exactly when more mass flows in than out. $\rho_tv_t$ is the flux (mass per unit time crossing a point). Also called advection or Liouville equation.
(14.3)
The weak form: test the equation against smooth functions $\varphi$ and move the derivatives onto $\varphi$ by integration by parts. It makes sense for Diracs, which have no density.

Where the continuity equation comes from: conservation of mass

In one dimension, look at the mass in an interval $[a,b]$. Mass is neither created nor destroyed; it only crosses the endpoints. Per unit time, the mass crossing the point $a$ from left to right is $\rho_t(a)v_t(a)$ (density times speed). So

  1. $$\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\int_a^b\rho_t(x)\,\dd x=\rho_t(a)v_t(a)-\rho_t(b)v_t(b).$$What enters at $a$ minus what leaves at $b$ (a negative velocity at $a$ means mass leaving, and the formula still holds with the sign).
  2. $$\rho_t(a)v_t(a)-\rho_t(b)v_t(b)=-\int_a^b\partial_x(\rho_tv_t)\,\dd x.$$Fundamental theorem of calculus.
  3. $$\int_a^b\big(\partial_t\rho_t+\partial_x(\rho_tv_t)\big)\dd x=0\ \text{ for all }aDifferentiate under the integral sign; a continuous function whose integral vanishes on every interval is zero.

In $\R^d$ the same argument with a region $A$ and the divergence theorem (the outgoing flux through the boundary of $A$ is $\int_A\dv(\rho_tv_t)$, refresher) gives (14.2).

Worked example — a translation and a dilation
  • Translation. $T_t(x)=x+ta$: every particle moves with the constant velocity $v_t\equiv a$, and $\rho_t(x)=\rho_0(x-ta)$. Check: $\partial_t\rho_t=-\ps{a}{\nabla\rho_0(x-ta)}$ and $\dv(\rho_ta)=\ps{a}{\nabla\rho_0(x-ta)}$; they cancel.
  • Dilation. On the line, $T_t(x)=(1+t)x$ sends $\N(0,1)$ to $\al_t=\N(0,(1+t)^2)$. A particle at $x(t)=(1+t)x_0$ has velocity $x_0=\frac{x(t)}{1+t}$, so $v_t(x)=\frac{x}{1+t}$. With $s=1+t$ and $\rho_t(x)=\frac1s\phi(x/s)$ ($\phi$ the standard normal density): $\partial_t\rho_t=\rho_t\big(\frac{x^2}{s^3}-\frac1s\big)$ and $\partial_x\big(\rho_t\frac xs\big)=\frac{\rho_t}s\big(1-\frac{x^2}{s^2}\big)$: they cancel. Numbers at $t=0.5$, $x=1$: $\rho=0.21297$, $\partial_t\rho=-0.07888$, $-\partial_x(\rho v)=-0.07888$.
In the course OT4ML Prop. 14.2, p. 329

Proposition 14.2 (Lagrangian flows solve the continuity equation). Let $(T_t)_{t\in[0,1]}$ be a $C^1$ family of diffeomorphisms of $\R^d$ and define $\al_t=\pf{(T_t)}{\al_0}$. Assume that the derivatives below are integrable with respect to $\al_0$, and define the Eulerian velocity field by

$$v_t(T_t(y))=\partial_tT_t(y).$$

Then $(\al_t,v_t)$ solves the continuity equation in the weak sense (14.3). In particular, if $\al_0=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i(0)}$ is empirical, then $\al_t=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i(t)}$ is empirical as well, with particle velocities $\dot x_i(t)=v_t(x_i(t))$.

$v_t(T_t(y))=\partial_tT_t(y)$
The Eulerian velocity at the place $T_t(y)$ is the speed of the particle that is there (the one that started at $y$). This defines $v_t$ everywhere because $T_t$ is a bijection.
“in the weak sense”
The identity (14.3) for every test function $\varphi$; no density needed.
Idea of the proof

Averages of a test function along the motion are averages over the initial mass of $\varphi$ evaluated on the trajectories. Differentiate in time with the chain rule: the derivative of $\varphi(t,x(t))$ is $\partial_t\varphi+\ps{\nabla\varphi}{\dot x}$, and $\dot x$ is the velocity. Integrating over time gives (14.3).

Full proof

Let $\varphi\in C_c^1((0,1)\times\R^d)$.

  1. $$\int\varphi(t,x)\,\dd\al_t(x)=\int\varphi(t,T_t(y))\,\dd\al_0(y).$$Definition of the push-forward $\al_t=\pf{(T_t)}{\al_0}$, applied to the function $x\mapsto\varphi(t,x)$.
  2. $$\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\int\varphi(t,T_t(y))\,\dd\al_0(y)=\int\Big(\partial_t\varphi(t,T_t(y))+\ps{\nabla_x\varphi(t,T_t(y))}{\partial_tT_t(y)}\Big)\dd\al_0(y).$$Chain rule for $t\mapsto\varphi(t,T_t(y))$, and differentiation under the integral sign (allowed by the integrability assumption).
  3. $$\begin{aligned}&=\int\Big(\partial_t\varphi(t,T_t(y))+\ps{\nabla_x\varphi(t,T_t(y))}{v_t(T_t(y))}\Big)\dd\al_0(y)\\&=\int\big(\partial_t\varphi(t,x)+\ps{\nabla_x\varphi(t,x)}{v_t(x)}\big)\dd\al_t(x).\end{aligned}$$Definition of $v_t$; then the push-forward relation again, for the function $x\mapsto\partial_t\varphi(t,x)+\ps{\nabla_x\varphi(t,x)}{v_t(x)}$.
  4. $$\int_0^1\!\!\int\big(\partial_t\varphi+\ps{v_t}{\nabla_x\varphi}\big)\dd\al_t\,\dd t=\Big[\int\varphi(t,x)\,\dd\al_t(x)\Big]_{t=0}^{t=1}=0.$$Integrate step 3 in $t$; $\varphi$ vanishes near $t=0$ and $t=1$ (compact support in $(0,1)$). This is (14.3).

For an empirical measure the same computation is a finite sum over particles, $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\frac1n\sum_i\varphi(t,x_i(t))=\frac1n\sum_i\big(\partial_t\varphi+\ps{\nabla\varphi}{\dot x_i}\big)$. $\square$

Reminder — the continuity equation in probabilistic language

With $X_t=T_t(X_0)$, the weak form says $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\E[\varphi(X_t)]=\E[\ps{\nabla\varphi(X_t)}{v_t(X_t)}]$ for smooth $\varphi$ (StatOT Prop. 5.1, p. 142). This is the form used in §6: to check that a velocity moves a curve of laws, differentiate $\E[\varphi(X_t)]$. More: Reminders — the continuity equation.

One motion, many velocities

Given the particles we found the velocity. Conversely, given only the curve $t\mapsto\al_t$ (for instance snapshots of a population, without tracking individuals), can we recover the velocity? Not uniquely.

In the course OT4ML §14.1, p. 329

From measure evolutions to vector fields. For a given evolution $(\al_t)_t$, there are typically infinitely many velocity fields $v_t$ satisfying

$$\partial_t\al_t+\dv(\al_tv_t)=0.\qquad(14.5)$$

This non-uniqueness comes from the kernel of the weighted divergence. The linear space of vector fields that leave a measure $\al$ invariant is

$$\mathcal H_\al=\big\{v\in L^2(\al;\R^d)\ ;\ \dv(\al v)=0\text{ in distributions}\big\}.$$

It is usually non-trivial: for instance, if $\al$ is an isotropic Gaussian, $\mathcal H_\al$ contains rotational vector fields generated by anti-symmetric matrices.

If $v_t$ moves the curve and $w\in\mathcal H_{\al_t}$, then $v_t+w$ moves it too: $\dv(\al_t(v_t+w))=\dv(\al_tv_t)+0$. The particles move differently, the distribution does not.

Worked example — a rotation that moves nothing (StatOT §5.1, Fig. 5.1, p. 144)

Let $\al=\N(0,\Id_2)$ with density $\rho(x)=\frac1{2\pi}e^{-\norm x^2/2}$, so $\nabla\rho=-x\rho$, and let $v(x)=Jx$ with $J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ (rotation by $90°$, anti-symmetric).

  1. $$\dv(\rho v)=\ps{\nabla\rho}{Jx}+\rho\,\dv(Jx)=-\rho\ps{x}{Jx}+\rho\,\tr J.$$Product rule $\dv(\rho v)=\ps{\nabla\rho}{v}+\rho\dv v$, and $\dv(Jx)=\tr J$ for a linear field.
  2. $$\ps{x}{Jx}=x_1(-x_2)+x_2x_1=0,\qquad\tr J=0,\qquad\text{so}\quad\dv(\rho v)=0.$$A rotation field is orthogonal to the radius, and an anti-symmetric matrix has zero diagonal.
  3. The constant curve $\al_t=\al$ solves the continuity equation with $v=0$ and with $v=Jx$: every particle turns around the origin, the Gaussian does not change.$\partial_t\al_t=0=-\dv(\al Jx)$.
  4. Kinetic energies: $\int\norm{0}^2\dd\al=0$ against $\int\norm{Jx}^2\dd\al=\E\norm{X}^2=2$.$\norm{Jx}=\norm x$, and $\E\norm X^2=2$ for a standard Gaussian in $\R^2$ (checked by Monte Carlo: $2.006$ with $2\times10^5$ samples).

Choosing the most economical velocity

Among all the velocities that move a given curve, the natural choice is the one with the least kinetic energy: it explains the motion without useless swirling.

In the course OT4ML (14.7) and Prop. 14.3, p. 330

A more robust choice, used implicitly in flow matching, optimal transport and Wasserstein gradient flows, is to select among all admissible velocities the one with smallest kinetic energy:

$$\min_v\ \frac12\int_0^1\!\!\int_{\R^d}\norm{v_t(x)}^2\,\dd\al_t(x)\,\dd t\quad\text{subject to}\quad\partial_t\al_t+\dv(\al_tv_t)=0.\qquad(14.7)$$

Proposition 14.3 (Least-square velocities are gradients). Assume that $\al_t=\rho_t\,\dd x$ is a smooth positive density curve, that $\partial_t\rho_t$ has zero integral, and that boundary terms vanish. The minimizer of (14.7), if it exists, is a gradient field $v_t=\nabla\varphi_t$, where $\varphi_t$, unique up to an additive constant on each connected component, solves the weighted Poisson equation

$$-\dv(\rho_t\nabla\varphi_t)=\partial_t\rho_t.\qquad(14.8)$$
(14.7)
The curve $(\al_t)$ is fixed; only the velocity is chosen. The factor $\frac12$ is a convention (it does not change the minimizer).
gradient field
$v_t=\nabla\varphi_t$ for a scalar function $\varphi_t$: no rotational part.
(14.8)
Plug $v_t=\nabla\varphi_t$ into the continuity equation: $\partial_t\rho_t+\dv(\rho_t\nabla\varphi_t)=0$. It is an elliptic equation for $\varphi_t$ (a Poisson equation weighted by $\rho_t$).
Idea of the proof

At each time, the admissible velocities form an affine space $v^\star+\mathcal H_{\al_t}$. The shortest vector of an affine space is the one orthogonal to its direction $\mathcal H_{\al_t}$, and gradients are exactly what is orthogonal to the fields with $\dv(\rho w)=0$ (integration by parts).

Proof (orthogonality in $L^2(\al_t)$)

Fix $t$ and use the inner product $\ps{u}{w}_{\al_t}=\int\ps{u}{w}\rho_t\,\dd x$.

  1. If $v$ and $v'$ both satisfy the continuity equation, $w=v'-v$ satisfies $\dv(\rho_tw)=0$: $w\in\mathcal H_{\al_t}$.Subtract the two equations; $\partial_t\rho_t$ cancels.
  2. For any smooth $\varphi$ and $w\in\mathcal H_{\al_t}$: $\ps{\nabla\varphi}{w}_{\al_t}=\int\ps{\nabla\varphi}{\rho_tw}\,\dd x=-\int\varphi\,\dv(\rho_tw)\,\dd x=0$.Integration by parts (boundary terms vanish by assumption).
  3. If the admissible $v_t=\nabla\varphi_t$ is a gradient, then for every admissible $v'=v_t+w$: $\norm{v'}^2_{\al_t}=\norm{v_t}^2_{\al_t}+2\ps{v_t}{w}_{\al_t}+\norm{w}_{\al_t}^2=\norm{v_t}^2_{\al_t}+\norm{w}_{\al_t}^2\ge\norm{v_t}^2_{\al_t}$.Step 2 kills the cross term: Pythagoras. So an admissible gradient is the minimizer, and it is unique ($w=0$).
  4. Conversely, OT4ML introduces a Lagrange multiplier $\varphi_t$ for the constraint: after integration by parts, the Lagrangian at time $t$ is $\int\big(\frac12\norm{v_t}^2-\ps{\nabla\varphi_t}{v_t}\big)\dd\al_t+\int\varphi_t\partial_t\al_t$, minimized pointwise by $v_t=\nabla\varphi_t$; the constraint then gives (14.8).The minimizer of $\frac12\norm v^2-\ps{p}{v}$ is $v=p$. (This is the “formal saddle” argument of OT4ML p. 330; the existence of the solution $\varphi_t$ of (14.8) is the analytic part.)

In one dimension the velocity is unique. Integrate the continuity equation from $-\infty$ to $x$, with $F_t$ the CDF of $\al_t$: $\partial_tF_t(x)+\rho_t(x)v_t(x)=0$ (no flux at $-\infty$). So, wherever $\rho_t>0$,

$$v_t(x)=-\frac{\partial_tF_t(x)}{\rho_t(x)}.$$

Check on the dilation: $F_t(x)=\Phi(x/(1+t))$ gives $-\partial_tF_t/\rho_t=x/(1+t)$ (numerically $0.66667$ at $t=0.5$, $x=1$). In 1D there is no room to “turn around”: $\mathcal H_\al=\{0\}$ wherever $\rho>0$, since $(\rho w)'=0$ forces $\rho w$ to be a constant, which must be $0$ at $-\infty$.

In machine learning — continuous normalizing flows and neural ODEs

A continuous normalizing flow (Chen, Rubanova, Bettencourt, Duvenaud, Neural ordinary differential equations, NeurIPS 2018; Grathwohl et al., FFJORD, ICLR 2019) is exactly the Lagrangian picture with a neural network as velocity: $v_\theta(t,x)$ is, say, a multilayer perceptron, samples are produced by drawing $x_0\sim\N(0,\Id)$ and solving $\dot x=v_\theta(t,x)$ up to $t=1$. To train it by maximum likelihood one needs the density along trajectories, which the continuity equation gives: along a trajectory,

$$\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\log\rho_t(x(t))=\frac{\partial_t\rho_t+\ps{\nabla\rho_t}{v_t}}{\rho_t}=\frac{-\dv(\rho_tv_t)+\ps{\nabla\rho_t}{v_t}}{\rho_t}=-\dv v_t(x(t)),$$

the “instantaneous change of variables”. The log-likelihood of a data point therefore requires solving the ODE backwards and integrating the trace of the Jacobian of $v_\theta$ along the way — expensive in high dimension. Flow matching (§6) trains the same kind of model without simulating the ODE. Pointer: OT4ML Example 16.2, p. 408 (“simulation-free training of continuous normalizing flows”).

Pitfall — the velocity of a curve of distributions is not unique

Knowing $\al_t$ for all $t$ does not tell you how individual particles move (in dimension $d\ge2$): you can add any field that leaves $\al_t$ invariant. When a statement says “the velocity of the curve”, ask which one: the least-energy one (a gradient), the one of a given flow, or the one learned by a model (§6).

Reflexes
  • To check that $(\al_t,v_t)$ solves the continuity equation → compute $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\E[\varphi(X_t)]$ and compare with $\E[\ps{\nabla\varphi(X_t)}{v_t(X_t)}]$.
  • Particles given → velocity $v_t(T_t(y))=\partial_tT_t(y)$. Density given in 1D → $v_t=-\partial_tF_t/\rho_t$.
  • A velocity field with zero weighted divergence → it moves particles, not the distribution.

Answer

$\dot x=x$ gives $x(t)=e^tx(0)$, so $\al_t=\mathcal U[0,e^t]$ (density $e^{-t}$ on $[0,e^t]$). Kinetic energy $\int x^2\,\dd\al_t=\frac{e^{2t}}3$. Check of the continuity equation on $(0,e^t)$: $\partial_t\rho_t=-e^{-t}$ and $\partial_x(\rho_tx)=e^{-t}$.
