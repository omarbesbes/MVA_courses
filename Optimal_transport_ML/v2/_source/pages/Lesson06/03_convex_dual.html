3. Convexity and duality: momentum and Hamilton–Jacobi OT4ML §14.2, pp. 331–333

Written with the density $\rho$ and the momentum $m=\rho v$, the Benamou–Brenier problem minimizes a convex function under a linear constraint; its dual maximizes $\int\varphi_1\dd\al_1-\int\varphi_0\dd\al_0$ over potentials satisfying the Hamilton–Jacobi inequality $\partial_t\varphi+\frac14\norm{\nabla\varphi}^2\le0$, and these potentials are Kantorovich potentials in disguise.

Why the problem is not convex in $(\rho,v)$

Convexity is what makes an optimization problem tractable: no spurious local minima, duality, efficient algorithms. In the unknowns $(\rho,v)$ the Benamou–Brenier problem fails on both counts.

  • The objective $\rho\norm{v}^2$ is not convex in $(\rho,v)$: on the line, $f(\rho,v)=\rho v^2$ gives $f(2,0)=0$ and $f(0,2)=0$ but $f(1,1)=1$ at the midpoint.
  • The constraint $\partial_t\rho+\dv(\rho v)=0$ is bilinear (a product of unknowns): the set of its solutions is not convex.

The fix, due to Benamou and Brenier: use the momentum (or flux) $m=\rho v$ as the unknown instead of $v$. Then the constraint $\partial_t\rho+\dv m=0$ is linear, and the objective becomes $\rho\norm{v}^2=\norm{m}^2/\rho$.

In the course OT4ML Def. 14.6 and (14.13), p. 331

Although (14.9) is not jointly convex in $(\al_t,v_t)$, it becomes convex after replacing velocities by momenta. […]

Definition 14.6 (Quadratic perspective action).

$$J(a,m):=\begin{cases}\norm{m}^2/a,&a>0,\\0,&a=0\text{ and }m=0,\\+\infty,&a=0\text{ and }m\ne0,\end{cases}\qquad(a,m)\in[0,+\infty)\times\R^d.\qquad(14.11)$$

If $\lambda$ dominates $\al$ and $\abs{\omega}$, define $\ \mathbb J(\al,\omega):=\int J\big(\frac{\dd\al}{\dd\lambda}(x),\frac{\dd\omega}{\dd\lambda}(x)\big)\dd\lambda(x)$. (14.12)

The pointwise function is lower-semicontinuous, convex and positively 1-homogeneous: $J(\eta a,\eta m)=\eta J(a,m)$ for $\eta\ge0$. […] The Benamou–Brenier problem can now be written with the linear continuity equation in the pair $(\al_t,\omega_t)$:

$$\mathcal W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1)=\inf_{\substack{\partial_t\al_t+\dv\omega_t=0\\\al_{t=0}=\al_0,\ \al_{t=1}=\al_1}}\int_0^1\mathbb J(\al_t,\omega_t)\,\dd t.\qquad(14.13)$$
$(a,m)$
A density value $a\ge0$ and a momentum value $m\in\R^d$ at one point of space-time.
$\norm{m}^2/a$
$=a\norm{v}^2$ with $v=m/a$: mass times speed squared, the kinetic energy density.
$+\infty$ when $a=0$, $m\ne0$
“No momentum without mass”: where there is no mass, nothing can flow. It is the value that makes $J$ lower semicontinuous ($\norm m^2/a\to+\infty$ as $a\downarrow0$ with $m$ fixed).
$\omega=\al v$
The momentum as a vector-valued measure, $\omega(A)=\int_Av\,\dd\al$. With densities, $\omega=m\,\dd x$ and $m=\rho v$. (CompOT writes $J_t$ for it, and $\theta$ for the function $J$: CompOT (7.3)–(7.5), pp. 107–108.)
(14.12), $\lambda$
A way to integrate $J$ when the measures have no density: write both against a common reference $\lambda$. Because $J$ is 1-homogeneous, the result does not depend on the choice of $\lambda$ (the Radon–Nikodym factors cancel).
(14.13)
The same minimum $W_2^2$, now over a convex set (linear constraint) of a convex function.

Why $J$ is convex: a sup of linear functions

The cleanest proof shows at the same time where the dual comes from. For $a\ge0$ and $m\in\R^d$:

$$J(a,m)=\sup\Big\{a\,b+\ps{m}{c}\ :\ (b,c)\in\R\times\R^d,\ \ b+\tfrac14\norm{c}^2\le0\Big\}.$$
  1. For $a>0$: for fixed $c$ the best $b$ is $b=-\frac14\norm c^2$ (the coefficient $a$ of $b$ is positive), so the sup is $\sup_c\big(-\frac a4\norm c^2+\ps mc\big)$.Make $b$ as large as the constraint allows.
  2. $$\sup_c\Big(-\frac a4\norm c^2+\ps mc\Big)=\frac{\norm m^2}{a},\quad\text{attained at }c=\frac{2m}{a}.$$A concave quadratic in $c$; its gradient $-\frac a2c+m$ vanishes at $c=2m/a$, where the value is $-\frac{\norm m^2}a+\frac{2\norm m^2}a$.
  3. For $a=0$: the sup of $\ps mc$ over $c\in\R^d$ is $0$ if $m=0$ and $+\infty$ otherwise.Take $c=sm$ with $s\to\infty$.
  4. So $J$ is a supremum of linear functions of $(a,m)$: it is convex and lower semicontinuous, and 1-homogeneous (each linear function is).A pointwise sup of convex (here linear) functions is convex; a sup of continuous functions is l.s.c.; multiplying $(a,m)$ by $\eta\ge0$ multiplies every linear function by $\eta$.
Worked example — convexity on numbers

$J(1,2)=4$ and $J(3,0)=0$; at the midpoint, $J(2,1)=\frac12\le\frac12(4+0)=2$. Homogeneity: $J(2,4)=8=2J(1,2)$. And the sup formula at $(a,m)=(1,2)$ is attained at $c=2m/a=4$, $b=-4$: $1\cdot(-4)+2\cdot4=4$. (Numerical maximization over $c$ on a fine grid gives $4.000$, $0.500$ and $2.000$ at $(1,2)$, $(2,1)$ and $(\frac12,-1)$.)

Reminder — perspective functions

If $f$ is convex on $\R^d$, its perspective $(a,m)\mapsto a\,f(m/a)$ is jointly convex on $a>0$. Here $f=\norm\cdot^2$. Perspectives appear elsewhere in this course: the KL divergence $\sum_ib_i\log(b_i/a_i)$ is built from the perspective of $-\log$ (Lesson 7). More: Reminders — convex functions.

The dual problem: Hamilton–Jacobi potentials

In the course OT4ML Prop. 14.7, p. 332

Proposition 14.7 (Dual Benamou–Brenier problem). Assume, for simplicity, that the densities are smooth, compactly supported, and that boundary terms vanish. Then the convex dynamic value (14.13) has the dual formulation

$$\mathcal W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1)=\sup_\varphi\Big\{\int_{\R^d}\varphi_1\,\dd\al_1-\int_{\R^d}\varphi_0\,\dd\al_0\ ;\ \partial_t\varphi_t+\frac14\norm{\nabla\varphi_t}^2\le0\Big\},\qquad(14.14)$$

where the supremum is over smooth test potentials $\varphi:[0,1]\times\R^d\to\R$. […] If $(\rho,m)$ and $\varphi$ are smooth primal and dual optimizers, then

$$m_t=\frac{\rho_t}2\nabla\varphi_t,\qquad\partial_t\varphi_t+\frac14\norm{\nabla\varphi_t}^2=0\quad\text{on }\{\rho_t>0\}.\qquad(14.15)$$

Equivalently, the optimal Eulerian velocity is $v_t=m_t/\rho_t=\nabla\varphi_t/2$.

$\varphi_t(x)$
A scalar function of time and space: the Lagrange multiplier of the continuity equation. Think of it as a “price” of mass at place $x$ and time $t$.
$\partial_t\varphi+\frac14\norm{\nabla\varphi}^2\le0$
A Hamilton–Jacobi inequality: the price cannot grow, along any path, faster than the kinetic energy spent (made precise below). The $\frac14$ comes from the action without $\frac12$; with $\frac12\int\!\int\norm v^2$ one gets $\frac12\norm{\nabla\varphi}^2$ and $v=\nabla\varphi$.
$\int\varphi_1\dd\al_1-\int\varphi_0\dd\al_0$
The gain of buying the mass at the start prices and selling it at the end prices.
(14.15)
At the optimum the velocity is a gradient (as in Prop. 14.3), $v_t=\nabla\varphi_t/2$, and the inequality is an equality where there is mass.
Reminder — Young’s inequality, the engine of the duality

For $\rho>0$, $m,p\in\R^d$: $\ \ps{m}{p}\le\frac{\norm m^2}{\rho}+\frac{\rho}{4}\norm p^2$, with equality iff $p=2m/\rho$. Proof: $0\le\big\lVert\frac{m}{\sqrt\rho}-\frac{\sqrt\rho}2p\big\rVert^2=\frac{\norm m^2}\rho-\ps mp+\frac\rho4\norm p^2$. It is the Fenchel inequality for the pair $\frac{\norm m^2}{\rho}$ and $\frac\rho4\norm p^2$ (convex conjugates). More: Reminders — Legendre transform, Lagrangian duality.

Idea of the proof

Weak duality is one integration by parts and Young’s inequality: test the continuity equation against $\varphi$, and bound the momentum term by the kinetic energy plus a term killed by the Hamilton–Jacobi inequality. Strong duality (no gap) is Fenchel–Rockafellar duality for the convex problem (14.13); the equality cases give (14.15).

Proof of weak duality, and of the optimality conditions

Let $(\rho,m)$ satisfy $\partial_t\rho+\dv m=0$ with $\rho_0\dd x=\al_0$, $\rho_1\dd x=\al_1$, and let $\varphi$ be smooth with $\partial_t\varphi+\frac14\norm{\nabla\varphi}^2\le0$.

  1. $$\int\varphi_1\dd\al_1-\int\varphi_0\dd\al_0=\int_0^1\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\Big(\int\varphi_t\rho_t\,\dd x\Big)\dd t=\int_0^1\!\!\int\big(\rho_t\partial_t\varphi_t+\varphi_t\partial_t\rho_t\big)\dd x\,\dd t.$$Fundamental theorem of calculus in $t$, then the product rule.
  2. $$\int\varphi_t\,\partial_t\rho_t\,\dd x=-\int\varphi_t\,\dv m_t\,\dd x=\int\ps{m_t}{\nabla\varphi_t}\,\dd x.$$Continuity equation, then integration by parts (boundary terms vanish).
  3. $$\rho\,\partial_t\varphi+\ps{m}{\nabla\varphi}\le\rho\,\partial_t\varphi+\frac{\norm m^2}{\rho}+\frac\rho4\norm{\nabla\varphi}^2=\rho\Big(\partial_t\varphi+\frac14\norm{\nabla\varphi}^2\Big)+J(\rho,m)\le J(\rho,m).$$Young’s inequality with $p=\nabla\varphi$ (where $\rho=0$: if $m\ne0$ then $J=+\infty$ and the bound is trivial; if $m=0$ both sides are $0$); then $\rho\ge0$ and the Hamilton–Jacobi inequality.
  4. $$\int\varphi_1\dd\al_1-\int\varphi_0\dd\al_0\le\int_0^1\!\!\int J(\rho_t,m_t)\,\dd x\,\dd t.$$Steps 1–3. Every dual value is below every primal value: $\sup\le\inf$.
  5. Equality in step 3 requires equality in Young, $\nabla\varphi=2m/\rho$, i.e. $m=\frac\rho2\nabla\varphi$, and $\partial_t\varphi+\frac14\norm{\nabla\varphi}^2=0$ wherever $\rho>0$. This is (14.15).Both inequalities of step 3 must be equalities wherever there is mass.

That the sup equals the inf (no duality gap) is the Fenchel–Rockafellar theorem applied to the convex problem (14.13); OT4ML computes the pointwise infimum of $J(\rho,m)-\rho\partial_t\varphi-\ps m{\nabla\varphi}$ over $(\rho,m)$: it is $0$ if the Hamilton–Jacobi inequality holds and $-\infty$ otherwise — the sup formula for $J$ above, read backwards.

Worked example — dual potentials for Gaussians on the line
  • Translation $\N(0,1)\to\N(a,1)$: the optimal velocity is $v\equiv a$, so $\nabla\varphi_t=2a$ and $\varphi_t(x)=2ax+c(t)$. Hamilton–Jacobi with equality: $c'(t)+\frac14(2a)^2=0$, so $c(t)=-a^2t$. Dual value: $\int(2ax-a^2)\,\dd\N(a,1)-\int2ax\,\dd\N(0,1)=2a^2-a^2=a^2=W_2^2$. ✓
  • Dilation $\N(0,\sigma_0^2)\to\N(0,\sigma_1^2)$, $\sigma_t=(1-t)\sigma_0+t\sigma_1$: $v_t(x)=\frac{\sigma_1-\sigma_0}{\sigma_t}x$, so $\varphi_t(x)=\frac{\sigma_1-\sigma_0}{\sigma_t}x^2$. Check: $\partial_t\varphi_t=-\frac{(\sigma_1-\sigma_0)^2}{\sigma_t^2}x^2$ and $\frac14(\partial_x\varphi_t)^2=\frac{(\sigma_1-\sigma_0)^2}{\sigma_t^2}x^2$: they cancel. Dual value $\frac{\sigma_1-\sigma_0}{\sigma_1}\sigma_1^2-\frac{\sigma_1-\sigma_0}{\sigma_0}\sigma_0^2=(\sigma_1-\sigma_0)^2=W_2^2$. ✓ (For $\sigma_0=1$, $\sigma_1=2$: value $1$; numerical residual of the HJ equation at $(t,x)=(0.3,0.7)$: $4\times10^{-11}$.)

The dual is Kantorovich duality in disguise

Take any smooth curve $\ga$ from $x$ (at $t=0$) to $y$ (at $t=1$), and an admissible $\varphi$. OT4ML (p. 332–333) observes:

  1. $$\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\varphi_t(\ga(t))=\partial_t\varphi_t(\ga(t))+\ps{\nabla\varphi_t(\ga(t))}{\dot\ga(t)}\le\partial_t\varphi_t+\frac14\norm{\nabla\varphi_t}^2+\norm{\dot\ga(t)}^2\le\norm{\dot\ga(t)}^2.$$Chain rule; Young’s inequality $\ps pq\le\frac14\norm p^2+\norm q^2$ (the case $\rho=1$); the Hamilton–Jacobi inequality.
  2. $$\varphi_1(y)-\varphi_0(x)\le\int_0^1\norm{\dot\ga(t)}^2\dd t,\quad\text{and with the straight line,}\quad\varphi_1(y)-\varphi_0(x)\le\norm{x-y}^2.$$Integrate step 1 in time; the straight line at constant speed has action $\norm{x-y}^2$.

So $(-\varphi_0,\varphi_1)$ is an admissible pair for the static Kantorovich dual with cost $\norm{x-y}^2$ (Lesson 4): $f(x)+g(y)\le c(x,y)$ with $f=-\varphi_0$, $g=\varphi_1$. The largest $\varphi_1$ compatible with a given $\varphi_0$ is $\varphi_1(y)=\min_x\big(\varphi_0(x)+\norm{x-y}^2\big)$, a $c$-transform (the Hopf–Lax formula). On the translation example: $\min_x(2ax+(x-y)^2)$ is attained at $x=y-a$ and equals $2ay-a^2=\varphi_1(y)$ ✓ (also checked numerically at $y=1.3$, $a=3$: $-1.2$).

Reminder — Kantorovich duality (Lesson 4)

$\Lc(\al,\be)=\max\big\{\int f\dd\al+\int g\dd\be\ :\ f(x)+g(y)\le c(x,y)\big\}$, and the best $g$ for a given $f$ is the $c$-transform $f^c(y)=\min_x c(x,y)-f(x)$. Here $f=-\varphi_0$ and $f^c=\varphi_1$. See Lesson 4 and Reminders — OT duality.

In machine learning — potentials instead of velocity fields

(14.15) says that the optimal velocity is a gradient, $v_t=\nabla\varphi_t/2$. Several OT-based flow models build this structure into the network: OT-Flow (Onken et al., AAAI 2021) parameterizes a scalar potential $\Phi_\theta(t,x)$, uses its gradient as the velocity, and adds the residual of the Hamilton–Jacobi(–Bellman) equation as a penalty, so that training pushes $(\rho,\nabla\Phi_\theta)$ towards the primal–dual optimality conditions. A scalar network is cheaper to constrain than a vector field, and the divergence needed for the likelihood (§1) is a Laplacian of $\Phi_\theta$.

Going further — the link with $W_1$ and Beckmann’s formulation (Lesson 4)

Integrate the continuity equation over $t\in[0,1]$: $\dv\big(\int_0^1m_t\,\dd t\big)=\rho_0-\rho_1$. The time average of any admissible momentum is a flux $s$ with $\dv s=\al_0-\al_1$, the constraint of Beckmann’s formulation $W_1=\min\{\int\norm{s}:\dv s=\al_0-\al_1\}$. By Cauchy–Schwarz, $\int_0^1\!\!\int\norm{m_t}\le\big(\int_0^1\!\!\int\norm{m_t}^2/\rho_t\big)^{1/2}\big(\int_0^1\!\!\int\rho_t\big)^{1/2}$, and the last factor is $1$: minimizing both sides gives once more $W_1\le W_2$. Other “mobilities” in place of $\norm m^2/\rho$ give other transport distances (CompOT §7.5, OT4ML §14.4).

Pitfall — $\frac14$ or $\frac12$?

With the action $\int\!\!\int\norm v^2$ (OT4ML, CompOT, StatOT, this page) the Hamilton–Jacobi inequality is $\partial_t\varphi+\frac14\norm{\nabla\varphi}^2\le0$ and $v=\nabla\varphi/2$. With $\frac12\int\!\!\int\norm v^2$ (and then $\frac12W_2^2$ on the left), it is $\partial_t\varphi+\frac12\norm{\nabla\varphi}^2\le0$ and $v=\nabla\varphi$. Both are correct; do not mix them.

Find the dual potential for $\N(0,1)\to\N(4,2^2)$ and check that the dual value is $17$.

Answer

$v_t(x)=4+\frac{x-4t}{1+t}$, so $\varphi_t(x)=2\int v_t=8x+\frac{(x-4t)^2}{1+t}+c(t)$. The Hamilton–Jacobi equation reduces to $c'(t)+16=0$, so $c(t)=-16t$. Dual value: $\E[8X_1+\frac{(X_1-4)^2}2-16]-\E[X_0^2]=32+2-16-1=17=W_2^2$ ✓ (also by numerical quadrature).
