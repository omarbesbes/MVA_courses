7. Diffusion models and the probability-flow ODE OT4ML §16.1, pp. 410–415; compact notes Prop. 16.6, p. 127

Diffusion models noise the data with an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process and learn to denoise; Tweedie’s formula turns the denoiser into the score $\nabla\log\rho_t$, and the score into the flow-matching velocity — so sampling a diffusion model with its probability-flow ODE is a flow-matching ODE from Gaussian noise.

The problem: the other road to the same flows

Diffusion models were developed independently of flow matching, from a stochastic point of view: destroy the data by adding noise little by little (a diffusion), then learn to reverse the destruction. They use a different vocabulary — scores, denoisers, SDEs — but when one endpoint is an isotropic Gaussian and the coupling is independent, the two constructions are “closely related … up to the usual change of time parametrization” (OT4ML p. 411): they produce the same noisy distributions up to a change of clock and a rescaling of space (end of this section). This section builds the dictionary.

Noising: the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process

In the course compact notes Prop. 16.6, p. 127

Proposition 16.6 (Static Gaussian interpolants and Ornstein–Uhlenbeck noising). Let $\al\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$ and $\sigma>0$. Let $X_0\sim\al$ and $Z\sim\N(0,\sigma^2\Id)$ be independent, and set $X_t=a_tX_0+b_tZ$ on an open time interval where the differentiable scalar functions satisfy $a_t>0$ and $b_t>0$. If $\rho_t$ is the density of $X_t$, then its flow-matching velocity is

$$v_t(x)=\frac{\dot a_t}{a_t}x+\Big(\frac{\dot a_tb_t^2}{a_t}-\dot b_tb_t\Big)\sigma^2\nabla\log\rho_t(x).\qquad(16.12)$$

For the variance-preserving coefficients $a_\tau=e^{-\tau}$ and $b_\tau=\sqrt{1-e^{-2\tau}}$, with $\tau>0$, these marginals are exactly those of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck diffusion

$$\dd X^{\mathrm{OU}}_\tau=-X^{\mathrm{OU}}_\tau\,\dd\tau+\sqrt2\,\sigma\,\dd B_\tau,\qquad X^{\mathrm{OU}}_0\sim\al,\qquad(16.13)$$

and (16.12) reduces to $v_\tau(x)=-x-\sigma^2\nabla\log\rho_\tau(x)$. Moreover, the finite-time coefficients $a_t=\cos(\pi t/2)$ and $b_t=\sin(\pi t/2)$ describe the same marginal curve after the time change $\tau=-\log\cos(\pi t/2)$.

direction of time
Here the data are at time $0$ ($X_0\sim\al$ is the data) and the noise grows with $t$ or $\tau$: the noising direction. Sampling runs it backwards.
$a_t,b_t$
The schedule: how much signal and how much noise are in $X_t$.
$\nabla\log\rho_t$
The score of the noised density: the direction in which the density increases fastest, divided by the density.
(16.13)
The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck SDE: a pull $-X$ towards the origin plus Brownian kicks. Its law converges to $\N(0,\sigma^2\Id)$ whatever the data: it forgets the data.
“same marginals”
The random path of the SDE is not the static interpolant $a_\tau X_0+b_\tau Z$ (one Gaussian draw for all times), but at each fixed $\tau$ the two have the same law. Only the laws matter for the curve $(\rho_\tau)$.
$\cos/\sin$ schedule
$e^{-\tau}=\cos(\pi t/2)$ gives $\sqrt{1-e^{-2\tau}}=\sin(\pi t/2)$: the same curve run with another clock.
Why the OU process has these marginals
  1. $$\dd\big(e^\tau X_\tau\big)=e^\tau\big(\dd X_\tau+X_\tau\,\dd\tau\big)=\sqrt2\,\sigma\,e^\tau\,\dd B_\tau .$$Product rule (no Itô correction: $e^\tau$ is deterministic), then the SDE (16.13).
  2. $$X_\tau=e^{-\tau}X_0+\sqrt2\,\sigma\int_0^\tau e^{-(\tau-s)}\,\dd B_s .$$Integrate from $0$ to $\tau$ and multiply by $e^{-\tau}$.
  3. The stochastic integral is independent of $X_0$, Gaussian, centred, with covariance $2\sigma^2\int_0^\tau e^{-2(\tau-s)}\dd s\,\Id=\sigma^2(1-e^{-2\tau})\Id$.Itô isometry: a deterministic integrand gives a Gaussian integral with variance $\int(\text{integrand})^2$.
  4. So $X_\tau$ has the law of $a_\tau X_0+b_\tau Z$ with $a_\tau=e^{-\tau}$, $b_\tau=\sqrt{1-e^{-2\tau}}$, $Z\sim\N(0,\sigma^2\Id)$ independent of $X_0$.Steps 2–3: $b_\tau^2\sigma^2=\sigma^2(1-e^{-2\tau})$. “Variance preserving”: if $X_0$ has covariance $\sigma^2\Id$, so does every $X_\tau$ ($a^2+b^2=1$).

Tweedie’s formula: denoising = score

In the course OT4ML Prop. 16.6, p. 411

Proposition 16.6 (Tweedie identity). Let $W$ be a random vector in $\R^d$ with law $\be\in\Pc_1(\R^d)$. For $\sigma>0$, observe $Z=W+\sigma\eps$, where $\eps\sim\N(0,\Id)$ is independent of $W$. Denote by $\rho_\sigma$ the smooth positive density of $\be_\sigma=\be*\N(0,\sigma^2\Id)$, which is the law of $Z$. Then the conditional mean admits the following everywhere-defined version:

$$\E[W\mid Z=z]=z+\sigma^2\nabla\log\rho_\sigma(z)\qquad\text{for all }z\in\R^d .$$
$W$, $Z$
A clean signal $W$ and its noisy observation $Z$ (noise of standard deviation $\sigma$).
$\be*\N(0,\sigma^2\Id)$
Convolution: the law of a sum of independent variables. Its density is smooth even if $\be$ is a sum of Diracs.
$\E[W\mid Z=z]$
The best denoiser in mean square (the posterior mean).
the formula
To denoise $z$, take one gradient step of size $\sigma^2$ uphill on $\log\rho_\sigma$: the optimal denoiser is known from the score of the noisy density, and conversely. No knowledge of $\be$ beyond $\rho_\sigma$ is needed.
Idea of the proof

The noisy density is an average of Gaussian bumps centred at the possible signals. Differentiating a Gaussian bump brings down a factor $-(z-w)/\sigma^2$; averaging over the posterior turns $w$ into $\E[W\mid Z=z]$.

Full proof

Let $\varphi_\sigma(u)=(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-d/2}e^{-\norm u^2/2\sigma^2}$, so $\nabla\varphi_\sigma(u)=-\sigma^{-2}u\,\varphi_\sigma(u)$.

  1. $$\rho_\sigma(z)=\int\varphi_\sigma(z-w)\,\dd\be(w),\qquad\E[W\mid Z=z]=\frac{1}{\rho_\sigma(z)}\int w\,\varphi_\sigma(z-w)\,\dd\be(w).$$The density of $W+\sigma\eps$ is the average of the Gaussian densities centred at $w$; Bayes’ rule gives the posterior $\varphi_\sigma(z-w)\dd\be(w)/\rho_\sigma(z)$.
  2. $$\nabla\rho_\sigma(z)=\int\nabla_z\varphi_\sigma(z-w)\,\dd\be(w)=-\frac1{\sigma^2}\int(z-w)\,\varphi_\sigma(z-w)\,\dd\be(w).$$Differentiation under the integral (the Gaussian and its derivatives are bounded; $\be\in\Pc_1$ makes $\int\norm w\varphi_\sigma\dd\be$ finite).
  3. $$\nabla\rho_\sigma(z)=-\frac1{\sigma^2}\Big(z\,\rho_\sigma(z)-\rho_\sigma(z)\,\E[W\mid Z=z]\Big).$$Split $z-w$ and use the two formulas of step 1.
  4. $$\E[W\mid Z=z]=z+\sigma^2\frac{\nabla\rho_\sigma(z)}{\rho_\sigma(z)}=z+\sigma^2\nabla\log\rho_\sigma(z).\qquad\square$$Divide by $\rho_\sigma(z)>0$ and rearrange.
Worked example — two atoms, and a Gaussian
  • Atoms. $W=\pm1$ with probability $\frac12$, $\sigma=0.5$. As in §6, $\E[W\mid Z=z]=\tanh(z/\sigma^2)$. At $z=0.3$: the score is $(\log\rho_\sigma)'(0.3)=2.1346$, and $z+\sigma^2\times2.1346=0.8337=\tanh(1.2)$ ✓ (also at $z=1$ and $z=-0.6$: $0.99933$ and $-0.98367$ both ways).
  • Gaussian signal. $W\sim\N(m,s^2)$: $Z\sim\N(m,s^2+\sigma^2)$, score $-\frac{z-m}{s^2+\sigma^2}$, so Tweedie gives $\E[W\mid Z=z]=m+\frac{s^2}{s^2+\sigma^2}(z-m)$: the classical shrinkage towards the mean (Gaussian conditioning formula). With $m=1$, $s=2$, $\sigma=1$, $z=3$: $2.6$ both ways.

From the score to the velocity

In the course OT4ML p. 411 and Prop. 16.7, p. 412

[…] The next two propositions are written in the noising direction, from a data law $\al$ to a Gaussian; reversing time gives the corresponding sampling flow. They also give an explicit closed form for $v_t$ and show that it is a gradient field. In this setting, $v_t$ is also the solution of the constrained least-squares problem (14.8).

Proposition 16.7 (Gaussian-endpoint flow-matching field). Let $X\sim\al$ and $Y\sim\N(0,\Id)$ be independent. For $t\in(0,1)$ set $Z_t=(1-t)X+tY$, $\al_t=\mathrm{Law}(Z_t)=\rho_t\,\dd x$. The regression minimizer $v^\star:\R^d\times(0,1)\to\R^d$ of

$$\min_v\int_0^1\!\!\iint\big\lVert y-x-v\big((1-t)x+ty,t\big)\big\rVert^2\,\dd\al(x)\,\dd\N(y)\,\dd t$$

is $\displaystyle v^\star(x,t)=-\frac{1}{1-t}x-\frac{t}{1-t}\nabla\log\rho_t(x)$. In particular, for each $t\in(0,1)$ this field is a gradient field, $v^\star(\cdot,t)=-\nabla\Big(\frac{\norm\cdot^2}{2(1-t)}+\frac{t}{1-t}\log\rho_t\Big)$.

In the sampling direction (noise $X_0\sim\N(0,\Id)$ at $t=0$, data $X_1$ at $t=1$, as in the sheet), the same computation reads:

  1. $$X_t=tX_1+(1-t)X_0:\qquad\E[tX_1\mid X_t=x]=x+(1-t)^2\nabla\log\rho_t(x).$$Tweedie with $W=tX_1$ (the signal) and $\sigma=1-t$ (the noise level).
  2. $$v_t(x)=\frac{\E[X_1\mid X_t=x]-x}{1-t}=\frac{1}{1-t}\Big(\frac{x+(1-t)^2\nabla\log\rho_t(x)}{t}-x\Big)=\frac xt+\frac{1-t}t\nabla\log\rho_t(x).$$Formula (1) of the sheet, then simplify: $\frac{x}{t(1-t)}-\frac{x}{1-t}=\frac{x}{t}$.
  3. $v_t=\nabla\big(\frac{\norm x^2}{2t}+\frac{1-t}{t}\log\rho_t\big)$ is a gradient, so by Prop. 14.3 (§1) it is the least-energy velocity of the path $(\al_t)$.This is OT4ML’s remark “in this setting, $v_t$ is also the solution of (14.8)”; it is Prop. 16.7 with $t\mapsto1-t$ and the velocity reversed.

Check on the two-atom example of §6 at $t=0.5$, $x=0.2$: $\frac xt+\frac{1-t}t(\log\rho_t)'(x)=0.359898$, equal to the closed form $\frac{\tanh(0.4)-0.2}{0.5}$ ✓. Learning the score, learning the denoiser and learning the velocity are the same task: each one is an affine function of the others at fixed $(t,x)$.

For a general schedule $X=a_t\cdot\text{data}+b_t\cdot\text{noise}$, (16.12) gives the velocity; for the variance-preserving OU coefficients the factor of $x$ is $\dot a/a=-1$ and the factor of the score is $\frac{\dot ab^2}a-\dot bb=-1$ (we checked both at $\tau=0.1,0.7,2$), hence $v_\tau=-x-\sigma^2\nabla\log\rho_\tau$.

The probability-flow ODE and the reverse SDE

In the course OT4ML Remark 16.5, pp. 410–411

[…] Thus the natural noisy analogue of (16.2) regresses the instantaneous drift […] and learns the drift term of a Fokker–Planck equation, not a pure continuity equation unless the diffusion tensor vanishes. When $\al_t=\rho_t\,\dd x$ has a smooth positive density, the same marginal curve can be represented, at least formally, by a probability-flow ODE […]. In the scalar spatially homogeneous case $D_t=\sigma_t^2\Id$, this reduces to

$$\bar v_t=v_t-\frac{\sigma_t^2}2\nabla\log\rho_t,$$

which is the familiar score correction relating diffusion SDEs to probability-flow ODEs.

The mechanism is one line of calculus. An SDE $\dd X_t=v_t(X_t)\dd t+\sigma_t\dd B_t$ has densities solving the Fokker–Planck equation $\partial_t\rho_t+\dv(\rho_tv_t)=\frac{\sigma_t^2}2\Delta\rho_t$ (refresher). Since $\Delta\rho=\dv(\rho\nabla\log\rho)$,

$$\partial_t\rho_t+\dv\Big(\rho_t\Big(v_t-\frac{\sigma_t^2}2\nabla\log\rho_t\Big)\Big)=0:$$

the same densities solve a continuity equation with the velocity $\bar v_t$ — the probability-flow ODE $\dot x=\bar v_t(x)$. Conversely, from an ODE one can build SDEs with the same marginals for any noise level $g_t$: $\dd X=\big(\bar v_t+\frac{g_t^2}2\nabla\log\rho_t\big)\dd t+g_t\dd B_t$. Check on OU: drift $-x$, noise $\sqrt2\sigma$, so $\bar v=-x-\sigma^2\nabla\log\rho$, the velocity of the compact notes’ Prop. 16.6 ✓.

Intuition — two ways to sample from a diffusion model

Stochastic (DDPM-style): start from noise and run the reverse-time SDE, which adds fresh noise at every step; the endpoint of a given noise vector is random. Deterministic (probability-flow ODE, DDIM-style): integrate the ODE backwards; each noise vector has one image, the map noise → data is a well-defined transport map, and fewer, larger steps are possible. Both have the same marginal distributions at every time.

Schedules change the paths, not the map

OT4ML p. 414 notes that a monotone time change of the linear bridge “only changes the speed of the flow” and “leaves the spatial integral curves unchanged”, while “diffusion models use a genuinely different family of noising paths”: the variance-preserving schedule bends the trajectories differently (OT4ML Figs. 16.3–16.5). Yet the terminal map does not depend on the schedule. OT4ML states this for Gaussians (Remark 16.9, §8); here is the general argument (derived here).

Proposition (schedule-free form of the flow) — derived here

Let $X_t=a_tX+b_tZ$ with data $X$, noise $Z\sim\N(0,\Id)$ independent, $(a_0,b_0)=(0,1)$, $(a_1,b_1)=(1,0)$, $b_t>0$ on $[0,1)$ and $\lambda_t=a_t/b_t$ increasing from $0$ to $+\infty$. Along a trajectory of the flow-matching field, $y=x/b_t$ solves

$$\frac{\dd y}{\dd\lambda}=\E\big[X\ \big|\ \lambda X+Z=y\big],\qquad y(0)=x_0\ \text{(the noise sample)},$$

an ODE that does not involve the schedule; the endpoint is $x_1=\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}y(\lambda)/\lambda$. Hence all such schedules define the same map noise $\to$ data.

Proof
  1. $v_t(x)=\E[\dot a_tX+\dot b_tZ\mid X_t=x]$ and $x=a_t\E[X\mid X_t=x]+b_t\E[Z\mid X_t=x]$.Prop. 16.3 with the pathwise velocity $\dot a_tX+\dot b_tZ$; and $\E[X_t\mid X_t=x]=x$.
  2. $$\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\frac{x}{b_t}=\frac{v_t(x)}{b_t}-\frac{\dot b_tx}{b_t^2}=\frac{\dot a_tb_t-a_t\dot b_t}{b_t^2}\,\E[X\mid X_t=x]=\dot\lambda_t\,\E[X\mid X_t=x].$$Quotient rule; replace $\E[Z\mid\cdot]$ by $(x-a_t\E[X\mid\cdot])/b_t$ from step 1: the $x$ terms cancel.
  3. $\{X_t=x\}=\{\lambda_tX+Z=x/b_t\}$, so $\E[X\mid X_t=x]=\E[X\mid\lambda X+Z=y]$ at $\lambda=\lambda_t$, $y=x/b_t$.Divide $a_tX+b_tZ=x$ by $b_t>0$.
  4. Change variable $t\to\lambda$ (allowed since $\dot\lambda_t>0$): $\frac{\dd y}{\dd\lambda}=\E[X\mid\lambda X+Z=y]$. At $t=0$, $b_0=1$ and $y=x_0$; as $t\to1$, $x_t=b_ty=a_t\,y/\lambda_t\to\lim y/\lambda$ since $a_t\to1$.Steps 2–3; $b_t=a_t/\lambda_t$.

In machine learning — denoising diffusion models

Data: images $x$. Noising: $x_t=a_tx+b_t\eps$ with $\eps\sim\N(0,\Id)$ and a fixed schedule (variance preserving in DDPM: Ho, Jain, Abbeel, NeurIPS 2020). Model: a U-Net $\eps_\theta(x_t,t)$ trained by the regression $\E\norm{\eps_\theta(x_t,t)-\eps}^2$ — by Tweedie, $\eps_\theta\approx\E[\eps\mid x_t]=-b_t\nabla\log\rho_t(x_t)$, a denoising score matching estimator of the score (score matching: Hyvärinen 2005; its denoising form: Vincent 2011). Sampling: DDPM runs a stochastic reverse chain with about $1000$ steps; Song et al. (Score-based generative modeling through SDEs, ICLR 2021) introduced the probability-flow ODE, and the deterministic DDIM sampler (Song, Meng, Ermon, ICLR 2021) can be read as its discretization, producing good images in tens of steps; Karras et al. (NeurIPS 2022) reorganized the design space around this ODE view. Pointers: OT4ML p. 407 cites score matching, DDPM and score SDEs.

Pitfall — which way does time run?

OT4ML’s Props. 16.6–16.7 and the compact notes’ Props. 16.4 and 16.6 put the data at $t=0$ (noising); the exercise sheet puts the noise at $t=0$ (sampling). Translating changes $t$ into $1-t$ and the sign of the velocity: OT4ML’s $-\frac{x}{1-t}-\frac{t}{1-t}\nabla\log\rho_t$ becomes $\frac xt+\frac{1-t}t\nabla\log\rho_t$. Always check which endpoint is the Gaussian.

Pitfall — the score is singular at the data

For data made of atoms (or concentrated near a low-dimensional set), $\nabla\log\rho_t$ blows up as the noise vanishes (like $1/b_t^2$ at a fixed point away from the data). Samplers stop at a small positive noise level (OT4ML p. 414: “the integration is stopped at a small positive noising time before the Dirac endpoint”).

Reflexes
  • Denoiser ↔ score: $\E[W\mid Z=z]=z+\sigma^2\nabla\log\rho_\sigma(z)$.
  • Score ↔ velocity (noise at $0$, linear bridge): $v_t=\frac xt+\frac{1-t}t\nabla\log\rho_t$; general schedule: (16.12).
  • SDE ↔ ODE with the same marginals: subtract $\frac{\sigma^2}2\nabla\log\rho$ from the drift.
  • Change of schedule with the same endpoints → same terminal map (schedule-free variables $y=x/b$, $\lambda=a/b$).

For Gaussian data $X_1\sim\N(m,s^2)$ on the line and noise $X_0\sim\N(0,1)$ (linear bridge, independent), what is $\E[X_1\mid X_t=x]$?

Answer

$X_t\sim\N(tm,\sigma_t^2)$ with $\sigma_t^2=(1-t)^2+t^2s^2$, and $\mathrm{Cov}(X_1,X_t)=ts^2$. Gaussian conditioning: $\E[X_1\mid X_t=x]=m+\frac{ts^2}{\sigma_t^2}(x-tm)$. (Tweedie with $W=tX_1$, $\sigma=1-t$ gives the same.)
