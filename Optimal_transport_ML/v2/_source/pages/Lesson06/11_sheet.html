Notation of the sheet: $X_t=tX_1+(1-t)X_0$ with noise $X_0$ and data $X_1$ (as on this page); $\varphi_t$ is the flow of $v_t$ (on the rest of the page $\varphi$ is a dual potential and the flow is $T_t$); $\al,\be$ are the laws of $X_0,X_1$.

Exercise 1 — Affine changes of the coupling

In the course sheet, Exercise 1

Starting from a coupling $(X_0,X_1)$, we define the linear interpolation $X_t=tX_1+(1-t)X_0$. We remind that the velocity field defined in flow matching writes

$$v_t(x)=\E[X_1-X_0\mid X_t=x]=\frac1{1-t}\E[X_1-X_t\mid X_t=x]=\frac1{1-t}\big(\E[X_1\mid X_t=x]-x\big).\qquad(1)$$

Let $A\in\R^{d\times d}$ be invertible, $b\in\R^d$ and $c\in\R_+^*$. Show the following properties:

(i) The velocity $v_t^{A,b}$ defined from the coupling $(AX_0+b,AX_1+b)$ is given by $v_t^{A,b}(x)=Av_t(A^{-1}(x-b))$.

(ii) The velocity $v_t^b$ from the coupling $(X_0,X_1+b)$ is given by $v_t^b(x)=v_t(x-tb)+b$.

(iii) The velocity $v_t^c$ defined from the coupling $(X_0,cX_1)$ is given by

$$v_t^c=\frac{c}{1-t+tc}\,v_r\Big(\frac{x}{1-t+tc}\Big)+\frac{c-1}{1-t+tc}\,x,\qquad\text{with}\quad r=\frac{tc}{1-t+tc}.$$
Hint

For each new coupling, write the new interpolant and its pathwise velocity in terms of the old ones. Conditioning on an invertible function of $X_t$ is conditioning on $X_t$. In (iii), factor $s=1-t+tc$ out of $(1-t)X_0+tcX_1$, and write $cX_1-X_0$ as a combination of $X_r$ and $X_1-X_0$.

Solution

(i)

  1. The new interpolant is $(1-t)(AX_0+b)+t(AX_1+b)=AX_t+b$, and its pathwise velocity is $(AX_1+b)-(AX_0+b)=A(X_1-X_0)$.Linearity; the $b$’s cancel in the difference.
  2. $\{AX_t+b=x\}=\{X_t=A^{-1}(x-b)\}$.$A$ is invertible.
  3. $$v^{A,b}_t(x)=\E[A(X_1-X_0)\mid X_t=A^{-1}(x-b)]=A\,v_t\big(A^{-1}(x-b)\big).$$Linearity of conditional expectation, then the definition (1) of $v_t$.

(ii) The interpolant is $(1-t)X_0+t(X_1+b)=X_t+tb$, the pathwise velocity $X_1+b-X_0$, and $\{X_t+tb=x\}=\{X_t=x-tb\}$: $\ v^b_t(x)=\E[X_1-X_0\mid X_t=x-tb]+b=v_t(x-tb)+b$.

(iii) Let $s=1-t+tc>0$ and $r=tc/s\in[0,1]$, so that $1-r=\frac{1-t}{s}$.

  1. $$(1-t)X_0+t(cX_1)=s\big((1-r)X_0+rX_1\big)=sX_r .$$$\frac{1-t}{s}=1-r$ and $\frac{tc}{s}=r$.
  2. $$cX_1-X_0=(c-1)X_r+\big(c(1-r)+r\big)(X_1-X_0)=(c-1)X_r+\frac cs(X_1-X_0).$$Expand $(c-1)X_r=(c-1)(1-r)X_0+(c-1)rX_1$ and compare coefficients: $c-(c-1)r=c(1-r)+r$ for $X_1$, and $1+(c-1)(1-r)=c(1-r)+r$ for $X_0$; finally $c(1-r)+r=\frac{c(1-t)+tc}{s}=\frac cs$.
  3. $$v^c_t(x)=\E\big[cX_1-X_0\ \big|\ sX_r=x\big]=(c-1)\frac xs+\frac cs\,v_r\Big(\frac xs\Big).$$Condition step 2 on $\{sX_r=x\}=\{X_r=x/s\}$: $X_r$ becomes $x/s$, and $\E[X_1-X_0\mid X_r=x/s]=v_r(x/s)$. This is the formula of the sheet.

Reading. Flow matching commutes with any affine change of variables applied to both endpoints (i); translating the data shifts the field and its argument (ii); dilating the data rescales space and changes the clock $t\mapsto r$ (iii). The same changes act on the flows: if $x(t)$ solves $\dot x=v_t(x)$, then $Ax(t)+b$ solves the equation of (i), $x(t)+tb$ the one of (ii), and $s_t\,x(r_t)$ the one of (iii) (use $\dot s=c-1$ and $\dot r=c/s^2$). At $t=1$: the flow maps become $A\varphi_1(A^{-1}(x-b))+b$, $\varphi_1+b$ and $c\varphi_1$ — used in Exercise 2.

Check. For a random jointly Gaussian coupling in $\R^2$ (with means and cross-covariance), where all fields are explicit, the three identities hold at 200 random $(t,x)$ up to $4.4\times10^{-15}$, $1.8\times10^{-15}$ and $5.3\times10^{-15}$.

Exercise 2 — What becomes the optimal map?

In the course sheet, Exercise 2

Assume that $\al$ is an absolutely continuous probability measure with respect to the Lebesgue measure, and $\be$ is a probability measure on $\R^d$. Write $T^*$ the optimal transport map between $\al$ and $\be$ for the squared Euclidean cost. Can we say something on what $T^*$ becomes in the cases considered in Exercise 1?

Hint

Brenier: a map pushing the source to the target is optimal iff it is the gradient of a convex function. Write $T^*=\nabla\phi$ with $\phi$ convex and check which transformed maps are still such gradients. In case (i) the cost becomes $\norm{A(x-y)}^2$: when is it a multiple of $\norm{x-y}^2$?

Solution

Write $T^*=\nabla\phi$, $\phi$ convex (Brenier, Lesson 1). In each case the new source is the law of the new $X_0$ and the new target the law of the new $X_1$.

  • (ii) target translated by $b$. $T^*+b=\nabla(\phi+\ps{b}{\cdot})$ pushes $\al$ to the translated target and is the gradient of a convex function: it is the optimal map.
  • (iii) target dilated by $c>0$. $cT^*=\nabla(c\phi)$, with $c\phi$ convex: optimal.
  • (i) both endpoints moved by $x\mapsto Ax+b$. The map $S(x)=AT^*(A^{-1}(x-b))+b$ pushes the new source to the new target. If $A=\lambda Q$ is a similarity ($Q$ orthogonal, $\lambda>0$), then $S=\nabla\psi$ with $\psi(x)=\lambda^2\phi\big(\lambda^{-1}Q\T(x-b)\big)+\ps bx$, convex: optimal. Equivalently the cost $\norm{Ax-Ay}^2=\lambda^2\norm{x-y}^2$ is proportional to the original one. For a general $A$, $S$ is not optimal in general: its Jacobian $A\,\nabla T^*\,A^{-1}$ need not be symmetric.

Counterexample for (i). $\al=\N(0,\Id_2)$, $\be=\N(0,\mathrm{diag}(4,1))$, $T^*=\mathrm{diag}(2,1)$, $W_2^2=1$. Shear $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, $b=0$: the new laws are $\N\big(0,\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\big)$ and $\N\big(0,\begin{pmatrix}5&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\big)$, and $S=AT^*A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ is not symmetric. Its cost is $1.0000$, while the optimal map $\begin{pmatrix}1.698&-0.243\\-0.243&1.213\end{pmatrix}$ costs $0.7538$: $S$ is $32.7\%$ too expensive. For the similarity $A=1.8\,R_{0.7}$, $AT^*A^{-1}$ and the optimal map agree to $2\times10^{-16}$.

Why the question is asked: flow matching versus optimal transport. By Exercise 1 the flow-matching maps transform exactly like $S$ — for every invertible $A$ (the coupling $(AX_0+b,AX_1+b)$ is still independent if $(X_0,X_1)$ is). Optimal maps only follow similarities. So flow matching cannot be optimal in general: with independent pairs, $\N(0,\Id)\to\N(0,\mathrm{diag}(4,1))$ gives the optimal map $\mathrm{diag}(2,1)$ (commuting covariances, §8), and after the shear it gives $S$, not optimal — exactly the non-commuting situation of OT4ML Prop. 16.8 (the two new covariances do not commute).

Exercise 3 — On the line, flow matching is optimal

In the course sheet, Exercise 3

Assume that $\al$ and $\be$ are probability measures on $\R$. Let $(X_0,X_1)$ be a coupling between $\al$ and $\be$ such that the velocity field $v_t(x)=\E[X_1-X_0\mid X_t=x]$ has a unique flow on $[0,1]$. Show that the flow $\varphi_t$ associated to $v_t$ is such that $\varphi_1$ is an optimal transport map between $\al$ and $\be$.

Hint

Two trajectories of a one-dimensional ODE with unique solutions cannot cross. What does this say about $x\mapsto\varphi_1(x)$? Then: on the line, a coupling supported by the graph of a nondecreasing map is optimal.

Solution
  1. $\varphi_1$ transports $\al$ to $\be$. The curve $\al_t=\mathrm{Law}(X_t)$ solves the continuity equation with $v_t$ (Prop. 16.3, §6). The flow also produces a solution, $\pf{(\varphi_t)}{\al}$ (Prop. 14.2, §1), with the same initial value $\al$; for a velocity with a unique flow the solution is unique (among finite-energy solutions, by the superposition principle mentioned in §2; here $\int_0^1\!\int\abs{v_t}^2\dd\al_t\dd t\le\E\abs{X_1-X_0}^2$, finite when $\al,\be$ have second moments), so $\pf{(\varphi_t)}{\al}=\al_t$, and at $t=1$: $\pf{(\varphi_1)}{\al}=\be$.OT4ML p. 410: “if the continuity equation has a unique solution for this velocity, then $\pf{(T_t)}{\al_0}=\al_t$”.
  2. $\varphi_1$ is nondecreasing. Let $xUniqueness of the flow; intermediate value theorem.
  3. $\varphi_1$ is optimal. The plan $\pf{(\Id,\varphi_1)}{\al}$ couples $\al$ and $\be$ (step 1) and is supported on the graph of a nondecreasing map, a monotone set: on the line such a plan is the monotone (north-west corner) plan, which is optimal for $\abs{x-y}^2$ and for every convex cost of $x-y$ (Lesson 1). When $\al$ has no atoms, $\varphi_1=C_\be^{-1}\circ C_\al$ $\al$-a.e.One-dimensional optimal transport.

Remarks. The coupling does not matter: even the independent coupling yields the optimal map. The path is not the geodesic in general (two atoms: action $0.982$ against $W_2^2=0.404$, §6). In dimension $2$ the argument breaks down: trajectories can turn around each other without crossing, and §8 measures the price.

Check. Independent coupling of $\N(0,1)$ and $\frac12\N(-1,0.3^2)+\frac12\N(2,0.5^2)$: the field is given by Exercise 5 in closed form; integrating it (tolerance $10^{-11}$) from 9 quantiles of $\N(0,1)$ lands within $1.1\times10^{-10}$ of the monotone map $C_\be^{-1}(\Phi(z))$.

Exercise 4 — The Gaussian velocity field

In the course sheet, Exercise 4

Assume that $(X_0,X_1)\sim\N(0,\Sigma)$ with $\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_0&\Sigma_{10}\\\Sigma_{01}&\Sigma_1\end{pmatrix}$, for positive definite $\Sigma_0$ and $\Sigma_1$. Remind that if $(Z,T)$ is a Gaussian vector, then $\E[Z\mid T]=\E[Z]+\mathrm{Cov}(Z,T)\mathrm{Cov}(T,T)^{-1}(T-\E[T])$.

(i) Show that the velocity field $v_t(x):=\E[X_1-X_0\mid X_t=x]$ is given by

$$v_t(x)=\frac1{1-t}\Big(\big((1-t)\Sigma_{01}+t\Sigma_1\big)\Sigma_t^{-1}-\Id\Big)x,\qquad(2)$$

where $\Sigma_t=\mathrm{Cov}(X_t)=(1-t)^2\Sigma_0+(1-t)t(\Sigma_{01}+\Sigma_{10})+t^2\Sigma_1$.

(ii) Let $\Sigma_{01}=\Sigma_{10}=0$ and assume that $\Sigma_0$ and $\Sigma_1$ can be jointly diagonalized. Show that the flow $\varphi_t$ associated to $v_t$ is such that $\varphi_1$ is an optimal transport map between $\al$ and $\be$.

Hint

(i) Apply the recalled formula with $Z=X_1$ and $T=X_t$, then use (1). In the block matrix, $\Sigma_{01}$ is the block below the diagonal: $\Sigma_{01}=\E[X_1X_0\T]=\mathrm{Cov}(X_1,X_0)$. (ii) Read “jointly diagonalized” as “diagonal in one orthonormal basis”. In that basis the problem splits into one-dimensional Gaussian problems.

Solution

(i)

  1. $\mathrm{Cov}(X_t)=\Sigma_t$ as stated, and $\mathrm{Cov}(X_1,X_t)=(1-t)\mathrm{Cov}(X_1,X_0)+t\,\mathrm{Cov}(X_1,X_1)=(1-t)\Sigma_{01}+t\Sigma_1$.Bilinearity of the covariance, with the block convention of the sheet ($\Sigma_{01}$ lower-left).
  2. $$\E[X_1\mid X_t=x]=\big((1-t)\Sigma_{01}+t\Sigma_1\big)\Sigma_t^{-1}x .$$Gaussian conditioning, all means zero. This needs $\Sigma_t$ invertible: automatic when the cross-covariance vanishes ($\Sigma_t\succeq(1-t)^2\Sigma_0\succ0$ for $t<1$), implicitly assumed by the sheet otherwise (it fails for $X_1=-X_0$ at $t=\frac12$, where $X_t=0$).
  3. $$v_t(x)=\frac{\E[X_1\mid X_t=x]-x}{1-t}=\frac1{1-t}\Big(\big((1-t)\Sigma_{01}+t\Sigma_1\big)\Sigma_t^{-1}-\Id\Big)x .\qquad(2)$$The third form of (1).

With $\Sigma_{01}=0$ this simplifies: since $t\Sigma_1-\Sigma_t=(1-t)\big(t\Sigma_1-(1-t)\Sigma_0\big)$, we get $v_t(x)=\big(t\Sigma_1-(1-t)\Sigma_0\big)\Sigma_t^{-1}x$, the field (16.11) of OT4ML Prop. 16.8 (§8).

(ii) Assume $\Sigma_0=PD_0P\T$ and $\Sigma_1=PD_1P\T$ with $P$ orthogonal and $D_0=\mathrm{diag}(d_{0,i})$, $D_1=\mathrm{diag}(d_{1,i})$ positive.

  1. $\Sigma_t=P\big((1-t)^2D_0+t^2D_1\big)P\T$ and $v_t(x)=P\,\Lambda_t\,P\T x$ with $\Lambda_t$ diagonal, $(\Lambda_t)_{ii}=\frac{td_{1,i}-(1-t)d_{0,i}}{(1-t)^2d_{0,i}+t^2d_{1,i}}$.Everything is diagonal in the basis $P$.
  2. In the coordinates $y=P\T x$, each $y_i$ solves $\dot y_i=(\Lambda_t)_{ii}y_i$, whose solution is $y_i(t)=y_i(0)\sqrt{\frac{(1-t)^2d_{0,i}+t^2d_{1,i}}{d_{0,i}}}$.Differentiate: $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\log\sqrt{(1-t)^2d_0+t^2d_1}=\frac{td_1-(1-t)d_0}{(1-t)^2d_0+t^2d_1}$.
  3. $$\varphi_1=P\,\mathrm{diag}\Big(\sqrt{d_{1,i}/d_{0,i}}\Big)P\T=\Sigma_0^{-1/2}\big(\Sigma_0^{1/2}\Sigma_1\Sigma_0^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\Sigma_0^{-1/2}.$$At $t=1$; for commuting matrices both expressions are computed eigenvalue by eigenvalue.
  4. $\varphi_1$ is symmetric positive definite and pushes $\N(0,\Sigma_0)$ to $\N(0,\Sigma_1)$: it is the gradient of the convex function $\frac12\ps{x}{\varphi_1x}$, hence the optimal map (Brenier). $\square$Lesson 1 (Gaussian Brenier map). Alternatively apply Exercise 3 to each coordinate.

Check. For a random correlated Gaussian coupling in $\R^2$, formula (2) agrees with the direct conditional expectation to $1.1\times10^{-14}$ at 200 random $(t,x)$; a least-squares regression of $X_1-X_0$ on $X_t$ over $2\times10^6$ simulated pairs ($t=0.37$) recovers its matrix to $10^{-3}$. Reading $\Sigma_{01}$ as $\mathrm{Cov}(X_0,X_1)$ instead gives errors of order $10$ when the cross-covariance is not symmetric: the block convention matters.

Note on the sheet — what “jointly diagonalized” means

Read it in the usual sense: a common basis of eigenvectors, i.e. one invertible $P$ with $P^{-1}\Sigma_0P$ and $P^{-1}\Sigma_1P$ diagonal. For symmetric matrices this is the same as one orthonormal basis, and the same as $\Sigma_0\Sigma_1=\Sigma_1\Sigma_0$; with this reading (ii) is true (solution above). Do not confuse it with diagonalization by a congruence: every pair of positive definite matrices admits an invertible $M$ with $M\Sigma_0M\T=\Id$ and $M\Sigma_1M\T$ diagonal, so with that reading (ii) would apply to all pairs and would be false. Example: $\Sigma_0=\mathrm{diag}(4,1)$ and $\Sigma_1=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ satisfy $M\Sigma_0M\T=\Id$, $M\Sigma_1M\T=\mathrm{diag}(0.349,2.151)$ for $M=U\T\Sigma_0^{-1/2}$ ($U$ the eigenvectors of $\Sigma_0^{-1/2}\Sigma_1\Sigma_0^{-1/2}$), yet $\Sigma_0\Sigma_1-\Sigma_1\Sigma_0=\begin{pmatrix}0&3\\-3&0\end{pmatrix}\ne0$ and the flow map costs $16.9\%$ more than $W_2^2$ (§8). The hypothesis that works is diagonalization by an orthogonal matrix, equivalently $\Sigma_0\Sigma_1=\Sigma_1\Sigma_0$ (OT4ML Prop. 16.8). Two smaller points: $\al$ and $\be$ are not defined in Exercise 4 (they are the laws of $X_0$ and $X_1$), and the sheet’s block names are reversed with respect to the usual $\Sigma_{ij}=\mathrm{Cov}(X_i,X_j)$ — consistent within the sheet, as formula (2) shows.

Exercise 5 — Gaussian mixtures, and noise to $K$ atoms

In the course sheet, Exercise 5

Assume that $(X_0,X_1)\sim\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k\N(m^k,\Sigma^k)$ with $m^k=\begin{pmatrix}m_0^k\\m_1^k\end{pmatrix}$ and $\Sigma^k=\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_0^k&\Sigma_{10}^k\\\Sigma_{01}^k&\Sigma_1^k\end{pmatrix}$ for positive definite $\Sigma_0^k$ and $\Sigma_1^k$. Write $v_t^k$ the velocity field (2) for the covariance matrix $\Sigma^k$ and write $w_t^k(x)=v_t^k(x-tm_1^k-(1-t)m_0^k)+m_1^k-m_0^k$. Show that the velocity field $v_t(x):=\E[X_1-X_0\mid X_t=x]$ is given by

$$v_t(x)=\sum_{k=1}^K\alpha^k(x)\,w_t^k(x),\qquad(3)$$

where $\alpha^k(x)=\frac{\pi_kp_t^k(x)}{\sum_{j=1}^K\pi_jp_t^j(x)}$, with $p_t^j$ is the Gaussian density of $\N(m_t^j,\Sigma_t^j)$ with $m_t^j=tm_1^j+(1-t)m_0^j$ and $\Sigma_t^j=t^2\Sigma_1^j+(1-t)^2\Sigma_0^j+t(1-t)(\Sigma_{10}^j+\Sigma_{01}^j)$. What happens if $X_0$ is a standard centered Gaussian and $X_1$ follows a discrete distribution of $K$ equal Dirac masses?

Hint

Introduce the hidden label $\kappa$ of the component, $\P(\kappa=k)=\pi_k$. Condition on $X_t$ and $\kappa$ first, then average over $\kappa$ given $X_t=x$ (tower property). Given $\kappa=k$, the centred pair $(X_0-m_0^k,X_1-m_1^k)$ is the coupling of Exercise 4. For the Dirac case take $m_0^k=0$, $\Sigma_0^k=\Id$, $m_1^k=y_k$ and $\Sigma_1^k=\Sigma_{01}^k=\Sigma_{10}^k=0$.

Solution
  1. $$\E[X_1-X_0\mid X_t=x]=\sum_k\P(\kappa=k\mid X_t=x)\,\E[X_1-X_0\mid X_t=x,\kappa=k].$$Tower property over the hidden label $\kappa$.
  2. $\P(\kappa=k\mid X_t=x)=\alpha^k(x)$.Given $\kappa=k$, $X_t$ is Gaussian with mean $m_t^k$ and covariance $\Sigma_t^k$ (linear combination of a Gaussian vector), density $p_t^k$; Bayes’ rule with prior $\pi_k$.
  3. Given $\kappa=k$, let $\tilde X_i=X_i-m_i^k$: a centred Gaussian coupling with covariance $\Sigma^k$, whose interpolant is $\tilde X_t=X_t-m_t^k$. So $\E[\tilde X_1-\tilde X_0\mid\tilde X_t=z]=v_t^k(z)$.Exercise 4 (i) for the component.
  4. $$\E[X_1-X_0\mid X_t=x,\kappa=k]=v_t^k(x-m_t^k)+m_1^k-m_0^k=w_t^k(x).$$$X_1-X_0=\tilde X_1-\tilde X_0+m_1^k-m_0^k$ and $\{X_t=x\}=\{\tilde X_t=x-m_t^k\}$; $m_t^k=tm_1^k+(1-t)m_0^k$.
  5. Steps 1, 2 and 4 give (3). $\square$Substitute $\alpha^k$ and $w_t^k$ in the sum of step 1.

Noise to $K$ atoms. Take $\pi_k=\frac1K$, $m_0^k=0$, $\Sigma_0^k=\Id$, $m_1^k=y_k$, $\Sigma_1^k=\Sigma_{01}^k=\Sigma_{10}^k=0$. (These $\Sigma_1^k$ are not positive definite as the sheet requires, but formula (2) still makes sense for $t<1$, since $\Sigma_t^k=(1-t)^2\Id$ is invertible.)

  1. $(2)$ gives $v_t^k(z)=\frac{1}{1-t}\big(0\cdot\Sigma_t^{-1}-\Id\big)z=-\frac{z}{1-t}$, so $w_t^k(x)=-\frac{x-ty_k}{1-t}+y_k=\frac{y_k-x}{1-t}$.$(1-t)\Sigma_{01}^k+t\Sigma_1^k=0$; $m_t^k=ty_k$.
  2. $\alpha^k(x)\propto\exp\big(-\frac{\norm{x-ty_k}^2}{2(1-t)^2}\big)$, i.e. a softmax.$p_t^k$ is the density of $\N(ty_k,(1-t)^2\Id)$; the normalizing constants are the same for all $k$.
  3. $$v_t(x)=\sum_{k=1}^K\mathrm{softmax}_k\Big(-\frac{\norm{x-ty_k}^2}{2(1-t)^2}\Big)\frac{y_k-x}{1-t}=\frac{\E[X_1\mid X_t=x]-x}{1-t}.$$(3); the posterior mean of the atoms is $\sum_k\alpha^k(x)y_k$.

What happens. Each particle is pulled towards the posterior average of the atoms, with a strength growing like $\frac1{1-t}$. Early on ($t$ small) the softmax is flat and all particles head towards the mean of the atoms; as $t\to1$ it becomes a hard assignment (the posterior concentrates on the atom whose $ty_k$ is closest) and each particle lands exactly on one atom — the two-atom example of §6 is the case $K=2$, $y=\pm1$. The noise space is thus cut into cells, one per atom: these are the cells of the probability-flow map of a diffusion model (all schedules give the same map, §7). On the line the cells are the quantile intervals of the optimal map (Exercise 3); in the plane they are not the Laguerre cells of optimal transport in general (about 5.7% of the mass differs for three atoms in a flat triangle, §8).

Check. For atoms $(-2,0),(2,1),(0,-2)$ at $t=\frac12$ and $x=(0.5,0.3)$: the formula gives $v=(2.7341,1.1585)$ (posterior weights $0.016,0.949,0.035$); among $2.4\times10^8$ simulated pairs, the $37\,820$ with $X_{1/2}$ within $0.02$ of $x$ have average $X_1-X_0=(2.736,1.160)\pm0.006$. The general formula (3) and the softmax form agree to $10^{-6}$.
