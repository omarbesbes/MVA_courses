What you will be able to do after this page
  1. Describe a moving distribution in two ways — particles following a velocity field (Lagrangian) and a density obeying the continuity equation $\partial_t\rho_t+\dv(\rho_tv_t)=0$ (Eulerian) — and pass from one to the other.
  2. State and prove the Benamou–Brenier formula: $W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1)$ is the least kinetic energy needed to move $\al_0$ onto $\al_1$, and the optimal motion is the displacement interpolation of Lesson 3.
  3. Explain why the change of variables $m=\rho v$ makes the problem convex, write its Hamilton–Jacobi dual, and describe how it is solved on a grid (staggered grid, Douglas–Rachford).
  4. Derive the flow-matching velocity as a conditional expectation, explain why it can be learned by plain least squares, and compute it for Gaussians, mixtures and atoms (the five exercises of the sheet).
  5. Connect diffusion models to flow matching through Tweedie’s formula and the probability-flow ODE.
  6. Decide when the map generated by a flow is optimal: always on the line, for Gaussians iff the covariances commute, and with optimal couplings; know what minibatch OT and rectified flows do; place Schrödinger bridges and multimarginal barycenters in the picture.

The story of the lesson in seven steps

Optimal transport can be computed as a motion: the cheapest way to move a distribution is a flow of particles along straight lines, and the generative models of machine learning build such flows — not always the cheapest — by regression.

  1. Moving mass (§1). A distribution can move in two equivalent descriptions: particles following a velocity field $v_t(x)$ (Lagrangian), or a density changing in time by the continuity equation $\partial_t\rho_t+\dv(\rho_tv_t)=0$ (Eulerian). Many velocities produce the same motion.
  2. The cheapest motion (§2). Among all motions from $\al_0$ to $\al_1$, the least total kinetic energy is $W_2^2(\al_0,\al_1)$ (Benamou–Brenier). The optimal motion moves each particle along a straight line at constant speed: the displacement interpolation of Lesson 3, built on Brenier’s map of Lesson 1.
  3. Convexity, duality, numerics (§3–§4). With the momentum $m=\rho v$ the problem becomes convex with a linear constraint; its dual is a Hamilton–Jacobi inequality whose solutions are Kantorovich potentials; on a grid it is solved by proximal splitting.
  4. Generative modelling (§5). Sampling new data is transporting noise onto the data distribution. A flow (an ODE) is a convenient way to build such a transport.
  5. Flow matching (§6). Choose a path by interpolating noise–data pairs; the velocity that moves it is a conditional expectation, which can be learned by plain least squares, without ever solving the ODE during training.
  6. Diffusion models (§7). Noising the data and learning to denoise is the same thing: by Tweedie’s formula, the denoiser gives the score, the score gives the velocity, and sampling integrates the probability-flow ODE.
  7. Optimal or not (§8–§10). The learned flow always transports noise onto data, but its map is optimal only in special cases (dimension 1, commuting Gaussians, optimal couplings). Adding noise to the paths gives Schrödinger bridges; coupling many measures at once gives multimarginal transport and barycenters.

Refresher — push-forward and change of variables (Lesson 1)

If $T:\R^d\to\R^d$ is a map and $\al$ a probability measure, the push-forward $\pf{T}{\al}$ moves the mass at $x$ to $T(x)$: $\int g\,\dd(\pf{T}{\al})=\int g(T(x))\,\dd\al(x)$ for every test function $g$; with random variables, $X\sim\al\Rightarrow T(X)\sim\pf{T}{\al}$. When $T$ is a diffeomorphism and $\al=\rho_0\,\dd x$, the image has a density $\rho_1$ given by the change of variables $\rho_1(T(x))\,\abs{\det\nabla T(x)}=\rho_0(x)$. Used in §1 (a flow map pushes $\al_0$ to $\al_t$), §6 (the interpolant $\al_t=\pf{(I_t)}{\pi}$). More: Reminders — push-forward, change of variables.

Refresher — ODEs and flows of vector fields

A time-dependent vector field $v:[0,1]\times\R^d\to\R^d$ defines the ODE $\dot x(t)=v_t(x(t))$. If $v$ is continuous in $t$ and Lipschitz in $x$ (uniformly in $t$), the Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem gives, for every starting point, a unique solution; the flow map $T_t$ sends $x(0)$ to $x(t)$. Two consequences are used repeatedly: (i) trajectories never cross (two solutions that meet at some time coincide at all times, by uniqueness), and (ii) $T_t$ is a bijection, smooth if $v$ is. In dimension one (i) means that the order of the particles is preserved. Used in §1, §6 and Exercise 3 of the sheet. More: Reminders — ODEs and flows.

Refresher — divergence and integration by parts

For a vector field $w=(w_1,\dots,w_d)$, $\dv w=\sum_i\partial_iw_i$. The divergence theorem says that $\int_A\dv w\,\dd x$ is the outward flux of $w$ through the boundary of $A$; on $\R^d$, if $w$ decays fast enough, $\int\dv w\,\dd x=0$, and for a smooth function $\varphi$, the integration by parts $\int\varphi\,\dv w\,\dd x=-\int\ps{\nabla\varphi}{w}\,\dd x$. Product rule: $\dv(\rho v)=\ps{\nabla\rho}{v}+\rho\,\dv v$. Used in §1 (weak form of the continuity equation), §3 (duality). More: Reminders — divergence.

Refresher — Brenier’s theorem and Gaussian maps (Lesson 1)

Brenier. On $\R^d$ with $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2$, if $\al$ has a density, there is a unique optimal plan, carried by the graph of $T=\nabla\varphi$ with $\varphi$ convex. Conversely the gradient of any convex function is the optimal map between $\al$ and its push-forward. So a map is optimal if and only if it is the gradient of a convex function.

Gaussians. Between $\N(0,\Sigma_0)$ and $\N(0,\Sigma_1)$ ($\Sigma_0,\Sigma_1$ positive definite) the optimal map is linear, $T(x)=Ax$, with $A=\Sigma_0^{-1/2}(\Sigma_0^{1/2}\Sigma_1\Sigma_0^{1/2})^{1/2}\Sigma_0^{-1/2}$, the unique symmetric positive definite matrix with $A\Sigma_0A=\Sigma_1$; $W_2^2=\tr\Sigma_0+\tr\Sigma_1-2\tr(\Sigma_0^{1/2}\Sigma_1\Sigma_0^{1/2})^{1/2}$. In 1D: $T(x)=m_1+\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_0}(x-m_0)$ and $W_2^2=(m_1-m_0)^2+(\sigma_1-\sigma_0)^2$. On the line, for any $\al$ with a continuous CDF, the optimal map is the monotone rearrangement $C_\be^{-1}\circ C_\al$, optimal for every convex cost of $x-y$. Used in §2, §8 and the sheet. More: Lesson 1, Reminders — Monge and Brenier, closed forms.

Refresher — displacement interpolation (Lesson 3)

If $\pi$ is an optimal plan between $\al_0$ and $\al_1$ for $\norm{x-y}^2$, the displacement interpolation is $\al_t=\pf{P_t}{\pi}$ with $P_t(x,y)=(1-t)x+ty$: every unit of mass travels on the segment from $x$ to $y$ at constant speed. When $\pi$ is given by a map $T$, $\al_t=\pf{((1-t)\Id+tT)}{\al_0}$ (McCann). It is a constant-speed geodesic: $W_2(\al_s,\al_t)=\abs{t-s}\,W_2(\al_0,\al_1)$. Used in §2 (it is the Benamou–Brenier minimizer) and §6 (flow matching with an OT coupling). More: Lesson 3 §6.

Refresher — conditional expectation as a least-squares projection

For square-integrable random vectors $Y$ (in $\R^d$) and $X$, the conditional expectation $\E[Y\mid X]=g^\star(X)$ is the best prediction of $Y$ from $X$ in mean square: $g^\star$ minimizes $\E\norm{Y-g(X)}^2$ over all measurable $g$. Equivalently it is the orthogonal projection of $Y$ onto the closed subspace of $L^2$ made of the functions of $X$, characterized by the orthogonality relation $\E\big[\ps{Y-g^\star(X)}{h(X)}\big]=0$ for every $h$. Consequences: Pythagoras, $\E\norm{Y-g(X)}^2=\E\norm{Y-g^\star(X)}^2+\E\norm{g^\star(X)-g(X)}^2$; and conditional Jensen, $\norm{\E[Y\mid X]}^2\le\E[\norm{Y}^2\mid X]$. Used in §6 (the flow-matching field), §7 (Tweedie), the sheet. More: Reminders — conditional laws.

Refresher — Gaussian vectors and Gaussian conditioning

If $(Z,T)$ is a Gaussian vector, the conditional expectation is affine: $\E[Z\mid T]=\E[Z]+\mathrm{Cov}(Z,T)\,\mathrm{Cov}(T,T)^{-1}(T-\E[T])$ (the formula recalled in Exercise 4 of the sheet). Linear combinations of jointly Gaussian vectors are Gaussian: if $X_0,X_1$ are independent centred Gaussians, $(1-t)X_0+tX_1\sim\N\big(0,(1-t)^2\Sigma_0+t^2\Sigma_1\big)$. Used in §6–§8 and the sheet. More: Reminders — Gaussian vectors, matrix square roots.

Refresher — Brownian motion, SDEs and the Fokker–Planck equation

A standard Brownian motion $B_t$ has independent Gaussian increments, $B_t-B_s\sim\N(0,(t-s)\Id)$. An SDE $\dd X_t=b_t(X_t)\,\dd t+g_t\,\dd B_t$ adds to the drift $b$ a random kick of variance $g_t^2\,\dd t$. The density $\rho_t$ of $X_t$ solves the Fokker–Planck equation $\partial_t\rho_t+\dv(\rho_tb_t)=\frac{g_t^2}2\Delta\rho_t$: the continuity equation plus a diffusion term. Example: the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process $\dd X=-X\,\dd\tau+\sqrt2\,\dd B$ forgets its starting point and converges to $\N(0,\Id)$. Used in §7 and §9. More: Reminders — Brownian motion, Fokker–Planck.
