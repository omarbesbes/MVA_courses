7. Wasserstein barycenters notes p. 4–5

The average of points is the point that minimises the weighted sum of squared distances to them; replacing points by distributions and the distance by a transport cost defines the average of distributions — the Wasserstein barycenter.

Barycenters of points, and the Fréchet mean

On the board notes p. 4

Wasserstein barycenters. $x_1,\dots,x_P$ points in $\R^d$, $\ \lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_P\ge0$, $\ \sum\lambda_k=1$, $\ x^\star=\sum\lambda_kx_k$:

$$B(x_1,\dots,x_P)=\argmin_x\ \sum_{k=1}^P\lambda_k\underbrace{\norm{x-x_k}^2}_{c(x,x_k)}$$

$c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2\ \to$ weighted mean; $\quad c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}\ \to$ weighted median.

[drawing: points on a curved surface] $\to$ “Fréchet mean”. $\ \to$ Generalization to proba measures?

Why the mean minimises squared distances. $F(x)=\sum_k\lambda_k\norm{x-x_k}^2$ is a strictly convex quadratic; its gradient $\nabla F(x)=2\sum_k\lambda_k(x-x_k)=2\big(x-\sum_k\lambda_kx_k\big)$ vanishes only at $x^\star=\sum_k\lambda_kx_k$ (we used $\sum_k\lambda_k=1$). So the barycenter is the weighted mean.

Changing the cost changes the average. With $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}$ (not squared), the minimiser is the (weighted, geometric) median. On $\R$ with points $0,1,10$ and equal weights: the mean is $11/3\approx3.67$, pulled by the outlier $10$; the median is $1$. Squaring the distance punishes large distances, so the mean compromises with the outlier; the median does not.

The Fréchet mean. The variational definition has a big advantage: it needs no addition. On a curved space — a circle, a sphere, a surface — you cannot form $\sum_k\lambda_kx_k$ (it leaves the space), but you can still minimise $\sum_k\lambda_k\,d(x,x_k)^2$ with the distance $d$ of the space. That minimiser is the Fréchet mean. The board’s drawing shows points on a curved surface: their Euclidean average falls outside it; the Fréchet mean stays on it.

The idea of the lecture: the space of probability measures with $W_2$ is such a “curved” space. Distributions can be added (the mixture $\sum_k\lambda_k\be_k$ is a distribution), but this linear average is the average for a different geometry: it minimises $\sum_k\lambda_k\norm{\rho-\rho_k}_{L^2}^2$ over densities, a geometry that does not move mass. To average shapes and positions, use the Fréchet mean for $W_2$.

Definition

On the board notes p. 4

$\be_1,\dots,\be_P$ proba measures, $\ \lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_P\ge0$, $\ \sum\lambda_k=1$:

$$B(\be_1,\dots,\be_P)\in\argmin_{\al\in\M}\ \sum_{k=1}^P\lambda_k\,\Lc(\al,\be_k)\qquad(\text{ex: }\Lc=W_p^p).$$

ex: $\be_k=\delta_{x_k}\ \Rightarrow\ $ solution $\delta_{B(x_1,\dots,x_P)}$.

$\be_1,\dots,\be_P$
The input distributions (for instance $P$ images, $P$ data sets, $P$ shapes).
$\lambda_k$
How much each input counts. $\lambda=(1-t,t)$ with $P=2$ gives the geodesic of §6. An input with $\lambda_k=0$ does not count at all and can be dropped.
$\Lc(\al,\be_k)$
The transport cost from the candidate average $\al$ to the $k$-th input. The standard choice is $W_2^2$ on $\R^d$.
“$\in\argmin$”
The minimiser need not be unique (see below), hence “$\in$”.
Worked example — the barycenter of Diracs is the Dirac at the barycenter
  1. $$\Lc(\al,\delta_{x_k})=\int c(x,x_k)\,\dd\al(x).$$The only coupling of $\al$ and $\delta_{x_k}$ sends all the mass to $x_k$ (§1, quick check).
  2. $$\sum_k\lambda_k\Lc(\al,\delta_{x_k})=\int F(x)\,\dd\al(x),\qquad F(x)=\sum_k\lambda_k\,c(x,x_k).$$Linearity of the integral.
  3. $$\int F\,\dd\al\ \ge\ \min F,$$ with equality if and only if $\al$ puts all its mass on the minimisers of $F$.An average of a function is at least its minimum.
  4. So the barycenter is $\delta_{x^\star}$ with $x^\star=\argmin F=B(x_1,\dots,x_P)$ — the weighted mean for $c=\norm{\cdot}^2$.When $F$ has a unique minimiser, $\delta_{x^\star}$ is the only distribution concentrated on it.

Compare with the mixture $\sum_k\lambda_k\delta_{x_k}$: it keeps $P$ separate atoms. The Wasserstein barycenter merges them into one atom at the average position. This is what “averaging positions instead of weights” means.

Histograms on a common grid: a linear program

On the board notes p. 5, top

ex: $\be_k$ discrete histograms on the same grid,

$$\min_{\substack{\al\text{ discrete}\\\text{on the same grid}}}\ \sum_{k=1}^P\lambda_k\,W_p^p(\al,\be_k)\ \longrightarrow\ \text{LP}$$ $$\min_{\al,P_1,\dots,P_P}\Big\{\sum_k\lambda_k\ps{P_k}{D^p}\ :\ \forall k,\ P_k\ind_{\text{grid}}=\al,\ \ P_k\T\ind_{\text{grid}}=\be_k\Big\}$$

[drawing: a small grid of points, two of them marked by crosses]

the grid
Fixed points $g_1,\dots,g_n$ (pixels of an image, bins of a histogram). All inputs and the unknown barycenter are weight vectors on these points: $\al,\be_k\in\R^n_+$, summing to $1$.
$D^p$
The matrix of the $p$-th powers of the distances between grid points, $(D^p)_{ij}=d(g_i,g_j)^p$.
$P_k$
A transport plan from the barycenter $\al$ to the input $\be_k$ ($n\times n$ matrix). $P_k\ind=\al$: its row sums are $\al$; $P_k\T\ind=\be_k$: its column sums are $\be_k$. (Careful: on the board the letter $P$ is also the number of inputs; $P_1,\dots,P_P$ are the $P$ plans.)
$\ps{P_k}{D^p}$
$\sum_{ij}(P_k)_{ij}\,d(g_i,g_j)^p$, the cost of the plan $P_k$.
“$\to$ LP”
Minimising over $\al$ of a sum of minima over plans is the same as minimising jointly over $(\al,P_1,\dots,P_P)$. The objective is linear in these unknowns, the constraints are linear equalities plus $P_k\ge0$: a linear program.

Size. The unknowns are $n+Pn^2$ numbers. For $P=3$ images of $100\times100$ pixels, $n=10^4$ and $Pn^2=3\times10^8$: far too large for a generic LP solver. In practice one adds an entropic regularization and uses Sinkhorn-type iterations (Lesson 7).

Worked example — what a fixed grid does to the barycenter

Grid $\{0,1,2,3,4\}\subset\R$, inputs $\be_1=\delta_0$, $\be_2=\delta_3$, weights $\frac12,\frac12$, $p=2$. By the Dirac computation above, a candidate $\al$ pays $\int F\,\dd\al$ with $F(x)=\frac12x^2+\frac12(x-3)^2$. On the grid, $F=(4.5,\ 2.5,\ 2.5,\ 4.5,\ 8.5)$.

  • The true barycenter $\delta_{1.5}$ (value $F(1.5)=2.25$) is not on the grid.
  • The grid barycenters are all the $\al$ carried by $\{1,2\}$ (value $2.5$): $\delta_1$, $\delta_2$, $\frac12\delta_1+\frac12\delta_2$, … — infinitely many. An LP solver returns one of them ($\delta_1$ in our test).

A fixed grid approximates the barycenter only up to the grid resolution, and can create artificial non-uniqueness and blur.

Existence and uniqueness (Agueh–Carlier)

On the board notes p. 5

[Agueh, Carlier 2010] $\ \X=\R^d$, $W_2$:

  • $\to$ existence of solution;
  • $\to$ uniqueness if one $\be_k$ is A.C.
Agueh–Carlier
M. Agueh and G. Carlier, Barycenters in the Wasserstein space, SIAM J. Math. Anal. 43(2), 2011 (preprint 2010). The paper that introduced Wasserstein barycenters for $W_2$ on $\R^d$ (inputs in $\Pc_2(\R^d)$).
A.C.
Absolutely continuous (with respect to Lebesgue measure): has a density. One input with a density and a positive weight $\lambda_k>0$ is enough for uniqueness. (An input with weight $0$ plays no role: adding a Gaussian with weight $0$ to the non-uniqueness example below leaves it non-unique.)
Worked example — without densities, uniqueness can fail

In $\R^2$, $\be_1=\frac12\big(\delta_{(-1,0)}+\delta_{(1,0)}\big)$ and $\be_2=\frac12\big(\delta_{(0,-1)}+\delta_{(0,1)}\big)$, weights $\frac12,\frac12$. Every point of $\be_1$ is at squared distance $2$ from every point of $\be_2$, so every coupling is optimal and $W_2^2(\be_1,\be_2)=2$. By §6 the barycenter value is at least $\frac12\cdot\frac12\cdot2=\frac12$.

  • Pair $(-1,0)$ with $(0,-1)$ and $(1,0)$ with $(0,1)$, and take midpoints: $\al_A=\frac12\big(\delta_{(-\frac12,-\frac12)}+\delta_{(\frac12,\frac12)}\big)$. Each atom is at squared distance $\frac12$ from its two partners: value $\frac12$. Optimal.
  • The other pairing gives $\al_B=\frac12\big(\delta_{(-\frac12,\frac12)}+\delta_{(\frac12,-\frac12)}\big)$, also of value $\frac12$. Optimal too.
  • Their mixture (uniform on the four points $(\pm\frac12,\pm\frac12)$) also has value $\frac12$: infinitely many barycenters.

The non-uniqueness comes from the non-uniqueness of the optimal plan between the inputs. A density on one input forces optimal plans to be maps (Brenier) and removes this freedom.

A useful fact: the mean of the barycenter is the barycenter of the means

For $W_2^2$ on $\R^d$, any barycenter $\al^\star$ has mean $\int x\,\dd\al^\star=\sum_k\lambda_k\int x\,\dd\be_k$.

Why (the mean–shape decomposition of $W_2$)

For any coupling, write $x=m_\al+u$ and $y=m_\be+v$ with $u,v$ centred. Then $\norm{x-y}^2=\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+2\ps{m_\al-m_\be}{u-v}+\norm{u-v}^2$, and the middle term integrates to $0$. Translations do not change the set of couplings of the centred measures, so

$$W_2^2(\al,\be)=\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+W_2^2(\tilde\al,\tilde\be),$$

where $\tilde\al,\tilde\be$ are the centred versions. In the barycenter objective, the mean $m_\al$ only appears in $\sum_k\lambda_k\norm{m_\al-m_{\be_k}}^2$, minimised by the mean of the means (barycenter of points!), while the shape part does not depend on $m_\al$. This is also why, for Gaussians (§8), means and covariances separate.

Pitfall — barycenter ≠ mixture

The mixture $\sum_k\lambda_k\be_k$ is a distribution, it is “an average”, and it is easy to compute — but it is not the Wasserstein barycenter. Two Diracs: mixture = two atoms; barycenter = one atom in the middle. Two Gaussians: mixture = two bumps; barycenter = one Gaussian.

On $\R$, what is the barycenter of $\delta_0,\delta_1,\delta_{10}$ with weights $(0.2,0.2,0.6)$, for $c=\abs{x-y}^2$? For $c=\abs{x-y}$?

Answer

Quadratic cost: $\delta_{x^\star}$ with $x^\star=0.2\cdot0+0.2\cdot1+0.6\cdot10=6.2$. Absolute cost: the weighted median; more than half of the weight sits at $10$, so the minimiser of $0.2\abs{x}+0.2\abs{x-1}+0.6\abs{x-10}$ is $x=10$: the barycenter is $\delta_{10}$.
