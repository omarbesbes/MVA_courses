4. Convergence of measures, and what $W_p$ measures notes p. 3

Distributions are compared through test functions: $\al_n\to\al$ means $\int\varphi\,\dd\al_n\to\int\varphi\,\dd\al$ for every $\varphi$ in a class; on a compact space $W_p$-convergence is exactly this “weak” convergence, and on $\R^d$ it is weak convergence plus convergence of the $p$-th moments.

The problem: what should “$\al_n\to\al$” mean?

For numbers or vectors there is one obvious notion of convergence. For distributions there are several, and the natural first guess is too strong. Asking $\al_n(A)\to\al(A)$ for every set $A$ fails on the simplest example: $\delta_{1/n}$ should converge to $\delta_0$ (the point $1/n$ converges to $0$), but with $A=\{0\}$, $\delta_{1/n}(A)=0$ for every $n$ while $\delta_0(A)=1$.

The way out is to look at distributions only through averages of nice functions: $\int\varphi\,\dd\al_n=\E[\varphi(X_n)]$. Continuous test functions do not distinguish $1/n$ from $0$ when $n$ is large, which is what we want. The choice of the class of test functions gives the different notions.

Reminder — convergence in law

In probability, $X_n$ converges in law to $X$ when $\E[\varphi(X_n)]\to\E[\varphi(X)]$ for every bounded continuous $\varphi$. The central limit theorem is a statement of this kind. It is exactly the weak convergence of the laws of this section, with test functions in $C_b$. Example: $X_n=1/n$ (a constant) converges in law to $X=0$, although $\P(X_n=0)=0\not\to1=\P(X=0)$. More on the Reminders page: convergence of random variables and of measures.

On the board notes p. 3, top

Reminder of convergence of measures (for $\X$ not compact):

$$\begin{aligned} &\textbf{(1)}\quad \al_n\xrightarrow{\text{weak}}\al &&\iff \int\varphi\,\dd\al_n\to\int\varphi\,\dd\al\quad\forall\varphi\in C_b(\X)\\ &\textbf{(2)}\quad \al_n\xrightarrow{\text{weak*}}\al &&\iff \int\varphi\,\dd\al_n\to\int\varphi\,\dd\al\quad\forall\varphi\in C_0(\X)\\ &\textbf{(3)}\quad \al_n\xrightarrow{\text{vague}}\al &&\iff \int\varphi\,\dd\al_n\to\int\varphi\,\dd\al\quad\forall\varphi\in C_c(\X) \end{aligned}$$

Prop: if $(\al_n)$ are probability measures and if $\al$ is a probability measure, then (1) $\iff$ (2) $\iff$ (3).

On a compact space $\X$, $C_b(\X)=C_0(\X)=C_c(\X)=C(\X)$.

Decoding the three classes of test functions

Take $\X=\R^d$ to fix ideas. All three are classes of continuous functions; they differ by what happens far away.

$C_b(\X)$
bounded continuous functions: $\sup\abs{\varphi}<\infty$. Examples: $\arctan x$, $\cos x$, the constant $1$.
$C_0(\X)$
continuous functions that tend to $0$ at infinity: $\varphi(x)\to0$ as $\norm{x}\to\infty$. Examples: $e^{-x^2}$, $1/(1+x^2)$. Not the constant $1$.
$C_c(\X)$
continuous functions with compact support: zero outside some bounded set. Example: the hat function $\max(0,1-\abs{x})$.

The names: “weak-*” because measures are the dual of $C_0$ (Riesz representation theorem), and convergence tested against the elements of the pre-dual is weak-* convergence in functional analysis. In probability, weak convergence is the familiar convergence in law: if $X_n\sim\al_n$ and $X\sim\al$, then $\al_n\to\al$ weakly means $\E[\varphi(X_n)]\to\E[\varphi(X)]$ for every bounded continuous $\varphi$ (the central limit theorem is a statement of this kind).

Why the proposition needs “$\al$ is a probability measure”

Worked example — a Dirac running away

On $\R$, let $\al_n=\delta_n$ (all the mass at the point $n$).

  • For $\varphi\in C_0$: $\int\varphi\,\dd\delta_n=\varphi(n)\to0$. So $\delta_n\to0$ weak-* and vaguely, where $0$ is the zero measure!
  • For $\varphi\equiv1\in C_b$: $\int1\,\dd\delta_n=1$ for all $n$, while $\int1\,\dd0=0$. So $\delta_n\not\to0$ weakly. In fact $\delta_n$ has no weak limit at all: a weak limit would also be a vague limit, hence $0$, which contradicts the test with $\varphi\equiv1$.

The mass has escaped to infinity: test functions that vanish at infinity cannot see it, the constant function can. The vague limit $0$ is not a probability measure (it has lost its mass). The proposition says this is the only thing that can go wrong: if the limit keeps total mass $1$, the three notions coincide.

Proposition (the board’s Prop, on $\X=\R^d$)

If $\al_n$ and $\al$ are probability measures on $\R^d$, then vague convergence implies weak convergence. Hence (1) $\iff$ (2) $\iff$ (3).

Idea of the proof

Weak $\Rightarrow$ weak-* $\Rightarrow$ vague is free ($C_c\subset C_0\subset C_b$). For the converse, cut a bounded test function into a compactly supported part, where vague convergence applies, and a part far away, where there is little mass — because $\al$ has mass $1$ and so do the $\al_n$, no mass can hide at infinity.

Full proof (vague $\Rightarrow$ weak)

Fix $\varphi\in C_b$ and $\eps>0$.

  1. Choose $R$ with $\al(B_R)\ge1-\eps$, where $B_R$ is the ball of radius $R$.$\al(B_R)\uparrow\al(\R^d)=1$ as $R\to\infty$ (continuity of measures). Here we use that $\al$ has mass $1$.
  2. Let $\chi(x)=\min\big(1,\max(0,R+1-\norm{x})\big)$: continuous, $0\le\chi\le1$, $\chi=1$ on $B_R$, $\chi=0$ outside $B_{R+1}$. So $\chi\in C_c$ and $\int\chi\,\dd\al\ge\al(B_R)\ge1-\eps$.A “cut-off” function.
  3. By vague convergence, $\int\chi\,\dd\al_n\to\int\chi\,\dd\al\ge1-\eps$, so $\int(1-\chi)\,\dd\al_n\le2\eps$ for $n$ large.$\int(1-\chi)\,\dd\al_n=1-\int\chi\,\dd\al_n$ because $\al_n$ has mass $1$. Little mass of $\al_n$ lies far away.
  4. Split $\varphi=\varphi\chi+\varphi(1-\chi)$: $$\Big\lvert\int\varphi\,\dd\al_n-\int\varphi\,\dd\al\Big\rvert\le\Big\lvert\int\varphi\chi\,\dd\al_n-\int\varphi\chi\,\dd\al\Big\rvert+\norm{\varphi}_\infty\!\int(1-\chi)\,\dd\al_n+\norm{\varphi}_\infty\!\int(1-\chi)\,\dd\al .$$Triangle inequality, and $\abs{\varphi(1-\chi)}\le\norm{\varphi}_\infty(1-\chi)$.
  5. The first term tends to $0$ (vague convergence: $\varphi\chi\in C_c$); the other two are at most $2\eps\norm{\varphi}_\infty$ and $\eps\norm{\varphi}_\infty$ for $n$ large. So $\limsup_n\big\lvert\int\varphi\,\dd\al_n-\int\varphi\,\dd\al\big\rvert\le3\eps\norm{\varphi}_\infty$ for every $\eps>0$: the limit is $0$.$\eps$ was arbitrary.
On a compact space there is only one notion

If $\X$ is compact, every continuous function is bounded (so $C(\X)=C_b(\X)$), and its support is a closed subset of the compact $\X$, hence compact (so $C(\X)=C_c(\X)$, and also $=C_0(\X)$: there is no “infinity” to escape to). The board’s line $C_b=C_0=C_c=C(\X)$ says: on a compact space we simply say weak convergence, $\al_n\wto\al$, meaning $\int\varphi\,\dd\al_n\to\int\varphi\,\dd\al$ for every continuous $\varphi$.

Worked example — Diracs converge weakly exactly when the points converge

$\delta_{x_n}\wto\delta_x$ means $\varphi(x_n)\to\varphi(x)$ for every continuous bounded $\varphi$.

  • If $x_n\to x$: true, by continuity of $\varphi$.
  • If $x_n\not\to x$: there is $r>0$ with $d(x_n,x)\ge r$ for infinitely many $n$. The “bump” $\varphi(y)=\max\big(0,1-d(y,x)/r\big)$ is continuous and bounded, with $\varphi(x)=1$ but $\varphi(x_n)=0$ for those $n$. So no weak convergence.

Compare with the first guess “$\al_n(A)\to\al(A)$ for all $A$”, which rejected $\delta_{1/n}\to\delta_0$: weak convergence accepts it. This is the convergence we want.

$W_p$ metrizes weak convergence

On the board notes p. 3

Prop: if $\X$ is compact, $W_p(\al_n,\al)\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}0$ if and only if $\al_n\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}\al$. “$W_p$ metrizes”.

Prop: if $\X$ is not compact, $\ W_p(\al_n,\al)\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}0\iff\al_n\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}\al$ and $\displaystyle\int d(x,x_0)^p\,\dd\al_n\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}\int d(x,x_0)^p\,\dd\al$.

“$W_p$ metrizes weak convergence”
Converging for the distance $W_p$ is the same thing as converging weakly. So $W_p(\al_n,\al)$ is a number that tells you how far you are from the limit, for a notion of convergence (weak) that was defined without any number.
$x_0$
Any fixed reference point (for instance $0\in\R^d$); the condition does not depend on the choice.
$\int d(x,x_0)^p\,\dd\al$
The $p$-th moment of $\al$: the average of the $p$-th power of the distance to $x_0$. For $p=2$ on $\R^d$ and $x_0=0$: $\E\norm{X}^2$.

The compact case

Reminder — Lipschitz functions

A function $f$ is $L$-Lipschitz if $\abs{f(x)-f(y)}\le L\,d(x,y)$ for all $x,y$: it cannot change faster than $L$ times the distance travelled. The smallest such $L$ is written $\Lip(f)$. On $\R$: $\abs{x}$ and $\sin x$ are 1-Lipschitz; $x^2$ is Lipschitz on every bounded interval but not on $\R$ (its slope is unbounded).

More on the Reminders page: continuous and Lipschitz functions.

Idea of the proof
  1. $W_p\to0\Rightarrow$ weak. For a Lipschitz test function, $\big\lvert\int f\,\dd\al_n-\int f\,\dd\al\big\rvert\le\Lip(f)\,W_1(\al_n,\al)$: move the mass with an optimal plan, and $f$ changes by at most $\Lip(f)$ times the distance moved. Continuous functions are uniform limits of Lipschitz ones.
  2. Weak $\Rightarrow W_p\to0$. Next lesson’s duality writes $W_1$ as a supremum of $\int f\,\dd(\al_n-\al)$ over 1-Lipschitz $f$; these $f$ form a compact family, and weak convergence is uniform on compact families. Then §3 transfers the result from $W_1$ to $W_p$.
Full proof of “$W_p\to0\Rightarrow$ weak”
  1. Let $f$ be $L$-Lipschitz ($\abs{f(x)-f(y)}\le L\,d(x,y)$) and let $\pi_n$ be an optimal coupling for $W_1(\al_n,\al)$. Then $$\int f\,\dd\al_n-\int f\,\dd\al=\int\big(f(x)-f(y)\big)\,\dd\pi_n(x,y).$$The marginals of $\pi_n$ are $\al_n$ and $\al$: $\int f(x)\,\dd\pi_n=\int f\,\dd\al_n$ and $\int f(y)\,\dd\pi_n=\int f\,\dd\al$.
  2. $$\Big\lvert\int f\,\dd\al_n-\int f\,\dd\al\Big\rvert\le L\int d(x,y)\,\dd\pi_n=L\,W_1(\al_n,\al)\le L\,W_p(\al_n,\al).$$Lipschitz bound inside the integral; optimality of $\pi_n$; and $W_1\le W_p$ (§3).
  3. Any continuous $f$ on the compact $\X$ is a uniform limit of Lipschitz functions $f_k$ (lemma below). Then $$\Big\lvert\int f\,\dd(\al_n-\al)\Big\rvert\le2\norm{f-f_k}_\infty+\Lip(f_k)\,W_p(\al_n,\al).$$Write $f=f_k+(f-f_k)$; the second piece integrates to at most $\norm{f-f_k}_\infty$ against each probability measure.
  4. Choose $k$ to make the first term small, then $n$ large to make the second small: $\int f\,\dd\al_n\to\int f\,\dd\al$.An “$\eps/2$” argument.

Lemma (Lipschitz approximation). For continuous $f$ on a compact metric space, $f_k(x)=\inf_y\big(f(y)+k\,d(x,y)\big)$ is $k$-Lipschitz, $f_k\le f$, and $f_k\to f$ uniformly. Why: an infimum of $k$-Lipschitz functions of $x$ is $k$-Lipschitz; $y=x$ gives $f_k\le f$; and by uniform continuity, for $\eps>0$ there is $\delta$ with $\abs{f(x)-f(y)}<\eps$ when $d(x,y)<\delta$, while the points with $d(x,y)\ge\delta$ give $f(y)+k\,d(x,y)\ge\min f+k\delta\ge\max f\ge f(x)$ once $k\ge(\max f-\min f)/\delta$. So $f_k(x)\ge f(x)-\eps$.

Full proof of “weak $\Rightarrow W_p\to0$” (uses Lesson 4)

We use the Kantorovich–Rubinstein formula, proved in the next lecture (duality): $W_1(\al,\be)=\sup\big\{\int f\,\dd(\al-\be):\ f\ \text{1-Lipschitz}\big\}$.

  1. Assume $\al_n\wto\al$ but $W_1(\al_n,\al)\not\to0$: along a subsequence, $W_1(\al_n,\al)>\eps$, so there are 1-Lipschitz $f_n$ with $\int f_n\,\dd(\al_n-\al)>\eps$.Definition of a supremum.
  2. We may assume $f_n(x_0)=0$ for a fixed $x_0$.Subtracting a constant does not change $\int f_n\,\dd(\al_n-\al)$, because $\al_n-\al$ has total mass $0$.
  3. The functions $f_n$ are 1-Lipschitz and bounded by $\diam(\X)$. By Arzelà–Ascoli, a further subsequence converges uniformly to some (1-Lipschitz) $f$.Equicontinuous and uniformly bounded functions on a compact space form a relatively compact family.
  4. $$\eps<\int f_n\,\dd(\al_n-\al)\le\int f\,\dd(\al_n-\al)+2\norm{f_n-f}_\infty\longrightarrow0,$$ a contradiction. So $W_1(\al_n,\al)\to0$.Weak convergence for the fixed continuous $f$, and uniform convergence $f_n\to f$.
  5. $$W_p(\al_n,\al)\le\diam(\X)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\,W_1(\al_n,\al)^{\frac1p}\longrightarrow0.$$The comparison of §3 with $(p,q)$ replaced by $(1,p)$. This is why the comparison of the $W_p$ was proved just before: it reduces everything to $W_1$.

The non-compact case: mass escaping to infinity

On $\R^d$ the first half survives: $W_p\to0$ still implies weak convergence (bounded Lipschitz test functions are enough to characterise weak convergence). But weak convergence no longer implies $W_p\to0$. The obstruction is the one of the running Dirac, in a milder form: a vanishing amount of mass travelling an exploding distance.

Worked example — the escaping mass

On $\R$, let $\al_n=\big(1-\tfrac1n\big)\delta_0+\tfrac1n\delta_n$ and $\al=\delta_0$.

  • Weak convergence holds: for bounded continuous $\varphi$, $\int\varphi\,\dd\al_n=\big(1-\frac1n\big)\varphi(0)+\frac1n\varphi(n)\to\varphi(0)$, because $\abs{\varphi(n)}/n\le\norm{\varphi}_\infty/n\to0$.
  • $W_1$ does not converge: the only coupling with $\delta_0$ moves mass $\frac1n$ over a distance $n$, so $W_1(\al_n,\al)=\frac1n\cdot n=1$ for every $n$. And $W_2(\al_n,\al)=\big(\frac1n\,n^2\big)^{1/2}=\sqrt n\to\infty$.
  • The moments do not converge: $\int\abs{x}\,\dd\al_n=\frac1n\cdot n=1\not\to0=\int\abs{x}\,\dd\al$. The board’s condition fails, as it must.

Weak convergence does not see the small mass (it vanishes), transport does (it pays mass × distance$^p$).

Why $W_p\to0$ forces the moments to converge (a one-line proof)

The $p$-th moment is itself a Wasserstein distance: the only coupling of $\al$ with $\delta_{x_0}$ sends everything to $x_0$, so $\int d(x,x_0)^p\,\dd\al=W_p(\al,\delta_{x_0})^p$. The triangle inequality of $W_p$ (§2) gives

$$\big\lvert W_p(\al_n,\delta_{x_0})-W_p(\al,\delta_{x_0})\big\rvert\le W_p(\al_n,\al)\longrightarrow0,$$

so the moments converge. The converse direction of the board’s second proposition (weak convergence + moments $\Rightarrow W_p\to0$) is harder: it uses Skorokhod’s representation and uniform integrability (Villani, Optimal transport, old and new, Theorem 6.9).

In machine learning — a training set approximates the population, but slowly in high dimension

Let $\hat\al_n=\frac1n\sum_{i\le n}\delta_{X_i}$ with $X_i$ independent samples of $\al$. By the law of large numbers, $\hat\al_n\wto\al$ almost surely, and with finite $p$-th moments $W_p(\hat\al_n,\al)\to0$: in the sense of this section, a large training set represents the population. How fast is the question of sample complexity (OT4ML §10.2, p. 198): in dimension $d>2p$ the rate is $n^{-1/d}$, the curse of dimensionality. Measured with POT: the average $W_1$ between two independent samples of size $n$ of the uniform law on $[0,1]^d$ (which should be $0$) is $0.045$, $0.022$, $0.011$ for $n=100,400,1600$ when $d=1$ (divided by $2$ when $n$ is multiplied by $4$: rate $n^{-1/2}$), but $0.73$, $0.61$, $0.51$ when $d=10$ (multiplied by only $0.84$). This is why ML often prefers variants of OT with better statistics, sliced (Lesson 5) and entropic (Lesson 7). The good news: the rate is governed by the dimension of the set the data live on, not by the ambient dimension.

Reflexes
  • When you see “$\al_n\to\al$” for distributions → ask “tested against which functions?”. On a compact space: continuous functions, and $W_p\to0$ is equivalent.
  • When you see a Lipschitz function integrated against $\al-\be$ → $\big\lvert\int f\,\dd(\al-\be)\big\rvert\le\Lip(f)\,W_1(\al,\be)$.
  • On $\R^d$, if weak convergence holds but $W_p$ does not converge → look for mass escaping to infinity (check the moments).

On $\R$, does $\al_n=\big(1-\frac1n\big)\delta_0+\frac1n\delta_{\sqrt n}$ converge to $\delta_0$ weakly? In $W_1$? In $W_2$?

Answer

Weakly: yes (same argument as the escaping mass). $W_1=\frac1n\sqrt n=n^{-1/2}\to0$: yes, and indeed the first moment $\frac1n\sqrt n\to0$. $W_2=\big(\frac1n\cdot n\big)^{1/2}=1$: no, and indeed the second moment $\frac1n\cdot n=1\not\to0$. The board’s proposition, applied with $p=1$ and $p=2$, predicts exactly this.
