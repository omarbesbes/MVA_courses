8. Two cases with closed forms: Gaussians and the line notes p. 5

The barycenter of Gaussians is the Gaussian whose mean is the average of the means and whose covariance solves a matrix fixed-point equation; on the line, the barycenter is obtained by averaging quantile functions.

Gaussian inputs

On the board notes p. 5

Gaussians: $\be_k=\N(m_k,\Sigma_k)$ and $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2$.

Prop: the barycenter is a Gaussian $\N(m^\star,\Sigma^\star)$ with

$$m^\star=\sum_{k=1}^P\lambda_km_k\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Sigma^\star\in\argmin_\Sigma\ \sum_{k=1}^P\lambda_k\,\B(\Sigma,\Sigma_k)^2,$$ $$\B(\Sigma,\Lambda)^2=\tr\Big(\Sigma+\Lambda-2\big(\Lambda^{1/2}\Sigma\Lambda^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\Big).$$

$\to\Sigma^\star$ is also the unique solution of the fixed point problem:

$$\Sigma^\star=\Psi(\Sigma^\star)\qquad\text{where}\qquad\Psi(\Sigma)=\sum_{k=1}^P\lambda_k\big(\Sigma^{1/2}\Sigma_k\Sigma^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}.$$

$\to$ Fixed-point algo: $\ \Sigma^{(\ell+1)}=\Psi\big(\Sigma^{(\ell)}\big)$. [drawing: ellipses]

$\N(m,\Sigma)$
Gaussian with mean $m$ and covariance $\Sigma$ (symmetric positive matrix). Picture: an ellipse centred at $m$, axes along the eigenvectors of $\Sigma$, half-lengths $\propto$ square roots of the eigenvalues — the ellipses of the board’s drawing.
$\B(\Sigma,\Lambda)$
The Bures distance between covariance matrices (Lesson 1): $W_2^2\big(\N(m,\Sigma),\N(m',\Lambda)\big)=\norm{m-m'}^2+\B(\Sigma,\Lambda)^2$.
$M^{1/2}$
Matrix square root (diagonalise, take the square roots of the eigenvalues). See the refresher.
$\Psi$
A map from covariance matrices to covariance matrices; the barycenter covariance is a fixed point of it.
“the unique solution”
Unique among positive definite matrices, as soon as one input with $\lambda_k>0$ has a positive definite $\Sigma_k$. The zero matrix is always a (useless) fixed point, since $\Psi(0)=0$; this is why the algorithm starts from a positive definite $\Sigma^{(0)}$.
$\Sigma^{(\ell)}$
The $\ell$-th iterate of the algorithm, starting from any positive definite $\Sigma^{(0)}$.
Reminder — square roots of positive matrices

A symmetric positive semi-definite matrix diagonalises in an orthonormal basis, $M=UDU\T$ with $D=\mathrm{diag}(d_i)$, $d_i\ge0$. Its square root is $M^{1/2}=UD^{1/2}U\T$: the only positive semi-definite matrix whose square is $M$. Example: $\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $3$ and $1$, with eigenvectors $(1,1)/\sqrt2$ and $(1,-1)/\sqrt2$. Its square root has eigenvalues $\sqrt3$ and $1$: $\frac12\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt3+1&\sqrt3-1\\\sqrt3-1&\sqrt3+1\end{pmatrix}$. Beware: in general $(AB)^{1/2}\ne A^{1/2}B^{1/2}$ (unless $A$ and $B$ commute), which is why the formulas keep the sandwich $\Lambda^{1/2}\Sigma\Lambda^{1/2}$. More on the Reminders page: matrix square root, trace, determinant, Gaussian vectors.

Why the means and covariances separate

Try a Gaussian candidate $\al=\N(m,\Sigma)$ in the barycenter objective, using the Gaussian formula for each term:

$$\sum_k\lambda_kW_2^2\big(\N(m,\Sigma),\be_k\big)=\underbrace{\sum_k\lambda_k\norm{m-m_k}^2}_{\text{only depends on }m}+\underbrace{\sum_k\lambda_k\B(\Sigma,\Sigma_k)^2}_{\text{only depends on }\Sigma}.$$

Minimise each part separately: the first is the barycenter of the points $m_k$ (§7), so $m^\star=\sum_k\lambda_km_k$; the second is the “Bures barycenter” problem of the board.

Why is the barycenter Gaussian at all (and not only the best Gaussian)?

The key fact is Gelbrich’s inequality: for any $\al,\be$ with means $m_\al,m_\be$ and covariances $\Sigma_\al,\Sigma_\be$,

$$W_2^2(\al,\be)\ \ge\ \norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+\B(\Sigma_\al,\Sigma_\be)^2,$$

with equality when both are Gaussian. So if you replace any candidate $\al$ by the Gaussian $\N(m_\al,\Sigma_\al)$ with the same mean and covariance, each term $W_2^2(\al,\be_k)$ (with $\be_k$ Gaussian) can only decrease: Gaussian candidates are never worse. A barycenter exists (Agueh–Carlier), hence a Gaussian one exists; if one $\Sigma_k$ with $\lambda_k>0$ is positive definite, that $\be_k$ has a density, the barycenter is unique, and therefore it is that Gaussian.

The one-dimensional case: average the standard deviations

In 1D, $\B(\sigma^2,\sigma_k^2)^2=\sigma^2+\sigma_k^2-2\sigma\sigma_k=(\sigma-\sigma_k)^2$. So the covariance problem is $\min_\sigma\sum_k\lambda_k(\sigma-\sigma_k)^2$, whose solution is the mean of the standard deviations:

$$\sigma^\star=\sum_k\lambda_k\sigma_k .$$
Worked example

$\be_1=\N(0,1)$, $\be_2=\N(4,9)$ (standard deviations $1$ and $3$), weights $\frac12,\frac12$: the barycenter is $\N(2,\,2^2)=\N(2,4)$.

  • Not the average of the variances (which would give variance $5$).
  • Not the mixture $\frac12\N(0,1)+\frac12\N(4,9)$, which is not even Gaussian (its variance is $5+4=9$: the average variance plus the spread of the means).

When all the $\Sigma_k$ commute (for instance all diagonal), the same thing happens eigenvalue by eigenvalue: $(\Sigma^\star)^{1/2}=\sum_k\lambda_k\Sigma_k^{1/2}$ — “average the square roots”. In general the matrices do not commute, and the fixed-point equation is the right generalisation.

Where the fixed-point equation comes from

Intuition — “the average of the transport maps is the identity”

From the barycenter $\al^\star=\N(m^\star,\Sigma)$, the optimal map to $\be_k$ is affine (Lesson 1): $T_k(x)=m_k+A_k(x-m^\star)$ with $A_k=\Sigma^{-1/2}\big(\Sigma^{1/2}\Sigma_k\Sigma^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\Sigma^{-1/2}$. We will see in §9 that a barycenter is characterised by “on average, the points do not need to move”:

$$\sum_k\lambda_kT_k(x)=x\quad\text{for all }x\qquad\iff\qquad\sum_k\lambda_km_k=m^\star\ \text{ and }\ \sum_k\lambda_kA_k=I .$$

Multiply $\sum_k\lambda_kA_k=I$ by $\Sigma^{1/2}$ on both sides: $\sum_k\lambda_k\big(\Sigma^{1/2}\Sigma_k\Sigma^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}=\Sigma$, i.e. $\Psi(\Sigma)=\Sigma$. The board’s fixed-point equation says exactly: the average of the optimal maps from the barycenter to the inputs is the identity.

The same equation by differentiation (first-order condition)

Write $E(\Sigma)=\sum_k\lambda_k\B(\Sigma,\Sigma_k)^2=\tr\Sigma+\sum_k\lambda_k\tr\Sigma_k-2\sum_k\lambda_k\tr\big(\Sigma_k^{1/2}\Sigma\Sigma_k^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}$. One can show that the derivative of $\Sigma\mapsto\tr\big(\Sigma_k^{1/2}\Sigma\Sigma_k^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}$ in a symmetric direction $Z$ is $\frac12\tr(A_kZ)$, with the matrix $A_k$ above (compact notes, proof of Prop. 12.6). Hence

$$DE(\Sigma)[Z]=\tr\Big(\big(I-\textstyle\sum_k\lambda_kA_k\big)Z\Big),$$

which vanishes for all $Z$ iff $\sum_k\lambda_kA_k=I$, i.e. $\Psi(\Sigma)=\Sigma$. $E$ is convex, so this stationary point is the minimiser.

The fixed-point algorithm — and a faster cousin

The board’s algorithm iterates $\Sigma^{(\ell+1)}=\Psi(\Sigma^{(\ell)})$. Let us run it in 1D, where $\Psi(v)=\sum_k\lambda_k\sqrt{v\,\sigma_k^2}=\sqrt v\,\bar\sigma$ with $\bar\sigma=\sum_k\lambda_k\sigma_k$. On the worked example ($\bar\sigma=2$), starting from the variance $v^{(0)}=1$:

$$v^{(\ell)}:\quad1,\ \ 2,\ \ 2.83,\ \ 3.36,\ \ 3.67,\ \ 3.83,\ \ 3.91,\ \ 3.96,\ \dots\ \longrightarrow\ 4=(\sigma^\star)^2 .$$

It converges, but slowly: in terms of $\sigma=\sqrt v$, the update is $\sigma\leftarrow\sqrt{\sigma\,\bar\sigma}$, so $\log\sigma$ moves halfway to $\log\bar\sigma$ at each step — the error is only halved.

What is known about convergence

Peyré–Cuturi (Computational OT, Remark 9.5) note that $\Psi$ is not a strict contraction, and that the iteration $\Sigma\leftarrow\Psi(\Sigma)$ “converges in practice” (we observed convergence on 800 random instances in dimensions 2 to 10, sometimes after more than 200 iterations). Álvarez-Esteban, del Barrio, Cuesta-Albertos and Matrán (2016) proposed the variant

$$\Sigma^{(\ell+1)}=\big(\Sigma^{(\ell)}\big)^{-1/2}\,\Psi\big(\Sigma^{(\ell)}\big)^{2}\,\big(\Sigma^{(\ell)}\big)^{-1/2},$$

for which convergence is proved, and which is much faster: in 1D it gives $v\leftarrow(\sqrt v\,\bar\sigma)^2/v=\bar\sigma^2$, the exact answer in one step. This variant is not a trick: it is exactly the general algorithm of the board’s last paragraph (§9), run on Gaussians.

In machine learning — the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID)

The standard score of image generators is a Gaussian $W_2$ (OT4ML Example 7.14, p. 129). Pass real and generated images through a pretrained network (Inception) to get feature vectors (2048 numbers per image), fit a Gaussian $\N(m_r,\Sigma_r)$ to the real features and $\N(m_g,\Sigma_g)$ to the generated ones, and report

$$\mathrm{FID}=\norm{m_r-m_g}^2+\tr\Big(\Sigma_r+\Sigma_g-2\big(\Sigma_r^{1/2}\Sigma_g\Sigma_r^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\Big),$$

the squared $W_2$ distance between the two Gaussians: the mean part plus the squared Bures distance of this section. Lower is better. Keep its limits in mind: it only sees the means and covariances of the features, and it is biased when few images are used.

Measures on the line: average the quantiles

On the board notes p. 5

1D case:

$$\sum_k\lambda_kW_2^2(\al,\be_k)=\sum_k\lambda_k\int_0^1\abs{C_\al^{-1}(t)-C_{\be_k}^{-1}(t)}^2\,\dd t$$ $$\Rightarrow\ \forall t,\qquad C_\al^{-1}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^P\lambda_k\,C_{\be_k}^{-1}(t).$$

[drawing: two CDFs $C_{\be_1}$, $C_{\be_2}$ on $[0,1]$]

$C_\al$, $C_\al^{-1}$
CDF and quantile function of $\al$ (refresher). $C_\al^{-1}(t)$ = the position of the $t$-th quantile.
first line
The 1D formula $W_2^2(\al,\be)=\int_0^1\abs{C_\al^{-1}-C_\be^{-1}}^2$, used for each input.
“$\Rightarrow$”
The unknown $\al$ only enters through its quantile function $q=C_\al^{-1}$, and the objective is $\int_0^1\Big(\sum_k\lambda_k\abs{q(t)-q_k(t)}^2\Big)\dd t$. Minimise separately for each $t$: for fixed $t$ this is the barycenter of the points $q_1(t),\dots,q_P(t)$ with weights $\lambda$, i.e. their weighted mean.
Detail: the average of quantile functions is a quantile function

Pointwise minimisation is legitimate only if the result $q=\sum_k\lambda_kq_k$ is itself the quantile function of a probability measure. It is: quantile functions are exactly the non-decreasing, left-continuous functions on $(0,1)$, and a positive combination of such functions is again one. (Its square-integrability follows from that of each $q_k$.) So $q$ is admissible, and it minimises the integrand at every $t$, hence the integral.

Intuition — average horizontally, not vertically

On a graph of CDFs (like the board’s drawing), the barycenter is obtained by averaging horizontally: at each height $t$, put the new curve at the average of the abscissas where the input CDFs reach $t$. Averaging vertically (averaging the values $C_{\be_k}(x)$ at each $x$) gives the CDF of the mixture. Horizontal = move mass; vertical = mix mass.

Worked examples
  • Two uniforms. $\be_1=\mathcal U_{[0,1]}$, $\be_2=\mathcal U_{[2,4]}$, weights $\frac12,\frac12$. Quantiles: $q_1(t)=t$, $q_2(t)=2+2t$. Average: $1+\frac32t$, the quantile function of $\mathcal U_{[1,2.5]}$. (The mixture would be two separate blocks.)
  • Gaussians. With $\Phi$ the CDF of $\N(0,1)$, $q_k(t)=m_k+\sigma_k\Phi^{-1}(t)$, so $\sum_k\lambda_kq_k=\bar m+\bar\sigma\,\Phi^{-1}$: the Gaussian $\N(\bar m,\bar\sigma^2)$ with $\bar\sigma=\sum_k\lambda_k\sigma_k$ — the same answer as the Gaussian proposition, as it should.
  • Diracs. $q_k\equiv x_k$, so the average is the constant $\sum_k\lambda_kx_k$: the Dirac at the mean (§7).
  • A uniform and a Dirac. $\be_1=\mathcal U_{[0,1]}$, $\be_2=\delta_3$, weights $\frac12,\frac12$: $\frac12t+\frac32$, the quantile function of $\mathcal U_{[1.5,2]}$ — the uniform is moved halfway and shrunk by half.

In machine learning — on the line, optimal transport is sorting

Between two samples of the same size $n$ on $\R$, the optimal plan matches the $i$-th smallest point of one with the $i$-th smallest of the other: computing $W_p$ costs a sort, $O(n\log n)$, instead of a linear program. Sliced Wasserstein distances (Lesson 5; OT4ML Def. 11.23, p. 230) exploit this in high dimension: project both clouds on random directions, sort, and average the 1D costs. The result is cheap and differentiable, and is used as a loss to train generative models and to synthesize or mix textures. The quantile-averaging barycenter of this section is also what the fair-score repair of §7 computes.

Reflexes
  • Gaussian inputs, quadratic cost → barycenter is Gaussian; mean = average of means; covariance = fixed point of $\Psi$ (in 1D: average of standard deviations).
  • Inputs on the line → average the quantile functions.
  • To check a barycenter formula → test it on Diracs (it must give the Dirac at the mean) and on Gaussians.
Pitfall — averaging the wrong thing

Averaging densities or CDFs gives the mixture. Averaging variances gives the wrong width. For $W_2$ barycenters, average positions: quantiles in 1D, standard deviations for 1D Gaussians, square roots of commuting covariances.

Barycenter of $\N(0,1)$ and $\N(4,9)$ with weights $\lambda=(\frac14,\frac34)$, and its objective value?

Answer

$m^\star=\frac14\cdot0+\frac34\cdot4=3$, $\sigma^\star=\frac14\cdot1+\frac34\cdot3=2.5$: the barycenter is $\N(3,\,6.25)$. Value: $\sum_k\lambda_k\big[(m^\star-m_k)^2+(\sigma^\star-\sigma_k)^2\big]=\frac14(9+2.25)+\frac34(1+0.25)=2.8125+0.9375=3.75$.
