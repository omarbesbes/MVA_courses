The sheet “Continuous Kantorovich formulation”, solved

Six exercises about couplings that are not matrices: what the marginal constraints force, Gaussian couplings as covariance matrices, a Monge map that exists in one direction only, a cost with a closed-form answer, and an optimal plan that no map can reach.

Source: exo-kanto-cont-uncorrected.pdf (4 pages). Exercises 1–4 are the core. Exercises 5–6 are marked “Optional – More difficult exercises” and come with the teacher’s own solutions; the solutions below follow them, correct two slips in Exercise 5 and a typo in Exercise 6. Every number in these solutions was checked by a script (exact fractions, numerical linear algebra, or the linear-programming solver of the POT library).

What the sheet trains
  • Reading the marginal constraints (Exercise 1): $\pi(A\times\Y)=\al(A)$ and $\pi(\X\times B)=\be(B)$ for all sets. Choosing the sets well answers most questions.
  • Gaussian couplings are covariance matrices (Exercises 2 and 5). The marginals fix the diagonal blocks, so only the cross-covariance is free. The best one sits on the boundary of the feasible set, where the coupling becomes a map: Brenier’s map.
  • Maps versus plans (Exercises 3 and 6). A map cannot split a point, a plan can. Sometimes a map exists in one direction only; sometimes no map is optimal at all.
  • A closed form (Exercise 4): when every trip goes through a hub, the optimal cost only depends on how much mass must leave or reach each point.
Reminder — couplings and the Kantorovich problem

A coupling of $\al\in\M(\X)$ and $\be\in\M(\Y)$ is a probability $\pi$ on $\X\times\Y$ with marginals $\al$ and $\be$:

$$\pi(A\times\Y)=\al(A),\qquad \pi(\X\times B)=\be(B)\qquad\text{for all measurable }A\subset\X,\ B\subset\Y.$$

In words: if $(X,Y)\sim\pi$, then $X\sim\al$ and $Y\sim\be$. The sheet writes $\Pi(\al,\be)$ for the set of couplings; this page writes $\U(\al,\be)$. The Kantorovich problem is $\Lc(\al,\be)=\inf_{\pi\in\U(\al,\be)}\int c\,\dd\pi$ (§1). A Monge map is a map $T$ with $\pf{T}{\al}=\be$; it gives the coupling $\pf{(\Id,T)}{\al}$, the law of $(X,T(X))$. More: joint laws and marginals, push-forward, the Kantorovich problem.

Sheet, Exercise 1 — Couplings in basic cases ★
The sheet exo-kanto-cont, Exercise 1, p. 1

(a) Let $\al,\be$ be finite positive measures with different total masses. What is $\Pi(\al,\be)$, the set of couplings?
(b) (i) What are the couplings between $\delta_x$ and $\mathcal U[0,1]$?
(ii) What are the couplings between $\frac12(\delta_x+\delta_y)$ and $\mathcal U[0,1]$?

For measures that are not probabilities (part (a)), a coupling is a positive measure $\pi$ with the same two marginal identities.

Hint

Write the marginal constraints with well-chosen sets. In (a), take the whole space. In (b)(i), take the set $A=\R\setminus\{x\}$. In (b)(ii), cut $\pi$ along the two atoms: what is left of $\pi$ above $x$, and above $y$?

Solution

(a) No coupling at all. Compute the total mass of $\pi$ in two ways:

  1. $$\pi(\X\times\Y)=\al(\X)\quad\text{and}\quad\pi(\X\times\Y)=\be(\Y).$$First marginal with $A=\X$; second marginal with $B=\Y$.
  2. $$\al(\X)\ne\be(\Y)\ \Longrightarrow\ \Pi(\al,\be)=\emptyset.$$The two numbers would have to be equal.

Transport conserves mass: you cannot fill a hole of 3 kg with 2 kg of sand. (Variants of OT that allow creating or destroying mass, called unbalanced OT, relax exactly this constraint.)

(b)(i) Only one coupling: the product.

  1. $$\pi\big((\R\setminus\{x\})\times[0,1]\big)=\delta_x(\R\setminus\{x\})=0.$$First marginal with $A=\R\setminus\{x\}$: $\delta_x$ puts no mass outside $x$.
  2. $$\pi\ \text{is carried by the vertical segment}\ \{x\}\times[0,1].$$The complement of this segment has $\pi$-mass $0$.
  3. $$\pi(\{x\}\times B)=\pi(\R\times B)=\abs{B}\quad\text{for every }B\subset[0,1].$$Nothing lies outside the segment, then second marginal; $\abs{B}$ is the length of $B$.
  4. $$\U\big(\delta_x,\mathcal U[0,1]\big)=\big\{\delta_x\otimes\mathcal U[0,1]\big\}.$$A measure on the segment is fixed by its values on the sets $\{x\}\times B$.

A single source point has no choice: it must send its mass everywhere, spread like the target. In probabilistic terms, $X=x$ is constant, and a constant is independent of everything, so $(X,Y)$ has the product law.

(b)(ii) Infinitely many couplings. If $x=y$, this is (i). Assume $x\ne y$. As in (i), $\pi$ is carried by $\{x,y\}\times[0,1]$. Cut it along the two atoms:

$$\mu_x(B):=2\,\pi(\{x\}\times B),\qquad \mu_y(B):=2\,\pi(\{y\}\times B).$$
  1. $$\mu_x([0,1])=2\,\pi(\{x\}\times[0,1])=2\cdot\tfrac12=1,\ \text{and the same for }\mu_y.$$First marginal with $A=\{x\}$: the atom $x$ has mass $\frac12$. So $\mu_x$ and $\mu_y$ are probability measures: where the mass of $x$ goes, and where the mass of $y$ goes.
  2. $$\tfrac12\mu_x(B)+\tfrac12\mu_y(B)=\pi(\R\times B)=\abs{B}.$$Add the two pieces, then use the second marginal.
  3. $$\tfrac12\mu_x=g(t)\,\dd t\quad\text{with }0\le g\le1,\qquad \tfrac12\mu_y=\big(1-g(t)\big)\,\dd t.$$By step 2, $\frac12\mu_x(B)\le\abs{B}$: a measure below the length measure has a density bounded by $1$ (Radon–Nikodym). The rest of the length is $\frac12\mu_y$.
  4. $$\int_0^1g(t)\,\dd t=\tfrac12.$$This is $\frac12\mu_x([0,1])$, by step 1.

Conversely, every measurable $g:[0,1]\to[0,1]$ with $\int_0^1g=\frac12$ gives a coupling,

$$\pi_g=\delta_x\otimes g(t)\,\dd t+\delta_y\otimes\big(1-g(t)\big)\,\dd t.$$

How to read $g$. $g(t)$ is the fraction of the mass found at $t$ that comes from $x$: $\P(X=x\mid Y=t)=g(t)$. A coupling is a rule that, given the destination $t$, decides at random where the mass came from. The only constraint is that $x$ provides half of the mass in total.

Examples, with $x=0$, $y=1$ and the cost $\abs{u-v}^2$:

$g$what it doescost $\int\abs{u-v}^2\dd\pi_g$
$\ind_{[0,1/2]}$monotone: $0$ serves the left half, $1$ the right half$\frac1{12}\approx0.083$ (the optimum, Exercise 3)
$\frac12$independent: each point is served half by $0$, half by $1$$\frac13\approx0.333$
$\ind_{[1/2,1]}$anti-monotone: $0$ serves the right half$\frac7{12}\approx0.583$ (the worst)

Compare with (i): one source point means no freedom at all, two source points already mean infinitely many couplings. This freedom is what Kantorovich’s problem optimizes over.

Sheet, Exercise 2 — Gaussian couplings in $\R$ (zero means) ★
The sheet exo-kanto-cont, Exercise 2, p. 1

Let $\al=\N(0,\sigma_\al^2)$ and $\be=\N(0,\sigma_\be^2)$ with $\sigma_\al,\sigma_\be\ge0$. Consider Gaussian couplings $\pi=\N(0,\Sigma)$ on $\R\times\R$ with

$$\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_\al^2&c\\c&\sigma_\be^2\end{pmatrix}\succeq0.$$

(a) Express the quadratic OT cost $\E[(X-Y)^2]$ in terms of $c$. (b) Determine the constraint on $c$ implied by $\Sigma\succeq0$. (c) Solve the Kantorovich problem restricted to Gaussian couplings by minimizing over feasible $c$. (d) What is the rank of $\Sigma$ as a function of $(\sigma_\al,\sigma_\be,c)$, and what is the support of $\pi$ in each case? Compare the optimal Gaussian coupling with the conclusion of Brenier’s theorem.

$\pi=\N(0,\Sigma)$
$(X,Y)$ is a Gaussian vector with $\mathrm{Var}\,X=\sigma_\al^2$, $\mathrm{Var}\,Y=\sigma_\be^2$ and $\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=c$. Its marginals are automatically $\al$ and $\be$ (the marginals of a Gaussian vector are Gaussian, with the diagonal variances). So every admissible $c$ gives a coupling, and $c$ is the only free parameter.
$\Sigma\succeq0$
“$\Sigma$ is positive semi-definite”: $v\T\Sigma v\ge0$ for every $v$. Every covariance matrix has this property, since $v\T\Sigma v=\mathrm{Var}(v_1X+v_2Y)\ge0$.
“restricted to Gaussian couplings”
We minimize over a subset of $\U(\al,\be)$ only. The answer could, in principle, be larger than the true optimum; part (c) checks that here it is not.
Reminder — when is a $2\times2$ symmetric matrix positive semi-definite?

$\begin{pmatrix}p&q\\q&r\end{pmatrix}\succeq0$ if and only if $p\ge0$, $r\ge0$ and $pr-q^2\ge0$. Its two eigenvalues have product $pr-q^2$ (the determinant) and sum $p+r$ (the trace); both are $\ge0$ exactly when the eigenvalues are. The rank is the number of non-zero eigenvalues. More: positive semi-definite matrices, Gaussian vectors.

Hint

(a) Expand the square; with zero means, $\E[X^2]$ is a variance and $\E[XY]$ a covariance. (b) Use the reminder above. (c) The cost is affine in $c$. (d) If $\det\Sigma=0$, compute $\mathrm{Var}\big(Y-\frac{c}{\sigma_\al^2}X\big)$.

Solution

(a)

  1. $$\E[(X-Y)^2]=\E[X^2]-2\,\E[XY]+\E[Y^2]=\sigma_\al^2+\sigma_\be^2-2c.$$Zero means: $\E[X^2]=\mathrm{Var}\,X$ and $\E[XY]=\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=c$.

The marginals fix $\sigma_\al^2+\sigma_\be^2$; only the covariance can lower the cost. The more $X$ and $Y$ move together, the less mass travels.

(b)

  1. $$\Sigma\succeq0\iff\sigma_\al^2\sigma_\be^2-c^2\ge0\iff\abs{c}\le\sigma_\al\sigma_\be.$$The diagonal entries are $\ge0$ anyway; the determinant condition remains.

This is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $\abs{\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)}\le\sigma_\al\sigma_\be$: the correlation $\rho=c/(\sigma_\al\sigma_\be)$ lies in $[-1,1]$.

(c)

  1. $$\min_{\abs{c}\le\sigma_\al\sigma_\be}\big(\sigma_\al^2+\sigma_\be^2-2c\big)\ \text{is attained at}\ c^\star=\sigma_\al\sigma_\be.$$The cost decreases when $c$ increases: take the largest feasible $c$, i.e. correlation $\rho=1$.
  2. $$\sigma_\al^2+\sigma_\be^2-2\sigma_\al\sigma_\be=(\sigma_\al-\sigma_\be)^2.$$A perfect square.

So the Gaussian-restricted value is $(\sigma_\al-\sigma_\be)^2$. Example: $\sigma_\al=2$, $\sigma_\be=1$. The optimal coupling costs $1$, the independent one ($c=0$) costs $5$, and the anti-correlated one ($c=-2$) costs $9$.

Nothing was lost by the restriction. On the line, $W_2$ is computed with quantiles (refresher above). The quantile function of $\N(0,\sigma^2)$ is $\sigma\,\Phi^{-1}$, where $\Phi$ is the standard Gaussian CDF, so

$$W_2^2(\al,\be)=\int_0^1\big(\sigma_\al\Phi^{-1}(t)-\sigma_\be\Phi^{-1}(t)\big)^2\dd t=(\sigma_\al-\sigma_\be)^2\int_0^1\Phi^{-1}(t)^2\,\dd t=(\sigma_\al-\sigma_\be)^2,$$

because $\int_0^1\Phi^{-1}(t)^2\,\dd t=\E[Z^2]=1$ for $Z\sim\N(0,1)$. The best of all couplings happens to be Gaussian. This is the 1D case of the Gaussian formula of §8.

(d) Rank and support. $\det\Sigma=\sigma_\al^2\sigma_\be^2-c^2$.

  • Rank 2 when $\abs{c}<\sigma_\al\sigma_\be$ (then $\sigma_\al,\sigma_\be>0$). $\pi$ has a density on $\R^2$ and its support is the whole plane: every $x$ is sent to a whole Gaussian of $y$’s.
  • Rank 1 when $\abs{c}=\sigma_\al\sigma_\be$ and $(\sigma_\al,\sigma_\be)\ne(0,0)$. If $\sigma_\al>0$, then $$\mathrm{Var}\Big(Y-\tfrac{c}{\sigma_\al^2}X\Big)=\sigma_\be^2-2\tfrac{c}{\sigma_\al^2}c+\tfrac{c^2}{\sigma_\al^4}\sigma_\al^2=\sigma_\be^2-\tfrac{c^2}{\sigma_\al^2}=0,$$ so $Y=\frac{c}{\sigma_\al^2}X$ almost surely: the support is the line $y=\frac{c}{\sigma_\al^2}x$. If $\sigma_\al=0$ (so $c=0$), then $X=0$ and the support is the vertical line $\{0\}\times\R$.
  • Rank 0 when $\sigma_\al=\sigma_\be=0$: $\pi=\delta_{(0,0)}$.

Comparison with Brenier. At the optimum $c^\star=\sigma_\al\sigma_\be$ (with $\sigma_\al>0$), $Y=\frac{\sigma_\be}{\sigma_\al}X$: the coupling is carried by the graph of the map $T(x)=\frac{\sigma_\be}{\sigma_\al}x$. This map is increasing and is the derivative of the convex function $\varphi(x)=\frac{\sigma_\be}{2\sigma_\al}x^2$. That is exactly Brenier’s theorem: $\al$ has a density, so the optimal plan is unique and given by the gradient of a convex function (refresher above).

Two more lessons hide in (d). First, $\rho=-1$ also gives a map, $T(x)=-\frac{\sigma_\be}{\sigma_\al}x$, but it is the worst coupling (cost $(\sigma_\al+\sigma_\be)^2$). Being a map is not enough; Brenier’s map is the monotone one. Second, when $\sigma_\al=0$ (so $\al=\delta_0$ has no density), the only coupling is $\delta_0\otimes\be$, carried by a vertical line: no map at all. Brenier’s hypothesis fails, and so does its conclusion.

Sheet, Exercise 3 — Uniform → two Diracs; two Diracs → uniform ★
The sheet exo-kanto-cont, Exercise 3, p. 1

Fix two distinct points $x\ne y$ in $\R$ and let $\al=\mathcal U[0,1]$, $\be=\frac12\delta_x+\frac12\delta_y$. Consider the quadratic cost $c(u,v)=\abs{u-v}^2$ (the answers are the same for any $\abs{u-v}^p$, $p\ge1$, in 1D).
(a) Find the $W_p$-optimal Monge map $T:[0,1]\to\R$ that pushes $\al$ to $\be$.
(b) Is there a Monge map $S:\R\to[0,1]$ such that $\pf{S}{\be}=\al$?
(c) Describe an optimal Kantorovich coupling $\pi\in\Pi(\al,\be)$ between $\al$ and $\be$.

Hint

(a) On the line the optimal plan is monotone: $T=C_\be^{-1}\circ C_\al$. (b) Whatever $S$ is, what does $\pf{S}{\be}$ look like? Does $\mathcal U[0,1]$ look like that? (c) Use (a), and Exercise 1(b)(ii).

Solution

Name the points so that $x

(a) Send the left half to $x$ and the right half to $y$.

  1. $$C_\al(s)=s\ \text{ on }[0,1],\qquad C_\be^{-1}(t)=\begin{cases}x,&t\le\frac12,\\ y,&t>\frac12.\end{cases}$$The CDF of the uniform law is the identity; the quantile function of $\be$ jumps from $x$ to $y$ at level $\frac12$.
  2. $$T(s)=C_\be^{-1}\big(C_\al(s)\big)=\begin{cases}x,&s\le\frac12,\\ y,&s>\frac12.\end{cases}$$The monotone rearrangement, optimal on the line for every cost $\abs{u-v}^p$ with $p\ge1$.
  3. $$\pf{T}{\al}=\tfrac12\delta_x+\tfrac12\delta_y=\be.$$Each half of $[0,1]$ has mass $\frac12$. The value of $T$ at the single point $s=\frac12$ does not matter: that point has mass $0$.
  4. $$W_p^p(\al,\be)=\int_0^{1/2}\abs{s-x}^p\,\dd s+\int_{1/2}^1\abs{s-y}^p\,\dd s.$$The cost of the map $T$.

With $x=0$, $y=1$: $W_2^2=2\int_0^{1/2}s^2\,\dd s=\frac1{12}$, so $W_2=\frac1{\sqrt{12}}\approx0.289$, and $W_1=2\int_0^{1/2}s\,\dd s=\frac14$. A linear program between $\be$ and $\mathcal U[0,1]$ discretized by 4000 equal atoms at the midpoints $(i-\frac12)/4000$ gives $0.083333$, the same value.

(b) No map in the other direction. For every map $S$,

$$\pf{S}{\be}=\tfrac12\delta_{S(x)}+\tfrac12\delta_{S(y)},$$

a measure with at most two atoms. But $\mathcal U[0,1]$ has no atom: every single point has mass $0$. So $\pf{S}{\be}\ne\al$ for every $S$, whatever the cost. A map cannot split a Dirac: the mass sitting at $x$ travels as one block to the single point $S(x)$.

(c) A plan works in both directions. Take the plan of the map of (a),

$$\pi=\pf{(\Id,T)}{\al}=\big(\text{law of }(U,T(U)),\ U\sim\mathcal U[0,1]\big).$$

It is optimal, since its cost is $W_p^p(\al,\be)$. Read backwards, it describes what the atoms do: the mass $\frac12$ at $x$ spreads uniformly over $[0,\frac12]$, the mass at $y$ over $[\frac12,1]$. This is the coupling $\pi_g$ of Exercise 1(b)(ii) with $g=\ind_{[0,1/2]}$. Because the cost is symmetric, exchanging the two coordinates gives an optimal plan from $\be$ to $\al$, with the same value: $W_p(\be,\al)=W_p(\al,\be)$.

Reflex

When you see a source with atoms and a target without atoms → no Monge map exists, but a Kantorovich plan does. Monge’s problem is not symmetric; Kantorovich’s is.

Sheet, Exercise 4 — Bottleneck cost: metric property and closed-form OT value ★★
The sheet exo-kanto-cont, Exercise 4, p. 1

Let $(\X,\mathcal B)$ be a measurable space and let $m:\X\to(0,\infty)$ be measurable (strictly positive everywhere). Define

$$c(x,y):=\begin{cases}0,&x=y,\\ m(x)+m(y),&x\ne y.\end{cases}$$

(a) Show that $d(x,y):=c(x,y)$ is a metric on $\X$.
(b) For probability measures $\al,\be$ on $(\X,\mathcal B)$ with $\int m\,\dd\al+\int m\,\dd\be<\infty$, compute the Kantorovich value $W_c(\al,\be):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\al,\be)}\int c(x,y)\,\dd\pi(x,y)$ in closed form.
(c) Give an explicit optimal plan in terms of the maximal common submeasure $\mu:=\al\wedge\be$.

$m(x)$
A toll attached to each point. Moving mass from $x$ to $y\ne x$ costs the toll of $x$ plus the toll of $y$, however close $x$ and $y$ are; staying put is free.
the picture
A hub-and-spoke network (a star): every point $x$ is joined to a central hub by a road of length $m(x)$, and every trip goes through the hub. Then $m(x)+m(y)$ is the length of the trip $x\to\text{hub}\to y$.
$\mu=\al\wedge\be$
The largest measure below both $\al$ and $\be$: their common part. For histograms $\mu_i=\min(a_i,b_i)$; for densities $\min(\rho_\al,\rho_\be)$. Then $\al-\mu$ is the excess of $\al$ over $\be$ and $\be-\mu$ the excess of $\be$ over $\al$. These two leftovers live on disjoint sets (where $\al$ is larger, where $\be$ is larger), and both have mass $1-\mu(\X)=\frac12\norm{\al-\be}_\TV$ (§5).
Hint

(a) Only the triangle inequality needs work: distinguish whether the middle point $y$ is one of the two ends. (b) Split a coupling into the part on the diagonal $\{x=y\}$ (mass that stays, free) and the rest (mass that moves, and pays its two tolls). How much mass can stay?

Solution

(a) A metric. $d\ge0$ and $d$ is symmetric. If $x\ne y$ then $d(x,y)=m(x)+m(y)>0$, because $m>0$; so $d(x,y)=0$ only when $x=y$. Triangle inequality $d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z)$:

  • if $x=z$, the left side is $0$;
  • if $x\ne z$ and $y\in\{x,z\}$, the right side is $0+d(x,z)$, equal to the left side;
  • if $y\notin\{x,z\}$: the right side is $m(x)+2m(y)+m(z)\ge m(x)+m(z)$, the left side.

In the hub picture, a stop at $y$ means visiting the hub twice.

(b) The answer.

$$\boxed{\ W_c(\al,\be)=\int m\,\dd(\al-\mu)+\int m\,\dd(\be-\mu)=\int m\,\dd\abs{\al-\be}\ }$$

For histograms, $W_c=\sum_im_i\abs{a_i-b_i}$. Only the amount of mass that must leave or reach each point matters, never where it goes: every trip passes through the hub.

Lower bound. Let $\pi\in\U(\al,\be)$ and let $D=\{(x,x)\}$ be the diagonal. (For $c$ to be measurable, $D$ must be a measurable set. This holds on $\R^d$, on finite sets and on every separable metric space; the sheet assumes it tacitly.)

  1. $$\pi=\pi_D+\pi_O,\qquad \pi_D=\pi\ \text{restricted to}\ D,\quad \pi_O=\pi\ \text{restricted to the complement}.$$The mass that stays, and the mass that moves.
  2. $$\nu(A):=\pi_D\big(\{(x,x):x\in A\}\big)\le\min\big(\al(A),\be(A)\big),\qquad\text{so}\ \ \nu\le\mu.$$$\nu(A)\le\pi(A\times\X)=\al(A)$ and $\nu(A)\le\pi(\X\times A)=\be(A)$. The mass that stays at $x$ can exceed neither what $\al$ has there nor what $\be$ needs there.
  3. $$\pi_O\ \text{has marginals}\ \al-\nu\ \text{and}\ \be-\nu.$$Remove the diagonal part, of marginals $\nu$ and $\nu$, from $\pi$.
  4. $$\int c\,\dd\pi=\int\big(m(x)+m(y)\big)\dd\pi_O(x,y)=\int m\,\dd(\al-\nu)+\int m\,\dd(\be-\nu).$$$c=0$ on $D$. Off $D$, the term $m(x)$ only sees the first marginal of $\pi_O$, and $m(y)$ only the second.
  5. $$\int c\,\dd\pi\ \ge\ \int m\,\dd(\al-\mu)+\int m\,\dd(\be-\mu).$$$\nu\le\mu$ and $m>0$ give $\int m\,\dd\nu\le\int m\,\dd\mu$; the right side is the largest saving possible.

(c) An optimal plan. Let $\theta=1-\mu(\X)$, the mass that must move. If $\theta=0$, then $\al=\be$ and the plan “nothing moves”, $\pf{(\Id,\Id)}{\al}$, costs $0$. Otherwise set

$$\pi^\star=\pf{(\Id,\Id)}{\mu}+\frac1\theta\,(\al-\mu)\otimes(\be-\mu).$$
  1. $$\text{first marginal: }\ \mu+\frac{(\be-\mu)(\X)}{\theta}\,(\al-\mu)=\mu+(\al-\mu)=\al.$$The first marginal of a product $p\otimes q$ is $q(\X)\,p$; here $(\be-\mu)(\X)=\theta$. The second marginal is $\be$ in the same way.
  2. $$\int c\,\dd\pi^\star=0+\frac1\theta\Big(\theta\!\int m\,\dd(\al-\mu)+\theta\!\int m\,\dd(\be-\mu)\Big).$$The diagonal part is free. The two leftovers live on disjoint sets, so the product part gives no mass to the diagonal and always pays $m(x)+m(y)$.

This equals the lower bound, so $\pi^\star$ is optimal and the formula of (b) holds. In words: keep the common part in place, and send the excess of $\al$ to the deficit of $\be$ in any way at all (here: independently). Any way is optimal, since every trip costs the two tolls.

Example. $\X=\{1,2,3\}$, $m=(1,2,3)$, $a=(0.5,0.3,0.2)$, $b=(0.2,0.3,0.5)$. Then $\mu=(0.2,0.3,0.2)$: the excess $0.3$ sits at point 1, the deficit $0.3$ at point 3. $W_c=1\times0.3+3\times0.3=1.2$: move $0.3$ from 1 to 3, at the price $m_1+m_3=4$. A linear-programming solver on the $3\times3$ cost matrix gives $1.2$ too.

The 0/1 cost of §5 is a special case

With $m\equiv\frac12$, the cost is $c(x,y)=\ind_{x\ne y}$, and the formula gives $W_c=\frac12\abs{\al-\be}(\X)=\frac12\norm{\al-\be}_\TV$: the proposition of §5. This exercise is its weighted version.

In machine learning — fast transport on trees

A star is the simplest tree. On any tree whose edges $e$ have lengths $\ell_e$, with the path length as ground distance, $W_1$ has a closed form (OT4ML Prop. 2.50, p. 41):

$$W_1(\al,\be)=\sum_{e}\ell_e\,\abs{\al(V_e)-\be(V_e)},$$

where $V_e$ is the set of points cut off by the edge $e$ (on the side away from a chosen root). It costs one pass over the tree, with no linear program. For a star, with leaf $x$ attached by an edge of length $m(x)$, $V_e=\{x\}$ and the formula is $\sum_xm(x)\abs{\al(x)-\be(x)}$: this exercise. Trees fitted to the data (quadtrees, clustering trees, random trees) give fast surrogates of $W_1$ for comparing many histograms or point clouds, for instance documents represented by their word embeddings. Averaging over random trees gives the tree-sliced Wasserstein distances (OT4ML p. 41–42), cousins of the sliced distances of Lesson 5.

(c) Solve the Kantorovich problem restricted to Gaussian couplings by minimizing the cost over feasible $K$. (d) What is the rank of $\Sigma$ and the support of the coupling $\pi$ for (i) strict feasibility and (ii) an optimizer from part (c)? Compare the optimal Gaussian coupling with Brenier’s theorem.

This is Exercise 2 with matrices. $S_d^{++}$ is the set of symmetric positive definite $d\times d$ matrices, and $K=\E[XY\T]$ is the cross-covariance, the matrix version of $c$. The sheet gives a solution; it is followed here, step by step, with two corrections (see the note at the end).

Reminder — Schur complements

Let $M=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\B\T&C\end{pmatrix}$ be symmetric with $A\succ0$. Then

$$M\succeq0\iff C-B\T A^{-1}B\succeq0,\qquad \mathrm{rank}\,M=\mathrm{rank}\,A+\mathrm{rank}\big(C-B\T A^{-1}B\big).$$

Why: with $E=\begin{pmatrix}I&-A^{-1}B\\0&I\end{pmatrix}$ (invertible), $E\T ME=\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\0&C-B\T A^{-1}B\end{pmatrix}$. Multiplying by an invertible matrix on both sides changes neither the sign of $v\T Mv$ (substitute $v=Ew$) nor the rank. $C-B\T A^{-1}B$ is the Schur complement; for Gaussian vectors, it is the covariance of $Y$ that remains once $X$ is known. More: positive semi-definite matrices, matrix square roots.

Hint

(a) $\E\norm{X}^2=\tr\Sigma_\al$ and $\E\ps{X}{Y}=\tr K$. (b) Schur complement, then write $K=\Sigma_\al^{1/2}M\Sigma_\be^{1/2}$. (c) Maximize $\tr K=\tr(MS)$ over $\norm{M}_{op}\le1$, where $S=\Sigma_\be^{1/2}\Sigma_\al^{1/2}$; use a singular value decomposition of $S$. Then check that $K^\star=\Sigma_\al A$, with Brenier’s matrix $A$, reaches the bound.

Solution

(a)

  1. $$\E\norm{X-Y}^2=\E\norm{X}^2+\E\norm{Y}^2-2\,\E\ps{X}{Y}=\tr\Sigma_\al+\tr\Sigma_\be-2\tr K.$$$\E\norm{X}^2=\E\,\tr(XX\T)=\tr\E[XX\T]=\tr\Sigma_\al$, and in the same way $\E\ps{X}{Y}=\tr\E[XY\T]=\tr K$.

As in 1D, only the cross-covariance is free, and the goal is to make $\tr K$ as large as possible.

(b) Feasible $K$.

  1. $$\Sigma\succeq0\iff\Sigma_\be-K\T\Sigma_\al^{-1}K\succeq0.$$Schur complement with $A=\Sigma_\al\succ0$.
  2. $$M:=\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}K\Sigma_\be^{-1/2}:\qquad \Sigma_\be-K\T\Sigma_\al^{-1}K=\Sigma_\be^{1/2}\big(I-M\T M\big)\Sigma_\be^{1/2}.$$Substitute $K=\Sigma_\al^{1/2}M\Sigma_\be^{1/2}$: then $K\T\Sigma_\al^{-1}K=\Sigma_\be^{1/2}M\T M\Sigma_\be^{1/2}$.
  3. $$\Sigma\succeq0\iff I-M\T M\succeq0\iff\norm{M}_{op}\le1.$$$\Sigma_\be^{1/2}$ is invertible. $I-M\T M\succeq0$ means $\norm{Mv}^2\le\norm{v}^2$ for every $v$, i.e. the operator norm (the largest singular value) of $M$ is at most $1$.

So the feasible cross-covariances are exactly $K=\Sigma_\al^{1/2}M\Sigma_\be^{1/2}$ with $\norm{M}_{op}\le1$. Another equivalent form, from the Schur complement of the other block: $K\Sigma_\be^{-1}K\T\preceq\Sigma_\al$. In 1D, $M=c/(\sigma_\al\sigma_\be)=\rho$, and $\norm{M}_{op}\le1$ is $\abs\rho\le1$: Exercise 2(b).

(c) Upper bound on $\tr K$. Let $S:=\Sigma_\be^{1/2}\Sigma_\al^{1/2}$, with singular value decomposition $S=U\,\mathrm{diag}(s)\,V\T$ ($U,V$ orthogonal, $s_i\ge0$).

  1. $$\tr K=\tr\big(\Sigma_\al^{1/2}M\Sigma_\be^{1/2}\big)=\tr\big(M\,\Sigma_\be^{1/2}\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\big)=\tr(MS).$$The trace is invariant under cyclic permutations: $\tr(ABC)=\tr(BCA)$.
  2. $$\tr(MS)=\tr\big(V\T MU\,\mathrm{diag}(s)\big)=\sum_is_i\,(V\T MU)_{ii}.$$Cyclic permutation again.
  3. $$(V\T MU)_{ii}\le\norm{V\T MU}_{op}=\norm{M}_{op}\le1\ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ \tr K\le\sum_is_i.$$A diagonal entry $e_i\T Ne_i$ is at most $\norm{N}_{op}$; orthogonal matrices do not change the operator norm. This is von Neumann’s trace inequality in the case we need.
  4. $$\sum_is_i=\tr\big(S\T S\big)^{1/2}=\tr\big(\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}.$$The singular values of $S$ are the square roots of the eigenvalues of $S\T S=\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al^{1/2}$.

Equality in step 3 needs $(V\T MU)_{ii}=1$ for all $i$, i.e. $M=VU\T$ (when all $s_i>0$).

The bound is reached by Brenier’s map. Let $A=\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}\big(\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}$ (refresher above), $Y=AX$, and

$$K^\star=\mathrm{Cov}(X,AX)=\Sigma_\al A=\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\big(\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}.$$
  1. $$\tr K^\star=\tr\big(\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}.$$Cyclic permutation: the factors $\Sigma_\al^{1/2}$ and $\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}$ cancel. This is the upper bound.
  2. $$\Sigma_\be-K^{\star\T}\Sigma_\al^{-1}K^\star=\Sigma_\be-A\Sigma_\al A=0.$$$K^{\star\T}=A\Sigma_\al$ ($A$ and $\Sigma_\al$ are symmetric), and $A\Sigma_\al A=\Sigma_\be$ because $A$ pushes $\N(0,\Sigma_\al)$ to $\N(0,\Sigma_\be)$. So $K^\star$ is feasible, by (b).

Plugging into (a), the Gaussian-restricted value is

$$\tr\Sigma_\al+\tr\Sigma_\be-2\tr\big(\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}=\B(\Sigma_\al,\Sigma_\be)^2,$$

the squared Bures distance of §8. It is the true $W_2^2$: by Brenier’s theorem (see (d)), no coupling, Gaussian or not, does better.

Example. $\Sigma_\al=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$, $\Sigma_\be=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&4\end{pmatrix}$. Then $\tr\big(\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\approx4.1144$ (the singular values of $S$ are $2.9335$ and $1.1809$), so $W_2^2\approx4+5-8.2288=0.7712$ and $W_2\approx0.878$. The independent coupling ($K=0$) costs $9$. Brenier’s matrix is $A\approx\begin{pmatrix}0.804&-0.281\\-0.281&1.533\end{pmatrix}$, and $K^\star=\Sigma_\al A\approx\begin{pmatrix}1.328&0.972\\0.243&2.786\end{pmatrix}$: a cross-covariance need not be symmetric.

(d) Rank and support.

  • Strict feasibility ($\norm{M}_{op}<1$): the Schur complement $\Sigma_\be^{1/2}(I-M\T M)\Sigma_\be^{1/2}$ is positive definite, so $\mathrm{rank}\,\Sigma=d+d=2d$. The coupling has a density on $\R^{2d}$; its support is all of $\R^{2d}$.
  • At the optimum $K^\star$: the Schur complement is $0$, so $\mathrm{rank}\,\Sigma=d$. Indeed $Y=AX$ almost surely: $$\mathrm{Cov}(Y-AX)=\Sigma_\be-K^{\star\T}A-AK^\star+A\Sigma_\al A=\Sigma_\be-A\Sigma_\al A=0.$$ The support is $\{(x,Ax):x\in\R^d\}$, a $d$-dimensional subspace of $\R^{2d}$: the graph of $T(x)=Ax$. In the example, the eigenvalues of $\Sigma$ are $0,0,2.555,6.445$.
  • Brenier. $\al$ has a density ($\Sigma_\al\succ0$), so the $W_2$-optimal coupling among all couplings is unique and carried by the graph of $\nabla\varphi$ with $\varphi$ convex. Here $\varphi(x)=\frac12x\T Ax$ is convex because $A$ is symmetric positive definite, and $\nabla\varphi(x)=Ax$. The Gaussian optimum is the global optimum.

Check in 1D with $\sigma_\al=2$, $\sigma_\be=1$: the boxed formula gives $c=\sigma_\al^3\sigma_\be=8$, outside the feasible interval $\abs{c}\le\sigma_\al\sigma_\be=2$ of Exercise 2. In machine learning — linear transport maps: colour transfer and domain adaptation

Fit a Gaussian to each of two data sets (the RGB colours of two photos, or the features of two domains) and move the first data set with the affine Brenier map $x\mapsto m_\be+A(x-m_\al)$. In colour transfer, this gives the first photo the mean colour and the colour covariance of the second. Among the affine maps that do so, it is the one that moves the colours least in mean squared displacement, even if the colours are not Gaussian: for an affine map this quantity only depends on the means and covariances, so the computation of (c) applies. This is Pitié and Kokaram’s “linear Monge–Kantorovich” colour mapping (OT4ML §2.3, Fig. 2.4, p. 27, shows colour transfer as a transport map in RGB space and mentions such early affine methods). The popular domain-adaptation baseline CORAL (Sun, Feng and Saenko, 2016) instead uses whitening then recolouring, $B=\Sigma_\be^{1/2}\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}$. It also maps $\N(0,\Sigma_\al)$ onto $\N(0,\Sigma_\be)$, since $B\Sigma_\al B\T=\Sigma_\be$, but it is not symmetric and moves the points more: $\E\norm{X-BX}^2\approx0.804$ against $0.771$ for $A$ in the example above. Many maps match the covariances; only one is the optimal transport.

Sheet, Exercise 6 (optional) — A non-Monge optimal plan in $\R^2$ ★★
The sheet exo-kanto-cont, Exercise 6, p. 3

Let $\al$ be the uniform probability measure on the vertical segment $S_0:=\{(0,s):s\in[0,1]\}$ (with respect to 1D length, normalized), and let $\be:=\frac12\,\mathcal U(S_-)+\frac12\,\mathcal U(S_+)$, $S_\pm:=\{(\pm1,t):t\in[0,1]\}$. We consider the quadratic cost $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2$ on $\R^2\times\R^2$.
(a) (Lower bound) Show that for any coupling $\pi\in\Pi(\al,\be)$, $\int\norm{x-y}^2\,\dd\pi(x,y)\ge1$.
(b) (An optimal coupling) Define $\pi^\star$ by the disintegration: for $X=(0,s)\sim\al$, $\pi^\star(\,\cdot\mid X=(0,s))=\frac12\delta_{(-1,s)}+\frac12\delta_{(1,s)}$. Check that $\pi^\star\in\Pi(\al,\be)$ and compute its cost. Conclude that the Kantorovich value equals $1$.
(c) (No Monge minimizer) Let $T:S_0\to S_-\cup S_+$ be a measurable map with $\pf{T}{\al}=\be$. Show that $\int\norm{x-T(x)}^2\,\dd\al(x)>1$, hence no Monge map attains the Kantorovich minimum.
(d) (Structure of the optimizer and comparison with Brenier) Describe $\supp(\pi^\star)$ and explain why it is not contained in the graph of any map $x\mapsto T(x)$. Comment on why this does not contradict Brenier’s theorem.

Reminder — defining a plan by disintegration

“$\pi(\,\cdot\mid X=x)=\kappa_x$” means: draw $X\sim\al$, then draw $Y$ from the probability $\kappa_x$ attached to the point $x$. The pair $(X,Y)$ has the law $\pi$, defined by $\pi(A\times B)=\int_A\kappa_x(B)\,\dd\al(x)$. Its first marginal is $\al$ automatically; only the second one must be checked. More: conditional laws and disintegration.

Hint

(a) Compute $\norm{x-y}^2$ for $x\in S_0$ and $y\in S_\pm$. (c) If a map had cost $1$, what would it do to the height $s$? Then look at the mass it puts on $S_-$ between two heights.

Solution

(a) Every trip has length at least 1. Write $x=(0,s)$ and $y=(\xi,t)$ with $\xi\in\{-1,1\}$:

$$\norm{x-y}^2=\xi^2+(t-s)^2=1+(t-s)^2\ge1.$$

Integrating against any coupling gives $\int\norm{x-y}^2\,\dd\pi\ge1$: the horizontal gap between the segments is unavoidable.

(b) A plan with no vertical motion. Under $\pi^\star$, the point at height $s$ sends half of its mass to the left and half to the right, at the same height.

  1. $$\pi^\star\big(S_0\times(\{-1\}\times J)\big)=\int_J\tfrac12\,\dd s=\tfrac12\abs{J}\quad\text{for every }J\subset[0,1].$$Only the heights $s\in J$ send mass to $\{-1\}\times J$, half of it each. (The sheet’s solution writes $\pi^\star\big(S_-\times(\{-1\}\times J)\big)$; the first factor must be the source segment $S_0$, since $\pi^\star$ lives on $S_0\times(S_-\cup S_+)$.)
  2. $$\text{second marginal}=\tfrac12\,\mathcal U(S_-)+\tfrac12\,\mathcal U(S_+)=\be.$$Step 1 and the same computation for $S_+$; the first marginal is $\al$ by construction.
  3. $$\int\norm{x-y}^2\,\dd\pi^\star=\int\big(1+(t-s)^2\big)\,\dd\pi^\star=1.$$$t=s$ for $\pi^\star$-almost every pair.

With (a), $\pi^\star$ is optimal and the Kantorovich value is $1$.

(c) Every map costs more than 1. Let $T(0,s)=(\xi(s),u(s))$ with $\xi(s)\in\{-1,1\}$ and $u(s)\in[0,1]$, and $\pf{T}{\al}=\be$.

  1. $$\int\norm{x-T(x)}^2\,\dd\al(x)=\int_0^1\big(1+(u(s)-s)^2\big)\,\dd s=1+\int_0^1(u(s)-s)^2\,\dd s.$$The computation of (a), with $t=u(s)$.
  2. $$\text{cost}=1\ \Longrightarrow\ u(s)=s\ \text{for almost every }s.$$The integral of a non-negative function vanishes only if the function vanishes almost everywhere.
  3. $$\abs{A_-\cap J}=\tfrac12\abs{J}\ \text{ for every interval }J,\quad A_-:=\{s:\xi(s)=-1\}.$$With $u(s)=s$, the mass that $T$ puts on $S_-$ at heights in $J$ is the length of $A_-\cap J$. The target $\be$ requires $\frac12\abs{J}$.
  4. $$\int_J\ind_{A_-}=\int_J\tfrac12\ \text{ for all }J\ \Longrightarrow\ \ind_{A_-}=\tfrac12\ \text{almost everywhere}.$$Two functions with the same integral on every interval are equal almost everywhere. But an indicator only takes the values $0$ and $1$: contradiction.

So no map has cost $1$: every Monge map costs strictly more than the Kantorovich value, and Monge’s problem has no minimizer here.

Maps can come arbitrarily close. Cut $[0,1]$ into $n$ strips of length $h=\frac1n$. Send the lower half of each strip to the left segment and the upper half to the right segment, each stretched by a factor $2$ onto the whole strip (right panel of the figure). This map pushes $\al$ to $\be$. On a lower half, a point at distance $r\in[0,\frac h2]$ above the bottom of its strip goes up by $r$. On an upper half, a point at distance $r'\in[0,\frac h2]$ below the top of its strip goes down by $r'$. Each half costs $\int_0^{h/2}r^2\,\dd r=\frac{h^3}{24}$, so the total vertical cost is $n\cdot2\cdot\frac{h^3}{24}=\frac1{12n^2}$. Total: $1+\frac1{12n^2}$, that is $1.0833$, $1.0093$ and $1.0008$ for $n=1,3,10$. The infimum over maps is $1$, but it is not attained. Kantorovich’s problem, with its minimizer $\pi^\star$, is the right completion of Monge’s problem (the relaxation of Lesson 2).

(d) Structure and Brenier.

$$\supp(\pi^\star)=\big\{\big((0,s),(-1,s)\big):s\in[0,1]\big\}\cup\big\{\big((0,s),(1,s)\big):s\in[0,1]\big\}.$$

Every $x=(0,s)$ has two partners, $(-1,s)$ and $(1,s)$, each with half of the mass. The graph of a map has one partner per point, so it cannot contain this support. No contradiction with Brenier: his theorem asks that $\al$ have a density with respect to the area measure of $\R^2$, and here $\al$ lives on a segment, a set of area $0$. The hypothesis fails, and an optimal plan need not come from a map.

Pitfall — averaging the partners does not give a transport map

A common way to turn a plan into a map is the barycentric projection $\bar T(x)=\E_\pi[Y\mid X=x]$, the average of the partners of $x$ (OT4ML Def. 12.32, p. 281). OT domain adaptation uses it to move source samples onto the target domain, and its label version to transfer labels (OT4ML Example 3.22, p. 61). Here $\bar T(0,s)=\frac12(-1,s)+\frac12(1,s)=(0,s)$: the identity. It moves nothing and certainly does not push $\al$ to $\be$. When the optimal plan splits mass, its barycentric projection can land where the target has no mass at all.
