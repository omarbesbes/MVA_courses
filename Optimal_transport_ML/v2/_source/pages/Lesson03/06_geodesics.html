6. Displacement interpolation and geodesics notes p. 4

To go from $\al$ to $\be$, move every unit of mass along the straight segment from its starting point to its destination (given by an optimal plan) at constant speed: the distributions $\al_t$ you see along the way form a shortest path for $W_2$.

The problem: how to go from one distribution to another?

Between two points $x$ and $y$ of $\R^d$ the natural path is the segment $(1-t)x+ty$. Between two distributions there are two natural candidates, and they behave very differently.

Mixture (“vertical”)

$m_t=(1-t)\al+t\be$. Nothing moves: the mass of $\al$ fades out where it is while the mass of $\be$ fades in where it is. For images: a cross-fade, with ghosting.

Displacement (“horizontal”)

Every unit of mass travels from its starting point $x$ to its destination $y$, along the segment, at constant speed: at time $t$ it is at $(1-t)x+ty$. For images: a morphing.

For the second one we need to know which $x$ goes to which $y$: that is exactly what an optimal transport plan tells us.

Displacement interpolation

On the board notes p. 4

Displacement interpolation. $\al,\be\in\M(\X)$,

$$P_t(x,y)=(1-t)x+ty,\qquad P_0(x,y)=x,\qquad P_1(x,y)=y,$$ $$\al_t:=\pf{P_t}{\pi}\quad=\ \text{distribution of }(1-t)X+tY\ \text{ where }(X,Y)\sim\pi .$$

[drawing: $\al_0=\al$, a set of concentric circles, deformed through $\al_t$ into $\al_1=\be$, an irregular blob]

If $\pi=\pf{(\Id,T)}{\al}$ (Monge solution), then $\al_t$ is the distribution of $(1-t)X+tT(X)$ with $X\sim\al$.

$\al_t$ = displacement interpolation, McCann.

$\pi$
An optimal plan between $\al$ and $\be$ for the quadratic cost $\norm{x-y}^2$ (implicit on the board). It decides who goes where.
$P_t(x,y)$
The point at fraction $t$ of the way from $x$ to $y$. At $t=0$ it is $x$, at $t=1$ it is $y$: $P_0$ and $P_1$ are the two projections of §1.
$\al_t=\pf{P_t}{\pi}$
Take every pair $(x,y)$ with its weight $\pi$, and put that weight at the intermediate point $(1-t)x+ty$. For a discrete plan, $\al_t=\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\,\delta_{(1-t)x_i+ty_j}$.
endpoints
$P_0(x,y)=x$ keeps the starting point and $P_1(x,y)=y$ the arrival point, so $\al_0=\al$ and $\al_1=\be$ (the marginals of $\pi$). Beware a clash of names on the board: in §1, $P_1$ and $P_2$ were the first and second projections (the index was a coordinate); here the index of $P_t$ is a time, so $P_0$ is the first projection and $P_1$ the second.
Monge case
If the optimal plan is a map, $Y=T(X)$, then $\al_t=\pf{\big((1-t)\Id+tT\big)}{\al}$: every point $x$ slides towards $T(x)$. This is McCann’s interpolation (Lesson 1). By Brenier, this is the case when $\al$ has a density.
Worked examples
  • Two Diracs. $\al=\delta_x$, $\be=\delta_y$: the only plan is $\delta_{(x,y)}$, so $\al_t=\delta_{(1-t)x+ty}$, a single atom sliding along the segment. The mixture instead is $(1-t)\delta_x+t\delta_y$: two atoms, fixed, changing weights.
  • A Dirac that splits. $\al=\delta_0$, $\be=\frac12\delta_{-1}+\frac12\delta_1$ on $\R$. The plan sends half of the mass to each side: $\al_t=\frac12\delta_{-t}+\frac12\delta_t$. Mass can split, because plans can.
  • Two Gaussians on the line. $\al=\N(m_0,\sigma_0^2)$, $\be=\N(m_1,\sigma_1^2)$. The optimal map is $T(x)=m_1+\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_0}(x-m_0)$, so $(1-t)X+tT(X)$ is again Gaussian with mean $(1-t)m_0+tm_1$ and standard deviation $(1-t)\sigma_0+t\sigma_1$: $$\al_t=\N\big((1-t)m_0+tm_1,\ ((1-t)\sigma_0+t\sigma_1)^2\big).$$ The standard deviations are interpolated linearly (not the variances). The mixture is a two-bump distribution as soon as the means are far apart.

Constant speed: $W_2(\al_s,\al_t)=\abs{t-s}\,W_2(\al,\be)$

This is the property that makes $\al_t$ a “straight line” in the Wasserstein space: it travels at constant speed, and the distance covered between times $s$ and $t$ is proportional to $t-s$.

Proposition (constant speed) — on $\R^d$ with $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2$

If $\pi$ is optimal between $\al$ and $\be$ and $\al_t=\pf{P_t}{\pi}$, then $W_2(\al_s,\al_t)=\abs{t-s}\,W_2(\al,\be)$ for all $s,t\in[0,1]$.

Idea of the proof

Upper bound: follow the mass itself from time $s$ to time $t$; it moves by $(t-s)$ times its total displacement. Lower bound: if some piece of the path were a shortcut, the triangle inequality would make the whole trip shorter than $W_2(\al,\be)$, which is impossible.

Full proof
  1. Let $s\le t$. The plan $\pf{(P_s,P_t)}{\pi}$ couples $\al_s$ and $\al_t$: under it, the mass that went $x\to y$ is matched from $(1-s)x+sy$ to $(1-t)x+ty$.Its marginals are $\pf{P_s}{\pi}=\al_s$ and $\pf{P_t}{\pi}=\al_t$.
  2. $$W_2^2(\al_s,\al_t)\le\int\norm{P_t(x,y)-P_s(x,y)}^2\,\dd\pi=\int\norm{(t-s)(y-x)}^2\,\dd\pi=(t-s)^2\,W_2^2(\al,\be).$$Any coupling gives an upper bound; $P_t-P_s=(t-s)(y-x)$; $\pi$ is optimal for $\al,\be$.
  3. So $W_2(\al_s,\al_t)\le(t-s)\,W_2(\al,\be)$ for all $s\le t$. Apply it to the three pieces $[0,s]$, $[s,t]$, $[t,1]$ and use the triangle inequality (§2): $$\begin{aligned}W_2(\al,\be)&\le W_2(\al_0,\al_s)+W_2(\al_s,\al_t)+W_2(\al_t,\al_1)\\&\le\big(s+(t-s)+(1-t)\big)W_2(\al,\be)=W_2(\al,\be).\end{aligned}$$The two ends of the chain are equal, so every inequality in between is an equality.
  4. In particular $W_2(\al_s,\al_t)=(t-s)\,W_2(\al,\be)$.If it were strictly smaller, the middle sum would be strictly smaller than $W_2(\al,\be)$.

A geodesic space

On the board notes p. 4

Prop: $(\Pc_2(\R^d),W_2)$ is a geodesic space.

Reminder: $(\X,d)$ is a geodesic space if for any $(x,y)\in\X^2$ there exists a shortest path (= geodesic) joining $x$ and $y$: $\ga:[0,1]\to\X$ s.t. $\ga(0)=x$, $\ga(1)=y$ and $d\big(\ga(s),\ga(t)\big)=\abs{s-t}\,d(x,y)$.

$\ga:[0,1]\to\X$
A path (a curve) in the space, parametrised by time. (On the board the letter $\ga$ is a path here, not the third measure of §2.)
$d(\ga(s),\ga(t))=\abs{s-t}\,d(x,y)$
The path is a constant-speed shortest path: the distance covered between two times is proportional to the elapsed time, and the total length is exactly $d(x,y)$. In $\R^d$: the segment. On a sphere: an arc of great circle.
geodesic space
Any two points can be joined by such a path. Counter-example: the plane with the origin removed, $\R^2\setminus\{0\}$, between $x$ and $-x$ — the only candidate (the segment) goes through the missing point.
$\Pc_2(\R^d)$
Probability measures with a finite second moment, the natural home of $W_2$ on $\R^d$ (§2).

Proof of the proposition. Given $\al,\be\in\Pc_2(\R^d)$, take an optimal plan $\pi$ (it exists) and $\ga(t)=\al_t$. Then $\ga(0)=\al$, $\ga(1)=\be$, and the constant-speed proposition is exactly the condition $W_2(\al_s,\al_t)=\abs{s-t}\,W_2(\al,\be)$. $\square$

(For $W_1$ the mixture is a geodesic: $W_1(m_s,m_t)=(t-s)\,W_1(\al,\be)$. Geodesics of $W_1$ are not unique. This is one reason why $W_2$ is the distance of choice for interpolation.)

$\al_t$ is the weighted average of $\al$ and $\be$

On the board notes p. 4

We also have: $\displaystyle\ \al_t\in\argmin_\rho\ (1-t)\,W_2^2(\al,\rho)+t\,W_2^2(\be,\rho).$

Why this matters. In $\R^d$ the point $(1-t)x+ty$ is the minimiser of $z\mapsto(1-t)\norm{z-x}^2+t\norm{z-y}^2$ (set the gradient $2(1-t)(z-x)+2t(z-y)$ to zero). The board says that the same characterisation holds in the Wasserstein space: $\al_t$ is the weighted average of $\al$ and $\be$, with weights $(1-t,t)$, defined variationally. This is the bridge to the next section: averaging more than two distributions.

Proof (an algebraic identity and the triangle inequality)

Fix $\rho$ and write $A=W_2(\al,\rho)$, $B=W_2(\rho,\be)$, $D=W_2(\al,\be)$.

  1. $$(1-t)A^2+tB^2=t(1-t)(A+B)^2+\big((1-t)A-tB\big)^2 .$$Expand the right side: $t(1-t)(A^2+2AB+B^2)+(1-t)^2A^2-2t(1-t)AB+t^2B^2=(1-t)A^2+tB^2$.
  2. $$(1-t)A^2+tB^2\ \ge\ t(1-t)(A+B)^2\ \ge\ t(1-t)D^2 .$$Drop the square $\ge0$; then the triangle inequality $A+B\ge D$ (§2).
  3. For $\rho=\al_t$: $A=tD$ and $B=(1-t)D$ (constant speed), so $(1-t)A^2+tB^2=(1-t)t^2D^2+t(1-t)^2D^2=t(1-t)D^2$.The lower bound of step 2 is attained.
  4. Hence $\al_t$ minimises $\rho\mapsto(1-t)W_2^2(\al,\rho)+tW_2^2(\be,\rho)$, with minimal value $t(1-t)W_2^2(\al,\be)$.Step 2 holds for every $\rho$.

(Conversely, any minimiser must turn both inequalities into equalities, so it sits on some geodesic at the right fraction $t$: when $\al$ has a density, the minimiser is unique and it is $\al_t$.)

Reflexes
  • To interpolate between two distributions → interpolate positions along an optimal plan, not weights.
  • To compute $\al_t$ on the line → average quantile functions: $C_{\al_t}^{-1}=(1-t)C_\al^{-1}+tC_\be^{-1}$.
  • To prove that something is a geodesic → upper bound by a coupling, then the triangle inequality forces equality.

Compute the displacement interpolation between $\mathcal U_{[0,1]}$ and $\mathcal U_{[2,4]}$.

Answer

The monotone map is $T(x)=2+2x$. With $X\sim\mathcal U_{[0,1]}$: $(1-t)X+t(2+2X)=2t+(1+t)X$, uniform on $[2t,\ 2t+1+t]=[2t,\ 1+3t]$. At $t=0$: $[0,1]$; at $t=1$: $[2,4]$; the length grows linearly from $1$ to $2$.
