What you will be able to do after this page

  2. Compare $W_p$ and $W_q$ on a compact space, and explain why they have the same convergent sequences without being “equivalent”.
  3. Tell apart weak, weak-*, vague and strong (total variation) convergence, and say which one $W_p$ measures — on three test sequences: a moving Dirac, Riemann sums, and mass escaping to infinity.
  4. Prove $W_p^p=\frac12\norm{\al-\be}_\TV$ for the 0/1 distance, and the comparison $W_1$ versus total variation on a finite space (the two exercises of the sheet).
  5. Build the displacement interpolation $\al_t$, prove it is a constant-speed geodesic, and explain why the mixture $(1-t)\al+t\be$ is not one.
  6. Define Wasserstein barycenters, compute them for Diracs, Gaussians and measures on the line, write the fixed-grid linear program, and run (and justify) the fixed-point algorithm.

The story of the lecture in five steps

A transport cost between two distributions becomes a distance; a distance gives a notion of convergence and a geometry; in that geometry one can draw shortest paths and compute averages of distributions.

  1. Recap (§1). Kantorovich’s problem gives a number $\Lc(\al,\be)$: the cost of the cheapest way to move the mass of $\al$ onto $\be$ when moving a unit of mass from $x$ to $y$ costs $c(x,y)$.
  2. A distance (§2–§3). If the cost is a distance raised to a power, $c=d^p$ with $p\ge1$, then $W_p=\Lc^{1/p}$ is a distance between probability measures. The key tool is the gluing of two transport plans. Different $p$ give comparable distances.
  3. Convergence (§4–§5). What does “$\al_n\to\al$” mean for distributions? $W_p$-convergence is weak convergence (plus moments when the space is not compact). Total variation (the “strong” notion) is much stricter: it does not see how far mass moves, only whether it moves.
  4. Shortest paths (§6). Moving each unit of mass along a straight line, at constant speed, gives the displacement interpolation $\al_t$. It is a shortest path (a geodesic) for $W_2$; fading one distribution into the other is not.
  5. Averages (§7–§9). The weighted average of points minimizes a sum of squared distances. Replace points by distributions and distances by $W_2$: you get Wasserstein barycenters. Closed forms for Diracs, Gaussians and 1D measures; a linear program on a grid; and a fixed-point algorithm in general.

Refresher — probability measures, Diracs, integrals

In this course, a probability measure $\al$ on a space $\X$ (a subset of $\R^d$, a compact set, a finite set…) distributes a total mass $1$ over $\X$: $\al(A)\ge0$ is the mass of the set $A$, and $\al(\X)=1$. The set of all of them is written $\M(\X)$ in the lecture (“positive measures of mass 1”). Three kinds appear all the time:

  • A Dirac $\delta_x$: all the mass sits at the single point $x$. Then $\int f\,\dd\delta_x=f(x)$.
  • A discrete measure $\al=\sum_i a_i\delta_{x_i}$ with weights $a_i\ge0$, $\sum_ia_i=1$ (a histogram): mass $a_i$ at the point $x_i$. Then $\int f\,\dd\al=\sum_i a_if(x_i)$.
  • A measure with a density $\al=\rho(x)\,\dd x$: then $\al(A)=\int_A\rho$ and $\int f\,\dd\al=\int f(x)\rho(x)\,\dd x$. Example: the uniform law $\mathcal U[0,1]$, density $1$ on $[0,1]$.

Probabilistic reading: “$X\sim\al$” means that the random variable $X$ has law $\al$, i.e. $\P(X\in A)=\al(A)$; then $\E[f(X)]=\int f\,\dd\al$.

Refresher — push-forward $\pf{T}{\al}$

If $T:\X\to\Y$ is a map, the push-forward (image measure) $\pf{T}{\al}$ is the measure obtained by moving each bit of mass of $\al$ from $x$ to $T(x)$:

$$(\pf{T}{\al})(B)=\al\big(T^{-1}(B)\big),\qquad \int g\,\dd(\pf{T}{\al})=\int g\big(T(x)\big)\,\dd\al(x).$$

Random variables: if $X\sim\al$ then $T(X)\sim\pf{T}{\al}$. Discrete: $\pf{T}{\Big(\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i}\Big)}=\sum_ia_i\delta_{T(x_i)}$ (the weights travel with their points). Used here for the marginals of a coupling ($P_1(x,y)=x$), for the displacement interpolation ($P_t(x,y)=(1-t)x+ty$) and for the barycenter algorithm ($\pf{T}{\al}$ with an averaged map $T$).

Refresher — couplings, and why an optimal one exists

A coupling (transport plan) of $\al$ and $\be$ is a probability measure $\pi$ on pairs $(x,y)$ whose two marginals are $\al$ and $\be$. For histograms $a\in\R^n$, $b\in\R^m$ it is a matrix $P\in\R_+^{n\times m}$ with

$$\sum_j P_{ij}=a_i\ \ (\text{row sums}),\qquad \sum_i P_{ij}=b_j\ \ (\text{column sums}).$$

$P_{ij}$ is the mass sent from $x_i$ to $y_j$. There is always at least one coupling, the independent one $P_{ij}=a_ib_j$ (the product measure $\al\otimes\be$), so the set of couplings is never empty. The minimum of $\int c\,\dd\pi$ over couplings is attained (general Kantorovich problem, Lesson 2) (for a continuous cost on a compact space, and more generally for lower semi-continuous costs on Polish spaces such as $\R^d$): there is an optimal coupling. Used here in the proof that $W_p(\al,\be)=0$ forces $\al=\be$, and every time we write “let $\pi$ be an optimal plan”.

Refresher — optimal transport on the line (CDF and quantiles)

For a probability $\al$ on $\R$, the cumulative distribution function is $C_\al(x)=\al\big((-\infty,x]\big)$ and the quantile function is its generalized inverse $C_\al^{-1}(t)=\inf\{x:\ C_\al(x)\ge t\}$, $t\in(0,1)$. The quantile function is non-decreasing; $C_\al^{-1}(t)$ is “the point below which a fraction $t$ of the mass lies”.

On the line, the optimal plan for $\abs{x-y}^p$ with $p\ge1$ is the monotone one (sorted mass goes to sorted mass: the $t$-th quantile of $\al$ goes to the $t$-th quantile of $\be$), and

$$W_p^p(\al,\be)=\int_0^1\abs{C_\al^{-1}(t)-C_\be^{-1}(t)}^p\,\dd t.$$

When $\al$ has a density, the monotone plan is the map $T=C_\be^{-1}\circ C_\al$. Used here for every 1D computation (Riemann sums, Gaussians on the line, 1D barycenters).

Refresher — Brenier’s theorem and Gaussians (Lesson 1)

Brenier. On $\R^d$ with $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2$: if $\al$ has a density, there is a unique optimal plan, and it is carried by the graph of a map $T=\nabla\varphi$ with $\varphi$ convex: $\pi=\pf{(\Id,T)}{\al}$, i.e. $Y=T(X)$. Conversely, the gradient of any convex function is the optimal map between $\al$ and $\pf{(\nabla\varphi)}{\al}$. So Kantorovich (plans) and Monge (maps) give the same answer.

Gaussians. Between $\N(m_\al,\Sigma_\al)$ and $\N(m_\be,\Sigma_\be)$ the optimal map is affine, $T(x)=m_\be+A(x-m_\al)$ with the symmetric positive matrix

$$A=\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}\big(\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\Sigma_\al^{-1/2},$$

and $W_2^2=\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+\B(\Sigma_\al,\Sigma_\be)^2$ with the Bures distance $\B(\Sigma,\Lambda)^2=\tr\big(\Sigma+\Lambda-2(\Lambda^{1/2}\Sigma\Lambda^{1/2})^{1/2}\big)$. In 1D: $T(x)=m_\be+\frac{\sigma_\be}{\sigma_\al}(x-m_\al)$ and $W_2^2=(m_\al-m_\be)^2+(\sigma_\al-\sigma_\be)^2$. Here $M^{1/2}$ is the matrix square root: diagonalize $M=UDU\T$ (with $D\ge0$) and set $M^{1/2}=UD^{1/2}U\T$. Used here in §6 (geodesics) and §8–§9 (barycenters).
