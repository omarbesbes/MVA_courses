3. Comparing $W_p$ and $W_q$ notes p. 2

On a compact space, $W_q$ is squeezed between $W_p$ and a power of $W_p$: all the Wasserstein distances agree on which sequences converge, even though their values can be very different.

The question

We now have a whole family of distances $W_1,W_2,W_3,\dots$ on the same set of distributions. Which one should we use? Do they at least agree on what “close” means? The answer of the lecture: on a compact space, yes for convergence, no for the numbers.

On the board notes p. 2

Topology of $(\M(\X),W_p)$. Prop. Assume $\X$ compact. For $1\le p\le q$ we have

$$W_p(\al,\be)\le W_q(\al,\be)\le\big(\diam(\X)\big)^{\frac{q-p}{q}}\,W_p(\al,\be)^{p/q},\qquad\text{where }\diam(\X)=\sup_{x,y}d(x,y).$$

Proof: take $\varphi(s)=s^{q/p}$; since $q\ge p$, $\varphi$ is convex on $\R^+$.

$$\begin{aligned}W_p(\al,\be)^q&\le\Big(\int d(x,y)^p\,\dd\pi(x,y)\Big)^{q/p}\underset{\text{Jensen}}{\le}\int\varphi\big(d(x,y)^p\big)\,\dd\pi\\&=\int d(x,y)^q\,\dd\pi=W_q(\al,\be)^q\quad\text{if $\pi$ opt.}\end{aligned}$$ $$d(x,y)^q\le d(x,y)^p\,\underbrace{d(x,y)^{q-p}}_{\le\,\diam(\X)^{q-p}}.$$

R.k: the notion of converging sequence is the same for all $W_q$. R.k 2: all Wasserstein define the same topology (on a compact space).

$W_p(\delta_x,\delta_y)=d(x,y)$; if $x_n\to x$ in $(\X,d)$ then $W_p(\delta_{x_n},\delta_x)\to0$ as $n\to+\infty$.

Decoding

$\diam(\X)$
The largest distance between two points of $\X$. It is finite because $\X$ is compact (hence bounded). For $\X=[0,1]$, $\diam=1$.
left inequality
“$W_p$ increases with $p$.” Think of $W_p$ as an average size of the displacements $d(X,Y)$ under the best coupling: the $L^p$ average gives more and more weight to the long displacements as $p$ grows.
right inequality
“$W_q$ is controlled by a power of $W_p$.” Because no displacement can exceed $\diam(\X)$, raising them to a higher power cannot make them explode.
“same topology”
A sequence $\al_n$ converges to $\al$ for $W_p$ if and only if it converges for $W_q$. In a metric space, knowing the convergent sequences is knowing the topology (open sets, continuous functions…).
Refresher — Jensen’s inequality

If $\varphi$ is convex and $U$ is a random variable (or $\pi$ a probability measure), then

$$\varphi\big(\E[U]\big)\le\E\big[\varphi(U)\big],\qquad\varphi\Big(\int f\,\dd\pi\Big)\le\int\varphi\circ f\,\dd\pi .$$

Picture: the graph of a convex function lies below its chords, so “the function of the average is below the average of the function”. With $\varphi(s)=s^{r}$, $r\ge1$, it says $\E[U]^r\le\E[U^r]$ for $U\ge0$: this is the familiar fact that $L^p$ norms on a probability space increase with $p$. Here we use $r=q/p\ge1$.

The proof, step by step

Idea of the proof
  1. Each inequality compares two transport costs; pick, for each, the coupling that makes one side exact (optimal for $W_q$ on the left, optimal for $W_p$ on the right).
  2. Left: compare the $L^p$ and $L^q$ averages of the displacement under the same coupling — Jensen.
  3. Right: bound $d^q$ by $d^p$ pointwise, using that $d\le\diam(\X)$.
Proof of $W_p\le W_q$
  1. Let $\pi$ be an optimal coupling for $W_q$: $\int d^q\,\dd\pi=W_q(\al,\be)^q$.“if $\pi$ opt.” on the board: the coupling is optimal for the larger exponent $q$.
  2. $$W_p(\al,\be)^p\le\int d(x,y)^p\,\dd\pi(x,y).$$$\pi$ is a coupling of $\al$ and $\be$, maybe not optimal for $d^p$, and $W_p^p$ is the minimum.
  3. $$W_p(\al,\be)^q\le\Big(\int d^p\,\dd\pi\Big)^{q/p}=\varphi\Big(\int d^p\,\dd\pi\Big),\qquad\varphi(s)=s^{q/p}.$$Raise step 2 to the power $q/p$; $s\mapsto s^{q/p}$ is increasing on $\R^+$.
  4. $$\varphi\Big(\int d^p\,\dd\pi\Big)\le\int\varphi\big(d^p\big)\,\dd\pi=\int d^q\,\dd\pi.$$Jensen: $\varphi$ is convex on $\R^+$ because $q/p\ge1$, and $\pi$ is a probability measure. Then $\varphi(d^p)=(d^p)^{q/p}=d^q$.
  5. $$W_p(\al,\be)^q\le W_q(\al,\be)^q,\quad\text{so}\quad W_p(\al,\be)\le W_q(\al,\be).$$Step 1, then take the $q$-th root (increasing).

Note that compactness was not used here: $W_p\le W_q$ holds on any space (when both are finite).

Proof of $W_q\le\diam(\X)^{(q-p)/q}\,W_p^{p/q}$
  1. Let $\pi$ be an optimal coupling for $W_p$ this time: $\int d^p\,\dd\pi=W_p(\al,\be)^p$.Now we want an upper bound on $W_q$, so we test $W_q$ on the coupling that is good for $W_p$.
  2. $$d(x,y)^q=d(x,y)^p\,d(x,y)^{q-p}\le d(x,y)^p\,\diam(\X)^{q-p}\qquad\text{for all }x,y.$$$d(x,y)\le\diam(\X)$ and $q-p\ge0$, so $d^{q-p}\le\diam^{q-p}$.
  3. $$W_q(\al,\be)^q\le\int d^q\,\dd\pi\le\diam(\X)^{q-p}\int d^p\,\dd\pi=\diam(\X)^{q-p}\,W_p(\al,\be)^p.$$$\pi$ is admissible for $W_q$; integrate step 2; then step 1.
  4. $$W_q(\al,\be)\le\diam(\X)^{\frac{q-p}{q}}\,W_p(\al,\be)^{\frac pq}.$$Take the $q$-th root of both sides.
Note on the sources: your notes have the right exponent

The 2019 course notes (CourseOT.pdf, Proposition 8, p. 16) print the last factor as $W_p(\al,\be)^{q/p}$. The proof above gives $W_p^{p/q}$, as written in your notes. The example below confirms it: with $q/p$ one would get $W_2\le\eps^2$, but in fact $W_2=\sqrt\eps$.

The example that shows everything

Worked example — moving a small mass $\eps$ by a distance $1$

On $\X=[0,1]$ (so $\diam=1$), take $\al=\delta_0$ and $\be=(1-\eps)\delta_0+\eps\delta_1$. We computed in §2 that $W_p(\al,\be)=\eps^{1/p}$. So

$$W_1=\eps,\qquad W_2=\sqrt\eps,\qquad W_4=\eps^{1/4}.$$
  • Left inequality: $\eps\le\sqrt\eps\le\eps^{1/4}$ for $\eps\in[0,1]$. ✓
  • Right inequality with $(p,q)=(1,2)$: $W_2\le1^{1/2}\,W_1^{1/2}=\sqrt\eps$. It is an equality: the bound cannot be improved.
  • Not equivalent: $W_2/W_1=1/\sqrt\eps\to\infty$ as $\eps\to0$. There is no constant $C$ with $W_2\le C\,W_1$ for all pairs. (Compare with norms in finite dimension, which are all equivalent up to constants.)
  • Same convergence: still, $W_1\to0$ if and only if $W_2\to0$ (both iff $\eps\to0$).
Intuition — what $p$ measures

Moving a little mass far is cheap for $W_1$ (cost “mass × distance”) and expensive for large $p$ (cost “(mass × distance$^p$)$^{1/p}$”, dominated by the distance). In the limit $p\to\infty$ one gets $W_\infty$, the largest displacement needed, which does not care about the mass at all. So: $W_1$ for average displacement, large $p$ when you care about the worst displacements.

Diracs: the topology of $\X$ is inside

The last line of the board: since $W_p(\delta_x,\delta_y)=d(x,y)$, we have $W_p(\delta_{x_n},\delta_x)\to0$ if and only if $x_n\to x$. The map $x\mapsto\delta_x$ sends the space $\X$ into the Wasserstein space without distortion; convergence of points is convergence of their Diracs. (This is the example that will separate $W_p$ from the total variation in §5.)

Pitfall — “same topology” does not mean “same rates”

For $\al_n=(1-\frac1n)\delta_0+\frac1n\delta_1$ and $\al=\delta_0$: $W_1(\al_n,\al)=\frac1n$ but $W_2(\al_n,\al)=\frac1{\sqrt n}$. Both converge, at different speeds. When a paper says “the error decays like $n^{-1/2}$ in $W_2$”, the rate is tied to the choice of $p$.

Pitfall — compactness is needed for the right inequality

On $\R$ (not bounded), take $\al_n=(1-\frac1n)\delta_0+\frac1n\delta_{\sqrt n}$ and $\al=\delta_0$. Then $W_1(\al_n,\al)=\frac1n\sqrt n=\frac1{\sqrt n}\to0$, but $W_2(\al_n,\al)^2=\frac1n(\sqrt n)^2=1$: $W_2$ does not converge. A vanishing mass travelling a growing distance is invisible to $W_1$ but not to $W_2$. We meet this phenomenon again in §4.

Reflexes
  • To compare two transport costs → use, for each side, the coupling that is optimal for the other side, and compare the integrands.
  • When you see a power $s^{r}$ with $r\ge1$ and an average → think Jensen.
  • To show a bound is sharp, or that two distances are not equivalent → move a small mass $\eps$ by a fixed distance.

On $\X=[0,1]$, suppose $W_1(\al_n,\al)\le\frac1n$. What can you say about $W_3(\al_n,\al)$?

Answer

Right inequality with $(p,q)=(1,3)$ and $\diam=1$: $W_3\le W_1^{1/3}\le n^{-1/3}\to0$. Left inequality: $W_3\ge W_1$, which gives nothing useful here. The rate $n^{-1/3}$ is attained by the pair of the worked example with $\eps=\frac1n$.
