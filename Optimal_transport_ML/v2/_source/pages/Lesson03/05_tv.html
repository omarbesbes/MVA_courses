5. The strong topology: total variation notes p. 3–4

Total variation measures how much mass differs, wherever it is: it is the transport cost for the “blind” distance that charges $1$ for any move, so it ignores how far mass travels — Diracs never converge for it, while they do for $W_p$.

Definition and first examples

On the board notes p. 3

Def (strong topology on $\M(\X)$): the total variation (TV) norm of $\M(\X)$ is defined as

$$\norm{\al-\be}_\TV=\sup_{\norm{f}_\infty\le1}\int f\,\dd(\al-\be)\qquad\Big\}\ \text{dual norm of the $L^\infty$ norm on $C(\X)$.}$$

ex: if $\al-\be=f\,\dd x$ then $\norm{\al-\be}_\TV=\norm{f}_{L^1}$; if $\al-\be=\sum a_i\delta_{x_i}$ then $\norm{\al-\be}_\TV=\sum\abs{a_i}$.

$\al-\be$
A signed measure: positive where $\al$ has more mass than $\be$, negative where it has less. Its total mass is $\al(\X)-\be(\X)=0$.
$\sup_{\norm{f}_\infty\le1}$
Look for the test function bounded by $1$ that best separates $\al$ from $\be$. Ideally, $f=+1$ where $\al$ is larger and $f=-1$ where $\be$ is larger: it collects the total amount of mass by which they differ, $\abs{\al-\be}(\X)$. This ideal $f$ usually jumps, so it is not continuous; but continuous functions bounded by $1$ can approach it as closely as we want, so the supremum over $C(\X)$ has the same value (it is approached, not always attained).
$\abs{\al-\be}(\X)$
The total mass of the absolute value of the signed measure $\al-\be$: add the excess of $\al$ over $\be$ (where $\al-\be>0$) and the excess of $\be$ over $\al$ (where $\al-\be<0$). For densities $\int\abs{\rho_\al-\rho_\be}$, for histograms $\sum_i\abs{a_i-b_i}$.
“dual norm”
The TV norm is to measures what the sup norm is to functions: it is the size of $\al-\be$ seen as a linear form $f\mapsto\int f\,\dd(\al-\be)$ on $\big(C(\X),\norm{\cdot}_\infty\big)$.
the two examples
In the first one, $f$ is the density of the signed measure $\al-\be$, not a test function (the board uses the letter $f$ for both); call it $\rho=\rho_\al-\rho_\be$. The ideal test function is the sign of $\rho$, and $\norm{\al-\be}_\TV=\int\abs{\rho}=\int\abs{\rho_\al-\rho_\be}$. In the second, $a_i$ is the weight of $\al-\be$ at $x_i$, and $\norm{\al-\be}_\TV=\sum_i\abs{a_i}$.

For probability measures, $0\le\norm{\al-\be}_\TV\le2$, and the value $2$ is reached exactly when $\al$ and $\be$ live on disjoint sets (no overlap at all).

Worked example

On three points, $a=(0.5,\,0.3,\,0.2)$ and $b=(0.2,\,0.3,\,0.5)$. Then $\norm{a-b}_\TV=0.3+0+0.3=0.6$. The best test function is $f=(+1,\ \text{anything},\ -1)$: $\sum_if_i(a_i-b_i)=0.3+0.3=0.6$.

Total variation is a transport cost

On the board notes p. 3

Prop: write $d$ the 0/1 distance $d(x,y)=\ind_{x\ne y}$. Then $\ W_p(\al,\be)^p=\frac12\norm{\al-\be}_\TV$. $\ \hookrightarrow$ proof as an exercise.

This is Exercise 1 of the sheet Wasserstein distances and duality:

Exercise sheet — Exercise 1 (total variation and 0/1 cost)

Let $d(x,y)=\ind_{x\ne y}$ be the discrete 0/1 distance. We wish to show that for any $p\ge1$, and probability measures $\al$ and $\be$, $W_p^p(\al,\be)=\frac12\norm{\al-\be}_\TV$. Start with two discrete probability measures $\al=\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i}$ and $\be=\sum_ib_i\delta_{x_i}$ on the same finite set $\{x_i\}_i$, with weights $a=(a_i)$ and $b=(b_i)$ on the same support.

Intuition

With the 0/1 distance, moving a unit of mass costs $1$, wherever it goes; leaving it in place costs $0$. So the best plan leaves in place as much mass as possible and moves the rest anywhere it is needed. At the point $x_i$ at most $\min(a_i,b_i)$ can stay. The mass that must move is the excess of $\al$ over $\be$, and $\norm{\al-\be}_\TV$ counts each moved unit twice (once as an excess where it leaves, once as a deficit where it arrives): hence the $\frac12$.

Full solution of Exercise 1
  1. $$d^p=d\quad\text{for every }p\ge1,\qquad\text{so}\qquad W_p^p(\al,\be)=\min_{P\in\U(a,b)}\sum_{i\ne j}P_{ij}=1-\max_{P\in\U(a,b)}\sum_iP_{ii}.$$$d$ only takes the values $0$ and $1$, and $0^p=0$, $1^p=1$. The cost of a plan is the mass it moves, i.e. $1$ minus the mass $\sum_iP_{ii}$ it leaves in place. (This is why the answer does not depend on $p$.)
  2. For every plan, $P_{ii}\le\min(a_i,b_i)$, so $\sum_iP_{ii}\le m:=\sum_i\min(a_i,b_i)$.$P_{ii}$ is one term of row $i$, which sums to $a_i$, and one term of column $i$, which sums to $b_i$ (all terms are $\ge0$).
  3. Let $r=1-m$. If $r=0$ then $a=b$ and $P=\mathrm{diag}(a)$ keeps everything. Otherwise define $$P^\star=\mathrm{diag}\big(\min(a,b)\big)+\frac{\big(a-\min(a,b)\big)\big(b-\min(a,b)\big)\T}{r}.$$Keep the common part in place; send the excess of $a$ to the deficit points of $b$, proportionally. ($\min(a,b)$ is taken entrywise.)
  4. $P^\star$ is a coupling: its entries are $\ge0$, and its row sums are $$\begin{aligned}\textstyle\sum_jP^\star_{ij}&=\min(a_i,b_i)+\big(a_i-\min(a_i,b_i)\big)\frac{\sum_j\big(b_j-\min(a_j,b_j)\big)}{r}\\&=\min(a_i,b_i)+\big(a_i-\min(a_i,b_i)\big)\frac{1-m}{r}=a_i ;\end{aligned}$$ the column sums are $b_j$ in the same way.$\sum_jb_j=1$ and $\sum_j\min(a_j,b_j)=m$.
  5. $P^\star$ keeps exactly $m$ in place: $P^\star_{ii}=\min(a_i,b_i)$.The second term of $P^\star_{ii}$ is $\big(a_i-\min(a_i,b_i)\big)\big(b_i-\min(a_i,b_i)\big)/r=0$: one of the two factors vanishes. So by step 2, $P^\star$ is optimal.
  6. $$\begin{aligned}W_p^p(\al,\be)=1-m&=\sum_i\frac{a_i+b_i}2-\sum_i\min(a_i,b_i)\\&=\frac12\sum_i\big(a_i+b_i-2\min(a_i,b_i)\big)=\frac12\sum_i\abs{a_i-b_i}.\end{aligned}$$$\sum_ia_i=\sum_ib_i=1$, and $u+v-2\min(u,v)=\max(u,v)-\min(u,v)=\abs{u-v}$.
  7. $$W_p^p(\al,\be)=\tfrac12\norm{\al-\be}_\TV.\qquad\square$$The board’s second example of TV: for a discrete difference, $\norm{\al-\be}_\TV=\sum_i\abs{a_i-b_i}$.

On the numbers of the worked example above: $\min(a,b)=(0.2,0.3,0.2)$, $m=0.7$, $r=0.3$; the excess $(0.3,0,0)$ goes to the deficit $(0,0,0.3)$, so $P^\star=\begin{pmatrix}0.2&0&0.3\\0&0.3&0\\0&0&0.2\end{pmatrix}$ and $W_p^p=0.3=\frac12\cdot0.6$. (Checked against an exact LP solver, and on 500 random pairs of histograms.)

General measures. The same proof works with the common part $\al\wedge\be$ in place of $\min(a,b)$: the largest measure that is below both $\al$ and $\be$. For densities it has density $\min(\rho_\al,\rho_\be)$; for histograms, weights $\min(a_i,b_i)$; in general one writes $\al$ and $\be$ with densities with respect to $\mu=\al+\be$ (they exist because $\al\le\mu$ and $\be\le\mu$) and takes the minimum of the two densities. The diagonal of any coupling carries at most $\al\wedge\be$, and the plan “keep $\al\wedge\be$ in place, move the rest by a product coupling” attains it.

Note on the sources: a factor $\frac12$

Peyré–Cuturi, Computational Optimal Transport (Remark 2.26, p. 30) states that with the cost matrix $\ind_{n\times n}-\mathrm{I}_n$, $L_C(a,b)=\norm{a-b}_1$. The correct value is $\frac12\norm{a-b}_1$, as on the board: for $a=(1,0)$ and $b=(0,1)$ the only coupling moves a mass $1$ at cost $1$, while $\norm{a-b}_1=2$. Watch out also for conventions: some books define the “total variation distance” as $\frac12\norm{\al-\be}_\TV$ (values in $[0,1]$); this page always uses the board’s $\norm{\al-\be}_\TV=\abs{\al-\be}(\X)$ (values in $[0,2]$).

Diracs never converge for the strong topology

On the board notes p. 3

Remark: $\norm{\delta_{x_n}-\delta_x}_\TV=2$ (to check) if $x_n\ne x$, whereas $W_p(\delta_{x_n},\delta_x)=d(x_n,x)$.

$\to$ Dirac measures never converge for the strong topology.

Let us check the “2”. If $x_n\ne x$, the difference $\delta_{x_n}-\delta_x$ is a discrete signed measure with weight $+1$ at $x_n$ and $-1$ at $x$, so by the board’s formula $\norm{\delta_{x_n}-\delta_x}_\TV=\abs{+1}+\abs{-1}=2$. With test functions: a continuous $f$ with $\norm{f}_\infty\le1$, $f(x_n)=1$ and $f(x)=-1$ exists (two small bumps of opposite signs), and gives $\int f\,\dd(\delta_{x_n}-\delta_x)=2$, the largest possible value. With the proposition: for the 0/1 distance, $W_p^p(\delta_{x_n},\delta_x)=1$, so $\frac12\norm{\cdot}_\TV=1$. Three ways, same answer. ✓

So however close $x_n$ is to $x$, the total variation stays at its maximum $2$; it drops to $0$ only if $x_n=x$ exactly. Total variation only asks whether mass moved, never how far: it is the transport cost for a ground distance in which all distinct points are at distance $1$. $W_p$ uses the real distance $d$, and sees that $\delta_{x_n}$ is close to $\delta_x$.

Riemann sums: discrete measures converging to a density

On the board notes p. 3, bottom, and p. 4, top

example. $f$ continuous $[0,1]\to[a,b]$:

$$\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^nf\Big(\frac kn\Big)\longrightarrow\int_0^1f(t)\,\dd t\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^n\delta_{k/n}\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}\mathcal U_{[0,1]},\qquad\Big\langle f,\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^n\delta_{k/n}\Big\rangle\to\big\langle f,\mathcal U_{[0,1]}\big\rangle .$$ $$W_p\Big(\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^n\delta_{k/n},\ \mathcal U_{[0,1]}\Big)\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}0.$$
$\ps{f}{\mu}$
Duality bracket: $\ps{f}{\mu}=\int f\,\dd\mu$. So $\ps{f}{\frac1n\sum_k\delta_{k/n}}=\frac1n\sum_kf(k/n)$, a Riemann sum, and $\ps{f}{\mathcal U_{[0,1]}}=\int_0^1f$.
$\mathcal U_{[0,1]}$
The uniform law on $[0,1]$ (density $1$ on $[0,1]$).
the implication
Riemann sums of a continuous function converge to its integral: that is literally the statement that $\mu_n=\frac1n\sum_k\delta_{k/n}$ converges weakly to $\mathcal U_{[0,1]}$. Since $[0,1]$ is compact, §4 then gives $W_p(\mu_n,\mathcal U_{[0,1]})\to0$.

We can do better than “$\to0$”: we can compute the distance. On the line the optimal plan is monotone (refresher): the uniform mass on $\big(\frac{k-1}n,\frac kn\big]$ goes to the atom $\frac kn$.

  1. $$W_p^p(\mu_n,\mathcal U_{[0,1]})=\int_0^1\abs{C_{\mu_n}^{-1}(t)-t}^p\,\dd t,\qquad C_{\mu_n}^{-1}(t)=\frac kn\ \text{ for }t\in\Big(\frac{k-1}n,\frac kn\Big].$$The quantile formula; the quantile function of $\mathcal U_{[0,1]}$ is $t\mapsto t$, that of $\mu_n$ is a staircase.
  2. $$=\sum_{k=1}^n\int_{(k-1)/n}^{k/n}\Big(\frac kn-t\Big)^p\,\dd t=n\int_0^{1/n}u^p\,\dd u=n\cdot\frac{(1/n)^{p+1}}{p+1}=\frac{1}{(p+1)\,n^p}.$$Cut $[0,1]$ into the $n$ steps; on each, substitute $u=\frac kn-t\in[0,\frac1n)$.
  3. $$W_p(\mu_n,\mathcal U_{[0,1]})=\frac{(p+1)^{-1/p}}{n}:\qquad W_1=\frac1{2n},\qquad W_2=\frac1{\sqrt3\,n}.$$Take the $p$-th root. Both decay like $1/n$.

And in total variation? $\mu_n$ lives on $n$ points, a set of length zero, to which $\mathcal U_{[0,1]}$ gives no mass. The two measures live on disjoint sets, so $\norm{\mu_n-\mathcal U_{[0,1]}}_\TV=2$ for every $n$. Discrete measures never converge in total variation to a measure with a density — which is the typical situation in machine learning, where data sets (finite samples) approximate continuous distributions. This is a strong argument for transport distances.

Strong versus weak: three sequences, four behaviours

On a compact space, convergence in total variation implies convergence in $W_p$: keeping the common part in place and moving the rest (at most $\frac12\norm{\al-\be}_\TV$ of mass) over distances at most $\diam(\X)$ gives $W_p^p(\al,\be)\le\diam(\X)^p\cdot\frac12\norm{\al-\be}_\TV$. The converse is false (Diracs, Riemann sums). On a non-compact space even the first implication fails (escaping mass).

On a finite space the two topologies coincide (Exercise 2)

Exercise sheet — Exercise 2 (equivalence of topologies on a finite discrete metric space)

Let $(\X,d)$ be a finite discrete metric space. Define $d_{\min}:=\min_{x\ne y}d(x,y)$ and $d_{\max}:=\max_{x,y}d(x,y)$. Show that for all probability measures $\al,\be$ on $\X$,

$$\frac{d_{\min}}2\norm{\al-\be}_\TV\ \le\ W_1(\al,\be)\ \le\ \frac{d_{\max}}2\norm{\al-\be}_\TV .$$

Conclude that the $W_1$–topology and the total variation topology coincide on $\mathcal P(\X)$.

Full solution of Exercise 2

  2. For every coupling $\pi$: $\ d_{\min}\int\ind_{x\ne y}\,\dd\pi\le\int d\,\dd\pi\le d_{\max}\int\ind_{x\ne y}\,\dd\pi$.Integrate step 1 against $\pi\ge0$.
  3. Lower bound. For every $\pi$, $\int d\,\dd\pi\ge d_{\min}\int\ind_{x\ne y}\,\dd\pi\ge d_{\min}\cdot\frac12\norm{\al-\be}_\TV$. Take the minimum over $\pi$: $W_1(\al,\be)\ge\frac{d_{\min}}2\norm{\al-\be}_\TV$.$\int\ind_{x\ne y}\,\dd\pi$ is at least its minimum over couplings, which is $\frac12\norm{\al-\be}_\TV$ by Exercise 1 with $p=1$.
  4. Upper bound. Let $\pi^\star$ be optimal for the 0/1 cost. Then $W_1(\al,\be)\le\int d\,\dd\pi^\star\le d_{\max}\int\ind_{x\ne y}\,\dd\pi^\star=\frac{d_{\max}}2\norm{\al-\be}_\TV$.$\pi^\star$ is admissible for $W_1$; step 2; Exercise 1.
  5. Conclusion. $W_1$ and $\norm{\cdot}_\TV$ are equivalent up to the constants $\frac{d_{\min}}2$ and $\frac{d_{\max}}2$: $W_1(\al_n,\al)\to0\iff\norm{\al_n-\al}_\TV\to0$. Same convergent sequences, same topology.Each one is bounded by a constant times the other.

Example. $\X=\{0,1,3\}\subset\R$: $d_{\min}=1$, $d_{\max}=3$. For $\delta_0,\delta_1$: $W_1=1=\frac{d_{\min}}2\cdot2$ (equality on the left). For $\delta_0,\delta_3$: $W_1=3=\frac{d_{\max}}2\cdot2$ (equality on the right). For $\delta_1,\delta_3$: $W_1=2$, strictly in between.

Why this does not contradict the Diracs. On $[0,1]$, distinct points can be arbitrarily close: “$d_{\min}$” would be $0$ and the lower bound is empty. That is exactly the room in which $\delta_{1/n}\to\delta_0$ in $W_1$ but not in TV. Finiteness of the space is what makes the two notions agree.

Reflexes
  • When you see $\norm{\al-\be}_\TV$ → think “total mass that differs”; for mutually singular measures (e.g. discrete vs density) it is $2$.
  • When the ground distance is $\ind_{x\ne y}$ → transport = total variation: $W_p^p=\frac12\norm{\cdot}_\TV$, keep the common part, move the rest.
  • To compare two transport costs with comparable ground costs $c_1\le c_2$ → integrate against a good coupling: $\mathcal L_{c_1}\le\mathcal L_{c_2}$.
Pitfall — “strong” is not “better”

Strong convergence (TV) is the more demanding notion, which makes it a worse notion for learning: an empirical distribution never gets close to a continuous one in TV. Weak convergence, measured by $W_p$, is the one under which samples approximate their law.

The densities differ on $[0,a)$ (only the first, value $1$) and on $(1,1+a]$ (only the second, value $1$): $\norm{\cdot}_\TV=\int\abs{\rho_1-\rho_2}=a+a=2a$. The monotone plan is the translation $x\mapsto x+a$, so $W_1=a$. Here both go to $0$ as $a\to0$: shifting a density a little changes it a little in TV — unlike shifting a Dirac.
