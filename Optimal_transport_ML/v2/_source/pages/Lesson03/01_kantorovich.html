1. Recap: the Kantorovich problem notes p. 1

Choose how the random pair $(X,Y)$ is dependent, keeping the laws of $X$ and $Y$ fixed, so that the average cost $\E[c(X,Y)]$ is as small as possible.

On the board notes p. 1, top

$\al,\be\in\M(\X)\times\M(\Y)$,

$$\U(\al,\be)=\big\{\pi\in\M(\X\times\Y)\ ;\ \pf{P_1}{\pi}=\al,\ \ \pf{P_2}{\pi}=\be\big\},\qquad P_1(x,y)=x,\quad P_2(x,y)=y.$$

Kantorovich formulation of OT ($c$ = cost function):

$$\Lc(\al,\be)=\inf_{\pi\in\U(\al,\be)}\int_{\X\times\Y}c(x,y)\,\dd\pi(x,y)=\inf_{X\sim\al,\ Y\sim\be}\E\big[c(X,Y)\big].$$

Brenier: on $\R^d$, $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2$ $\to$ equivalence with Monge.

Decoding the board

$\pi\in\M(\X\times\Y)$
A probability measure on pairs $(x,y)$. Read $\pi(A\times B)$ as “the mass that starts in $A$ and ends in $B$”.
$\pf{P_1}{\pi}=\al$
“The first marginal of $\pi$ is $\al$”: forgetting where the mass goes, you recover where it comes from. Explicitly $\pi(A\times\Y)=\al(A)$ for every set $A$. For matrices: the row sums of $P$ are $a$.
$\pf{P_2}{\pi}=\be$
“The second marginal is $\be$”: $\pi(\X\times B)=\be(B)$. For matrices: the column sums of $P$ are $b$.
$\int c\,\dd\pi$
The total cost of the plan: each unit of mass going from $x$ to $y$ pays $c(x,y)$. For matrices: $\sum_{i,j}P_{ij}\,c(x_i,y_j)=\ps{P}{C}$.
$\inf_{X\sim\al,\,Y\sim\be}\E[c(X,Y)]$
The same problem in the language of random variables. A coupling is the joint law of a random pair $(X,Y)$ whose individual laws are prescribed. The laws of $X$ and of $Y$ are fixed; what you choose is how $X$ and $Y$ depend on each other.
Worked example — all the couplings of a tiny problem

Take $\al=\be=\frac12\delta_0+\frac12\delta_1$ (a fair coin, twice) and $c(x,y)=\abs{x-y}^2$. A coupling is a $2\times2$ matrix with row sums and column sums $\frac12$:

$$P_s=\begin{pmatrix}s&\tfrac12-s\\[2pt] \tfrac12-s&s\end{pmatrix},\qquad 0\le s\le\tfrac12 .$$

Its cost is $\sum_{ij}(P_s)_{ij}\abs{x_i-y_j}^2=2\big(\tfrac12-s\big)=1-2s$ (only the off-diagonal entries move mass, by a distance $1$).

  • $s=\frac14$: the independent coupling ($X$ and $Y$ two independent coins). Cost $\frac12$.
  • $s=0$: $Y=1-X$ (anti-correlated coins). Cost $1$, the worst.
  • $s=\frac12$: $Y=X$ (the same coin read twice). Cost $0$, the best.

So $\Lc(\al,\al)=0$, reached by the coupling “$Y=X$”. Keep this in mind: it is exactly the argument of §2 that $W_p(\al,\al)=0$.

Brenier, in one sentence (why it was recalled)

On $\R^d$ with $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2$, if $\al$ has a density, the optimal coupling does not split mass: it is $\pi=\pf{(\Id,T)}{\al}$ with $T=\nabla\varphi$, $\varphi$ convex (“$Y=T(X)$”). So the relaxed problem of Kantorovich has the same solution as the problem of Monge. It was recalled at the start because the second half of the lecture uses maps: the displacement interpolation is built from $T$ (§6), and the barycenter algorithm averages maps $T_k$ (§9). See the refresher.

Fine print: “inf” is in fact “min”

The board writes $\inf$. Lesson 2 proved that the infimum is attained (the set of couplings is compact for weak convergence, and the cost is continuous or lower semi-continuous). We will use this in §2: “let $\pi$ be an optimal plan”.

What is $\U(\delta_x,\delta_y)$, and what is $\Lc(\delta_x,\delta_y)$?

Answer

All the mass starts at $x$ and all of it must arrive at $y$, so there is a single coupling, $\pi=\delta_{(x,y)}$ (the pair $(X,Y)$ is the constant $(x,y)$). Hence $\Lc(\delta_x,\delta_y)=c(x,y)$. Diracs are the simplest test case of every statement of this page.

What is $\U(\al,\delta_y)$ for an arbitrary $\al$?

Answer

Every unit of mass must go to $y$: the only coupling is $\al\otimes\delta_y$ (the pair $(X,y)$ with $X\sim\al$). Hence $\Lc(\al,\delta_y)=\int c(x,y)\,\dd\al(x)$. We use this in §7 to find the barycenter of Diracs.
