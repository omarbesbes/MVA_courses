2. $W_p$ is a distance notes p. 1–2

Raise a ground distance to a power $p\ge1$, solve the transport problem, take the $p$-th root: the result is a distance between probability measures, and its triangle inequality comes from “gluing” two transport plans end to end.

Why we want a distance

Kantorovich gives a number $\Lc(\al,\be)$ to every pair of distributions. To do more than compare two distributions once — to say that a sequence of distributions converges, to draw the shortest path between two of them, to average several of them — we need this number to behave like a distance. Two observations show that this is not automatic, and what the right construction is.

  • Not every cost works. Take $c(x,y)=\abs{x-y}^2$ on $\R$ and three Diracs. Then $\Lc(\delta_0,\delta_2)=4$, but $\Lc(\delta_0,\delta_1)+\Lc(\delta_1,\delta_2)=1+1=2$: going directly is more expensive than stopping half-way, so the triangle inequality fails.
  • Usual distances between densities are blind to geometry. $\norm{\rho_\al-\rho_\be}_{L^2}$ is not defined for Diracs, and it gives the same value to two bumps that do not overlap, whether they are $1$ or $1000$ apart. A transport distance sees how far the mass has to travel: we will see that $W_p(\delta_x,\delta_y)=d(x,y)$.

The fix found in class: start from a genuine distance $d$, use the cost $c=d^p$ with $p\ge1$, and take the $p$-th root at the end. For the example above, $W_2(\delta_0,\delta_2)=\sqrt4=2\le W_2(\delta_0,\delta_1)+W_2(\delta_1,\delta_2)=1+1$.

The statement

On the board notes p. 1

Metric properties. Proposition ($p\ge1$). Assume $\X=\Y$ is a metric space with a distance $d$, i.e.

$$d(x,y)=d(y,x)\ge0,\qquad d(x,y)=0\iff x=y,\qquad d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z).$$

For 2 measures $\al,\be\in\M(\X)$ define

$$W_p(\al,\be)=\mathcal L_{d^p}(\al,\be)^{1/p}=\Big(\inf_{\pi\in\U(\al,\be)}\int d^p(x,y)\,\dd\pi\Big)^{1/p}.$$

Then $W_p$ is a distance, called the $p$-Wasserstein distance.

Decoding

metric space $(\X,d)$
A set with a distance. The three conditions are: symmetry and positivity; definiteness (distance zero only between a point and itself); triangle inequality (a detour through $y$ never shortens the trip from $x$ to $z$). Examples: $\R^d$ with $\norm{x-y}$; a finite set with any distance matrix; a sphere with the arc length.
$p\ge1$
Written in red on the board: the proof of the triangle inequality uses Minkowski’s inequality, which is false for $p<1$ (see below).
$\mathcal L_{d^p}$
The Kantorovich cost of §1 with $c(x,y)=d(x,y)^p$.
$(\cdots)^{1/p}$
The $p$-th root. It is not decoration: it is what makes the triangle inequality true (example above).
“$W_p$ is a distance”
The same three properties as $d$, but for distributions: $W_p(\al,\be)=W_p(\be,\al)\ge0$; $W_p(\al,\be)=0\iff\al=\be$; $W_p(\al,\ga)\le W_p(\al,\be)+W_p(\be,\ga)$.
Where $W_p$ is finite

On a compact $\X$ (bounded), $d^p$ is bounded, so $W_p<\infty$ for all probability measures: this is the setting of the board, $\M(\X)$. On $\R^d$ one restricts to $\Pc_p(\R^d)$, the measures with $\int\norm{x}^p\dd\al<\infty$; then the independent coupling already has a finite cost, because $\norm{x-y}^p\le2^{p-1}(\norm{x}^p+\norm{y}^p)$. The metric space $(\Pc_p(\R^d),W_p)$ is called the Wasserstein space.

First computations
  • Two Diracs. The only coupling is $\delta_{(x,y)}$ (§1), so $W_p(\delta_x,\delta_y)=\big(d(x,y)^p\big)^{1/p}=d(x,y)$ for every $p$. The ground space sits inside the Wasserstein space without distortion: $x\mapsto\delta_x$ preserves distances.
  • A small amount of mass moved. On $\R$, let $\al=\delta_0$ and $\be=(1-\eps)\delta_0+\eps\delta_1$. The only coupling of $\delta_0$ with $\be$ sends mass $1-\eps$ from $0$ to $0$ (cost $0$) and mass $\eps$ from $0$ to $1$ (cost $1^p$). So $W_p(\al,\be)=\eps^{1/p}$: $W_1=\eps$ but $W_2=\sqrt\eps$. Remember this pair: it shows in §3 that $W_1$ and $W_2$ can be very different numbers.

Symmetry, positivity, and “$W_p=0$ only if $\al=\be$”

On the board notes p. 1

Proof: symmetry, + positiveness derive from sym and posi of $d$.

$$\begin{aligned}W_p(\al,\be)=0\ &\Rightarrow\ \exists\text{ coupling }(X,Y)\text{ of }\pi\text{ s.t. }\E\big(\norm{X-Y}^p\big)=0\ \Rightarrow\ X=Y\ \text{a.s.}\\&\Rightarrow\ X\text{ and }Y\text{ have the same distribution}\ \Rightarrow\ \al=\be.\end{aligned}$$

Positivity. $d^p\ge0$, so every $\int d^p\,\dd\pi$ is $\ge0$, and so is their infimum.

Symmetry. Flip each pair: the map $s(x,y)=(y,x)$ turns a coupling $\pi$ of $(\al,\be)$ into a coupling $\pf{s}{\pi}$ of $(\be,\al)$, and back. It does not change the cost, because $d(y,x)=d(x,y)$. So the two infima are taken over the same set of numbers. (For matrices: write $D_{ij}=d(x_i,y_j)$, an $n\times m$ matrix. Then $P\in\U(a,b)\iff P\T\in\U(b,a)$; the reversed problem, from $\be$ to $\al$, has cost matrix $\big(d(y_j,x_i)^p\big)_{ji}=(D^p)\T$ because $d$ is symmetric; and $\ps{P\T}{(D^p)\T}=\ps{P}{D^p}$.)

Definiteness. One direction is the worked example of §1: if $\al=\be$, take $X\sim\al$ and $Y=X$; this coupling costs $\E[d(X,X)^p]=0$, so $W_p(\al,\al)=0$. The other direction is the chain on the board:

  1. Assume $W_p(\al,\be)=0$ and let $\pi$ be an optimal coupling, so $\int d^p\,\dd\pi=0$.An optimal coupling exists (Lesson 2). This is why the board can say “there exists a coupling with $\E=0$”: without existence we would only have couplings with cost $\to0$.
  2. Let $(X,Y)\sim\pi$. Then $\E\big[d(X,Y)^p\big]=0$.This is the same integral, written with random variables.
  3. So $d(X,Y)=0$ almost surely, i.e. $X=Y$ almost surely.A non-negative random variable with zero mean is zero almost surely; then definiteness of $d$.
  4. For every set $A$: $\al(A)=\P(X\in A)=\P(Y\in A)=\be(A)$. Hence $\al=\be$.If two random variables are almost surely equal they have the same law; and $X\sim\al$, $Y\sim\be$ by definition of a coupling.

The triangle inequality: the idea of “gluing”

We want $W_p(\al,\ga)\le W_p(\al,\be)+W_p(\be,\ga)$. Since $W_p(\al,\ga)$ is a minimum over couplings of $\al$ and $\ga$, it suffices to exhibit one coupling of $\al$ and $\ga$ whose cost is at most the right-hand side. The natural candidate: travel from $\al$ to $\be$ with an optimal plan, then from $\be$ to $\ga$ with another optimal plan. Two questions:

  • With maps it would be easy: if $\pf{T}{\al}=\be$ and $\pf{S}{\be}=\ga$, then $\pf{(S\circ T)}{\al}=\ga$, and pointwise $d\big(x,S(T(x))\big)\le d\big(x,T(x)\big)+d\big(T(x),S(T(x))\big)$.
  • With plans the mass arriving at a middle point $y_j$ comes from several $x_i$, and the mass leaving $y_j$ goes to several $z_k$. We must decide which part of the arriving mass leaves for which destination. The simplest rule: don’t look at where it came from. Each bit of mass at $y_j$ leaves for $z_k$ in the proportion used by the second plan, $\pi_{\be\ga}(j,k)/b_j$.

That rule is the gluing. Probabilistically: build a triple $(X,Y,Z)$ in which $(X,Y)$ follows the first plan, $(Y,Z)$ follows the second, and $Z$ is chosen knowing only $Y$.

The discrete proof, step by step

On the board notes p. 1, bottom

Triangle inequality. Let’s start with the discrete case:

$$\al=\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i},\qquad \be=\sum_jb_j\delta_{y_j},\qquad \ga=\sum_kc_k\delta_{z_k}.$$

Write $\pi_{\al\be}$ and $\pi_{\be\ga}$ the optimal plans between $\al$ and $\be$, and between $\be$ and $\ga$. We construct a 3D tensor $S$ by gluing these 2 plans:

[drawing: $\al\xrightarrow{\ \pi_{\al\be}\ }\be\xrightarrow{\ \pi_{\be\ga}\ }\ga$]

$$S_{ijk}:=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\pi_{\al\be}(i,j)\,\pi_{\be\ga}(j,k)}{b_j}&\text{if }b_j\ne0,\\[6pt] 0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$ $$\sum_iS_{ijk}=\pi_{\be\ga}(j,k)\qquad\Big|\qquad\sum_kS_{ijk}=\pi_{\al\be}(i,j)\qquad\Rightarrow\ \text{the 3 marginals of $S$ are }(a,b,c).$$
On the board notes p. 2, top
$$\text{Define }\ \pi_{\al\ga}:=\sum_{i,k}\Big(\sum_jS_{ijk}\Big)\delta_{(x_i,z_k)}\in\U(\al,\ga).$$ $$\begin{aligned} W_p(\al,\ga)&\le\Big(\sum_{i,k}\pi_{\al\ga}(i,k)\,d(x_i,z_k)^p\Big)^{1/p}\le\Big(\sum_{i,j,k}S_{ijk}\big(d(x_i,y_j)+d(y_j,z_k)\big)^p\Big)^{1/p}\\ &\le\Big(\sum_{ijk}S_{ijk}\,d(x_i,y_j)^p\Big)^{1/p}+\Big(\sum_{ijk}S_{ijk}\,d(y_j,z_k)^p\Big)^{1/p}\\ &\le\underbrace{\Big(\sum_{ij}\pi_{\al\be}(i,j)\,d(x_i,y_j)^p\Big)^{1/p}}_{W_p(\al,\be)}+\underbrace{\Big(\sum_{jk}\pi_{\be\ga}(j,k)\,d(y_j,z_k)^p\Big)^{1/p}}_{W_p(\be,\ga)} \end{aligned}$$

In red: $\norm{a}_{p,S}:=\big(\sum S_{ijk}\,a_{ijk}^p\big)^{1/p}$.

Decoding $S$: mass that travels along a path

Read $S_{ijk}$ as the mass that goes $x_i\to y_j\to z_k$. The formula is a product of two readable factors:

$$S_{ijk}=\underbrace{\pi_{\al\be}(i,j)}_{\text{mass from }x_i\text{ arriving at }y_j}\times\underbrace{\frac{\pi_{\be\ga}(j,k)}{b_j}}_{\text{fraction of the mass at }y_j\text{ that leaves for }z_k}.$$

The second factor is a proportion: the plan $\pi_{\be\ga}$ sends a total $b_j$ out of $y_j$ (its row sums are $b$), of which $\pi_{\be\ga}(j,k)$ goes to $z_k$. The rule applies the same proportions to the mass coming from every $x_i$ — the “don’t look where it came from” rule. When $b_j=0$, no mass passes through $y_j$, and $S_{ijk}=0$.

Step 1 — the three marginals of $S$

  1. $$\sum_kS_{ijk}=\frac{\pi_{\al\be}(i,j)}{b_j}\sum_k\pi_{\be\ga}(j,k)=\frac{\pi_{\al\be}(i,j)}{b_j}\,b_j=\pi_{\al\be}(i,j)\qquad(b_j\ne0).$$Row $j$ of $\pi_{\be\ga}$ sums to $b_j$ (first marginal of $\pi_{\be\ga}$ is $\be$). Interpretation: all the mass from $x_i$ arriving at $y_j$ leaves $y_j$ somewhere.
  2. If $b_j=0$, both sides are $0$.Column $j$ of $\pi_{\al\be}$ has non-negative entries summing to $b_j=0$, so $\pi_{\al\be}(i,j)=0$ for all $i$. (This is why dividing by $b_j$ is harmless.)
  3. $$\sum_iS_{ijk}=\frac{\pi_{\be\ga}(j,k)}{b_j}\sum_i\pi_{\al\be}(i,j)=\frac{\pi_{\be\ga}(j,k)}{b_j}\,b_j=\pi_{\be\ga}(j,k).$$Column $j$ of $\pi_{\al\be}$ sums to $b_j$ (second marginal of $\pi_{\al\be}$ is $\be$).
  4. $$\sum_{j,k}S_{ijk}=\sum_j\pi_{\al\be}(i,j)=a_i,\qquad\sum_{i,k}S_{ijk}=b_j,\qquad\sum_{i,j}S_{ijk}=\sum_j\pi_{\be\ga}(j,k)=c_k.$$Sum the identities of lines 1–3 once more. So the three “one-index marginals” of $S$ are $a$, $b$, $c$: $S$ is a probability on triples $(x_i,y_j,z_k)$ whose pair-marginals are the two plans.

Step 2 — a coupling of $\al$ and $\ga$

Forget the middle point: $\pi_{\al\ga}(i,k)=\sum_jS_{ijk}$ is the mass that goes from $x_i$ to $z_k$ (through any $y_j$). Its row sums are $\sum_{j,k}S_{ijk}=a_i$ and its column sums are $\sum_{i,j}S_{ijk}=c_k$ (Step 1, line 4), so $\pi_{\al\ga}\in\U(\al,\ga)$. In matrix form, $\pi_{\al\ga}=\pi_{\al\be}\,\mathrm{diag}(1/b)\,\pi_{\be\ga}$ (with $1/b_j$ replaced by $0$ when $b_j=0$).

Step 3 — the chain of inequalities, line by line

  1. $$W_p(\al,\ga)\le\Big(\sum_{i,k}\pi_{\al\ga}(i,k)\,d(x_i,z_k)^p\Big)^{1/p}$$$W_p^p$ is the minimum of the cost over couplings, and $\pi_{\al\ga}$ is one coupling (Step 2). Any coupling gives an upper bound.
  2. $$=\Big(\sum_{i,j,k}S_{ijk}\,d(x_i,z_k)^p\Big)^{1/p}$$Replace $\pi_{\al\ga}(i,k)$ by its definition $\sum_jS_{ijk}$ (the summand does not depend on $j$).
  3. $$\le\Big(\sum_{i,j,k}S_{ijk}\big(d(x_i,y_j)+d(y_j,z_k)\big)^p\Big)^{1/p}$$Triangle inequality of $d$: $d(x_i,z_k)\le d(x_i,y_j)+d(y_j,z_k)$; and $t\mapsto t^p$ is increasing on $[0,\infty)$; and $S_{ijk}\ge0$.
  4. $$\le\Big(\sum_{ijk}S_{ijk}\,d(x_i,y_j)^p\Big)^{1/p}+\Big(\sum_{ijk}S_{ijk}\,d(y_j,z_k)^p\Big)^{1/p}$$Minkowski’s inequality $\norm{u+v}_{p,S}\le\norm{u}_{p,S}+\norm{v}_{p,S}$ for the weighted norm written in red on the board, with $u_{ijk}=d(x_i,y_j)$ and $v_{ijk}=d(y_j,z_k)$. It needs $p\ge1$ (refresher below).
  5. $$=\Big(\sum_{ij}\pi_{\al\be}(i,j)\,d(x_i,y_j)^p\Big)^{1/p}+\Big(\sum_{jk}\pi_{\be\ga}(j,k)\,d(y_j,z_k)^p\Big)^{1/p}$$In the first sum the summand does not depend on $k$: sum over $k$ first and use $\sum_kS_{ijk}=\pi_{\al\be}(i,j)$. In the second, sum over $i$ first and use $\sum_iS_{ijk}=\pi_{\be\ga}(j,k)$ (Step 1).
  6. $$=W_p(\al,\be)+W_p(\be,\ga).$$The plans $\pi_{\al\be}$ and $\pi_{\be\ga}$ were chosen optimal.
Refresher — Minkowski’s inequality, and why $p\ge1$

Let $w_m\ge0$ be weights (here $m=(i,j,k)$ and $w=S$). For $p\ge1$, the quantity $\norm{u}_{p,w}=\big(\sum_mw_m\abs{u_m}^p\big)^{1/p}$ is a (semi-)norm: it is the $\ell^p$ norm of the vector $(w_m^{1/p}u_m)_m$. In particular it satisfies the triangle inequality, which is Minkowski’s inequality:

$$\Big(\sum_mw_m\abs{u_m+v_m}^p\Big)^{1/p}\le\Big(\sum_mw_m\abs{u_m}^p\Big)^{1/p}+\Big(\sum_mw_m\abs{v_m}^p\Big)^{1/p}.$$

The same holds for random variables: $\E[\abs{U+V}^p]^{1/p}\le\E[\abs{U}^p]^{1/p}+\E[\abs{V}^p]^{1/p}$ (the triangle inequality of the $L^p$ norm). It is false for $p<1$: with $p=\frac12$, $w=(1,1)$, $u=(1,0)$, $v=(0,1)$, the left side is $(1+1)^2=4$ and the right side is $1+1=2$. That is why the board writes $p\ge1$ in red.

Worked example — gluing by hand

On the line, take

$$\al=\tfrac12\delta_0+\tfrac12\delta_2,\qquad\be=\tfrac14\delta_0+\tfrac12\delta_1+\tfrac14\delta_2,\qquad\ga=\tfrac12\delta_1+\tfrac12\delta_3 .$$

The general case: gluing with random variables

On the board notes p. 2

General case: triangle inequality. $(\al,\be,\ga)$: $(X,Y)$ optimal for $\mathrm{OT}(\al,\be)$,

$$\pi_{\al\be}=\P(X,Y)=\P_Y\times\P_{X|Y},\qquad\pi_{\be\ga}=\P(\tilde Y,Z)=\P_{\tilde Y}\times\P_{Z|\tilde Y},$$ $$\pi_{\al\be\ga}=\P_{X|Y}\ \P_{Z|\tilde Y}\ \P_Y .$$
$\P(X,Y)$
The joint law of the pair $(X,Y)$; here it is the optimal plan $\pi_{\al\be}$.
$\P_Y$
The law of $Y$ alone, i.e. $\be$.
$\P_{X|Y}$
The conditional law of $X$ given $Y$: “if you know that $Y=y$, where did this mass come from?”. In the discrete case, $\P(X=x_i\mid Y=y_j)=\pi_{\al\be}(i,j)/b_j$.
$\P_Y\times\P_{X|Y}$
The chain rule: a joint law is “first draw $Y$, then draw $X$ given $Y$”.
$(\tilde Y,Z)$
A pair following the second optimal plan $\pi_{\be\ga}$. Since $\tilde Y$ and $Y$ both have law $\be$, the gluing identifies them.
$\pi_{\al\be\ga}$
The glued law of a triple: draw $Y\sim\be$; then, given $Y=y$, draw $X$ from $\P_{X|Y=y}$ and $Z$ from $\P_{Z|\tilde Y=y}$, independently of each other. “$X$ and $Z$ are conditionally independent given $Y$.”

In the discrete case this recipe gives back exactly $S$: $\ \P(X=x_i,Y=y_j,Z=z_k)=b_j\cdot\frac{\pi_{\al\be}(i,j)}{b_j}\cdot\frac{\pi_{\be\ga}(j,k)}{b_j}=S_{ijk}$. The general proof is then the discrete one, written with expectations:

  1. $$W_p(\al,\ga)\le\E\big[d(X,Z)^p\big]^{1/p}$$The law of $(X,Z)$ is a coupling of $\al$ and $\ga$: $X\sim\al$ because $(X,Y)\sim\pi_{\al\be}$, and $Z\sim\ga$ because $(Y,Z)\sim\pi_{\be\ga}$.
  2. $$\le\E\big[\big(d(X,Y)+d(Y,Z)\big)^p\big]^{1/p}$$Triangle inequality of $d$, pointwise, and $t\mapsto t^p$ increasing.
  3. $$\le\E\big[d(X,Y)^p\big]^{1/p}+\E\big[d(Y,Z)^p\big]^{1/p}$$Minkowski’s inequality in $L^p$ (the triangle inequality of the norm $\E[\abs{U}^p]^{1/p}$), valid for $p\ge1$.
  4. $$=W_p(\al,\be)+W_p(\be,\ga).$$$(X,Y)$ follows the optimal plan $\pi_{\al\be}$, and $(Y,Z)$ follows the optimal plan $\pi_{\be\ga}$.
Fine print: the gluing lemma

For measures that are not discrete, the conditional laws $\P_{X|Y=y}$ must exist as a well-behaved family indexed by $y$ (a “disintegration”). This is true on all the spaces of this course ($\R^d$, compact metric spaces, and more generally Polish spaces). The statement “given two couplings with a common marginal, there is a law on triples with these two pair-marginals” is called the gluing lemma (Villani, Optimal transport, old and new, ch. 1).

Intuition — gluing is composition of plans

When both plans are maps, $Y=T(X)$ and $Z=S(Y)$, the conditional laws are Diracs and the glued triple is $(X,T(X),S(T(X)))$: gluing is composition $S\circ T$. For general plans it is the best one can do without remembering origins. The same trick proves the triangle inequality of many other transport distances.

Pitfall — forgetting the root, or taking $p<1$

$W_2^2$ is not a distance: $W_2^2(\delta_0,\delta_2)=4>W_2^2(\delta_0,\delta_1)+W_2^2(\delta_1,\delta_2)=2$. Always take the $p$-th root before using the triangle inequality. (For $0without root that satisfies the triangle inequality.)

Pitfall — the glued plan is not the optimal plan

The glued plan $\pi_{\al\ga}$ is only admissible. In the worked example it costs $\sqrt2$ while $W_2(\al,\ga)=1$. Do not try to compute $W_p(\al,\ga)$ by gluing.

In machine learning — comparing documents with the word mover’s distance

Represent a document by the histogram of its words, each word placed at its embedding vector (word2vec, GloVe…): $\al_{\rm doc}=\sum_wa_w\delta_{e_w}$. The word mover’s distance is $W_1$ between two such measures (OT4ML Example 4.11, p. 78; Kusner et al., 2015). “Obama speaks to the media in Illinois” and “The President greets the press in Chicago” share no word, yet they are close: every word has a nearby partner (Obama → President, media → press, Illinois → Chicago). For $k$-nearest-neighbour classification of documents, you need many distances; two facts from this section make it affordable. Since $W_1$ is a distance, the usual tricks of metric search apply. And $\norm{m_\al-m_\be}\le W_1(\al,\be)$, where $m_\al,m_\be$ are the means (the argument of Exercise 2 of “More exercises”: $\norm{\E[X-Y]}\le\E\norm{X-Y}$ for every coupling). Kusner et al. call this cheap lower bound the word centroid distance and use it to rank the candidates before any linear program is solved; a second, tighter lower bound then lets them skip the exact computation for most documents.

Reflexes
  • To prove an upper bound on $W_p(\al,\ga)$ → exhibit one coupling of $\al$ and $\ga$ and bound its cost.
  • To build a coupling of $\al$ and $\ga$ from couplings through $\be$ → glue: same middle point, conditionally independent ends.
  • To test any claim about $W_p$ → try Diracs first: $W_p(\delta_x,\delta_y)=d(x,y)$.

Where exactly did the proof use $p\ge1$, and where did it use that $\pi_{\al\be}$ and $\pi_{\be\ga}$ are optimal?

Answer

$p\ge1$: only in Minkowski’s inequality (line 4 of the chain in Step 3, and line 3 of the general proof). Optimality: only in the very last line, to recognise $W_p(\al,\be)$ and $W_p(\be,\ga)$. With non-optimal plans you get $W_p(\al,\ga)\le(\text{cost of }\pi_{\al\be})^{1/p}+(\text{cost of }\pi_{\be\ga})^{1/p}$, which is also true but weaker.

Compute $W_p\big((1-s)\delta_0+s\delta_1,\ (1-t)\delta_0+t\delta_1\big)$ on $\R$ for $s\le t$, and check the triangle inequality with a third value $r$ in between.

Answer

The common mass stays in place; the excess $t-s$ of the second measure at $1$ must come from $0$, at distance $1$. So $W_p=(t-s)^{1/p}$. For $s\le r\le t$: $(t-s)^{1/p}\le(r-s)^{1/p}+(t-r)^{1/p}$ holds because $u\mapsto u^{1/p}$ is concave and vanishes at $0$, hence subadditive. For $p=1$ it is an equality.
