5. Sinkhorn as alternating KL projections OT4ML §9.1, pp. 172–175

The entropic plan is the KL projection of the tilted Gibbs kernel $(a\otimes b)\odot e^{-C/\eps}$ onto the couplings; a row step is the KL projection onto “rows sum to $a$”, a column step onto “columns sum to $b$”; and alternating projections onto two affine sets converge to the projection onto their intersection.

Why a geometric point of view

§4 presented Sinkhorn as a trick that happens to work. This section explains why it works and what it converges to, with a picture you already know from Euclidean geometry: to reach the intersection of two lines, project alternately on each of them. The distance used here is not Euclidean but the KL divergence; with it, the projections are exactly the row and column rescalings.

Reminder — Bregman divergences

For a differentiable strictly convex function $\Phi$, the Bregman divergence $B_\Phi(P|Q)=\Phi(P)-\Phi(Q)-\ps{\nabla\Phi(Q)}{P-Q}$ is the gap between $\Phi$ and its tangent at $Q$: it is $\ge0$, and $0$ only at $P=Q$, but it is not symmetric. Two examples: $\Phi=\frac12\norm{\cdot}^2$ gives $\frac12\norm{P-Q}^2$; the negative entropy $\Phi(P)=\sum P_{ij}\log P_{ij}$ gives $\KL(P|Q)$ (the $-P+Q$ terms come from the tangent). Definition 9.1 (Bregman divergence). Let $\Omega\subset\R^{n\times m}$ be convex with nonempty interior, and let $\Phi:\R^{n\times m}\to\R\cup\{+\infty\}$ be a proper lower-semicontinuous Legendre function with $\mathrm{dom}(\Phi)=\Omega$. […] For $P\in\Omega$ and $Q\in\mathrm{int}(\Omega)$, the Bregman divergence generated by $\Phi$ is $\ B_\Phi(P|Q):=\Phi(P)-\Phi(Q)-\ps{\nabla\Phi(Q)}{P-Q}$.

[…] For the negative entropy $\Phi(P)=\sum_{i,j}P_{i,j}\log P_{i,j}$ on the nonnegative orthant, extended by $+\infty$ outside it, one obtains $B_\Phi(P|Q)=\KL(P|Q)$.

Definition 9.2 (Bregman projection). For $Q\in\mathrm{int}(\Omega)$ and a nonempty convex set $\mathcal C$, define $\ \mathrm{Proj}^{B_\Phi}_{\mathcal C}(Q):=\argmin_{P\in\mathcal C\cap\Omega}B_\Phi(P|Q)$.

Legendre function
Strictly convex and differentiable inside its domain, with a gradient that blows up at the boundary. For the negative entropy the boundary is “some entry is $0$”, where the derivative $\log P_{ij}+1$ is $-\infty$: this is why projections never touch the boundary (the barrier of §1).
$\mathrm{Proj}^{\KL}_{\mathcal C}(Q)$
The point of $\mathcal C$ closest to $Q$ in KL, measured as $\KL(P|Q)$ with the unknown $P$ in the first argument.

The entropic plan is a KL projection

In the course OT4ML (8.9), p. 143 · Prop. 9.3 and (9.1), p. 173

For every $P\in\U(a,b)$,

$$\ps{P}{C}+\eps\KL(P|a\otimes b)=\eps\KL\big(P\,\big|\,(a\otimes b)\odot e^{-C/\eps}\big)+\eps\Big(1-\sum_{i,j}a_ib_je^{-C_{i,j}/\eps}\Big).\qquad(8.9)$$

Consequently, entropic OT is the generalized-KL projection of the tilted Gibbs reference $(a\otimes b)\odot e^{-C/\eps}$ onto the transport polytope. […] Thus the unique solution $P_\eps$ of (8.1) is the KL projection of the tilted Gibbs reference onto $\U(a,b)$:

$$P_\eps=\mathrm{Proj}^{\KL}_{\U(a,b)}(K^\eps_{a,b}):=\argmin_{P\in\U(a,b)}\KL(P|K^\eps_{a,b}).\qquad(9.1)$$
$K^\eps_{a,b}=(a\otimes b)\odot e^{-C/\eps}$
The tilted Gibbs reference: the independent coupling, reweighted entry by entry by the Gibbs kernel. It is not a coupling (its mass is $\sum_{ij}a_ib_je^{-C_{ij}/\eps}<1$ when $C\ge0$).
(8.9)
Adding the linear cost $\ps{P}{C}$ to $\eps\KL(P|a\otimes b)$ just moves the reference point of the KL: “cost + entropy” = “distance to a tilted reference”.
Proof of (8.9)

Write $Q=K^\eps_{a,b}$, so $\log Q_{ij}=\log(a_ib_j)-C_{ij}/\eps$, and take $P\in\U(a,b)$ (mass $1$).

  1. $$\eps\KL(P|Q)=\eps\sum_{ij}P_{ij}\log\frac{P_{ij}}{a_ib_j}+\sum_{ij}P_{ij}C_{ij}-\eps\sum_{ij}P_{ij}+\eps\sum_{ij}Q_{ij}.$$Definition of KL with $\log\frac{P_{ij}}{Q_{ij}}=\log\frac{P_{ij}}{a_ib_j}+\frac{C_{ij}}{\eps}$.
  2. $$=\eps\KL(P|a\otimes b)+\ps{P}{C}-\eps+\eps\sum_{ij}a_ib_je^{-C_{ij}/\eps}.$$On couplings, $\KL(P|a\otimes b)=\sum P\log\frac{P}{a\otimes b}$ (the masses cancel) and $\sum_{ij}P_{ij}=1$.

Rearrange. Since the last term does not depend on $P$, minimizing the entropic objective over $\U(a,b)$ is minimizing $\KL(\cdot|K^\eps_{a,b})$ over $\U(a,b)$: this is (9.1). $\square$

Check on the running example at $\eps=0.5$: $K^\eps_{a,b}=\begin{pmatrix}0.15&0.0203\\0.047367&0.35\end{pmatrix}$ (mass $0.567668$), and both sides of (8.9) at $P_\eps$ equal $0.330739$.

KL projections onto one marginal constraint are scalings

In the course OT4ML Prop. 9.5, (9.2)–(9.3), pp. 174–175 · compact Prop. 9.5, p. 46

Denote the two affine marginal sets by $\ \mathcal C^1_a:=\{P\in\R^{n\times m}\,;\,P\ind_m=a\}$ and $\mathcal C^2_b:=\{P\in\R^{n\times m}\,;\,P\T\ind_n=b\}$. […] The two half-steps of Algorithm 9.1 specialize to

$$P^{(\ell+1)}:=\mathrm{Proj}^{\KL}_{\mathcal C^1_a}(P^{(\ell)})\qquad\text{and}\qquad P^{(\ell+2)}:=\mathrm{Proj}^{\KL}_{\mathcal C^2_b}(P^{(\ell+1)}).\qquad(9.2)$$

Proposition 9.5 (KL projections are scalings). […] In particular, when $P>0$ and $a,b>0$, these formulas reduce to

$$\mathrm{Proj}^{\KL}_{\mathcal C^1_a}(P)=\operatorname{diag}\Big(\frac{a}{P\ind_m}\Big)P,\qquad\mathrm{Proj}^{\KL}_{\mathcal C^2_b}(P)=P\operatorname{diag}\Big(\frac{b}{P\T\ind_n}\Big).$$

[…] These iterations are equivalent to Sinkhorn iterations (8.5).

$\operatorname{diag}(a/P\ind_m)P$
Multiply row $i$ of $P$ by $a_i/r_i$, where $r_i$ is its current sum: exactly the row step of Sinkhorn.
(9.2)
One index per half-step: $P^{(\ell)}\to P^{(\ell+1)}$ is a row projection and $P^{(\ell+1)}\to P^{(\ell+2)}$ a column projection (read it for even $\ell$). Algorithm 9.1 of the book writes the same with half-integer indices $P^{(\ell-\frac12)}$, $P^{(\ell)}$.
Proof for positive matrices

The constraint $P\ind_m=a$ acts on each row separately, and so does $\KL(\cdot|Q)=\sum_i\sum_j(\dots)$. So it suffices to solve, for one row $q>0$ with sum $s=\sum_jq_j$ and a target mass $\mu=a_i>0$:

  1. $$\min_{p\ge0,\ \sum_jp_j=\mu}\ \sum_j\Big(p_j\log\frac{p_j}{q_j}-p_j+q_j\Big).$$The row-$i$ part of $\KL(P|Q)$ and of the constraint.
  2. $$\log\frac{p_j}{q_j}=\lambda\quad\text{for all }j,\ \text{i.e.}\ p_j=e^\lambda q_j .$$Lagrange condition: the derivative of the objective in $p_j$ is $\log(p_j/q_j)$, and the constraint has gradient $(1,\dots,1)$. The minimizer is positive (infinite slope of $t\log t$ at $0$, as in §1), so $p\ge0$ is inactive.
  3. $$e^\lambda=\frac{\mu}{s},\qquad p=\frac{a_i}{s}\,q .$$The constraint $\sum_jp_j=\mu$. The objective is strictly convex and the constraint affine, so this stationary point is the unique minimizer.

Columns: the same with the transpose. $\square$

Worked example — the first projection, and an exact Pythagoras

Start from $P^{(0)}=K^{0.5}_{a,b}=\begin{pmatrix}0.15&0.0203\\0.047367&0.35\end{pmatrix}$, whose row sums are $(0.1703,\ 0.397367)$. The row projection multiplies both rows by $0.3/0.1703=0.7/0.397367=1.7616$:

$$P^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}0.264239&0.035761\\0.083442&0.616558\end{pmatrix},$$

the same matrix as $\operatorname{diag}(u^{(1)})K$ in §4 (the factors $a_ib_j$ of the reference are absorbed by the scalings). Now take any matrix of $\mathcal C^1_a$, for instance $Q=\begin{pmatrix}0.2&0.1\\0.3&0.4\end{pmatrix}$ (rows $0.3$, $0.7$). Then

$$\KL(Q|P^{(0)})=0.391825=\underbrace{0.257938}_{\KL(Q|P^{(1)})}+\underbrace{0.133887}_{\KL(P^{(1)}|P^{(0)})}.$$

An exact Pythagorean identity: the projection is “orthogonal” for KL.

Alternating projections converge to the projection

In the course OT4ML Prop. 9.4, p. 174 · compact Prop. 9.4, p. 46

Proposition 9.4 (Convergence of cyclic Bregman projections). Let $\Phi$ be a Legendre generator on a finite-dimensional convex domain, and let $\mathcal C_1,\mathcal C_2$ be closed convex sets whose intersection meets the interior of the domain. Generate $(P^{(\ell-1/2)},P^{(\ell)})$ by Algorithm 9.1 from an interior point $P^{(0)}$. Assume that the projections are well-defined and that the full half-step sequence remains in a compact subset of the interior of the domain. Then there exists $\bar P\in\mathcal C_1\cap\mathcal C_2$ such that $P^{(\ell)}\to\bar P$ and $P^{(\ell-1/2)}\to\bar P$. If, in addition, $\mathcal C_1$ and $\mathcal C_2$ are affine, then $\ \bar P=\mathrm{Proj}^{B_\Phi}_{\mathcal C_1\cap\mathcal C_2}(P^{(0)})$.

Algorithm 9.1
Cyclic projections: $P^{(\ell-\frac12)}=\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathcal C_1}(P^{(\ell-1)})$, $P^{(\ell)}=\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathcal C_2}(P^{(\ell-\frac12)})$.
“affine”
Defined by linear equalities, like the two marginal constraints. For affine sets the limit is not just some point of the intersection, it is the closest one to the starting point. With $P^{(0)}=K^\eps_{a,b}$ and (9.1), the closest one is $P_\eps$: Sinkhorn converges to the entropic plan.
Idea of the proof (for KL and the two marginal sets)

Pythagoras: for every $Q$ in the set onto which one projects, $\KL(Q|P)=\KL(Q|P^+)+\KL(P^+|P)$. Take $Q=P_\eps$, which lies in both sets: at every half-step, the KL distance to the solution decreases exactly by the size of the step $\KL(P^+|P)$, which equals the marginal defect being repaired. The decreases add up to at most the initial distance, so the defects tend to $0$; the iterates keep the cross-ratios of $K$, so their limit points are of the scaling form with the right marginals, i.e. equal to $P_\eps$.

Full proof that Sinkhorn converges to $P_\eps$ (KL case)

Let $P^{(k)}$ be the half-step iterates, starting from $P^{(0)}=K$ or $K^\eps_{a,b}$, odd $k$ being row projections and even $k\ge2$ column projections. Write $r^{(k)}=P^{(k)}\ind_m$, $s^{(k)}=(P^{(k)})\T\ind_n$.

  1. If $P^+=\operatorname{diag}(a/r)P$ with $r=P\ind_m$, then for every $Q\in\mathcal C^1_a$: $$\begin{aligned}\KL(Q|P)-\KL(Q|P^+)&=\sum_{ij}Q_{ij}\log\frac{a_i}{r_i}+\sum_{ij}(P_{ij}-P^+_{ij})\\&=\sum_ia_i\log\frac{a_i}{r_i}-\sum_ia_i+\sum_ir_i=\KL(a|r).\end{aligned}$$$\log\frac{P^+_{ij}}{P_{ij}}=\log\frac{a_i}{r_i}$; $\sum_jQ_{ij}=a_i$ because $Q\in\mathcal C^1_a$; $\sum_jP^+_{ij}=a_i$ and $\sum_jP_{ij}=r_i$.
  2. $$\KL(P^+|P)=\sum_{ij}P^+_{ij}\log\frac{a_i}{r_i}-\sum_ia_i+\sum_ir_i=\KL(a|r).$$Same computation with $Q=P^+$, which belongs to $\mathcal C^1_a$. Steps 1–2 give Pythagoras: $\KL(Q|P)=\KL(Q|P^+)+\KL(P^+|P)$. The column step is symmetric, with $\KL(b|s)$.
  3. $$\begin{aligned}&\KL(P_\eps|P^{(k)})=\KL(P_\eps|P^{(k+1)})+\KL(P^{(k+1)}|P^{(k)}),\\&\text{so}\quad\sum_{k\ge0}\KL(P^{(k+1)}|P^{(k)})\le\KL(P_\eps|P^{(0)})<\infty .\end{aligned}$$$P_\eps$ belongs to both $\mathcal C^1_a$ and $\mathcal C^2_b$; telescope, and use $\KL\ge0$.
  4. $\KL(a|r^{(k)})\to0$ along even $k$ and $\KL(b|s^{(k)})\to0$ along odd $k$; hence $r^{(k)}\to a$ and $s^{(k)}\to b$ along the whole sequence of full cycles.The terms of a convergent series tend to $0$; by steps 1–2 they are the marginal defects. Pinsker’s inequality turns KL into $\ell^1$ (the vectors have mass $1$ after the first projection).
  5. The iterates stay in a compact set of positive matrices.After the first projection they have mass $1$, so entries are in $[0,1]$. If an entry $P^{(k)}_{ij}$ tended to $0$ along a subsequence, the term $P_{\eps,ij}\log(P_{\eps,ij}/P^{(k)}_{ij})$ would tend to $+\infty$ ($P_\eps>0$), while every term of $\KL(P_\eps|P^{(k)})$ is $\ge0$; this contradicts $\KL(P_\eps|P^{(k)})\le\KL(P_\eps|P^{(0)})$ (step 3).
  6. Every limit point $\bar P$ of the full-cycle iterates is positive, has marginals $a$ and $b$, and satisfies $\frac{\bar P_{ij}\bar P_{kl}}{\bar P_{il}\bar P_{kj}}=\frac{K_{ij}K_{kl}}{K_{il}K_{kj}}$.Steps 4–5; each $P^{(k)}$ is of the form $\operatorname{diag}(u)K\operatorname{diag}(v)$, whose cross-ratios are those of $K$ (quiz of §2), and cross-ratios are continuous on positive matrices.
  7. $\bar P=P_\eps$, hence $P^{(k)}\to P_\eps$.A positive matrix with the cross-ratios of $K$ is of the form $\operatorname{diag}(u)K\operatorname{diag}(v)$: take $u_i=\bar P_{i1}/K_{i1}$ and $v_j=\bar P_{1j}/(u_1K_{1j})$, and check $\bar P_{ij}=u_iK_{ij}v_j$ with the cross-ratio of $(i,j),(1,1)$. With the right marginals, it is $P_\eps$ (§2, uniqueness). A sequence in a compact set with a single limit point converges. $\square$
Worked example — the distance to the solution decreases by the defect

Along the half-steps of the running example, $\KL(P_\eps|P^{(k)})$ takes the values

$$0.095258,\ \ 0.046560,\ \ 0.021765,\ \ 0.009307,\ \ 0.003824,\ \ 0.001491\qquad(k=1,\dots,6).$$

The first decrease, $0.095258-0.046560=0.048698$, is the marginal defect $\KL(b|s^{(1)})$ of $P^{(1)}$, whose column sums are $s^{(1)}=(0.347681,\ 0.652319)$; the second, $0.024794$, is the row defect $\KL(a|r^{(2)})$ with $r^{(2)}=(0.407413,\ 0.592587)$; and so on, exactly (step 3 of the proof).

Why KL, and not the Euclidean distance?

With $\Phi=\frac12\norm{\cdot}^2$, the projection onto “rows sum to $a$” must also keep $P\ge0$; it becomes a projection onto a simplex in each row (a thresholding $(Q_{ij}-\tau_i)_+$), with no closed form for $\tau_i$, and since the sets “rows sum to $a$ and $P\ge0$” are not affine, the cyclic projections converge to some coupling, not in general to the closest one (OT4ML pp. 175–176). The negative entropy enforces positivity for free and keeps the constraint sets affine: this is the special property of KL that makes Sinkhorn so simple.

In machine learning — Wasserstein barycenters by iterative Bregman projections

The barycenter of histograms $b_1,\dots,b_S$ on a common grid (Lesson 3 solved its unregularized version as a linear program) is $\min_a\sum_s\lambda_s\mathrm L^\eps_C(a,b_s)$, with the penalty $-\eps\mathrm H(P_s)$ of (8.1) (CompOT (9.15), p. 148; the KL penalty of this page would add the term $\eps\mathrm H(a)$, which changes the minimizer). It is again a KL projection, now onto the set of $S$-tuples of plans $(P_s)_s$ with $P_s\T\ind=b_s$ and a common first marginal $P_1\ind=\dots=P_S\ind$. Alternating the KL projections gives Sinkhorn-like updates on scalings $(u_s,v_s)$: $v_s\leftarrow b_s/(K\T u_s)$, then the barycenter is the weighted geometric mean $a=\prod_s(Kv_s)^{\lambda_s}$ and $u_s\leftarrow a/(Kv_s)$ (CompOT (9.17)–(9.20), pp. 148–149; Benamou, Carlier, Cuturi, Nenna and Peyré, SIAM J. Sci. Comput. 2015). On images or 3D grids, $K$ is a Gaussian blur, so each product $Kv$ is a convolution (Solomon et al., ACM Trans. Graph. 2015): averaging shapes, interpolating textures or colour histograms all reduce to repeated blurs and entrywise products. (The first fast algorithms for Wasserstein barycenters, by Cuturi and Doucet, ICML 2014, also relied on entropic smoothing.)

Reflexes
  • Entropic OT = KL projection of $(a\otimes b)\odot e^{-C/\eps}$ onto $\U(a,b)$; a Sinkhorn half-step = KL projection onto one marginal constraint.
  • Affine constraints + Bregman projection → Pythagoras with equality → the limit is the projection of the starting point.
  • Decrease of $\KL(P_\eps|P^{(k)})$ at a half-step = KL of the marginal being repaired.

Why does the Pythagorean identity of step 1–2 need $Q\in\mathcal C^1_a$? What becomes of it for $Q\notin\mathcal C^1_a$?

Answer

The step $\sum_{ij}Q_{ij}\log\frac{a_i}{r_i}=\sum_ia_i\log\frac{a_i}{r_i}$ uses the row sums of $Q$. For a general $Q$ with row sums $\rho$: $\KL(Q|P)-\KL(Q|P^+)=\sum_i\rho_i\log\frac{a_i}{r_i}+\sum_i(r_i-a_i)$, which can be negative: projecting can move you away from points that are not in the constraint set.
