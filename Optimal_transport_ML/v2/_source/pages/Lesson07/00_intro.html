What you will be able to do after this page
  1. Write the entropic transport problem $\min_P\ps{P}{C}+\eps\KL(P|a\otimes b)$, explain why its three usual conventions have the same solution, and read the penalty as the mutual information of the coupling.
  2. Prove that the solution exists, is unique and positive, and has the scaling form $P=\operatorname{diag}(u)K\operatorname{diag}(v)$ with the Gibbs kernel $K=e^{-C/\eps}$; derive Sinkhorn’s algorithm from it and run it by hand.
  3. Explain Sinkhorn in three equivalent ways: alternating row and column scalings, alternating KL projections on the two marginal constraints, and exact block ascent on the dual by soft $c$-transforms — and implement it in the log domain.
  4. State and prove the linear convergence of Sinkhorn in Hilbert’s projective metric, and explain why the rate actually observed is the squared correlation $\sigma_\eps^2$ of the plan, with about $1/\eps$ iterations.
  5. Describe entropic transport between general measures: the limits $\eps\to0$ (optimal transport) and $\eps\to\infty$ (independence), Gaussians in closed form, and the bias of order $\eps\log(1/\eps)$.
  6. Define the Sinkhorn divergence, prove that it is non-negative, explain how it interpolates between optimal transport and MMD, and compare the sample complexities $n^{-1/d}$ (transport) and $n^{-1/2}$ (entropic transport at fixed $\eps$, MMD).
  7. Compute the gradients of Sinkhorn losses (dual potentials, barycentric projections) and use them in machine learning: generative models, domain adaptation, attention, barycenters, single-cell trajectories with unbalanced transport.

The story of the lesson in seven steps

Adding an entropy penalty to optimal transport turns a large linear program into a smooth, strictly convex problem whose solution is a rescaled Gibbs kernel, computed by alternately normalizing rows and columns; the price is a bias, which can be measured and largely removed, and the reward is speed, differentiability and statistics that do not suffer from the dimension.

  1. Why regularize (§1). The Kantorovich linear program is slow ($\approx n^3$ operations), its solutions are sparse vertices that jump when the data move, and its value is not differentiable. Adding $\eps\KL(P|a\otimes b)$ — the mutual information of the coupling — gives a unique, positive, smooth solution: the discrete Schrödinger problem.
  2. Its solution has a product form (§2–§3). $P=\operatorname{diag}(u)\,e^{-C/\eps}\operatorname{diag}(v)$: only $n+m$ unknowns. As $\eps\to0$ it tends to an optimal transport plan; as $\eps\to\infty$, to the independent coupling $a\otimes b$.
  3. Sinkhorn’s algorithm (§4–§5). Fix the rows, then the columns, and repeat: two matrix–vector products per iteration. Each half-step is a KL projection on one marginal constraint.
  4. The dual (§6–§7). In logarithmic variables, Sinkhorn alternates soft $c$-transforms, the smooth versions of the $c$-transforms of Lesson 4; run in the log domain it never underflows. It converges linearly, at a speed that degrades like $1/\eps$.
  5. General measures (§8). The same problem between densities: closed forms for Gaussians, a bias of order $\eps\log(1/\eps)$ with respect to $W_2^2$.
  6. Debiasing and statistics (§9–§10). Subtracting the self-transport terms gives the Sinkhorn divergence, a true divergence between optimal transport ($\eps\to0$) and MMD ($\eps\to\infty$), estimated from $n$ samples at the rate $n^{-1/2}$ instead of $n^{-1/d}$.
  7. Learning (§11–§12). The gradients of these losses are the dual potentials and the barycentric projections: generative models, domain adaptation, barycenters. Relaxing the marginals (unbalanced transport) only changes Sinkhorn into a damped iteration.

Refresher — the Kantorovich linear program (Lesson 2)

For histograms $a\in\R^n_+$, $b\in\R^m_+$ with the same total mass $1$, the couplings (transport plans) are the matrices

$$\U(a,b)=\big\{P\in\R^{n\times m}_+\ :\ P\ind_m=a,\ P\T\ind_n=b\big\},$$

whose row sums are $a$ and column sums are $b$. $P_{ij}$ is the mass sent from $x_i$ to $y_j$. The product $a\otimes b=(a_ib_j)_{ij}$ (the independent coupling) always belongs to $\U(a,b)$. The Kantorovich problem with cost matrix $C$ is the linear program $L_C(a,b)=\min_{P\in\U(a,b)}\ps{P}{C}$, $\ps{P}{C}=\sum_{ij}P_{ij}C_{ij}$. Facts used here: a minimum is attained at a vertex of the polytope $\U(a,b)$, which has at most $n+m-1$ non-zero entries (sparse); it need not be unique; on the line the monotone (north-west corner) plan is optimal for $\abs{x-y}^p$, $p\ge1$. See Reminders — linear programming, couplings, and the first companion, Lesson 2 §2.

Refresher — Kantorovich duality and $c$-transforms (Lesson 4)

The dual of the linear program is

$$L_C(a,b)=\max_{f\in\R^n,\ g\in\R^m}\Big\{\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}\ :\ f_i+g_j\le C_{ij}\ \text{for all }i,j\Big\}.$$

$f_i$ and $g_j$ are prices paid to send a unit of mass out of $x_i$ and into $y_j$; they may not exceed the cost of the trip. Given $g$, the best $f$ is the $c$-transform $f_i=g^{\bar c}_i=\min_j\,(C_{ij}-g_j)$. Alternating hard $c$-transforms does not solve the dual (it stalls after one cycle). Used here in §6, where Sinkhorn alternates soft $c$-transforms, and in §11 (potentials as gradients). See Reminders — OT duality and the first companion, Lesson 4 §3.

Refresher — entropy, relative entropy (KL) and Pinsker’s inequality

For a probability vector $p$, the Shannon entropy $\mathrm H(p)=-\sum_ip_i\log p_i$ (with $0\log0=0$) measures how spread $p$ is: it is $0$ for a Dirac, and maximal, $\log n$, for the uniform law on $n$ points. For non-negative vectors or matrices of the same size, the relative entropy (Kullback–Leibler divergence) is

$$\KL(P|Q)=\sum_{ij}\Big(P_{ij}\log\frac{P_{ij}}{Q_{ij}}-P_{ij}+Q_{ij}\Big)\ \ge0,$$

with equality if and only if $P=Q$ (it is $+\infty$ if $P_{ij}>0$ where $Q_{ij}=0$). The terms $-P_{ij}+Q_{ij}$ cancel in the sum when $P$ and $Q$ have the same total mass. KL is not symmetric and is not a distance, but it controls the total variation: Pinsker’s inequality $\norm{P-Q}_1\le\sqrt{2\KL(P|Q)}$ for probability vectors. Used in §1 (the penalty), §3 (limits), §5 (projections), §7. See Reminders — entropy and $\varphi$-divergences.

Refresher — Lagrange multipliers and convex conjugates

Lagrange. To minimize a smooth convex $F(P)$ under linear equality constraints $\mathcal A(P)=y$, introduce multipliers $\lambda$ and the Lagrangian $F(P)+\ps{\lambda}{y-\mathcal A(P)}$. At an interior minimizer, $\nabla F(P)=\mathcal A\T\lambda$; for convex problems with linear constraints this condition is also sufficient. Conjugate. The convex conjugate of $\varphi$ is $\varphi^*(s)=\sup_r\{rs-\varphi(r)\}$, and the Fenchel–Young inequality $\varphi(r)+\varphi^*(s)\ge rs$ holds for all $r,s$, with equality iff $s=\varphi'(r)$. The example used all along: for $\varphi(r)=r\log r-r+1$ ($r\ge0$), $\varphi^*(s)=e^s-1$, attained at $r=e^s$. Used in §2 (scaling form), §6 (the dual) and in the exercise sheet. See Reminders — Lagrange duality and Legendre transform.

Refresher — Gaussians and the Bures distance (Lesson 1)

Between $\N(m_\al,\Sigma_\al)$ and $\N(m_\be,\Sigma_\be)$ the optimal plan for $\norm{x-y}^2$ is given by an affine map, and $W_2^2=\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+\B(\Sigma_\al,\Sigma_\be)^2$ with $\B(\Sigma,\Lambda)^2=\tr\big(\Sigma+\Lambda-2(\Lambda^{1/2}\Sigma\Lambda^{1/2})^{1/2}\big)$; on the line, $W_2^2=(m_\al-m_\be)^2+(\sigma_\al-\sigma_\be)^2$. Used in §8–§9, where entropic transport between Gaussians has a closed form too. See Reminders — Gaussians and Lesson 3, the Brenier refresher.

Refresher — kernels and MMD

A symmetric function $k(x,y)$ is a positive definite kernel if $\sum_{ij}c_ic_jk(x_i,x_j)\ge0$ for all points and real coefficients; example: the Gaussian kernel $e^{-\norm{x-y}^2/\sigma^2}$. It defines the norm $\norm{\xi}_k^2=\iint k\,\dd\xi\,\dd\xi$ of a signed measure $\xi$, and the maximum mean discrepancy $\mathrm{MMD}_k(\al,\be)=\norm{\al-\be}_k$. It is computed with sums of kernel evaluations, with no optimization. Used in §9 (Sinkhorn divergences tend to a kernel norm as $\eps\to\infty$) and §10 (MMD is estimated at the rate $n^{-1/2}$). See Reminders — MMD.

Pitfall — four ways to write the penalty, one minimizer, four values

The resources do not all write the same penalty. On couplings $P\in\U(a,b)$ (total mass $1$), with the Shannon entropy $\mathrm H$:

SourceObjectiveequals, on $\U(a,b)$
OT4ML (8.1) (Def. 8.2)$\ps{P}{C}-\eps\mathrm H(P)$$\ps{P}{C}-\eps\mathrm H(P)$
OT4ML (8.8), 2019 notes (26), this page$\ps{P}{C}+\eps\KL(P|a\otimes b)$$\ps{P}{C}-\eps\mathrm H(P)+\eps\big(\mathrm H(a)+\mathrm H(b)\big)$
CompOT (4.2), with $\mathbf H(P)=-\sum P(\log P-1)$$\ps{P}{C}-\eps\mathbf H(P)$$\ps{P}{C}-\eps\mathrm H(P)-\eps$
exercise sheet, $H(P)=\sum P(\log P-1)$$\ps{P}{C}+\eps H(P)$$\ps{P}{C}-\eps\mathrm H(P)-\eps$

They differ by constants on $\U(a,b)$, so they have the same minimizer — but not the same value. On the running example at $\eps=0.5$ the four values are $-0.3213$, $0.3307$, $-0.8213$ and $-0.8213$. Values matter for the bias (§3, §8), the Sinkhorn divergence (§9) and convexity (Exercise 6 of the sheet). The sheet also uses the letter $H$ for minus the entropy, and OT4ML Prop. 8.17 uses $\mathrm H(\al)=\int\rho_\al\log\rho_\al$ (also minus an entropy): always check the sign.
