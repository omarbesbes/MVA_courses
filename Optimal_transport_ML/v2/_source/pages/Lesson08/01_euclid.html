1. The model: gradient flows in $\R^d$ OT4ML p. 356 · CompOT p. 153

The gradient flow $\dot x=-\nabla h(x)$ can be discretised explicitly (gradient descent: take the slope where you stand) or implicitly (take the slope where you land, by solving a small minimisation problem); the implicit step only needs a distance, which is why it is the one that will move measures.

Why start with points

The whole lesson answers one question: how do you run gradient descent when the unknown is a probability measure? Machine learning is full of such problems: fit a cloud of generated samples to a data set (minimise an MMD), sample a Bayesian posterior (minimise a KL divergence), train a wide neural network (minimise a risk that depends on the distribution of its neurons). For a vector $x\in\R^d$ the answer is gradient descent, $x-\tau\nabla h(x)$. For a measure $\al$ the same formula breaks down: “$\al-\tau g$” is a signed measure, whose mass can become negative. Before fixing this (§2), we look at the Euclidean scheme from the angle that will survive: a step is a minimisation problem that balances “decrease the energy” against “do not move too far”.

Explicit and implicit Euler steps

In the course OT4ML §15.1, p. 356

Euclidean gradient flows. If we restrict (15.1) to finite dimensions and assume $\al_t=\delta_{x(t)}$ and $\al=\delta_x$ (single Dirac measures), this matches the implicit Euler scheme:

$$x(t+\tau)\in\argmin_x\Big\{\frac1{2\tau}\norm{x-x(t)}^2+h(x)\Big\},$$

where $h(x)=f(\delta_x)$. When $h$ is differentiable and the minimizer is unique, the solution is characterized by the implicit Euler formula $x(t+\tau)=(\Id+\tau\nabla h)^{-1}(x(t))$. In contrast, the explicit Euler scheme is: $x(t+\tau)=(\Id-\tau\nabla h)(x(t))=x(t)-\tau\nabla h(x(t))$. Both schemes converge as $\tau\to0$ to:

$$\dot x(t)=-\nabla h(x(t)).\qquad(15.2)$$

Where the implicit formula comes from. If $h$ is differentiable, the objective $\frac1{2\tau}\norm{x-x_k}^2+h(x)$ has zero gradient at its minimiser $x_{k+1}$:

$$\frac{x_{k+1}-x_k}{\tau}+\nabla h(x_{k+1})=0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad x_{k+1}=x_k-\tau\,\nabla h(x_{k+1}).$$

So the implicit step is a gradient step with the slope taken where you land; the explicit step takes it where you stand. Both are first-order approximations of (15.2): $\frac{x_{k+1}-x_k}\tau\approx\dot x$.

Worked example — a stiff quadratic, $h(x)=\frac L2x^2$ with $L=10$, $x_0=1$

The exact flow is $\dot x=-Lx$, so $x(t)=e^{-Lt}$. Each scheme multiplies $x_k$ by a fixed factor:

  • explicit: $x_{k+1}=x_k-\tau Lx_k=(1-\tau L)\,x_k$;
  • implicit: $x_{k+1}+\tau Lx_{k+1}=x_k$, so $x_{k+1}=\dfrac{x_k}{1+\tau L}$.

With $\tau=0.25>2/L$ the explicit factor has modulus $1.5>1$: the iterates oscillate and blow up, $(-1.5)^4=5.06$. The implicit factor $1/(1+\tau L)$ is in $(0,1)$ for every $\tau>0$: the iterates decrease towards the minimiser, whatever the step.

The implicit step does not need a gradient

For $h(x)=\abs{x}$ on $\R$ (no derivative at $0$), the implicit step is perfectly defined: minimising $\frac1{2\tau}(x-y)^2+\abs{x}$ gives the soft-thresholding $x=\mathrm{sign}(y)\max(\abs{y}-\tau,0)$. With $\tau=0.3$: $y=0.7\mapsto0.4$, $y=0.2\mapsto0$, $y=-1\mapsto-0.7$. Small values are set exactly to zero — the mechanism behind sparsity in the Lasso. Keep this in mind: on measures, the entropy will play the role of a non-smooth energy (it is $+\infty$ on point clouds), and the implicit step will handle it.

What a gradient flow guarantees: dissipation and rates

Three facts about $\dot x=-\nabla h(x)$ will be lifted, one by one, to measures in §7. Their Euclidean proofs fit in a few lines.

In the course OT4ML §15.2, p. 373

Dissipation and finite metric length. […] For the classical gradient flow of a smooth function $h:\R^d\to\R$, $\dot x_t=-\nabla h(x_t)$, the chain rule gives $\dfrac{\dd}{\dd t}h(x_t)=-\norm{\nabla h(x_t)}^2=-\norm{\dot x_t}^2.$

Three facts (Euclidean model; the Wasserstein versions are OT4ML Prop. 15.26, Thm. 15.28 and Prop. 15.29, §7)
  1. Dissipation. $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}h(x_t)=-\norm{\nabla h(x_t)}^2\le0$: the energy decreases, at the rate of the squared slope.
  2. Convex case. If $h$ is convex with a minimiser $x^\star$: $h(x_t)-h(x^\star)\le\dfrac{\norm{x_0-x^\star}^2}{2t}$.
  3. Strongly convex case. If $h$ is $\lambda$-strongly convex, it satisfies the Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL) inequality $\norm{\nabla h(x)}^2\ge2\lambda\big(h(x)-h^\star\big)$, and then $h(x_t)-h^\star\le e^{-2\lambda t}\big(h(x_0)-h^\star\big)$.
Idea of the proofs

(1) is the chain rule. (2): the distance to the minimiser decreases at a rate at least equal to the energy gap (convexity says the descent direction points towards $x^\star$); integrate. (3): PL turns the dissipation identity into a differential inequality $E'\le-2\lambda E$; Grönwall.

Full proofs

(1) $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}h(x_t)=\ps{\nabla h(x_t)}{\dot x_t}=\ps{\nabla h(x_t)}{-\nabla h(x_t)}=-\norm{\nabla h(x_t)}^2$.

(2)

  1. $$\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\tfrac12\norm{x_t-x^\star}^2=\ps{x_t-x^\star}{\dot x_t}=-\ps{\nabla h(x_t)}{x_t-x^\star}.$$Chain rule, and $\dot x_t=-\nabla h(x_t)$.
  2. $$-\ps{\nabla h(x_t)}{x_t-x^\star}\le-\big(h(x_t)-h(x^\star)\big).$$Convexity at $x_t$, evaluated at $y=x^\star$: $h(x^\star)\ge h(x_t)+\ps{\nabla h(x_t)}{x^\star-x_t}$.
  3. $$\int_0^t\big(h(x_s)-h^\star\big)\dd s\le\tfrac12\norm{x_0-x^\star}^2-\tfrac12\norm{x_t-x^\star}^2\le\tfrac12\norm{x_0-x^\star}^2.$$Integrate lines 1–2 from $0$ to $t$.
  4. $$t\,\big(h(x_t)-h^\star\big)\le\int_0^t\big(h(x_s)-h^\star\big)\dd s\le\tfrac12\norm{x_0-x^\star}^2.$$$s\mapsto h(x_s)$ is non-increasing by (1), so every value on $[0,t]$ is at least the last one. Divide by $t$.

(3)

  1. $$h^\star=\min_yh(y)\ge\min_y\Big(h(x)+\ps{\nabla h(x)}{y-x}+\tfrac\lambda2\norm{y-x}^2\Big)=h(x)-\frac{\norm{\nabla h(x)}^2}{2\lambda}.$$Strong convexity gives a lower bound by a parabola in $y$; its minimum is at $y=x-\nabla h(x)/\lambda$ (complete the square). Rearranged: the PL inequality.
  2. With $E(t)=h(x_t)-h^\star$: $E'(t)=-\norm{\nabla h(x_t)}^2\le-2\lambda E(t)$.(1), then PL.
  3. $E(t)\le e^{-2\lambda t}E(0)$.Grönwall (refresher).

For $h=\frac L2x^2$: $h(x_t)=\frac L2e^{-2Lt}x_0^2$, so (3) holds with equality and $\lambda=L$.

The implicit step as a “minimizing movement”

Look at the implicit step once more: it only uses the energy and the squared distance. Nothing in it requires a vector space. On any metric space $(\mathcal X,d)$ one can define

$$x^{(\ell+1)}\in\argmin_{x\in\mathcal X}\ \frac1{2\tau}d\big(x^{(\ell)},x\big)^2+h(x),$$

a minimizing movement. CompOT writes it explicitly (Remark 9.11, (9.31), p. 154, with $d^2+\tau F$, the same minimiser for a step $\tau/2$) and adds: “The implicit step is always stable, is also defined for nonsmooth $F$, but is usually not accessible in closed form” (p. 155). The general theory of the limits $\tau\to0$ in metric spaces is due to Ambrosio, Gigli and Savaré (OT4ML Defs. 15.8–15.9, p. 359). The plan of the lesson: take $\mathcal X=\Pc_2(\R^d)$ and $d=W_2$.

In machine learning — step sizes and proximal steps

Training a model by gradient descent is the explicit scheme applied to the loss $h(\theta)$ of its parameters $\theta$: the learning rate is $\tau$, and the worked example is the reason why a learning rate above $2/L$ ($L$ the largest curvature of the loss) makes training diverge. Proximal steps are used when part of the objective is non-smooth: for the Lasso, $\min_\theta\frac12\norm{y-A\theta}^2+\mu\norm{\theta}_1$, the proximal gradient method (ISTA) alternates a gradient step on the smooth part and a soft-thresholding on the $\ell^1$ part. StatOT (§6.2.2, (6.12)–(6.14), pp. 188–189) uses exactly this split to explain sampling algorithms: the potential energy is the smooth part, the entropy the non-smooth part, and “the proximal operator on the Wasserstein space” is the JKO step of §2.

Pitfall — “implicit” does not mean “free”

The implicit step is stable for every $\tau$, but each step is an optimisation problem; it is cheap only when that problem has structure (a closed form, convexity, a sparse Hessian). On measures we will meet both cases: closed forms for Diracs and Gaussians (§2), a convex problem in one dimension (§2), and particle methods everywhere else (§5).

Reflexes
  • When you see $\argmin_x\frac1{2\tau}d(x,x_k)^2+h(x)$ → an implicit Euler (proximal) step of the gradient flow of $h$ for the distance $d$; first-order condition $\frac{x_{k+1}-x_k}{\tau}=-\nabla h(x_{k+1})$.
  • To prove a rate for a gradient flow → differentiate the energy (dissipation), then find an inequality between the slope and the energy gap (convexity, PL) and integrate.

For $h(x)=\frac12x^2$ and $x_0=2$, compute one explicit and one implicit step with $\tau=0.5$, and the exact flow at $t=0.5$.

Answer

Explicit: $2-0.5\cdot2=1$. Implicit: $x+0.5x=2$, so $x=4/3\approx1.333$. Exact: $2e^{-0.5}\approx1.213$, in between. We will meet these three numbers again in §2: they are the JKO step of a Dirac.
