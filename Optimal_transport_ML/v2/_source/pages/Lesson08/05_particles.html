5. Particles and densities: Lagrangian and Eulerian flows OT4ML (15.5), p. 360

Started from $n$ Diracs, a Wasserstein gradient flow stays a sum of $n$ Diracs whose positions solve $\dot x_i=-\nabla_Wf(\al_t)(x_i)=-n\nabla_{x_i}F(X)$: plain gradient descent on the positions, $n$ times faster; densities on a grid (Eulerian) and moving particles (Lagrangian) are two discretisations of the same equation, and only the second one is dimension-free — provided the energy makes sense on a point cloud.

Particles follow the Wasserstein gradient

In the course OT4ML §15.1, (15.5), p. 360

Discrete evolutions. If $f$ admits a first variation whose spatial gradient is well defined at empirical measures, the characteristic form of (15.4) preserves the number and weights of the atoms. For equal weights, write $\al_t=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i(t)}$. The particles $X(t):=(x_i(t))_i$ evolve according to the coupled ODEs

$$\dot x_i(t)=-n\nabla_{x_i}F(X(t)),\qquad(15.5)$$

where $F(X):=f\big(\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}\big)$ and the factor $n$ comes from the empirical Wasserstein metric $\frac1n\sum_i\norm{\dot x_i}^2$.

“characteristic form”
The continuity equation moves mass along the curves $\dot x=v_t(x)$ (its characteristics). Starting from atoms, each atom simply follows its own characteristic: the number of atoms and their weights never change.
$F(X)$
The energy, seen as an ordinary function of the $nd$ coordinates of the particles. Example: $f=\int V\dd\al$ gives $F(X)=\frac1n\sum_iV(x_i)$.
$-n\nabla_{x_i}F$
The usual gradient of $F$ with respect to the position of particle $i$, multiplied by $n$.
$\frac1n\sum_i\norm{\dot x_i}^2$
The kinetic energy $\int\norm{v}^2\dd\al$ of the motion when each particle carries mass $\frac1n$: the Wasserstein metric on configurations. A gradient for the metric $\frac1n\norm{\cdot}^2$ is $n$ times the Euclidean gradient.

Why $n\nabla_{x_i}F(X)=\nabla_Wf(\al)(x_i)$. Move particle $i$ alone by $\eps u$. This moves the mass $\frac1n$ sitting at $x_i$ with velocity $u$, so by Proposition 15.7 the energy changes at the rate $\int\ps{\nabla_Wf}{v}\dd\al=\frac1n\ps{\nabla_Wf(\al)(x_i)}{u}$. Hence $\nabla_{x_i}F(X)=\frac1n\nabla_Wf(\al)(x_i)$, and (15.5) is the Wasserstein flow evaluated at the particles. Check on the example of §3, $f=\frac12\mathrm{Var}$ and $X=(0,1,5)$: $F(X)=\frac12\cdot\frac13\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2$, so $\nabla_{x_i}F=\frac13(x_i-\bar x)$ and $3\nabla_{x_i}F=x_i-\bar x=(-2,-1,3)$, the Wasserstein gradient found in §3. ✓

Pitfall — gradient descent on positions has a different clock

Automatic differentiation of a loss $F(X)$ written as a function of the positions returns $\nabla_{x_i}F=\frac1n\nabla_Wf(x_i)$. Gradient descent $X\leftarrow X-\tau\nabla F(X)$ therefore follows the Wasserstein flow with the time step $\tau/n$: same trajectories, $n$ times slower. To compare runs with different numbers of particles, multiply the learning rate by $n$ (equivalently, differentiate $nF$ instead of $F$).

Two ways to discretise a flow

In the course CompOT Rem. 9.12, pp. 155–156

Remark 9.12 (Lagrangian discretization using particles systems). The finite-dimensional problem in (9.26) can be interpreted as the Eulerian discretization of a flow over the space of measures (9.27). An alternative way to discretize the problem, using the so-called Lagrangian method using particles systems, is to parameterize instead the solution as a (discrete) empirical measure moving with time, where the locations of that measure (and not its weights) become the variables of interest. […] If the energy $F$ is finite for discrete measures, then one can simply define $\mathcal F(X)=F(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{x_i})$. […] For functions such as generalized entropy, which are only finite for measures having densities, one should apply a density estimator to convert the point cloud into a density […].

Machine learning lives in high dimension, so it uses the Lagrangian picture almost exclusively: the particles are samples, neurons or tokens (§8–§11). The JKO widget of §2 was already Lagrangian (particles at quantile levels), in one dimension where the entropy can be written with the gaps between neighbours.

Mean-field limit

When the velocity of each particle depends on the empirical measure of all of them, the system is a mean-field interacting particle system (StatOT §5.8, p. 165: “each particle interacts with the others only through the effect of their distribution, and these interactions have the same form for all the particles”). If the initial empirical measures converge to a density $\al_0$, the particle flows converge to the solution of the PDE started at $\al_0$: $n$ particles are a Monte Carlo approximation of the flow. With noise, this is propagation of chaos and the limit is the McKean–Vlasov equation (OT4ML p. 364).

Interacting particles: aggregation and patterns

Interaction energies $f(\al)=\frac12\iint W(x-y)\dd\al(x)\dd\al(y)$ (StatOT’s normalisation, gradient $\int\nabla W(x-y)\dd\al(y)$) are finite on point clouds, so their particle flows $\dot x_i=-\frac1n\sum_{j\ne i}\nabla W(x_i-x_j)$ are exact. (For a kernel that is infinite at $0$, like the Coulomb kernel below, the self-interaction terms $j=i$ are simply left out.) Attraction at long range and repulsion at short range produce stable shapes.

Worked example — attraction and Coulomb repulsion make a uniform disk

In $\R^2$, let $W(z)=\frac12\norm{z}^2-\log\norm{z}$: quadratic attraction, logarithmic (Coulomb) repulsion. Claim: the equilibrium is the uniform probability on a disk of radius $1$ (density $\frac1\pi$).

  1. At an equilibrium, $\nabla_Wf=0$ on the support, so $\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(x)=\int W(x-y)\dd\al(y)$ is constant there.Stationary states of a gradient flow (§4 reflex).
  2. $\Delta_x\frac12\norm{x-y}^2=2$ and $\Delta_x\big(-\log\norm{x-y}\big)=-2\pi\,\delta_y$ in $\R^2$.$\Delta\frac12\norm{x}^2=d=2$; $-\frac1{2\pi}\log\norm{x}$ is the fundamental solution of $-\Delta$ in the plane.
  3. $0=\Delta\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(x)=\int\big(2-2\pi\delta_y(x)\big)\dd\al(y)=2-2\pi\rho(x)$ inside the support.Apply the Laplacian to the constant of line 1, inside the support; line 2.
  4. $\rho=\frac1\pi$ on the support, which has area $\pi$ since the mass is $1$: a disk of radius $1$ (centred at the conserved mean).Rotation invariance of the problem; uniqueness of the equilibrium is admitted.

Check with particles. With $300$ particles and explicit steps $0.01$ (the widget below, energy “attraction + Coulomb”), at $t\approx10$ the largest distance to the mean is $0.96$, and the fractions of particles within $0.5$ and $0.75$ of the mean are $0.25$ and $0.55$; the uniform unit disk gives $0.25$ and $0.5625$. (An independent run with another random start, in Python, gives $0.97$, $0.25$ and $0.56$.)

In machine learning — particles are the parameters

Every system of this lesson where “the loss depends on a population” is an interacting particle system: generated samples fitted to data by an MMD (§8), neurons of a wide network (§10: the risk is a quadratic interaction between neurons plus a potential), tokens in a transformer (§11: attention is a normalised interaction), sampling particles in SVGD (§9). Equation (15.5) says that training such a system by gradient descent on the particles is, up to the factor $n$, a Wasserstein gradient flow of the population — which is what allows one to analyse it as a PDE when $n$ is large (the “mean-field limit”).

Reflexes
  • Particles of mass $\frac1n$ → $\dot x_i=-\nabla_Wf(x_i)=-n\nabla_{x_i}F(X)$.
  • Energy infinite on point clouds (entropy) → add noise (Langevin), estimate a density, or kernelise (SVGD).
  • Interaction energies conserve the mean of the particles (action = reaction).

Two particles on $\R$ at $x_1=-1$, $x_2=1$, with $W(z)=\frac12z^2$ (so $f=\frac14\iint(x-y)^2\dd\al\dd\al$). Write their ODEs and solve them.

Answer

$\dot x_1=-\frac12(x_1-x_2)$, $\dot x_2=-\frac12(x_2-x_1)$ (the sum over $j\ne i$ has one term, divided by $n=2$). The mean $0$ is conserved and the gap $g=x_2-x_1$ solves $\dot g=-g$, so $x_{1,2}(t)=\mp e^{-t}$. Same as the Wasserstein flow of §4: $\dot x=-(x-m)$.
