12. Cells: trajectories from snapshots OT4ML Ex. 3.24, Ex. 15.10

Sequencing a cell destroys it: each day one observes a new population, never the same cell twice. Transport reconstructs plausible trajectories from these snapshots — couplings between consecutive days give soft ancestor–descendant masses, and their products give lineages — and a gradient-flow model (a drift down a landscape $V$ plus diffusion) turns the snapshots into a dynamical law.

Snapshots and couplings

In the course OT4ML Ex. 3.24, pp. 61–62

Example 3.24 (Application to single-cell population dynamics). Single-cell sequencing observes different cells at each collection time. If $x_i^{[k]}\in\X$ denotes the state of cell $i$ at time $t_k$, write

$$\al_{t_k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_k}a_i^{[k]}\delta_{x_i^{[k]}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf a^{[k]}=(a_i^{[k]})_{i=1}^{n_k}\in\Sigma_{n_k}.$$

[…] A balanced transport between two consecutive snapshots is represented by a matrix $\mathbf P^{[k]}\in\R_+^{n_k\times n_{k+1}}$ satisfying $\mathbf P^{[k]}\mathbf 1_{n_{k+1}}=\mathbf a^{[k]}$ and $(\mathbf P^{[k]})\T\mathbf 1_{n_k}=\mathbf a^{[k+1]}$. Its entry $\mathbf P^{[k]}_{i,j}$ is a soft ancestor–descendant mass, not evidence that the same cell was observed twice. For every row with positive sum, the normalization

$$\mathbf K^{[k]}_{i,j}:=\frac{\mathbf P^{[k]}_{i,j}}{\sum_{j'=1}^{n_{k+1}}\mathbf P^{[k]}_{i,j'}}$$

defines a conditional transition probability; multiplying successive matrices $\mathbf K^{[k]}$ yields population-level lineage probabilities across several collection times. Waddington-OT modifies this balanced baseline by incorporating growth estimates and relaxing the marginal constraints through unbalanced transport, as discussed in Example 11.4. […] this visualization need not coincide with the ground geometry used to compute $\mathbf P^{[k]}$.

$x_i^{[k]}$
The state of a cell: its gene-expression profile (thousands of numbers, usually reduced to a few dozen principal components). Each day $k$ brings new cells: the index $i$ does not follow a cell from one day to the next.
$\mathbf P^{[k]}$
A transport plan between day $k$ and day $k+1$ (Lesson 2). $\mathbf P^{[k]}_{ij}$ is the mass that the reconstruction sends from cell $i$ to cell $j$ — a guess about descent, not an observation.
$\mathbf K^{[k]}$
Row $i$ of $\mathbf P^{[k]}$ divided by its mass: the law of the descendants of cell $i$ at the next day. A Markov matrix, like the attention matrices of §11.
$\mathbf K^{[0]}\mathbf K^{[1]}\cdots$
Chaining days: the law at day $k$ of the descendants of a cell of day $0$, hence “fate probabilities”: which fraction of the progeny of cell $i$ ends in a given cell type.
unbalanced, growth
Cells divide and die, so the mass is not conserved between days; Example 11.4 (p. 219) relaxes the marginal constraints with divergence penalties (Lesson 7).

A gradient-flow model of the population

In the course OT4ML Ex. 15.10, p. 360

Example 15.10 (Application to single-cell gradient-flow models). Instead of only coupling successive snapshots, one may posit that a latent population law evolves by

$$\partial_t\al_t+\dv(\al_tv_t)=0,\qquad v_t=-\nabla_Wf(\al_t)=-\nabla\delta f(\al_t).$$

Here $\delta f(\al_t)$ is the first variation, with the convention introduced above. For example, if $\al=\rho\dd x$, the energy $f(\al)=\int V\dd\al+\sigma^2\int\rho\log\rho\,\dd x$ gives a drift toward a potential landscape $V$ together with diffusion of strength $\sigma^2$. In single-cell modeling, $V$, interaction terms or a neural approximation of $v_t$ can be fitted from unpaired population snapshots. This links Waddington-landscape intuition to the mathematical language of dynamic OT, Fokker–Planck equations and flow matching.

$f=\int V\dd\al+\sigma^2\int\rho\log\rho$
The Fokker–Planck energy of §4 with temperature $\sigma^2$: velocity $-\nabla V-\sigma^2\nabla\log\rho$, PDE $\partial_t\rho=\dv(\rho\nabla V)+\sigma^2\Delta\rho$, and each cell follows the Langevin equation $\dd X=-\nabla V(X)\dd t+\sqrt{2\sigma^2}\dd B$ (§6).
“Waddington landscape”
Waddington’s picture (1957) of development as balls rolling down a landscape with branching valleys, one per cell fate: $V$ is that landscape, the valleys are its minima, and the Gibbs measure $\propto e^{-V/\sigma^2}$ is the long-time population.
“fitted from unpaired snapshots”
An inverse problem: find $V$ (or $v_t$) such that the flow started at the first snapshot passes near the next ones — measured with a transport or kernel discrepancy (§8).

Why a gradient model? Because snapshots cannot see motions that do not change the density. If cells with density $\N(0,\Id_2)$ rotate, $\dot x=Jx$ with $J$ the rotation by $90^\circ$, then $\dv(\rho Jx)=\ps{\nabla\rho}{Jx}+\rho\,\dv(Jx)=-\rho\ps{x}{Jx}+0=0$: every snapshot is the same Gaussian, and no method based on snapshots alone can detect the rotation. Restricting to drifts $-\nabla V$ removes this ambiguity (our remark; the identifiability of gradient-drift diffusions from snapshots is a theme of Lavenant et al., cited below).

Worked example — a toy Waddington landscape (our construction, computed by script)

Cells start at the top of the landscape $V(x)=\frac{x^4}4-\frac{x^2}2$ (a hill at $0$, valleys at $\pm1$: two fates) and follow the Fokker–Planck flow of Example 15.10 with $\sigma^2=0.05$, from $\N(0,0.15^2)$. We observe the population at days $0,1,2$ = times $0,\ 1.5,\ 3$ (densities computed on a grid by an implicit finite-volume scheme). The fraction of cells still undecided ($\abs{x}<0.25$) is $0.904$, $0.189$, $0.055$.

Question: a cell is at position $x$ on day $0$; with which probability will it be in the right valley ($x>0$) on day 2? The truth is $u(x)=\P(X_3>0\,|\,X_0=x)$, the solution of the backward Kolmogorov equation $\partial_tu=\sigma^2u''-V'u'$ (Monte Carlo check at $x=0.1$ with $2\times10^5$ paths: $0.6656$ vs $0.6653$). The transport reconstructions only see the three densities.   • Exact OT is monotone on the line (Lesson 3): the left half of day 0 goes to the left valley, deterministically. The fate of each cell is decided, which is false: noise near the hilltop decides.
  • Entropic OT (Lesson 7, cost $\frac12\abs{x-y}^2$) gives soft fates. The choice $\eps=2\sigma^2\Delta t$ is not arbitrary: for pure diffusion, the transition kernel over a time $\Delta t$ is $\propto e^{-\abs{x-y}^2/(4\sigma^2\Delta t)}=e^{-\frac12\abs{x-y}^2/\eps}$, and the entropic plan is then the true joint law of the cells at the two times (the static Schrödinger problem). The drift makes this approximate, less so over a shorter interval: chaining the two daily couplings as in Example 3.24 is much closer to the truth than the direct coupling (largest error $0.010$ against $0.058$ in the table).
  • Interpolating the snapshots. Predicting day 1 from days 0 and 2 alone: the McCann midpoint (average of the quantile functions, Lesson 3, §6) is at $W_2$-distance $0.180$ from the true day 1, the mixture $\frac12(\al_0+\al_2)$ at $0.185$ (day 2 alone: $0.262$). Neither is good: displacement interpolation moves every cell at constant speed on a straight line, whereas cells leave the hilltop slowly and then fast (at day 1 the mean of $\abs{x}$ is $0.586$, the midpoint predicts $0.476$). A dynamical model — here, the Fokker–Planck flow of $V$ — carries the right clock.
In machine learning — trajectory inference for single cells

Waddington-OT (Schiebinger et al., Cell 2019; [479] in OT4ML) analyses a reprogramming time course (fibroblasts to stem cells, sampled every half day over 18 days, OT4ML Fig. 3.7): entropic, unbalanced couplings between consecutive days in a space of gene expression, with growth rates estimated from proliferation genes, chained as in Example 3.24 to compute the ancestors and the fate probabilities of every cell. TrajectoryNet (Tong et al., ICML 2020, [525]) fits a continuous normalising flow regularised by the Benamou–Brenier kinetic energy (Lesson 6), so that the cells follow near-optimal continuous paths through all snapshots. Lavenant, Zhang, Kim and Schiebinger ([334], 2021) give a mathematical theory of trajectory inference for diffusions with gradient drift — the model of Example 15.10 — and recover the law of the trajectories by an entropy-regularised transport problem over all time points. GENOT (Klein, Uscidda, Theis, Cuturi, NeurIPS 2024, [315]) learns entropic (Gromov–)Wasserstein couplings with flow matching, so that one can sample descendants of a new cell, even between different measurement modalities. Concrete picture: the table above, with the line replaced by a 30-dimensional space of principal components and the two valleys by two cell types.

Pitfalls
  • A coupling is not a lineage measurement. “A soft ancestor–descendant mass, not evidence that the same cell was observed twice” (Ex. 3.24). Validate on held-out days or with lineage barcodes.
  • The picture is not the geometry. Couplings are computed in expression space; the 2D drawings (force layouts, UMAP) are for display only: “this visualization need not coincide with the ground geometry used to compute $\mathbf P^{[k]}$” (p. 62).
  • Mass is not conserved (division, death, sampling): balanced plans force spurious transitions; use unbalanced transport (Ex. 11.4).
  • Snapshots cannot see density-preserving motions (rotation above): assume gradient drifts, or bring extra information.
Reflexes
  • Unpaired snapshots → one empirical measure per day; consecutive days → transport plans; rows normalised → Markov matrices; products → lineages and fates.
  • Noisy dynamics → entropic couplings, $\eps\approx2\sigma^2\Delta t$; short intervals are better than long ones.
  • Mechanistic model → gradient flow of $\int V\dd\al+\sigma^2\int\rho\log\rho$: landscape $V$, Fokker–Planck PDE, Langevin cells, Gibbs equilibrium $e^{-V/\sigma^2}$.

Day 0: two cells at $0$ and $1$; day 1: two cells at $0.4$ and $3$ (equal masses, cost $\abs{x-y}^2$). Give the exact OT plan $\mathbf P^{[0]}$ and $\mathbf K^{[0]}$. Then day 2: cells at $1$ and $2$; compute $\mathbf K^{[0]}\mathbf K^{[1]}$. What is wrong with a balanced model if, in truth, the cell at $3$ died?

Answer

On the line the optimal plan is monotone: $0\to0.4$, $1\to3$, so $\mathbf P^{[0]}=\frac12\Id$ and $\mathbf K^{[0]}=\Id$. Day 1 → day 2: monotone again, $0.4\to1$, $3\to2$, $\mathbf K^{[1]}=\Id$, so $\mathbf K^{[0]}\mathbf K^{[1]}=\Id$: the cell at $1$ on day 0 is declared the ancestor of the cell at $2$ on day 2, through the cell at $3$. If the cell at $3$ died, its mass had to go somewhere: a balanced plan must send all the mass of day 1 to day 2, so it invents a transition $3\to2$ (and $1\to3$ before it). An unbalanced plan can let that mass disappear at a cost (Ex. 11.4).
