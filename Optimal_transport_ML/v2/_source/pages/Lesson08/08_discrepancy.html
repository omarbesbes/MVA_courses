8. Particle flows of discrepancies: MMD, energy distance, Sinkhorn OT4ML pp. 362–364

To move a cloud of samples onto data, descend a discrepancy that makes sense on point clouds: an MMD is an interaction energy plus a potential, so its particle flow is exact — particles repel each other and are attracted by the data — but with finitely many particles it only builds a kernel quadrature of the target; transport discrepancies ($W_2$, Sinkhorn) give straighter, matching-based motions.

MMD flows: self-repulsion plus attraction to the data

In the course OT4ML §15.1, p. 362

If $k$ is positive definite, or more generally conditionally positive definite on signed measures of zero total mass as for the energy-distance kernel $k(x,y)=-\norm{x-y}$, and one minimizes the squared kernel discrepancy to a teacher distribution $\be$, then

$$\norm{\al-\be}_k^2=\iint k\,\dd\al\dd\al-2\int\Big(\int k(x,y)\dd\be(y)\Big)\dd\al(x)+\text{constant}.$$

Thus MMD-type training energies are exactly an interaction energy plus a linear potential; the teacher distribution appears through the potential $x\mapsto-2\int k(x,y)\dd\be(y)$. The corresponding empirical Wasserstein gradient flow is

$$\dot x_i(t)=-\frac2n\sum_j\nabla_xk(x_i(t),x_j(t))+2\int\nabla_xk(x_i(t),y)\dd\be(y).$$

The first term is a kernelized self-interaction, while the second is the attraction induced by the continuous teacher kernel mean. At the continuum level, for characteristic positive-definite kernels, and for the Euclidean energy-distance kernel on probability measures, [we] have $\be$ as the unique minimizer of $\norm{\al-\be}_k^2$. For a finite number of particles, however, the flow can only form a kernelized quadrature of $\be$, and small particle systems may cover the target modes poorly.

The table of §3 applied to the two parts: $-\nabla_W$ of the interaction is $-2\int\nabla_xk(x,y)\dd\al(y)$, here an average over the $n$ particles; $-\nabla_W$ of the potential is $+2\int\nabla_xk(x,y)\dd\be(y)$.
“kernelized quadrature”
With $n$ particles, the flow converges (at best) to the $n$-point cloud closest to $\be$ in MMD: a set of quadrature nodes for integrating functions of the kernel’s space against $\be$, not a sample of $\be$.

Energy distance. For $k(x,y)=-\norm{x-y}$, $\nabla_xk(x,y)=-\frac{x-y}{\norm{x-y}}$, a unit vector. The flow becomes $\dot x_i=\frac2n\sum_j\frac{x_i-x_j}{\norm{x_i-x_j}}-\frac2m\sum_j\frac{x_i-y_j}{\norm{x_i-y_j}}$ for data $y_1,\dots,y_m$: each particle is pushed away from every other particle and pulled towards every data point, with forces of unit size whatever the distance. Far-away data are felt as strongly as nearby data. With a Gaussian kernel $e^{-\norm{x-y}^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ the forces vanish beyond a few $\sigma$: a target far from the initial cloud is simply invisible.

Worked example — one particle cannot fit two data points

On $\R$, energy distance, $\be=\frac12(\delta_{-1}+\delta_1)$ and a single particle $\al=\delta_x$:

$$\mathrm{MMD}^2(\delta_x,\be)=\underbrace{0}_{\iint k\dd\al\dd\al}+\underbrace{\abs{x+1}+\abs{x-1}}_{-2\iint k\dd\al\dd\be}-\underbrace{1}_{-\iint k\dd\be\dd\be}=\abs{x+1}+\abs{x-1}-1 .$$

It equals $1$ for every $x\in[-1,1]$ ($3$ at $x=\pm2$): the energy is flat between the two data points, the velocity is zero there, and the best a single particle can do is $\mathrm{MMD}^2=1$. Two particles placed at $\pm1$ reach $0$. Check with the one-dimensional formula $\mathrm{ED}^2=2\int\abs{C_\al-C_\be}^2\dd x$ (OT4ML Fig. 15.4 caption): for $x\in[-1,1]$, $C_\al-C_\be=\mp\frac12$ on the two sides of $x$ inside $[-1,1]$, so $\mathrm{ED}^2=2\cdot\frac14\cdot2=1$. ✓

Fix also $\be\in\Pc_2(\R)$. For the conditionally positive kernel $k(x,y)=-\abs{x-y}$ […], the squared MMD […], equivalently the squared energy distance, $\mathcal E_\be(\al):=\mathrm{MMD}_k^2(\al,\be)=\iint-\abs{x-y}\,\dd(\al-\be)(x)\dd(\al-\be)(y)$, is geodesically convex as a function of $\al$.

So on the line the energy-distance flow converges at the rate $O(1/t)$ of §7, while in higher dimension, and for Gaussian or Laplace kernels, MMD is not geodesically convex (OT4ML p. 368) and no general convergence guarantee is available — the stragglers of the figure are one symptom.

Transport discrepancies: $W_2$ and Sinkhorn

$W_2$. For $f=W_2^2(\cdot,\be)$ the velocity is $2(T(x)-x)$ with $T$ the Brenier map to $\be$ (§3): every particle moves on a straight line towards its partner, and the flow follows the McCann geodesic, slowing down exponentially (Exercise 6, §13). For particles and an empirical target, $T$ is an optimal assignment, recomputed as the cloud moves.

In machine learning — generative modelling with discrepancy flows

Generators trained on a discrepancy. MMD-GANs and Sinkhorn generative models train a generator $G_\theta$ by gradient descent on $\theta\mapsto\mathcal D\big(\pf{(G_\theta)}{\zeta},\be\big)$ (OT4ML (16.15), p. 415, citing Dziugaite et al. 2015 and Genevay et al. 2018); the per-sample directions are the Wasserstein gradients of this section, pulled back through the network (§3). One-step models built from Wasserstein flows. OT4ML (16.19)–(16.20), p. 417, describes the alternative that follows the flow of this section literally: at each training step, compute the Wasserstein velocity $w_t=-\nabla_W\mathcal E_\be(\al_t)$ at the generated samples (an MMD, a smoothed KL, or a Sinkhorn divergence), fit a network $U_\eta$ to it by least squares $\min_\eta\int\norm{U_\eta-w_t}^2\dd\al_t$, move the generator by $G_\theta\leftarrow G_\theta+\tau\,U_\eta\circ G_\theta$, and distil the result back into a fixed architecture, so that sampling stays a single network evaluation (the book cites W-Flow, with the Sinkhorn divergence, and the “drifting” models). Pitfall — kernel bandwidth and particle count

A Gaussian-kernel MMD flow started far from the data does not move towards it (the attraction is exponentially small), and the self-repulsion then just spreads the cloud. The energy distance has no bandwidth and always sees the data, but finite clouds stop at quadratures, and laggards can take very long. In practice: multi-scale kernels, more particles, or a transport discrepancy.

Reflexes
  • MMD$^2$ → interaction (repulsion between particles) + potential (attraction by the data); velocity $-\frac2n\sum_j\nabla_xk(x_i,x_j)+2\int\nabla_xk(x_i,y)\dd\be(y)$.
  • Energy-distance kernel → unit forces, no bandwidth; in 1D geodesically convex.
  • $W_2^2$ → straight lines to the Brenier image; Sinkhorn divergence → barycentric projection on the data minus barycentric projection on the cloud.

With the Gaussian kernel $k(x,y)=e^{-(x-y)^2/2}$, a single particle at $x$ and the target $\be=\delta_0$ on $\R$: write $\mathrm{MMD}^2(\delta_x,\delta_0)$ and the particle velocity. What happens if $x=10$?

Answer

$\mathrm{MMD}^2=k(x,x)-2k(x,0)+k(0,0)=2-2e^{-x^2/2}$. The particle flow ($n=1$) is $\dot x=-\frac{\dd}{\dd x}\mathrm{MMD}^2=-2xe^{-x^2/2}$: towards $0$. At $x=10$ the speed is $20e^{-50}\approx4\times10^{-21}$: the particle does not move in any reasonable time, although the loss is almost $2$, its maximum. With $k=-\abs{x-y}$ instead, $\mathrm{MMD}^2=2\abs{x}$ and the speed is $2$ wherever the particle is.
