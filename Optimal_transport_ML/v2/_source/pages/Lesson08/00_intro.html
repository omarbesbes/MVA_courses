What you will be able to do after this page
  1. Explain why “gradient descent on a probability measure” needs a distance rather than a subtraction, and write the JKO step $\al^{(\ell+1)}\in\argmin_\al\frac1{2\tau}W_2^2(\al^{(\ell)},\al)+f(\al)$ as the implicit Euler step of the Wasserstein geometry.
  2. Compute first variations $\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$ and Wasserstein gradients $\nabla_W f=\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$ of the usual energies (potential, entropy, interaction, KL, MMD, $W_2^2$), and write the limit PDE $\partial_t\al_t=\dv\big(\al_t\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al_t)\big)$.
  3. Recognise the classical equations as gradient flows: transport by $-\nabla V$, the heat equation (entropy), aggregation (interaction), Fokker–Planck (KL to a Gibbs measure) — and pass from densities (Eulerian) to particles (Lagrangian) and to the Langevin SDE.
  4. Prove the rates: energy dissipation, $O(1/t)$ for geodesically convex energies, $e^{-2\lambda t}$ under $\lambda$-convexity or a Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality; state the log-Sobolev, Talagrand and HWI inequalities and check them on Gaussians.
  5. Run particle flows of discrepancies (MMD, energy distance, Sinkhorn) and Gaussian closures (Bures–Wasserstein flows), and say what they are used for: generative modelling, sampling, variational inference.
  6. Read the training of a two-layer network as a Wasserstein gradient flow of its neurons, the layers of a transformer as interacting tokens, and single-cell time courses as snapshots of a flow.
  7. Solve every exercise of the sheet Gradient Flows — Exercises (and twelve more), and know which statements of the resources contain slips.

The story of the lesson in seven steps

To minimise an energy over probability measures, move the mass downhill in the transport geometry: implicit steps measured by $W_2$ (JKO) converge to a continuity equation whose velocity is minus the gradient of the first variation; this one equation describes diffusion, sampling, particle systems, wide neural networks and transformers.

  1. The model (§1). In $\R^d$, the gradient flow $\dot x=-\nabla h(x)$ is discretised either explicitly (gradient descent) or implicitly (a proximal step, a small minimisation problem). Only the implicit step makes sense with nothing but a distance.
  2. The JKO step (§2). Replace the Euclidean distance by $W_2$: each step finds a measure with lower energy that is close to the current one in transport distance. For a Dirac it is the proximal step; for the entropy and a Gaussian it can be computed by hand.
  3. Derivatives on measures (§3–§4). The first variation $\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$ (perturb by mixing) is a function; its gradient $\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$ (perturb by moving) is the Wasserstein gradient. Letting $\tau\to0$ gives the PDE $\partial_t\al_t=\dv(\al_t\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al})$: transport, heat, aggregation, Fokker–Planck.
  4. Particles and noise (§5–§6). Started from $n$ Diracs, the flow is an ODE for $n$ particles — exactly gradient descent on their positions, sped up by $n$. Energies with an entropy are not defined on particles: noise replaces them, and the Fokker–Planck flow becomes the Langevin diffusion, the engine of sampling and of diffusion models.
  5. How fast (§7). Convexity along $W_2$ geodesics (displacement convexity) plays the role of convexity: $O(1/t)$ rates, and $e^{-2\lambda t}$ under $\lambda$-convexity. For KL, the rate is the log-Sobolev inequality.
  6. Machine-learning energies (§8–§9). MMD, energy distance and Sinkhorn are defined on particle clouds and give generative particle flows; Gaussian families give finite-dimensional flows of means and covariances (Bures–Wasserstein), used in variational inference.
  7. Learning systems as flows of measures (§10–§12). The neurons of a wide two-layer network, the tokens of a transformer, the cells of a developing organism: three populations moving by a velocity that depends on the whole population.

Refresher — gradient descent, convexity, strong convexity, Grönwall

A differentiable $h:\R^d\to\R$ is convex if it lies above its tangents: $h(y)\ge h(x)+\ps{\nabla h(x)}{y-x}$ for all $x,y$. It is $\lambda$-strongly convex ($\lambda>0$) if it lies above tangent parabolas: $h(y)\ge h(x)+\ps{\nabla h(x)}{y-x}+\frac\lambda2\norm{y-x}^2$; for a $C^2$ function this means $\nabla^2h\succeq\lambda\Id$. Example: $h(x)=\frac L2\norm{x}^2$ is $L$-strongly convex. Gradient descent $x_{k+1}=x_k-\tau\nabla h(x_k)$ is stable when $\nabla h$ is $L$-Lipschitz and $\tau<2/L$.

Grönwall’s lemma. If $E'(t)\le-cE(t)$ for $t\ge0$, then $E(t)\le E(0)e^{-ct}$. Proof: $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\big(e^{ct}E(t)\big)=e^{ct}\big(E'+cE\big)\le0$, so $e^{ct}E(t)$ is non-increasing. Used in §1 and §7 (rates), §6 (Langevin). more · convexity

Refresher — push-forward and the continuity equation

The push-forward $\pf{T}{\al}$ moves each bit of mass of $\al$ from $x$ to $T(x)$: $\int g\,\dd(\pf{T}{\al})=\int g(T(x))\,\dd\al(x)$; if $X\sim\al$ then $T(X)\sim\pf{T}{\al}$. Now let every point move along a velocity field: $\dot x(t)=v_t(x(t))$, and let $\al_t$ be the law of $x(t)$ when $x(0)\sim\al_0$. Then $\al_t$ solves the continuity equation

$$\partial_t\al_t+\dv(\al_tv_t)=0,$$

which means, in weak form, $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\int\varphi\,\dd\al_t=\int\ps{\nabla\varphi}{v_t}\,\dd\al_t$ for all smooth test functions $\varphi$.

Why: $\int\varphi\,\dd\al_t=\E[\varphi(x(t))]$ and $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\varphi(x(t))=\ps{\nabla\varphi(x(t))}{v_t(x(t))}$ (chain rule). The strong form follows by one integration by parts. Mass is conserved: it is moved, never created. Particle (Lagrangian) and density (Eulerian) descriptions are the same object: the PDE for $\al_t$ is equivalent to the ODE $\dot x=v_t(x)$ for its particles. Used everywhere from §2 on. This is the language of Lesson 6 (dynamic OT, Benamou–Brenier). more · push-forward

Refresher — $W_2$, Brenier maps, McCann interpolation, quantiles (Lessons 1–3)

$W_2^2(\al,\be)=\min_{\pi\in\U(\al,\be)}\int\norm{x-y}^2\dd\pi$. Brenier: if $\al$ has a density, the optimal plan is a map $T=\nabla\varphi$ with $\varphi$ convex, and $W_2^2(\al,\be)=\int\norm{x-T(x)}^2\dd\al$. McCann interpolation: $\al_s=\pf{\big((1-s)\Id+sT\big)}{\al}$, $s\in[0,1]$, is a constant-speed geodesic: $W_2(\al_s,\al_r)=\abs{s-r}\,W_2(\al,\be)$ (Lesson 3, §6). On the line: $W_2^2(\al,\be)=\int_0^1\abs{Q_\al(s)-Q_\be(s)}^2\dd s$ with the quantile functions $Q=C^{-1}$ (Lesson 3 refresher). Gaussians: $W_2^2\big(\N(m,\sigma^2),\N(m',\sigma'^2)\big)=(m-m')^2+(\sigma-\sigma')^2$ on $\R$, and $\norm{m-m'}^2+\B(\Sigma,\Sigma')^2$ in $\R^d$ (Lesson 3 refresher). Used in §2 (JKO), §3 (gradient of $W_2^2$), §7 (geodesic convexity), §9 (Gaussians). more

Refresher — Kantorovich potentials for the quadratic cost (Lesson 4)

For the cost $c(x,y)=\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$, duality reads $\frac12W_2^2(\al,\be)=\max_{\varphi(x)+\psi(y)\le c(x,y)}\int\varphi\,\dd\al+\int\psi\,\dd\be$. At the optimum, $\frac12\norm{x}^2-\varphi(x)=u(x)$ is convex and its gradient is the Brenier map: $T=\nabla u=\Id-\nabla\varphi$, i.e. $\nabla\varphi(x)=x-T(x)$. Used in §3 (the first variation of $\frac12W_2^2(\cdot,\be)$ is $\varphi$) and in Exercise 6 of the sheet. more

Refresher — divergence, Laplacian, integration by parts

For a vector field $w=(w_1,\dots,w_d)$, $\dv w=\sum_i\partial_iw_i$; the Laplacian is $\Delta g=\dv(\nabla g)=\sum_i\partial_i^2g$. Integration by parts (no boundary term when $w$ or $\varphi$ decays fast enough, or is compactly supported):

$$\int\varphi\,\dv w\,\dd x=-\int\ps{\nabla\varphi}{w}\,\dd x .$$

Two identities used again and again: $\dv(\rho\nabla\log\rho)=\dv(\nabla\rho)=\Delta\rho$ (because $\rho\nabla\log\rho=\nabla\rho$), and $\dv(\rho\,\nabla g)=\ps{\nabla\rho}{\nabla g}+\rho\,\Delta g$. Used in §3 (Proposition 15.7), §4 (heat and Fokker–Planck), §6 (Langevin). more

Refresher — entropy, KL, Fisher information, with Gaussian formulas

For $\al=\rho\,\dd x$, the neg-entropy is $\int\rho\log\rho\,\dd x$ (minus the Shannon entropy; it is small for spread-out densities). The relative entropy is $\KL(\al|\be)=\int\log\frac{\dd\al}{\dd\be}\dd\al\ge0$ (Jensen), with equality iff $\al=\be$. The relative Fisher information is $\mathcal I(\al|\be)=\int\norm{\nabla\log\frac{\dd\al}{\dd\be}}^2\dd\al$. Gaussian formulas on $\R$ (all used below):

$$\int\rho\log\rho=-\log\sigma-\tfrac12\log(2\pi e)\quad\text{for }\N(m,\sigma^2),$$ $$\begin{aligned}\KL\big(\N(m,s^2)\,|\,\N(0,1)\big)&=\tfrac12\big(s^2+m^2-1-\log s^2\big),\\ \mathcal I\big(\N(m,s^2)\,|\,\N(0,1)\big)&=m^2+\big(s-\tfrac1s\big)^2 .\end{aligned}$$

(For the last one: $\nabla\log\frac{\rho}{\rho_\be}(x)=-\frac{x-m}{s^2}+x$; with $x=m+sZ$ this is $m+(s-\frac1s)Z$, whose mean square is $m^2+(s-\frac1s)^2$.) more · $f$-divergences

Refresher — Brownian motion and stochastic differential equations, just what is needed

A standard Brownian motion $B_t$ in $\R^d$ starts at $0$, has independent increments, and $B_t-B_s\sim\N(0,(t-s)\Id)$. The SDE $\dd X_t=b(X_t)\,\dd t+\sqrt2\,\dd B_t$ means $X_t=X_0+\int_0^tb(X_s)\dd s+\sqrt2B_t$; it is simulated by the Euler–Maruyama scheme $X_{k+1}=X_k+h\,b(X_k)+\sqrt{2h}\,\xi_k$ with independent $\xi_k\sim\N(0,\Id)$. The one fact behind every computation of §6: over a short time $h$, the noise moves the point by $O(\sqrt h)$ (much more than the drift, $O(h)$), and its second-order effect is deterministic: $\E\big[\ps{\nabla^2\varphi\,\sqrt2B_h}{\sqrt2B_h}\big]=2h\,\Delta\varphi$. The law $\al_t$ of $X_t$ then solves the Fokker–Planck equation $\partial_t\al_t=-\dv(\al_tb)+\Delta\al_t$. more · Fokker–Planck

Refresher — kernels and the maximum mean discrepancy

A symmetric kernel $k$ is positive definite if $\sum_{i,j}c_ic_jk(x_i,x_j)\ge0$ for all points and coefficients. The MMD between two measures is $\mathrm{MMD}_k^2(\al,\be)=\iint k\,\dd(\al-\be)\,\dd(\al-\be)=\iint k\,\dd\al\dd\al-2\iint k\,\dd\al\dd\be+\iint k\,\dd\be\dd\be$. Examples: the Gaussian kernel $e^{-\norm{x-y}^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ and the energy-distance kernel $k(x,y)=-\norm{x-y}$, which is only conditionally positive definite (non-negative on zero-mass signed measures), enough to make $\mathrm{MMD}^2\ge0$. MMD is defined for any measures, including point clouds. Used in §5 and §8. more

Notation decoder

The resources use slightly different notation, and three of their conventions change the clock of a flow by a factor 2 (see the last rows). This page follows the book (OT4ML), except for the Wasserstein gradient, written $\nabla_W$ as on the exercise sheet.

SymbolReadMeaning, and other notations in the resources
$\Pc_2(\R^d)$“measures with finite second moment”Probability measures with $\int\norm{x}^2\dd\al<\infty$; the space where $W_2$ is finite (the sheet writes $\mathsf W_2(\R^d)$).
$\al,\ \al_t,\ \rho,\ \rho_t$“the measure, at time $t$; its density”$\al=\rho\,\dd x$ when $\al$ has a density. StatOT writes $\mu$, and often identifies $\mu$ with its density.
$f(\al)$“the energy”A function of a measure, to be minimised (entropy, KL, MMD, risk of a network). The syllabus and many papers write $F(\rho)$, CompOT $F(\al)$, StatOT $\mathcal F(\mu)$.
$\tau$, $\al^{(\ell)}$“step size”, “$\ell$-th JKO iterate”JKO time step; $\al^{(\ell)}$ approximates $\al_t$ at $t=\ell\tau$.
$\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al)(x)$“first variation of $f$ at $\al$”A function of $x$, defined up to a constant (§3). OT4ML writes $\delta f(\al)$, CompOT $F'(\al)$, StatOT $\delta\mathcal F(\mu)$.
$\nabla_Wf(\al)$“Wasserstein gradient”The vector field $\nabla_x\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al)$ (§3). The sheet writes $\nabla_{\mathsf W}f$; OT4ML and StatOT use a double-struck nabla.
$\dv$, $\Delta$“divergence”, “Laplacian”See the refresher above.
$V$, $\be=Z^{-1}e^{-V}\dd x$“potential”, “Gibbs measure”$\be$ is the target of the Fokker–Planck flow (StatOT calls it $\pi$). In §11, $V$ is also the value matrix of attention.
$H,\ W,\ I$“entropy, distance, Fisher information”In §7: $H=\KL(\al|\be)$, $W=W_2(\al,\be)$, $I=\mathcal I(\al|\be)$.
$\lambda$, $\kappa$“convexity constant”, “PL constant”$\lambda$-geodesic convexity; Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality with constant $\kappa$ (§7).
$n$, $X=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$, $F(X)$“particles”$F(X)=f\big(\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}\big)$: the energy seen as a function of the positions (§5).
$k(x,y)$, $W(x-y)$“interaction kernel”Energies $\iint k\,\dd\al\dd\al$ (OT4ML) or $\frac12\iint W(x-y)\dd\al\dd\al$ (StatOT): the factor $\frac12$ removes a factor 2 from the gradient.
$B_t$, $h$“Brownian motion”, “time step”Langevin dynamics and its Euler–Maruyama discretisation (§6).
$\N(m,\Sigma)$, $\B$“Gaussian”, “Bures”Gaussian closures (§9); on $\R$ the Gaussians are the half-plane of pairs $(m,\sigma)$.
$x=(u,v)$“a neuron”OT4ML §15.4: $u\in\R^d$ inner weights, $v$ outer weight, feature $v\,\sigma(\ps{u}{z})$. The sheet’s Exercise 5 swaps the roles: $u\in\R$ outer, $v\in\R^d$ inner.
$Q,K,V$, $\eps$“query, key, value”, “bandwidth”Attention (§11); $\eps$ is the width of the Gaussian kernel $e^{-\norm{x-y}^2/(2\eps)}$ (Geshkovski et al. use $\be=1/\eps$).
clock conventions“factors of 2”(1) CompOT’s step $\argmin W_2^2+\tau F$ is a JKO step of size $\tau/2$. (2) $\iint k\,\dd\al\dd\al$ has gradient $2\int\nabla_xk$, $\frac12\iint W$ has $\int\nabla W$. (3) Gradient descent on positions, $\dot x_i=-\nabla_{x_i}F$, runs the Wasserstein flow $n$ times slower. Same paths, different speeds.
