6. Noise instead of entropy: Langevin dynamics StatOT §6.2 · OT4ML p. 364

The Fokker–Planck flow of $\KL(\cdot|e^{-V})$ is also the law of the Langevin diffusion $\dd X_t=-\nabla V(X_t)\dd t+\sqrt2\,\dd B_t$: independent noisy particles replace the entropy term that point clouds cannot evaluate; discretised by Euler–Maruyama it is the basic sampling algorithm (with a bias of order $h$), and the same flow, run backwards with a learned score, is a diffusion model.

Why noise

The entropy is $+\infty$ on a point cloud, so the particle method of §5 cannot run the heat or Fokker–Planck flows. But there is another way to make a cloud spread exactly like the heat equation: shake each point independently. The law of $\sqrt2B_t$ is $\N(0,2t\Id)$, the heat kernel of $\partial_t\rho=\Delta\rho$. Adding a drift $-\nabla V$ gives the Fokker–Planck equation. The course notes of 2019 summarise the trade-off in one line:

In the course CourseOT (2019), §10.4, p. 42

Langevin Flows. 1 random particles as opposed to many deterministic particles. Crucial in high dimension.

Read it as: to follow a Fokker–Planck flow, either move many particles together with a velocity that depends on their density (deterministic, but the density must be estimated — impossible in high dimension), or move each particle alone with noise (no density needed; the law of a single particle is the solution).

The Langevin diffusion follows the Wasserstein gradient flow of KL

In the course StatOT Thm. 6.11, Cors. 6.12–6.14, p. 186

Theorem 6.11 (Fokker–Planck equation). The marginal law $\mu_t:=\mathrm{law}(X_t)$ of the Langevin diffusion with potential $V$ is given by the solution to the Fokker–Planck equation $\partial_t\mu_t=\Delta\mu_t+\dv(\mu_t\nabla V)$.

Corollary 6.12. The marginal law of the Langevin diffusion with potential $V$ is the Wasserstein gradient flow of $\KL(\cdot\,\|\,\pi)$, where $\pi$ has density proportional to $\exp(-V)$.

Corollary 6.13. If $(\mu_t)_{t\ge0}$ is the marginal law of a (rescaled) Brownian motion $(\sqrt2B_t)_{t\ge0}$, then $(\mu_t)_{t\ge0}$ solves the heat equation $\partial_t\mu_t=\Delta\mu_t$, and it is the Wasserstein gradient flow of the entropy functional $\mathcal H$.

Corollary 6.14. Let $\pi$ be an $\al$-strongly log-concave measure, and let $(\mu_t)_{t\ge0}$ denote the marginal law of the Langevin diffusion with stationary distribution $\pi$. Then, $\KL(\mu_t\,\|\,\pi)\le e^{-2\al t}\KL(\mu_0\,\|\,\pi)$.

Langevin diffusion
The SDE $\dd X_t=-\nabla V(X_t)\dd t+\sqrt2\,\dd B_t$ (StatOT p. 184): gradient descent on $V$ plus Brownian noise of variance $2\,\dd t$ per unit time.
$\mu_t$
The law of the random position $X_t$: what you would see by running many independent copies and making a histogram.
$\pi\propto\exp(-V)$
The target (the Gibbs measure $\be$ of §4). StatOT’s $\al$ in Corollary 6.14 is a number, the strong log-concavity constant ($\nabla^2V\succeq\al\Id$) — our $\lambda$.
Cor. 6.14
Exponential convergence in KL, at a rate fixed by the curvature of $V$; proved in §7 (it is the log-Sobolev inequality).
Idea of the proof

Follow the expectation of a test function. Over a short time $t$, the noise moves $X$ by $O(\sqrt t)$, so a second-order Taylor expansion is needed; the second-order term of the noise has a deterministic mean, $t\,\Delta\varphi$. This gives the generator $\varphi\mapsto\Delta\varphi-\ps{\nabla\varphi}{\nabla V}$; two integrations by parts move it onto the law: Fokker–Planck. Then §4 identifies Fokker–Planck with the gradient flow of KL.

Proof (StatOT’s informal argument, pp. 184–186)
  1. Condition on $X_0=x_0$: $X_t=x_0-\int_0^t\nabla V(X_s)\dd s+\sqrt2B_t$, with $\int_0^t\nabla V=O(t)$ and $B_t=O(\sqrt t)$.Integral form of the SDE; $B_t\sim\N(0,t\Id)$ (refresher).
  2. $$\begin{aligned}\varphi(X_t)=\varphi(x_0)&+\Big\langle\nabla\varphi(x_0),-t\nabla V(x_0)+\sqrt2B_t\Big\rangle\\&+\ps{\nabla^2\varphi(x_0)B_t}{B_t}+O(t^{3/2}).\end{aligned}$$Taylor expansion of $\varphi$ to second order; in the quadratic term only $\sqrt2B_t$ is large enough to matter: $\frac12\ps{\nabla^2\varphi\,\sqrt2B_t}{\sqrt2B_t}=\ps{\nabla^2\varphi\,B_t}{B_t}$.
  3. $$\E\varphi(X_t)=\varphi(x_0)+t\big(\Delta\varphi(x_0)-\ps{\nabla\varphi(x_0)}{\nabla V(x_0)}\big)+O(t^{3/2}).$$$\E B_t=0$ and $\E[B_tB_t\T]=t\Id$, so $\E\ps{\nabla^2\varphi B_t}{B_t}=t\tr\nabla^2\varphi=t\Delta\varphi$.
  4. $\partial_t\E\varphi(X_t)=\E[\mathcal L\varphi(X_t)]$ with $\mathcal L\varphi=\Delta\varphi-\ps{\nabla\varphi}{\nabla V}$, i.e. $\int\varphi\,\partial_t\mu_t=\int(\Delta\varphi-\ps{\nabla\varphi}{\nabla V})\dd\mu_t$.Line 3 at every time, by the Markov property (restart from $X_t$).
  5. $$\int(\Delta\varphi-\ps{\nabla\varphi}{\nabla V})\,\mu_t\,\dd x=\int\varphi\,\big(\Delta\mu_t+\dv(\mu_t\nabla V)\big)\dd x .$$Integrate by parts twice in the first term, once in the second (refresher).
  6. Since this holds for every $\varphi$: $\partial_t\mu_t=\Delta\mu_t+\dv(\mu_t\nabla V)$, which is the Wasserstein gradient flow of $\KL(\cdot|\pi)$ (§4). With $V=0$: the heat equation, flow of the entropy. $\square$Comparison with (6.9) in StatOT, our §4.

One flow, three representations

In the course StatOT §6.2.1, p. 187

Ultimately, we have the equivalence (FP) $\Leftrightarrow$ (LD) $\Leftrightarrow$ (WGF) in the sense that they correspond to the same curve on the space of probability measures — clearly at the particle level, they are different — but these three differing perspectives provide new avenues for algorithm design and theoretical study.

(FP) is the PDE, (LD) the Langevin diffusion, (WGF) the deterministic particles $\dot x=-\nabla\log\frac{\mu_t}{\pi}(x)=-\nabla V(x)-\nabla\log\mu_t(x)$ — the third form of OT4ML (15.10). They agree as curves of measures but not as particles: Langevin paths are rough and cross each other; deterministic particles are smooth and, in one dimension, keep their order (each follows a quantile of $\mu_t$). Only (LD) needs no knowledge of $\mu_t$.

Euler–Maruyama, and why it is biased

In the course StatOT §6.2.2, (6.11), p. 188

[…] non-asymptotic convergence guarantees can be established for the Euler–Maruyama scheme

$$X_{k+1}=X_k-h\nabla V(X_k)+\N(0,2h\Id),\qquad k=0,1,2,\dots,\qquad(6.11)$$

which parallels the complexity theory for optimization. Unlike the situation in optimization, however, the discretization (6.11) is asymptotically biased: the stationary distribution of the Markov chain (6.11) does not equal the target $\pi$.

Worked example — the bias of Euler–Maruyama on the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck target

Target $\N(0,1)$, $V=\frac12x^2$: the chain is $X_{k+1}=(1-h)X_k+\sqrt{2h}\,\xi_k$. If $X_k\sim\N(m_k,v_k)$, then $X_{k+1}$ is Gaussian with $m_{k+1}=(1-h)m_k$ and $v_{k+1}=(1-h)^2v_k+2h$. The fixed point solves $v=(1-h)^2v+2h$:

$$v_\infty=\frac{2h}{1-(1-h)^2}=\frac{2h}{2h-h^2}=\frac1{1-h/2}.$$

For $h=0.01,\ 0.05,\ 0.1,\ 0.2,\ 0.4$: $v_\infty=1.0050,\ 1.0256,\ 1.0526,\ 1.1111,\ 1.2500$ instead of $1$. The bias is of order $h/2$, whatever the number of particles: more particles reduce the Monte Carlo noise, not the bias. StatOT (p. 189) explains it as a mismatched splitting: the step is a gradient step on $V$ followed by the exact heat flow of the entropy, and “the combination of gradient descent and gradient flow does not produce an unbiased splitting scheme” — a JKO (proximal) step on the entropy would remove the bias, but it is intractable.

In machine learning — sampling a posterior with Langevin dynamics

In Bayesian learning, the posterior over parameters $\theta$ is $\pi(\theta)\propto e^{-V(\theta)}$ with $V(\theta)=-\log p(\text{data}|\theta)-\log p(\theta)$: known up to its normalising constant, which is all Langevin needs. The Euler–Maruyama chain $\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-h\nabla V(\theta_k)+\sqrt{2h}\,\xi_k$ (the unadjusted Langevin algorithm) is gradient descent on the negative log-posterior plus calibrated noise; by Corollary 6.12 its law follows the Wasserstein gradient flow of $\KL(\cdot|\pi)$, and by Corollary 6.14 it converges exponentially fast when $V$ is strongly convex — up to the $O(h)$ bias of the worked example. StatOT §6.2 (pp. 183–193) develops this “sampling as optimization” view: proximal samplers, mirror and Newton Langevin, underdamped (kinetic) Langevin as the analogue of Nesterov acceleration. (Going further: with minibatch gradients instead of $\nabla V$, the same chain is stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics, Welling and Teh, 2011.)

In machine learning — diffusion models run a Fokker–Planck flow backwards

Noising is a Wasserstein gradient flow. Score-based diffusion models (Song et al., 2021, cited in OT4ML §16.1) push the data distribution $\al_0$ towards $\N(0,\Id)$ by an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, $\dd X_t=-X_t\dd t+\sqrt2\dd B_t$ (up to a choice of time scale): by Corollary 6.12, the laws $\al_t$ follow the Wasserstein gradient flow of $\KL(\cdot|\N(0,\Id))$, and converge exponentially (§7). Generating runs it backwards. The deterministic representation (15.10) of this flow is $\dot x=-x-\nabla\log\rho_t(x)$ — the probability-flow ODE; integrated backwards in time from Gaussian noise, $\dot y=y+\nabla\log\rho_{T-s}(y)$, it transports $\N(0,\Id)$ back to the data (OT4ML p. 411 calls $\frac{\sigma_t^2}2\nabla\log\rho_t$ “the familiar score correction relating diffusion SDEs to probability-flow ODEs”). The only unknown is the score $\nabla\log\rho_t$, learned by denoising score matching (§4). Concretely, for images: $x$ is an image, $\rho_0$ the data distribution, a network $s_\theta(x,t)$ is trained to predict $\nabla\log\rho_t(x)$ from noisy images, and sampling integrates the backward ODE (or the backward SDE) with $s_\theta$.

Note on the sources — a stray “+” in StatOT

In the Taylor expansion of StatOT p. 185, the line “$\varphi(X_t)=\varphi(x_0)-t\ps{\nabla\varphi(x_0)}{\nabla V(x_0)}++\sqrt2\ps{\nabla\varphi(x_0)}{B_t}$” has one “+” too many. Harmless: the two lines around it are correct (line 2 of the proof above).

Pitfall — same law, different particles

“Langevin particles follow the Wasserstein gradient flow” is a statement about laws. A single Langevin path is not a gradient-flow trajectory of anything (it is not even differentiable); the deterministic particles $\dot x=-\nabla V-\nabla\log\mu_t$ are, but they need the density $\mu_t$. Do not mix the two pictures when you compute a velocity.

Reflexes
  • Entropy in the energy, particles in the algorithm → replace $-\nabla\log\rho$ by Brownian noise: $\dd X=-\nabla V\dd t+\sqrt2\dd B$.
  • Euler–Maruyama on a Gaussian target → track $(m_k,v_k)$ exactly; stationary variance $\frac1{1-h/2}$.
  • Diffusion model → forward: Fokker–Planck flow of $\KL(\cdot|\N(0,\Id))$; backward: probability-flow ODE with a learned score.
