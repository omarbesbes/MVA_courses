10. Training a two-layer network: a Wasserstein flow of its neurons OT4ML §15.4, pp. 386–390

Write a wide two-layer network as an average over its neurons: it becomes linear in the empirical measure of the neurons, gradient descent on the weights becomes the Wasserstein gradient flow of a convex (along mixtures) risk, and global convergence becomes a question about where the neurons can go: if they see every direction, a stationary point is a global minimiser.

Neurons as particles

The loss of a neural network is not convex in its weights, and permuting the hidden neurons does not change the function: the weights are a set of neurons, not a vector. Both difficulties disappear when the network is written as an average over its neurons — the mean-field parametrisation — because an average only depends on the empirical measure of the neurons.

Wasserstein training of two-layer MLPs. The key modeling step is to forget the ordering of the hidden neurons and to regard the network weights as a probability measure on parameter space. […] We use $z\in\R^d$ for the input data and $y\in\R^{d'}$ for the label. A neuron is a particle $x=(u,v)\in\R^d\times\R^{d'}$, where $u$ is the inner weight and $v$ is the outer vector weight. For a scalar nonlinearity $\sigma$, define the vector-valued feature $\psi(x,z)=v\,\sigma(\ps{u}{z})\in\R^{d'}$. The width-$n$ network and its mean-field version are

$$\begin{aligned}G_X(z)&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\psi(x_i,z),\\ G_\al(z)&=\int\psi(x,z)\,\dd\al(x),\qquad\al=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}.\end{aligned}$$

[…] Let $\zeta$ be a probability distribution on data-label pairs $(z,y)$. The population risk is $f(\al)=\int\ell(G_\al(z),y)\,\dd\zeta(z,y)$ […]. Since $\al\mapsto G_\al$ is linear, $f$ is convex as a function of $\al$ whenever $\ell(\cdot,y)$ is convex. For the empirical neuron law $\al_X=n^{-1}\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$, the Wasserstein metric induces on particles the rescaled metric $n^{-1}\sum_i\norm{\dot x_i}^2$. The corresponding particle flow is $\dot x_i=-n\nabla_{x_i}F(X)$, $F(X)=f\big(\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}\big)$, which is the gradient flow of $F(X)=f(\al_X)$ for this Wasserstein particle metric, equivalently Euclidean gradient descent with the time scale multiplied by $n$.

[…] The first variation is $\delta f(\al)(x)=\int\ps{\nabla_1\ell(G_\al(z),y)}{\psi(x,z)}\dd\zeta(z,y)$, (15.28) and the Wasserstein gradient in parameter space is $\nabla_Wf(\al)(x)=\nabla_x\delta f(\al)(x)=\int[D_x\psi(x,z)]\T\nabla_1\ell(G_\al(z),y)\,\dd\zeta(z,y)$.

$x=(u,v)$
One hidden neuron: its input weights $u$ and its output weight $v$ (the sheet’s Exercise 5 swaps the letters, see the notation decoder).
$\frac1n\sum_i$
The mean-field normalisation: the output is an average of the neurons’ contributions, so the network depends on the neurons only through their empirical measure $\al$, and $n\to\infty$ makes sense ($\al$ becomes a density over neuron space: an infinitely wide network).
“convex as a function of $\al$”
Along mixtures: $G_\al$ is linear in $\al$ and the loss is convex in the prediction. This convexity is hidden by the parametrisation by weights, and it is not geodesic convexity (§7).
$\delta f(\al)(x)$
For the square loss, the correlation between the current residual $G_\al(z)-y$ and the feature $\psi(x,\cdot)$ of a candidate neuron $x$: adding a bit of neuron $x$ helps when this correlation is negative.
$\dot x_i=-n\nabla_{x_i}F$
Exactly (15.5) of §5: backpropagation on the weights is the Wasserstein flow of the neurons, up to the factor $n$ in the clock. StatOT Prop. 6.16 (p. 202) states the same: the Wasserstein gradient flow started at the empirical measure of the weights stays empirical and follows the (time-rescaled) Euclidean gradient flow.
In the course OT4ML (15.29)–(15.30), p. 387

For the squared Euclidean loss $\ell(s,y)=\frac12\norm{s-y}^2$, the energy is the sum of a quadratic interaction and a linear potential:

$$\begin{aligned}f(\al)=\frac12\iint k(x,x')\dd\al(x)\dd\al(x')&+\int g(x)\dd\al(x)\\&+\frac12\int\norm{y}^2\dd\zeta(z,y),\end{aligned}\qquad(15.29)$$

with $k(x,x')=\int\ps{\psi(x,z)}{\psi(x',z)}\dd\zeta(z,y)$, $g(x)=-\int\ps{y}{\psi(x,z)}\dd\zeta(z,y)$. (15.30) […] These kernels are generally not convex in the particle variable, so the geodesic-convex convergence theory above does not apply directly.

So the neurons are interacting particles (§5): they repel each other when they do the same job (the kernel $k$ is large for neurons with similar features and outputs of the same sign) and they are attracted by the potential $g$, low where the feature correlates with the target.

Worked example — the interaction between two ReLU neurons

Take scalar outputs, $\sigma=\mathrm{ReLU}$ and Gaussian inputs $z\sim\N(0,\Id_2)$. For neurons $x=(u,v)$, $x'=(u',v')$ whose input weights make an angle $\theta$,

$$\begin{aligned}k(x,x')&=vv'\,\E\big[\sigma(\ps{u}{z})\,\sigma(\ps{u'}{z})\big]\\&=vv'\,\norm{u}\norm{u'}\,\frac{\sin\theta+(\pi-\theta)\cos\theta}{2\pi}\end{aligned}$$

(the “arc-cosine kernel”; a classical Gaussian computation). Values of the fraction for $\theta=0,\frac\pi3,\frac\pi2,\frac{2\pi}3,\pi$: $0.5,\ 0.3045,\ 0.1592,\ 0.0545,\ 0$ (Monte Carlo with $2\times10^6$ samples: $0.5,\ 0.3042,\ 0.1588,\ 0.0544,\ 0$). Two neurons with the same direction and outputs of the same sign interact most; opposite directions do not interact at all (their ReLUs are never active together).

When is a stationary point a global minimiser?

The risk is convex along mixtures, but the flow moves along transport paths and only moves the mass that is already there: it can stop at a measure that is not optimal because no neuron sits where a new one would help. The book gives two answers.

In the course OT4ML Prop. 15.51, p. 388

Proposition 15.51 (Affine convexity and stationary positive densities). Let $\Omega\subset\R^d$ be connected, let $f=Q+\int V\dd\al+C$ be as above on $\Pc(\Omega)$, and assume that $Q$ is classically convex. Suppose that a Wasserstein gradient flow $\al_t$ of $f$ converges to $\al_\infty=\rho_\infty\dd x$, where $\rho_\infty>0$ almost everywhere on $\Omega$, and that $\inf_{t\ge0}f(\al_t)>-\infty$. Assume that $\delta f(\al_\infty)\in C^1(\Omega)$ and that the squared Wasserstein slope is lower semicontinuous along the convergence […]. Then $\al_\infty$ is a global minimizer of $f$.

Proof (OT4ML pp. 388–389)
  1. $\int_0^\infty\!\!\int_\Omega\norm{\nabla\delta f(\al_t)}^2\dd\al_t\,\dd t<+\infty$.Integrate the dissipation identity (§4): the total decrease of $f$ is finite because $f$ is bounded below.
  2. $\int\norm{\nabla\delta f(\al_\infty)}^2\dd\al_\infty=0$.Line 1 forces the inner integral to tend to $0$ along a sequence $t_n\to\infty$; then the assumed lower semicontinuity.
  3. $\nabla\delta f(\al_\infty)=0$ on $\Omega$, so $\delta f(\al_\infty)$ is a constant $c$.$\rho_\infty>0$ almost everywhere and the gradient is continuous; $\Omega$ is connected.
  4. For every $\be$: $f(\be)\ge f(\al_\infty)+\int\delta f(\al_\infty)\dd(\be-\al_\infty)=f(\al_\infty)+c-c=f(\al_\infty)$.Convexity of $f$ along mixtures (the classical convexity of $Q$) gives the tangent inequality.

“Strict positivity is essential to this argument: without it, stationarity controls the first variation only on the support explored by the limiting measure” (p. 389).

Proposition 15.52 (Formal global optimality for two-homogeneous mean-field flows). Assume that the feature is positively two-homogeneous in the neuron variable, $\psi(\lambda x,z)=\lambda^2\psi(x,z)$ $(\lambda>0)$, and that $f(\al)=J(G_\al)$ with $J$ convex and differentiable as a functional of the predictor. Let $\al$ be a smooth stationary point of the Wasserstein flow, so that $\nabla_x\delta f(\al)(x)=0$ on $\supp(\al)$. Assume also full directional support: for every unit direction $\omega$, the support of $\al$ intersects the ray $\{\lambda\omega:\lambda>0\}$. Then $\al$ is a global minimizer of $f$ over the mean-field model class.

two-homogeneous
For a ReLU neuron, $\psi((\lambda u,\lambda v),z)=\lambda v\,\sigma(\lambda\ps{u}{z})=\lambda^2\psi((u,v),z)$: scaling a neuron by $\lambda$ scales its output by $\lambda^2$. Only the direction of a neuron matters for what it can do; its norm is an amount.
full directional support
Every direction of neuron space is represented by at least one neuron of the limit. This is what replaces the positive density of Proposition 15.51.
rigorous version
“The rigorous Chizat–Bach theorem replaces the full directional support assumption by propagation and overparameterization hypotheses ensuring that any negative first-variation direction is represented by the evolving support” (p. 390): it assumes that the initialisation covers all directions and that the flow converges.
Idea of the proof

The first variation $h=\delta f(\al)$ is itself two-homogeneous. Stationarity on a ray gives $h=0$ along it (radial derivative $2rh(\omega)=0$). If some competitor were better, convexity in $\al$ would produce a direction $\omega$ with $h(\omega)<0$; but that ray meets the support, where $h=0$ — contradiction.

A certificate you can compute. For a stationary $\al$, global optimality holds iff $h\ge0$ everywhere. For ReLU neurons with scalar output, $h(u,v)=v\,\E\big[(G_\al(z)-y)\,\sigma(\ps{u}{z})\big]=v\,\norm{u}\,g(\hat u)$ with

$$g(\theta)=\E\big[(G_\al(z)-y)\,\sigma(\ps{u_\theta}{z})\big],\qquad u_\theta=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),$$

and since $v$ can have either sign, optimality means $g\equiv0$ on the circle of directions: the residual is orthogonal to every ReLU feature. If $g(\theta)\ne0$, a new neuron in direction $\theta$ with outer weight of sign $-g(\theta)$ lowers the risk.

When a neuron cannot change the sign of its output weight (derived here)

Along the flow, each ReLU neuron keeps $c_i=\norm{u_i}^2-v_i^2$ constant: $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\big(\norm{u}^2-v^2\big)=2\ps{u}{\dot u}-2v\dot v=-2v\,\E\big[r\,\ind_{\ps uz>0}\ps uz\big]+2v\,\E\big[r\,\sigma(\ps uz)\big]=0$, with $r=G_\al-y$, because $\ind_{s>0}\,s=\sigma(s)$. If $c_i<0$ (output weight larger than the input weights), then $v_i^2=\norm{u_i}^2-c_i\ge-c_i>0$ for ever: $v_i$ never changes sign. So if all neurons start with $v>0$ and $c_i<0$, the network can never build a negative contribution — the mechanism of the stall in the second preset of the widget ($\norm{u}\approx0.3$, $v\approx0.35$). Neurons with $c_i>0$ can cross $v=0$ (in the first preset a few do).

In machine learning — the mean-field theory of wide networks

Four papers released within a month in 2018 (Mei–Montanari–Nguyen, Sirignano–Spiliopoulos, Rotskoff–Vanden-Eijnden, Chizat–Bach; StatOT p. 208) showed that training a wide two-layer network by (stochastic) gradient descent is, as the width $n\to\infty$, a Wasserstein gradient flow of the neuron distribution, and Chizat and Bach proved that for ReLU-type (two-homogeneous) networks this flow, started from an initialisation that covers all directions, can only converge to a global minimiser — although the loss in the weights is non-convex. Two caveats that the course stresses: (1) the normalisation $\frac1n$ is essential; with $\frac1{\sqrt n}$ the weights barely move and training is in the “lazy” or neural-tangent-kernel regime, a linear model around the initialisation (StatOT p. 209); (2) the theorem says nothing about rates, and the risk is not geodesically convex, so the time to converge is not controlled. Adding entropy (noise) to the dynamics gives the mean-field Langevin dynamics, for which Mei, Montanari and Nguyen prove convergence (OT4ML p. 389).

Pitfall — “global convergence” has hypotheses

It is a statement about the infinite-width limit, about the limit $t\to\infty$, and it needs the support of the neurons to see every direction. A finite network, or an initialisation concentrated in a few directions, can stall at a stationary point that is not optimal (second preset of the widget). The certificate $g$ detects it.

Reflexes
  • Mean-field network $\frac1n\sum_i\psi(x_i,\cdot)$ → a measure of neurons; gradient descent on weights = Wasserstein flow of that measure (clock $\times n$).
  • Square loss → interaction energy (kernel $\E[\psi\psi']$) + potential ($-\E[y\psi]$).
  • Global optimality of a stationary point → the first variation $h$ must be $\ge0$ everywhere, not only on the support; for ReLU, $g\equiv0$ on the circle.

A one-neuron “network” $G(z)=v\,\sigma(\ps uz)$ fits $y(z)=\sigma(z_1)-\sigma(-z_1)=z_1$ on Gaussian inputs in $\R^2$. Can it reach zero risk? What does the certificate say at its best fit?

Answer

No: one ReLU is zero on a half-plane, while $z_1$ is not. Its best fit takes $u$ along $e_1$ and fits $z_1$ on the half-plane $z_1>0$, leaving the residual $-z_1\ind_{z_1<0}=\sigma(-z_1)$. Then $g(\pi)=\E\big[\sigma(-z_1)\cdot\sigma(-z_1)\big]=\frac12>0$: a second neuron in the direction $\theta=\pi$ with a negative output weight would lower the risk — it is the missing teacher neuron $-\sigma(-z_1)$.
