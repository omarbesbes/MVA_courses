4. The limit PDE: one equation, four classical flows OT4ML pp. 358–364

As $\tau\to0$, each JKO step moves the mass with velocity $-\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$, so the steps converge to the continuity equation $\partial_t\al_t=\dv\big(\al_t\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al_t)\big)$; with a potential it is transport by $-\nabla V$, with the entropy the heat equation, with an interaction an aggregation equation, with a KL to $e^{-V}$ the Fokker–Planck equation — and the energy always decreases at the rate $\int\norm{\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}}^2\dd\al_t$.

From the JKO step to the velocity field

In the course OT4ML §15.1, p. 359

From the JKO step to the velocity field. A first-order expansion of the JKO step explains why (15.4) uses the vector field $\nabla_Wf(\al)$. This is a formal local argument: assume that $\al_t$ is absolutely continuous and that, for the nearby competitors selected by the JKO step, the optimal map from $\al_t$ can be written as $\Id+\tau v$. For these optimal displacement representatives, $W_2^2\big(\al_t,\pf{(\Id+\tau v)}{\al_t}\big)=\tau^2\norm{v}^2_{L^2(\al_t)}$. The local JKO objective therefore reads

$$\min_v\ \frac\tau2\norm{v}^2_{L^2(\al_t)}+f\big(\pf{(\Id+\tau v)}{\al_t}\big),$$

[…] The two expansions […] give the first-order problem

$$\min_v\ f(\al_t)+\tau\int\Big[\frac12\norm{v(x)}^2+\ps{\nabla_Wf(\al_t)(x)}{v(x)}\Big]\dd\al_t(x)+o(\tau).$$

Its pointwise minimizer is $v=-\nabla_Wf(\al_t)$, which gives the velocity in the continuity equation.

competitors $\pf{(\Id+\tau v)}{\al_t}$
Near $\al_t$, look for the next step among the measures obtained by moving the mass by $\tau v$; the unknown is now a velocity field $v$.
$\frac\tau2\norm{v}^2_{L^2(\al_t)}$
The JKO price $\frac1{2\tau}W_2^2$ of that move, $\frac1{2\tau}\cdot\tau^2\norm{v}^2_{L^2}$.
“the two expansions”
$\pf{(\Id+\tau v)}{\al_t}=\al_t-\tau\dv(v\al_t)+o(\tau)$ and $f\big(\pf{(\Id+\tau v)}{\al_t}\big)=f(\al_t)+\tau\int\ps{\nabla_Wf}{v}\dd\al_t+o(\tau)$: Proposition 15.7 (§3).
“pointwise minimizer”
The integrand $\frac12\norm{v}^2+\ps{g}{v}$ is minimised, at each $x$ separately, by $v=-g$ (complete the square: $\frac12\norm{v+g}^2-\frac12\norm{g}^2$).

So each JKO step moves every bit of mass by about $-\tau\nabla_Wf$, and the staircase curve $\al_\tau(t)$ converges to a curve whose mass moves with velocity $v_t=-\nabla_Wf(\al_t)$. By the continuity equation (refresher), $\partial_t\al_t+\dv(\al_tv_t)=0$, that is

$$\boxed{\ \partial_t\al_t=\dv\Big(\al_t\,\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al_t)\Big)\ }\qquad\text{(OT4ML (15.4); StatOT Def. 5.14, p. 153).}$$
How rigorous is this?

The book calls the argument formal: it assumes that the JKO minimiser is reached by a small optimal displacement and that $f$ is smooth. The rigorous statement is a theorem about curves of maximal slope in metric spaces (OT4ML Defs. 15.8–15.9, pp. 359–360, after Ambrosio, Gigli and Savaré): under convexity, compactness and lower-semicontinuity assumptions, the staircase curves converge as $\tau\to0$, and the limit satisfies (15.4) when everything is smooth. Jordan, Kinderlehrer and Otto proved it in 1998 for the Fokker–Planck energy. On this page we use (15.4) as the definition of the Wasserstein gradient flow, as StatOT does.

A more precise version of “each step moves the mass by $-\tau\nabla_Wf$” uses the first variation of $\frac12W_2^2$ computed in §3: if $T$ is the optimal map from the new iterate $\al^{(\ell+1)}$ back to the old one, the optimality condition of the JKO step reads $\frac{T(x)-x}{\tau}=\nabla_Wf(\al^{(\ell+1)})(x)$ — a particle that ends at $x$ came from $T(x)$, moving with the velocity $-\nabla_Wf$ evaluated at the end of the step: an implicit Euler step, exactly as in §1 (derived formally in the solution of Exercise 6, §13).

The energy always decreases: dissipation

In the course StatOT (5.10), p. 153

A quick calculation using the definition of the Wasserstein gradient flow $(\mu_t)_{t\ge0}$ of $\mathcal F$ yields

$$\partial_t\mathcal F(\mu_t)=\ps{\nabla_W\mathcal F(\mu_t)}{v_t}_{\mu_t}=-\norm{\nabla_W\mathcal F(\mu_t)}^2_{\mu_t}\qquad(5.10)$$

where $v_t=-\nabla_W\mathcal F(\mu_t)$ is the tangent vector at time $t$. This equality, which states that the objective functional is dissipated at a rate equal to the squared norm of the gradient, is a generic fact about gradient flows.

  1. $$\frac{\dd}{\dd t}f(\al_t)=\int\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al_t)\,\partial_t\al_t .$$First variation with the zero-mass perturbation $\partial_t\al_t\,\dd t$ (StatOT p. 152).
  2. $$=\int\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}\,\dv\Big(\al_t\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}\Big)=-\int\Big\lVert\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al_t)\Big\rVert^2\dd\al_t\ \le0 .$$The PDE, then one integration by parts. This is fact (1) of §1, lifted to measures: the energy decreases at the rate of the squared slope.
Worked example — the dissipation rate of a Fokker–Planck flow

Take $f=\KL(\cdot|\N(0,1))$ and $\al_0=\N(2,0.5^2)$. From the refresher: $f(\al_0)=\frac12(0.25+4-1-\log0.25)=2.3181$ and the squared slope is the Fisher information $\mathcal I=m^2+(s-\frac1s)^2=4+(0.5-2)^2=6.25$. So the KL should start decreasing at the rate $6.25$. With the exact solution of the next subsections ($m_t=2e^{-t}$, $s_t^2=1-0.75e^{-2t}$), a finite difference gives $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\KL=-6.24998$ at $t=0$. ✓

Potential energy: transport by $-\nabla V$

In the course OT4ML Ex. 15.11, pp. 360–361

Example 15.11 (Linear potentials generate independent particles). Take a linear functional $f(\al)=\int h(x)\,\dd\al(x)$. Then $\delta f(\al)=h$ is independent of $\al$, and the flow (15.4) becomes $\frac{\partial\al_t}{\partial t}+\dv(-\nabla h\,\al_t)=0$. This implies particles move independently according to the usual gradient flow (15.2).

Each particle follows $\dot x=-\nabla V(x)$ and ignores all the others: the flow is the push-forward of $\al_0$ by the flow map of the ODE. Example $V(x)=\frac12x^2$: $x(t)=x_0e^{-t}$, so $\N(m,s^2)$ becomes $\N(me^{-t},s^2e^{-2t})$ — the measure shrinks towards the minimiser of $V$ and concentrates (its limit is $\delta_0$). There is no diffusion: a Dirac stays a Dirac (§2).

Entropy: the heat equation

In the course OT4ML Ex. 15.13, p. 361

Example 15.13 (Entropy generates heat and porous-medium flows). The canonical density-dependent functional is the Shannon neg-entropy $f(\al)=\int\log\big(\frac{\dd\al}{\dd x}(x)\big)\dd\al(x)$. If $\al=\rho\dd x$, then $\delta f(\al)=\log\rho+1$, up to an immaterial additive constant, so $\nabla_Wf(\al)=\nabla\log\rho$ is the score. The flow (15.4) becomes the heat equation $\partial_t\rho_t=\Delta\rho_t$. […] For a generalized entropy $f(\rho\dd x)=\int g(\rho(x))\dd x$ with a scalar convex function $g$, one obtains nonlinear diffusions in the smooth-density regime: $\frac{\partial\rho_t}{\partial t}=\Delta(P(\rho_t))$, where the pressure $P$ satisfies $P'(s)=sg''(s)$. For example, $g(s)=s\log(s)$ gives $P(s)=s$ […], while $g(s)=s^m/(m-1)$, $m>1$, gives $P(s)=s^m$ up to an additive constant and yields the porous-medium equation.

  1. $$\partial_t\rho=\dv\big(\rho\nabla(\log\rho+1)\big)=\dv\Big(\rho\,\frac{\nabla\rho}{\rho}\Big)=\dv(\nabla\rho)=\Delta\rho .$$(15.4) with the first variation of the entropy; $\rho\nabla\log\rho=\nabla\rho$ (refresher).
  2. For $\int g(\rho)$: $\rho\nabla g'(\rho)=\rho g''(\rho)\nabla\rho=\nabla P(\rho)$ with $P'(s)=sg''(s)$, so $\partial_t\rho=\dv(\nabla P(\rho))=\Delta P(\rho)$.Chain rule. For $g=s\log s$, $g''=1/s$ and $P'=1$; for $g=s^m/(m-1)$, $P'(s)=ms^{m-1}$, $P=s^m$.

A diffusion without randomness. The heat flow from $\N(m,s^2)$ is $\N(m,s^2+2t)$. Read along particles: the velocity is $-\nabla\log\rho_t(x)=\frac{x-m}{s^2+2t}$, linear in $x$, and the particle starting at $x_0$ follows $x(t)=m+(x_0-m)\sqrt{1+2t/s^2}$ — deterministic, and it never crosses another particle. With $s^2=0.25$, the particle starting at $x_0=1$ is at $3$ at $t=1$ (ODE solver: $3.000000$). Particles spread because each one is pushed down the density, i.e. along minus the score. The random picture (Brownian particles with the same law) is §6.

Pitfall — a PDE does not determine its energy, nor its metric
In the course OT4ML Rem. 15.14, p. 361

The heat equation already illustrates that a PDE does not determine a unique gradient-flow structure. […] In the Hilbert geometry of densities, the squared distance and the Dirichlet energy are $d^2_{L^2}(\al,\be)=\int\abs{\rho_\al-\rho_\be}^2\dd x$, $\mathcal D(\al)=\frac12\int\norm{\nabla\rho}^2\dd x$, so $\delta\mathcal D/\delta\rho=-\Delta\rho$ and the $L^2$ gradient flow $\partial_t\rho_t=-\delta\mathcal D/\delta\rho(\rho_t)$ gives $\partial_t\rho_t=\Delta\rho_t$. […] explaining a dynamics as a gradient flow requires specifying both an energy and a metric, and different pairs $(f,d)$ may produce the same PDE.

“The heat equation is a gradient flow” is incomplete: of $\frac12\int\norm{\nabla\rho}^2$ for $L^2$ (it decreases oscillations), and of $\int\rho\log\rho$ for $W_2$ (it spreads mass). Exercise 2 of the sheet computes both pairs for two energies (§13). Only the transport reading extends to particles and gives the rates of §7.

Interaction energy: aggregation

In the course OT4ML (15.9), pp. 361–362

Interaction energies. In a similar spirit, to obtain nonlinear evolutions without requiring the measure to have density, one can consider $f(\al):=\iint k(x,y)\,\dd\al(x)\dd\al(y)$. (15.9) For a symmetric kernel $k$: $\delta f(\al)(x)=2\int k(x,y)\dd\al(y)$, $\nabla_Wf(\al)(x)=2\int\nabla_xk(x,y)\dd\al(y)$. For $\al_0=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$, the flow (15.4) implies particles $(x_i(t))_i$ obey: $\dot x_i(t)=-\frac2n\sum_j\nabla k(x_i(t),x_j(t))$.

Each particle is pushed by all the others, through the kernel; the velocity depends on the whole measure. A positive bump $k$ (for example Gaussian) is repulsive (minimising $\iint k$ wants particles far apart); a convex growing $k$ such as $\norm{x-y}^2$ is attractive. Worked example with the variance of §3, $k=\frac14(x-y)^2$: the velocity is $-(x-m)$, so $x(t)=m+(x_0-m)e^{-t}$; the mean $m$ is conserved and the variance decays like $e^{-2t}$: the whole measure aggregates into the Dirac at its mean. Mixed kernels (repulsive at short range, attractive at long range) produce stable patterns — try them in the particle widget of §5.

KL to a Gibbs measure: the Fokker–Planck equation

In the course OT4ML Ex. 15.15, (15.10), pp. 363–364

Example 15.15 (Langevin drift as a free-energy flow). If $b=-\nabla V$, this linear Fokker–Planck equation [$\partial_t\rho_t=-\dv(b\rho_t)+\sigma^2\Delta\rho_t$] is the $W_2$ gradient flow of the free energy $\rho\mapsto\int V\rho\,\dd x+\sigma^2\int\rho\log\rho\,\dd x$. […] For a prescribed target $\be=\rho_\be\dd x$, take $b=\sigma^2\nabla\log\rho_\be$. The SDE, the Fokker–Planck equation, and the deterministic continuity equation with score velocity are then three representations of the same gradient flow:

$$\partial_t\rho_t=\sigma^2\dv\Big(\rho_t\nabla\log\frac{\rho_t}{\rho_\be}\Big),\qquad v_t=\sigma^2\big(\nabla\log\rho_\be-\nabla\log\rho_t\big).\qquad(15.10)$$
free energy
Potential energy plus $\sigma^2$ times the neg-entropy. With $\sigma=1$ and $\rho_\be=Z^{-1}e^{-V}$ it is $\KL(\al|\be)$ up to the constant $\log Z$.
$\sigma^2$
The temperature: the strength of the diffusion. Below, $\sigma=1$.
$v_t$
The velocity of the mass: towards higher target density ($\nabla\log\rho_\be=-\nabla V$), away from higher current density ($-\nabla\log\rho_t$). It vanishes exactly when $\rho_t\propto\rho_\be$: the Gibbs measure is the stationary state.
“three representations”
The PDE, the Langevin SDE (§6) and the deterministic particles $\dot x=v_t(x)$: three descriptions of the same curve of measures.

With $\sigma=1$: $\partial_t\rho=\dv\big(\rho\nabla(\log\rho+V)\big)=\Delta\rho+\dv(\rho\nabla V)$ — the Fokker–Planck equation. Example (Ornstein–Uhlenbeck): $V=\frac12x^2$, $\be=\N(0,1)$. From $\N(m,s^2)$, the velocity $-x+\frac{x-m_t}{s_t^2}$ is affine in $x$, so the law stays Gaussian (§9) with $\dot m=-m$ and $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}s^2=2(1-s^2)$:

$$\al_t=\N\big(m\,e^{-t},\ 1+(s^2-1)e^{-2t}\big).$$

From $\N(2,0.5^2)$: at $t=1$, $\al_1=\N(0.7358,\ 0.8985)$. The mean relaxes like $e^{-t}$, the variance like $e^{-2t}$, towards $\N(0,1)$.

In machine learning — scores

The Wasserstein gradient of the neg-entropy is the score $\nabla\log\rho$, and the velocity of every flow containing an entropy involves the score of the current law. Scores can be learned from samples without knowing $\rho$: score matching (Hyvärinen, 2005) and denoising score matching (Vincent, 2011) fit a network $s_\theta(x)\approx\nabla\log\rho(x)$ by least squares (both cited in OT4ML §16.1, p. 407). With a learned score, the particles $\dot x=-\nabla V(x)-s_\theta(x)$ simulate a Fokker–Planck flow without any noise; run backwards in time, the same idea generates data (§6). Diffusion models are built on exactly this pair of objects: a Fokker–Planck flow towards $\N(0,\Id)$ and the score along it.

Reflexes
  • $\partial_t\al=\dv(\al\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al})$ → read it on particles: $\dot x=-\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al_t)(x)$.
  • $\dv(\rho\nabla\log\rho)=\Delta\rho$ → an entropy in the energy means a Laplacian in the PDE.
  • Stationary states of a Wasserstein gradient flow → the first variation is constant on the support ($\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}=0$ there): for KL, $\rho\propto e^{-V}$.
  • Gaussian data and an affine velocity → the flow stays Gaussian; write ODEs for $(m,s^2)$.

Which energy has the flow $\partial_t\rho=\dv\big(\rho\,(x-\bar m)\big)$ on $\R$, where $\bar m$ is a fixed number? And $\partial_t\rho=\dv\big(\rho\,(x-m(\rho))\big)$ with $m(\rho)=\int x\rho$?

Answer

The first: velocity $-(x-\bar m)=-\nabla V$ with $V=\frac12(x-\bar m)^2$, a potential energy $\int V\dd\al$; mass contracts to $\bar m$. The second: velocity $-(x-m(\rho))$, the gradient of $\frac12(x-m)^2$, which is the first variation of $\frac14\iint(x-y)^2\dd\al\dd\al$ (up to a constant): an interaction energy, aggregation to the conserved mean. Same instantaneous velocity field, different energies: the first “knows” where $\bar m$ is, the second only sees the measure.
