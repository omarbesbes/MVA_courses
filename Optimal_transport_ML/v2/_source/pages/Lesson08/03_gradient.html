3. First variation and Wasserstein gradient OT4ML pp. 356–359

Perturbing a measure by mixing gives the first variation $\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$, a function (the value of adding a bit of mass at $x$); perturbing it by moving gives the Wasserstein gradient $\nabla_Wf=\nabla\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$, a vector field (the direction in which moving mass increases $f$ fastest).

Two ways to differentiate a function of a measure

For $h:\R^d\to\R$, the derivative answers “how does $h$ change when $x$ moves a little?”. For $f(\al)$ there are two ways to change $\al$ a little, the two moves of §2, and they give two derivatives:

The first variation

In the course OT4ML Def. 15.2, pp. 356–357

Definition 15.2 (First variation). Assume that $f(\al)<+\infty$. A measurable function $\delta f(\al)$ is a first variation of $f$ at $\al$ if, for every $\be\in\Pc(\R^d)$ such that $\delta f(\al)$ is integrable with respect to $\abs{\be-\al}$ and the mixture $\al_\vartheta$ remains in the domain of $f$ for small $\vartheta>0$,

$$f(\al_\vartheta)=f(\al)+\vartheta\int_{\R^d}[\delta f(\al)](x)\,\dd(\be-\al)(x)+o(\vartheta)\qquad(\vartheta\downarrow0).$$

Because $\be-\al$ has zero mass, $\delta f(\al)$ is determined only up to an additive constant.

$\al_\vartheta$
The mixture $(1-\vartheta)\al+\vartheta\be$ (defined just before, p. 356): remove a fraction $\vartheta$ of $\al$ everywhere and put it where $\be$ is.
$\delta f(\al)$
A function of $x\in\R^d$ (the sheet writes $\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(\al)(x)$). Read $\delta f(\al)(x)$ as “the marginal value of a unit of mass placed at $x$”: adding mass where $\delta f(\al)$ is large increases $f$.
$\int\delta f(\al)\,\dd(\be-\al)$
The directional derivative of $f$ at $\al$ in the direction $\be-\al$. In $\R^d$ the analogue is $h(x+\vartheta u)=h(x)+\vartheta\ps{\nabla h(x)}{u}+o(\vartheta)$: $\delta f(\al)$ plays the role of $\nabla h(x)$ and the integral plays the role of the inner product.
“up to a constant”
Since $\int c\,\dd(\be-\al)=c-c=0$, adding a constant to $\delta f(\al)$ changes nothing. Only differences of values, or the gradient $\nabla\delta f(\al)$, are meaningful.

How to compute it. Write $\al_\vartheta=\al+\vartheta\eta$ with $\eta=\be-\al$ (zero mass), expand $f(\al+\vartheta\eta)$ to first order in $\vartheta$, and read the coefficient of $\vartheta$ as $\int(\cdots)\,\dd\eta$. Four examples, each one line:

  1. Potential energy $f(\al)=\int V\dd\al$: $f(\al+\vartheta\eta)=f(\al)+\vartheta\int V\dd\eta$ exactly. So $\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}=V$.$f$ is linear in $\al$; its derivative is itself (OT4ML Ex. 15.11, p. 360).
  2. Interaction energy $f(\al)=\iint k(x,y)\dd\al(x)\dd\al(y)$, $k$ symmetric: $f(\al+\vartheta\eta)=f(\al)+2\vartheta\iint k(x,y)\dd\al(y)\dd\eta(x)+\vartheta^2\iint k\,\dd\eta\dd\eta$. So $\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(x)=2\int k(x,y)\dd\al(y)$.Expand the product $(\al+\vartheta\eta)\otimes(\al+\vartheta\eta)$; the two cross terms are equal because $k$ is symmetric (OT4ML (15.9), p. 361).
  3. Neg-entropy $f(\al)=\int\rho\log\rho$, with $\eta$ of density $\zeta$: $\frac{\dd}{\dd\vartheta}\big[(\rho+\vartheta\zeta)\log(\rho+\vartheta\zeta)\big]_{\vartheta=0}=\zeta(\log\rho+1)$. So $\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}=\log\rho+1$.Derivative of $s\log s$ is $\log s+1$; differentiate under the integral (OT4ML Ex. 15.13, p. 361). The constant $1$ can be dropped.
  4. Relative entropy $f(\al)=\KL(\al|\be)$ with $\be=Z^{-1}e^{-V}\dd x$: $\KL(\al|\be)=\int\rho\log\rho+\int V\dd\al+\log Z$, so $\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}=\log\rho+V+1=\log\frac{\rho}{\rho_\be}+\mathrm{const}$.Sum of lines 1 and 3; $\log\rho_\be=-V-\log Z$.
Worked example — three points and the variance

Let $f(\al)=\frac14\iint(x-y)^2\dd\al(x)\dd\al(y)$ on $\R$. Expanding the square, $f(\al)=\frac12\mathrm{Var}(\al)$. By line 2 with $k=\frac14(x-y)^2$:

$$\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(x)=\frac12\int(x-y)^2\dd\al(y)=\frac12(x-m)^2+\frac12\mathrm{Var}(\al),\qquad m=\int y\,\dd\al(y).$$

Take $\al=\frac13(\delta_0+\delta_1+\delta_5)$: $m=2$, $\mathrm{Var}=\frac{4+1+9}3=\frac{14}3$, $f(\al)=\frac73$. Mix towards $\be=\delta_3$. The definition predicts

$$\frac{\dd}{\dd\vartheta}f(\al_\vartheta)\Big|_{0}=\int\tfrac12(x-m)^2\dd(\delta_3-\al)=\tfrac12(3-2)^2-\tfrac12\mathrm{Var}(\al)=\tfrac12-\tfrac73=-\tfrac{11}6\approx-1.8333 .$$

Numerically, $\big(f(\al_\vartheta)-f(\al)\big)/\vartheta=-1.83338$ for $\vartheta=10^{-5}$. ✓ Adding mass at $3$, close to the mean, lowers the variance: $\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(3)=\frac12+\frac73$ is below the average value $\int\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}\dd\al=\frac73+\frac73$.

The Wasserstein gradient

In the course OT4ML Def. 15.5, (15.4), p. 358

Definition 15.5 (Wasserstein gradient). Assume that $f$ admits a smooth first variation $\delta f(\al)$. In the smooth formal calculus on $\Pc_2(\R^d)$, the Wasserstein gradient of $f$ at $\al$ is the gradient vector field $\nabla_Wf(\al)=\nabla_x\delta f(\al)$. […] The associated formal gradient flow is the continuity equation

$$\frac{\partial\al_t}{\partial t}+\dv\big(-\nabla_Wf(\al_t)\,\al_t\big)=0.\qquad(15.4)$$
In the course OT4ML Prop. 15.7, p. 358

Proposition 15.7 (Formal Wasserstein gradient). Assume that $f$ admits a smooth first variation $\delta f(\al)$ and that $\al$ has a smooth positive density. Let $v$ be a sufficiently regular velocity field, compactly supported or satisfying a no-flux boundary condition. For perturbations generated by $\pf{(\Id+\tau v)}{\al}$, the differential of $f$ is

$$\frac{\dd}{\dd\tau}\Big|_{\tau=0}f\big(\pf{(\Id+\tau v)}{\al}\big)=\int\ps{\nabla\delta f(\al)(x)}{v(x)}\,\dd\al(x).$$

Hence, for the Riemannian metric $\norm{v}_{L^2(\al)}^2=\int\norm{v}^2\dd\al$, the Wasserstein gradient is the vector field $\nabla_Wf(\al)=\nabla\delta f(\al)$.

$\nabla_x\delta f(\al)$
The ordinary gradient, in $x$, of the function $\delta f(\al)$: a vector field on $\R^d$. The additive constant disappears. (OT4ML and StatOT write it with a double-struck nabla; the sheet writes $\nabla_{\mathsf W}f$.)
$\pf{(\Id+\tau v)}{\al}$
Move every bit of mass from $x$ to $x+\tau v(x)$: a horizontal perturbation in the direction $v$.
$\norm{v}^2_{L^2(\al)}$
The size of a velocity field, weighted by the mass that moves: the kinetic energy of the motion. It is the metric hidden in $W_2$: $W_2^2\big(\al,\pf{(\Id+\tau v)}{\al}\big)=\tau^2\norm{v}^2_{L^2(\al)}$ when the displacement is optimal (OT4ML p. 359; StatOT §5.3).
“Riemannian gradient”
The derivative in the direction $v$ is the inner product $\ps{\nabla_Wf}{v}_{L^2(\al)}$: exactly how $\ps{\nabla h(x)}{u}$ represents $Dh(x)[u]$ in $\R^d$. Among unit velocities, $\ps{\nabla_Wf}{v}_{L^2(\al)}$ is most negative for $v=-\nabla_Wf/\norm{\nabla_Wf}$ (Cauchy–Schwarz): $-\nabla_Wf$ is the direction of steepest descent.
(15.4)
The gradient flow: every bit of mass moves with velocity $-\nabla_Wf(\al_t)$ at its current position; the continuity equation (refresher) turns this into a PDE for $\al_t$. §4 derives it from the JKO step.
Idea of the proof

Moving mass by $\tau v$ is, to first order, the zero-mass perturbation $-\tau\dv(\al v)$ (mass leaves where $v$ diverges). Plug it into the first variation, then integrate by parts to move the derivative from $\al v$ to $\delta f(\al)$.

Full proof (formal, as in the book)
  1. $$\int\psi\,\dd\big(\pf{(\Id+\tau v)}{\al}\big)=\int\psi\big(x+\tau v(x)\big)\dd\al(x)=\int\psi\,\dd\al+\tau\int\ps{\nabla\psi}{v}\dd\al+o(\tau)$$Definition of the push-forward, then a first-order Taylor expansion of the test function $\psi$.
  2. $$\tau\int\ps{\nabla\psi}{v}\,\rho\,\dd x=-\tau\int\psi\,\dv(\rho v)\,\dd x .$$Integration by parts (refresher); no boundary term because $v$ is compactly supported.
  3. So, as measures (tested against every $\psi$): $\pf{(\Id+\tau v)}{\al}=\al-\tau\dv(\al v)+o(\tau)$.Lines 1–2. The perturbation $-\tau\dv(\al v)$ has zero mass: $\int\dv(\al v)=0$.
  4. $$f\big(\pf{(\Id+\tau v)}{\al}\big)=f(\al)-\tau\int\delta f(\al)\,\dv(\al v)\,\dd x+o(\tau).$$First variation applied to the zero-mass perturbation of line 3 (formal: Definition 15.2 is stated for mixtures; the smooth calculus extends it to such perturbations).
  5. $$-\int\delta f(\al)\,\dv(\al v)\,\dd x=\int\ps{\nabla\delta f(\al)}{v}\,\dd\al .$$Integration by parts again, now with $\varphi=\delta f(\al)$ and $w=\al v$.
  6. The derivative is the $L^2(\al)$ inner product of $v$ with $\nabla\delta f(\al)$, which is therefore the Riesz representative — the gradient for that metric. $\square$Lines 4–5. This is OT4ML’s proof, p. 358–359; StatOT Prop. 5.10 (p. 152) does the same along a curve $\mu_t$.
Worked example, continued — the gradient pulls points to the mean

For $f=\frac12\mathrm{Var}$: $\nabla_Wf(\al)(x)=\frac{\dd}{\dd x}\frac12(x-m)^2=x-m$. On $\al=\frac13(\delta_0+\delta_1+\delta_5)$ it equals $(-2,-1,3)$ at the three points: the descent velocities $-\nabla_Wf=(2,1,-3)$ pull every point towards the mean $m=2$. Check of Proposition 15.7 with the dilation $v(x)=x$: the prediction is $\int(x-m)\,x\,\dd\al=\mathrm{Var}(\al)=\frac{14}3\approx4.6667$; numerically $\frac1\tau\big(f(\pf{(1+\tau)\Id}{\al})-f(\al)\big)=4.666669$ for $\tau=10^{-6}$. ✓ (Indeed $f$ of a dilation by $1+\tau$ is $(1+\tau)^2f$, whose derivative is $2f=\frac{14}3$.)

The gradient of $\frac12W_2^2$, and the barycenter algorithm of Lesson 3

The last row of the table deserves a derivation: it connects this lesson to Lesson 3, §9.

  1. $$\tfrac12W_2^2(\al,\be)=\max_{\varphi(x)+\psi(y)\le\frac12\norm{x-y}^2}\ \int\varphi\,\dd\al+\int\psi\,\dd\be .$$Kantorovich duality for the cost $\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$ (refresher).
  2. $$\frac{\delta}{\delta\al}\Big(\tfrac12W_2^2(\cdot,\be)\Big)(\al)=\varphi_\al\quad\text{(an optimal potential).}$$“Envelope theorem”: the derivative of a maximum of functions that are linear in $\al$ is the derivative of the maximised one, $\int\varphi_\al\dd\eta$ (formal; this is the solution of Exercise 6 of the sheet, §13).
  3. $$\nabla_W\Big(\tfrac12W_2^2(\cdot,\be)\Big)(\al)=\nabla\varphi_\al=\Id-T,$$ where $T$ is the Brenier map from $\al$ to $\be$.For the quadratic cost, $\frac12\norm{x}^2-\varphi_\al$ is convex with gradient $T$ (Lesson 4).
  4. An explicit gradient step of size $\tau$ moves $x$ to $x-\tau(x-T(x))=(1-\tau)x+\tau T(x)$: it lands on the McCann geodesic from $\al$ to $\be$, at the fraction $\tau$.Push $\al$ by $\Id-\tau\nabla_Wf$. With $\tau=1$ it lands on $\be$ in one step.

Now take the barycenter energy of Lesson 3, $F(\al)=\sum_k\lambda_k\frac12W_2^2(\al,\be_k)$. By linearity, $\nabla_WF(\al)=\sum_k\lambda_k(\Id-T_k)=\Id-\sum_k\lambda_kT_k$, where $T_k$ is the Brenier map from $\al$ to $\be_k$. A gradient step of size $1$ is

$$\al\ \longmapsto\ \pf{\big(\Id-\nabla_WF(\al)\big)}{\al}=\pf{\Big(\sum_k\lambda_kT_k\Big)}{\al},$$

which is exactly the fixed-point algorithm of Lesson 3 (“average the optimal maps, push the measure”). Its descent inequality ($F$ decreases by at least $W_2^2$ of the step, Lesson 3) is the descent property of a gradient step, and its fixed points, $\sum_k\lambda_kT_k=\Id$, are the zeros of the Wasserstein gradient. This is the interpretation of Zemel and Panaretos (2019) quoted in Lesson 3. Example: for $\be_1=\N(0,1)$, $\be_2=\N(4,9)$, $\lambda=(\frac12,\frac12)$, from any $\N(m,\sigma^2)$ the averaged map is $x\mapsto2+\frac2\sigma(x-m)$, and one step lands on the barycenter $\N(2,4)$.

In machine learning — the Wasserstein gradient is the training signal of a generator

A generative model produces samples $x=G_\theta(z)$ from noise $z\sim\zeta$; its law is $\al_\theta=\pf{(G_\theta)}{\zeta}$. To train it, one minimises a loss of the generated distribution, $f(\al_\theta)$, for instance $\mathrm{MMD}^2(\al_\theta,\be_{\text{data}})$ or a Sinkhorn divergence (Lesson 7). Changing $\theta$ in a direction $u$ moves each sample by $\partial_\theta G_\theta(z)\,u$, so Proposition 15.7 (with this displacement field) gives the chain rule

$$\nabla_\theta f(\al_\theta)=\int\big(\partial_\theta G_\theta(z)\big)^{\!\top}\,\nabla_Wf(\al_\theta)\big(G_\theta(z)\big)\,\dd\zeta(z).$$

In words: the Wasserstein gradient tells each generated sample where to move, and backpropagation pulls these per-sample directions back to the parameters. With the MMD loss, the direction at a sample $x$ is $2\int\nabla_xk(x,y)\dd(\al_\theta-\be)(y)$: away from the other generated samples, towards the data. This is what automatic differentiation computes when a loss is written as a function of a minibatch of generated samples (with the factor $n$ of §5). Generators trained this way with an MMD loss were proposed by Dziugaite, Roy and Ghahramani (2015), with Sinkhorn losses by Genevay, Peyré and Cuturi (2018) (both cited in OT4ML).

Pitfall — a function is not a vector field

$\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$ is a scalar function defined up to a constant; $\nabla_Wf$ is a vector field. The flow is driven by the gradient of the first variation: a first variation that is constant on the support of $\al$ produces no motion at all, even if it is very large. This is what happens at a stationary point (§10).

Pitfall — the factor 2 of interaction energies

$\iint k\,\dd\al\dd\al$ has first variation $2\int k(\cdot,y)\dd\al(y)$, not $\int k(\cdot,y)\dd\al(y)$: $\al$ appears twice. StatOT puts a $\frac12$ in front ($\frac12\iint W(x-y)$) precisely to remove it. Missing this factor does not change the trajectories, only the clock.

Reflexes
  • To compute $\nabla_Wf$: replace $\al$ by $\al+\vartheta\eta$, keep the term in $\vartheta$, write it $\int(\cdots)\dd\eta$, take the gradient in $x$ of $(\cdots)$.
  • Entropy → the score $\nabla\log\rho$; potential → $\nabla V$; interaction → a kernel average of $\nabla k$; $\frac12W_2^2(\cdot,\be)$ → $\Id-T$.
  • A sum of energies → a sum of gradients (KL $=$ potential $+$ entropy).

Compute $\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}$ and $\nabla_Wf$ for $f(\al)=\big(\int x\,\dd\al\big)^2$ on $\R$ (the squared mean). What does the flow do?

Answer

With $m=\int x\dd\al$: $f(\al+\vartheta\eta)=\big(m+\vartheta\int x\dd\eta\big)^2=m^2+2\vartheta m\int x\,\dd\eta+O(\vartheta^2)$, so $\frac{\delta f}{\delta\al}(x)=2mx$ and $\nabla_Wf=2m$, the same vector at every point. The flow translates the whole measure rigidly with velocity $-2m$: $\dot m=-2m$, $m(t)=m_0e^{-2t}$, and the shape never changes. (This is Exercise 4 (1) of the sheet with $A=1$.)
