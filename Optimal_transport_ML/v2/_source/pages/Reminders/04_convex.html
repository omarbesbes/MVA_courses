5. Convexity and optimization

Convex problems have no spurious local minima, convex functions are envelopes of their tangent lines (Legendre transform), constraints can be priced by multipliers (duality), linear programs are solved at vertices, gradient methods descend, and functionals of measures can be differentiated too.

Convex sets and functions, strict and strong convexity, subgradients

Used in Lesson 1 (Brenier: optimal maps are gradients of convex functions; convex costs on the line), Lesson 2 (the set of couplings is convex), Lesson 3 (Jensen), Lesson 4 (convex potentials, $c$-concavity), Lesson 7 (strict convexity of the entropic problem: a unique plan), Lesson 8 (convexity along geodesics, exponential convergence).

A set is convex if it contains the segment between any two of its points. A function is convex if its graph lies below its chords — equivalently above its tangents. Then every local minimum is global, and “the gradient vanishes” is enough to certify a minimum.

Definitions (standard)
  • $f:\R^d\to\R\cup\{+\infty\}$ is convex if $f\big((1-t)x+ty\big)\le(1-t)f(x)+tf(y)$ for $t\in[0,1]$; strictly convex if the inequality is strict for $x\ne y$, $t\in(0,1)$.
  • $\mu$-strongly convex: $f-\frac\mu2\norm{\cdot}^2$ is convex. $L$-smooth: $\nabla f$ is $L$-Lipschitz. For $f\in C^2$: convex $\iff\nabla^2f\succeq0$; $\mu$-strongly convex $\iff\nabla^2f\succeq\mu\mathrm I$; $L$-smooth $\iff-L\mathrm I\preceq\nabla^2f\preceq L\mathrm I$.
  • Subgradients: $\partial f(x)=\{s:\ f(y)\ge f(x)+\ps{s}{y-x}\ \forall y\}$, the slopes of the lines that touch the graph at $x$ from below. For differentiable convex $f$, $\partial f(x)=\{\nabla f(x)\}$.
What convexity buys (standard)
  1. First-order characterisation (differentiable $f$): $f$ convex $\iff f(y)\ge f(x)+\ps{\nabla f(x)}{y-x}$ for all $x,y$.
  2. Every local minimum is global, and $x^\star$ is a minimiser $\iff0\in\partial f(x^\star)$.
  3. Strictly convex: at most one minimiser. Strongly convex (and lower semi-continuous): exactly one, and $f(x)-f(x^\star)\ge\frac\mu2\norm{x-x^\star}^2$.
  4. Gradients of convex functions are monotone: $\ps{\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)}{x-y}\ge0$ (in 1D: $f'$ is non-decreasing).
  5. Sums with nonnegative weights, suprema of convex functions, and compositions with affine maps are convex.
Worked example
  • $f(x)=\abs x$: $\partial f(0)=[-1,1]$ (all the lines through the origin with slope in $[-1,1]$ stay below the graph), $\partial f(x)=\{\mathrm{sign}(x)\}$ otherwise; $0\in\partial f(0)$ certifies the minimum.
  • $f(x_1,x_2)=x_1^2+x_1x_2+x_2^2$: $\nabla^2f=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ with eigenvalues $1$ and $3$, so $f$ is $1$-strongly convex and $3$-smooth.
  • $x^4$: strictly convex, not strongly ($f''(0)=0$). $e^x$: strictly convex with no minimiser (infimum $0$, not attained). $\max(x^2,(x-2)^2)$ is convex; $\min(x^2,(x-2)^2)$ is not (two minimisers $0$ and $2$, and the chord between them lies below the bump at $1$).
Proof that a local minimum of a convex function is global

Let $x^\star$ be a local minimum and $y$ any point. For small $t>0$, $x_t=(1-t)x^\star+ty$ is close to $x^\star$, so $f(x^\star)\le f(x_t)\le(1-t)f(x^\star)+tf(y)$. Subtract $(1-t)f(x^\star)$ and divide by $t>0$: $f(x^\star)\le f(y)$.

Pitfalls
  • Strictly convex is not strongly convex ($x^4$); strict convexity gives uniqueness, not existence ($e^x$).
  • Convexity along straight lines (“flat” convexity) is not convexity along Wasserstein geodesics (“displacement” convexity), which Lesson 8 uses: a functional of measures can have one without the other.
In machine learning — convex losses, non-convex models

Least squares, logistic regression and support vector machines minimise convex losses in their parameters: any local minimum found by gradient descent is global. Neural networks are not convex in their weights. But the output of a two-layer network is linear in the distribution of its neurons, so the loss is convex as a function of that distribution — the starting point of the mean-field analysis of training as a Wasserstein gradient flow (OT4ML §15.4, p. 386; Lesson 8).

The Legendre–Fenchel transform (convex conjugate)

Used in Lesson 1 and Lesson 4 (Brenier: the quadratic $c$-transform is a Legendre transform; the inverse of $\nabla\varphi$ is $\nabla\varphi^*$; proof of Brenier’s theorem), Lesson 4 (dual formula of $f$-divergences, GANs), Lesson 7 (log-sum-exp and entropy are conjugate: soft $c$-transforms).

A convex function is the upper envelope of the lines below it. So it can be described by its supporting lines: for each slope $y$, keep the highest line of slope $y$ that stays below the graph. Its intercept is $-f^*(y)$, and $f^*$ is the convex conjugate (Legendre–Fenchel transform).

In the course OT4ML Def. 5.11, p. 100; p. 101

For $h:\R^d\to\R\cup\{+\infty\}$: $\ h^*(y)=\sup_{x\in\R^d}\big(\ps{x}{y}-h(x)\big)$ and $h^{**}=(h^*)^*$. If $h$ is proper with an affine minorant, the Fenchel–Moreau theorem identifies $h^{**}$ with the greatest lower semi-continuous convex function below $h$. For the cost $c(x,y)=\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$ and $\varphi(x)=\frac12\norm x^2-f(x)$, the $c$-transform is $f^c(y)=\frac12\norm y^2-\varphi^*(y)$.

Properties (standard)
  • $f^*$ is always convex and lower semi-continuous (a supremum of affine functions of $y$), even if $f$ is not.
  • Fenchel–Young: $f(x)+f^*(y)\ge\ps{x}{y}$ for all $x,y$, with equality iff $y\in\partial f(x)$ — iff the line of slope $y$ touches the graph at $x$.
  • For $f$ convex and lower semi-continuous: $f^{**}=f$. For $f$ smooth and strictly convex: $\nabla f^*=(\nabla f)^{-1}$.

Worked example — $f(x)=e^x$
  • For $y>0$, the concave function $x\mapsto xy-e^x$ is maximal where $y=e^x$, at $x^\star=\log y$: $f^*(y)=y\log y-y$. For $y=2$: $x^\star\approx0.693$, $f^*(2)=2\log2-2\approx-0.614$, and the tangent $\ell(x)=2x+0.614$ has intercept $-f^*(2)$.
  • Fenchel–Young at $(x,y)=(1,e)$: $f(1)+f^*(e)=e=1\cdot e$, equality since $e=f'(1)$. At $(0,2)$: $1-0.614=0.386>0$, strict since $2\ne f'(0)$.
  • $y<0$: $xy-e^x\to+\infty$ as $x\to-\infty$, so $f^*(y)=+\infty$ (no line of negative slope stays below $e^x$). $y=0$: $f^*(0)=\sup_x(-e^x)=0$, approached but not attained.
  • Non-convex: for the double well $f(x)=(x^2-1)^2$, $f^{**}$ is the convex envelope, $0$ on $[-1,1]$ and $f$ outside; $f(0)=1$ but $f^{**}(0)=0$.

Proofs of Fenchel–Young and of $\nabla f^*=(\nabla f)^{-1}$

Fenchel–Young. $f^*(y)\ge\ps{x}{y}-f(x)$ for every $x$, by definition of a supremum. Equality holds iff $x$ attains the supremum, i.e. $\ps{x'}{y}-f(x')\le\ps{x}{y}-f(x)$ for all $x'$, i.e. $f(x')\ge f(x)+\ps{y}{x'-x}$: $y\in\partial f(x)$.

Inverse gradients. For smooth strictly convex $f$, the supremum defining $f^*(y)$ is attained at the unique $x$ with $\nabla f(x)=y$. By the envelope theorem (below), $\nabla f^*(y)=x=(\nabla f)^{-1}(y)$. This is why the inverse of a Brenier map $\nabla\varphi$ is $\nabla\varphi^*$.

Pitfalls
  • Sign conventions: $f^*$ uses a $\sup$ of $\ps{x}{y}-f$; the $c$-transform of optimal transport uses an $\inf$ of $c-f$ (§9). Track the signs when you switch.
  • Infinite values are part of the transform ($\abs x\mapsto$ indicator of $[-1,1]$): do not drop them.
  • For a non-convex $f$, $f^{**}\ne f$: the conjugate forgets everything that is not in the convex envelope.
In machine learning — critics as dual variables

Duality turns a divergence into a maximisation over test functions, which a network can approximate. For $\KL$, whose generator $\varphi(s)=s\log s-s+1$ has conjugate $\varphi^*(u)=e^u-1$: $\KL(\al|\be)=\sup_f\int f\,\dd\al-\int(e^f-1)\,\dd\be$, attained at $f=\log\frac{\dd\al}{\dd\be}$ (OT4ML Prop. 7.24, p. 133). On $a=(0.5,0.3,0.2)$, $b=(0.2,0.3,0.5)$ the optimal $f$ gives $0.2749=\KL(a|b)$; $f=(1,0,-1)$ gives the lower bound $0.2724$. With $f$ a network evaluated on samples, this is an $f$-GAN discriminator, or a neural estimator of KL and mutual information. The Wasserstein-GAN critic is the analogous dual variable for $W_1$ (§4).

Compute the conjugate of $f(x)=\frac a2x^2$ ($a>0$) and check Fenchel–Young at $x=1$.

Answer

$xy-\frac a2x^2$ is maximal at $x=y/a$, so $f^*(y)=\frac{y^2}{2a}$. Then $\nabla f^*(y)=y/a$ is the inverse of $\nabla f(x)=ax$. At $x=1$ the contact slope is $y=f'(1)=a$, and $f(1)+f^*(a)=\frac a2+\frac a2=a=1\cdot a$: equality, as it should be at a contact point.

Lagrangian duality and KKT conditions

Used in Lesson 2 and Lesson 4 (the dual of the transport LP: the potentials are the multipliers of the marginal constraints), Lesson 6 (Benamou–Brenier: the multiplier of the continuity equation gives $v=\nabla\varphi$), Lesson 7 (entropic OT: the KKT conditions give $P=\mathrm{diag}(u)K\mathrm{diag}(v)$; the sheet uses the Lagrangian and the envelope theorem).

Replace hard constraints by prices: violating a constraint costs its multiplier times the violation. For each choice of prices, minimising the penalised objective gives a lower bound on the true minimum; the best lower bound is the dual problem. For convex problems the bound is tight, and the optimal prices certify optimality.

Lagrangian and dual (standard)

Primal problem: $p^\star=\min f(x)$ subject to $g_i(x)\le0$ and $h_j(x)=0$. Lagrangian, with $\lambda\ge0$ and $\mu$ free:

$$\mathcal L(x,\lambda,\mu)=f(x)+\sum_i\lambda_ig_i(x)+\sum_j\mu_jh_j(x),\qquad q(\lambda,\mu)=\inf_x\mathcal L(x,\lambda,\mu),\qquad d^\star=\sup_{\lambda\ge0,\mu}q(\lambda,\mu).$$
Duality theorems (standard)
  • Weak duality: $d^\star\le p^\star$ always.
  • Strong duality $d^\star=p^\star$ for convex problems ($f,g_i$ convex, $h_j$ affine) as soon as some feasible point satisfies all the inequalities strictly (Slater); for linear programs, as soon as the primal is feasible with a finite value.
  • KKT conditions: stationarity $\nabla f+\sum\lambda_i\nabla g_i+\sum\mu_j\nabla h_j=0$; primal feasibility; $\lambda\ge0$; complementary slackness $\lambda_ig_i(x)=0$. For a convex differentiable problem that satisfies Slater’s condition, $x$ is optimal iff there are multipliers satisfying KKT.
  • Envelope theorem: if $V(\theta)=\min_zF(z,\theta)$ with a unique smooth minimiser $z^\star(\theta)$, then $V'(\theta)=\partial_\theta F\big(z^\star(\theta),\theta\big)$ — no need to differentiate $z^\star$. In particular the multiplier of a constraint is, up to the sign convention, the derivative of the optimal value with respect to the level of that constraint (example 1 below).
Worked examples
  1. Equality constraint. $\min x_1^2+x_2^2$ s.t. $x_1+x_2=1$. $\mathcal L=x_1^2+x_2^2+\mu(1-x_1-x_2)$; stationarity gives $x_1=x_2=\mu/2$, the constraint $\mu=1$: $x^\star=(\frac12,\frac12)$, $p^\star=\frac12$. Dual function $q(\mu)=\mu-\frac{\mu^2}2$, maximal at $\mu=1$: $d^\star=\frac12=p^\star$. Sensitivity: with $x_1+x_2=c$, $p^\star(c)=\frac{c^2}2$, whose derivative at $c=1$ is $1=\mu$.
  2. Active inequality. $\min(x-2)^2$ s.t. $x\le1$: KKT gives $2(x-2)+\lambda=0$, $\lambda(x-1)=0$, $\lambda\ge0$, hence $x=1$, $\lambda=2>0$ (the constraint is active and has a positive price).
  3. Gibbs distribution — the softmax. $\min_{p\in\Sigma_n}\ps{c}{p}-\eps H(p)$ with $H(p)=-\sum_ip_i\log p_i$. The multiplier of $\sum_ip_i=1$ gives $c_i+\eps(\log p_i+1)+\mu=0$, so $p_i=e^{-c_i/\eps}/\sum_je^{-c_j/\eps}$, and the optimal value is the soft-min $-\eps\log\sum_ie^{-c_i/\eps}$. For $c=(0,1,2)$, $\eps=1$: $p\approx(0.665,0.245,0.090)$, value $\approx-0.408$; for $\eps=0.1$ the value is $\approx-4.5\times10^{-6}$, close to $\min_ic_i=0$.
  4. Envelope. $V(\theta)=\min_z(z^2-2\theta z)=-\theta^2$ with $z^\star=\theta$; $V'(1)=-2=\partial_\theta(z^2-2\theta z)|_{z=z^\star}=-2z^\star.$
Proof of weak duality

Let $x$ be feasible and $\lambda\ge0$. Then $\lambda_ig_i(x)\le0$ and $h_j(x)=0$, so $q(\lambda,\mu)\le\mathcal L(x,\lambda,\mu)\le f(x)$. Take the infimum over feasible $x$ and the supremum over $(\lambda,\mu)$: $d^\star\le p^\star$. Equality, with optimal $x^\star$ and $(\lambda^\star,\mu^\star)$, forces both inequalities to be equalities: $x^\star$ minimises $\mathcal L(\cdot,\lambda^\star,\mu^\star)$ (stationarity) and $\sum\lambda_i^\star g_i(x^\star)=0$ (complementary slackness).

Pitfalls
  • Multipliers of inequality constraints $g\le0$ must be $\ge0$ (with the convention $+\lambda g$); multipliers of equalities have no sign.
  • Without convexity there can be a duality gap $d^\star
  • In entropic OT the constraint $P\ge0$ needs no multiplier: the entropy keeps the solution positive automatically.
In machine learning — softmax and attention are Lagrangian solutions

Example 3 says that $\mathrm{softmax}(s/\eps)$ is the distribution that maximises $\ps{s}{p}+\eps H(p)$ over the simplex: an entropy-regularised argmax. Attention weights in transformers are such Gibbs distributions over keys, and Sinkhorn’s algorithm (Lesson 7) alternates two such normalisations so that both marginals of a plan are matched. Duality also explains support vector machines: only training points with a positive multiplier (the support vectors) enter the solution — complementary slackness.

Linear programming: polytopes, vertices, duality, complementary slackness

Used in Lesson 2 (discrete Kantorovich is a linear program; sparse optimal plans), Lesson 3 (barycenters on a fixed grid), Lesson 4 (the dual LP, potentials, complementary slackness).

A linear program minimises a linear function over a polytope (a set cut out by finitely many linear inequalities). The level sets of a linear function are parallel hyperplanes: sliding them until they last touch the polytope, the contact happens at a vertex — or along a whole face.

In the course OT4ML Prop. 3.16, p. 57; Remark 3.19, p. 58; Prop. 5.2–5.3, p. 95
  • A linear form on a non-empty compact convex set has a minimiser that is an extreme point.
  • For general histograms one can choose an optimal plan whose support has no cycle, hence at most $n+m-1$ nonzero entries.
  • Discrete Kantorovich duality: $\displaystyle\Lc(a,b)=\min_{P\in\U(a,b)}\ps{C}{P}=\max_{f_i+g_j\le C_{ij}}\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}$.
  • Complementary slackness: for $P\in\U(a,b)$ and admissible $(f,g)$, $\ps{C}{P}-\ps{f}{a}-\ps{g}{b}=\sum_{ij}P_{ij}(C_{ij}-f_i-g_j)\ge0$, and both are optimal iff $P_{ij}>0\Rightarrow f_i+g_j=C_{ij}$.
Worked example — the $2\times2$ transport problem of §1

$a=(\frac12,\frac12)$, $b=(\frac14,\frac34)$ on $\{0,1\}$, $C=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ (cost $\abs{x-y}$). The couplings form the segment $P(p)$, $p\in[0,\frac14]$ (§1), and the cost $\frac34-2p$ is minimal at the vertex $p=\frac14$:

$$P^\star=\begin{pmatrix}1/4&1/4\\0&1/2\end{pmatrix},\qquad\ps{C}{P^\star}=\tfrac14,\qquad 3=n+m-1\text{ nonzero entries.}$$

Dual certificate. Complementary slackness on the support: $f_1+g_1=0$, $f_1+g_2=1$, $f_2+g_2=0$. Choosing $f_1=0$ gives $g=(0,1)$, $f=(0,-1)$. The last constraint holds: $f_2+g_1=-1\le C_{21}=1$. Dual value $\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}=-\frac12+\frac34=\frac14$: equal to the primal value, so both are optimal. POT’s exact solver ot.emd returns the same plan and the potentials $(0.625,-0.375)$, $(-0.625,0.375)$: the same up to the shift $(f+t,g-t)$. On random $6\times5$ problems it returns plans with exactly $10=n+m-1$ nonzero entries.

Proof of weak duality and of complementary slackness

Use the marginal constraints: $\ps{f}{a}+\ps{g}{b}=\sum_if_i\sum_jP_{ij}+\sum_jg_j\sum_iP_{ij}=\sum_{ij}P_{ij}(f_i+g_j)$. Hence the gap $\ps{C}{P}-\ps{f}{a}-\ps{g}{b}=\sum_{ij}P_{ij}(C_{ij}-f_i-g_j)$ is a sum of nonnegative terms ($P\ge0$, $f_i+g_j\le C_{ij}$). It is zero iff every term is zero, i.e. iff $P$ only charges the entries where the constraint is tight. A zero gap proves optimality of both: no plan costs less than a dual value, no dual value exceeds a plan’s cost.

Pitfalls
  • The optimum can be a whole face (non-unique solutions; e.g. $\abs{x-y}$ on the line, §2). Solvers return a vertex.
  • Dual potentials are never unique: $(f+t\ind,g-t\ind)$ has the same value.
  • Size: $n\times m$ unknowns. Exact network-simplex solvers cost roughly $O(n^3\log n)$ for $n=m$, which is what motivates entropic regularization (Lesson 7).
In machine learning — earth mover’s distances

Comparing two histograms with a ground cost by this LP is the “earth mover’s distance” of image retrieval; comparing two documents seen as weighted clouds of word embeddings is the word mover’s distance (OT4ML Example 4.11, p. 78). In Python, ot.emd(a, b, M) from the POT library solves the LP exactly and returns a sparse plan.

Gradient descent and stochastic gradient descent

Used in Lesson 3 (the barycenter fixed-point algorithm is a descent method), Lesson 4 (semi-discrete OT by stochastic gradient ascent on the dual, OT4ML Prop. 6.9–6.10, p. 111–113), Lesson 6 (training flow matching), Lesson 7 (gradients of Sinkhorn losses), Lesson 8 (gradient flows; JKO = implicit Euler).

Walk downhill by small steps: $x_{k+1}=x_k-\tau\nabla f(x_k)$. With small steps you follow the gradient flow $\dot x=-\nabla f(x)$ (§4); with a noisy but unbiased estimate of the gradient you get stochastic gradient descent.

Convergence (standard)
  • Descent lemma. If $f$ is $L$-smooth and $\tau\le1/L$: $f(x_{k+1})\le f(x_k)-\frac\tau2\norm{\nabla f(x_k)}^2$.
  • Convex and $L$-smooth, $\tau\le1/L$: $f(x_k)-f^\star\le\dfrac{\norm{x_0-x^\star}^2}{2\tau k}$. $\mu$-strongly convex, $\tau=1/L$: $\norm{x_k-x^\star}^2\le\big(1-\frac\mu L\big)^k\norm{x_0-x^\star}^2$ — linear convergence, slow when the condition number $L/\mu$ is large.
  • Quadratics $f=\frac12x\T Hx$: each eigen-direction is multiplied by $1-\tau\lambda_i$ per step; convergence iff $0<\tau<2/\lambda_{\max}$.
  • SGD: $x_{k+1}=x_k-\tau_kg_k$ with $\E[g_k\mid x_k]=\nabla f(x_k)$. With steps $\sum\tau_k=\infty$, $\sum\tau_k^2<\infty$ (Robbins–Monro) it converges for convex problems with bounded noise; with a constant step it oscillates in a region whose size grows with $\tau$ and the noise.
  • Implicit Euler (proximal point): $x_{k+1}=\argmin_x f(x)+\frac1{2\tau}\norm{x-x_k}^2$. The JKO scheme of Lesson 8 is this step with $\norm{\cdot}^2$ replaced by $W_2^2$ (OT4ML Def. 15.1, p. 356).
Worked example
  • $f(x)=\frac12(x_1^2+10x_2^2)$: $L=10$, $\mu=1$. With $\tau=0.1$: $x_2$ is multiplied by $0$ (killed in one step) and $x_1$ by $0.9$; after $10$ steps $x=(0.349,0)$. With $\tau=0.19$: factors $0.81$ and $-0.9$ (zigzag); after $10$ steps $(0.122,0.349)$. With $\tau=0.21$: the factor $-1.1$ on $x_2$ makes it diverge ($2.59$ after $10$ steps).
  • SGD computing a mean: minimise $f(x)=\frac12\E(x-Y)^2$ with the stochastic gradient $x_k-y_k$ and steps $\tau_k=\frac1{k+1}$. Then $x_{k+1}=x_k-\frac{x_k-y_k}{k+1}$ is exactly the running average: on the samples $2,4,3,7$ the iterates are $2,3,3,4$ whatever $x_0$.
Proof of the descent lemma

$L$-smoothness gives the quadratic upper bound $f(y)\le f(x)+\ps{\nabla f(x)}{y-x}+\frac L2\norm{y-x}^2$ (integrate $\nabla f$ along the segment and use the Lipschitz bound). With $y=x-\tau\nabla f(x)$: $f(y)\le f(x)-\tau\big(1-\frac{L\tau}2\big)\norm{\nabla f(x)}^2\le f(x)-\frac\tau2\norm{\nabla f(x)}^2$ when $\tau\le1/L$.

Pitfalls
  • Too large a step diverges ($\tau>2/L$ on quadratics); ill-conditioning ($L/\mu$ large) makes small steps very slow.
  • On non-convex problems gradient descent finds stationary points, not necessarily global minima.
In machine learning — training is SGD, and SGD on neurons is a transport

Networks are trained by SGD and its variants (momentum, Adam) on minibatch losses; learning-rate decay schedules play the role of the Robbins–Monro conditions. For a wide two-layer network, gradient descent moves each neuron’s parameters as a particle; in the limit of infinitely many neurons, their distribution follows a Wasserstein gradient flow (OT4ML §15.4, p. 386; Lesson 8).

Derivatives of functionals of measures: the first variation

Used in Lesson 8 (Wasserstein gradient flows and the JKO scheme; the exercise sheet computes first variations of $\varphi$-divergences, Dirichlet energies and interaction energies), Lesson 3 (optimality of barycenters), Lesson 7 (gradients of OT losses are dual potentials).

How does $F(\al)$ change when $\al$ changes a little? There are two ways to perturb a measure. Vertically: re-weight the mass, $\al\to(1-\eps)\al+\eps\be$; the derivative is a function of $x$, the first variation $\frac{\delta F}{\delta\al}$. Horizontally: move the mass, $\al\to\pf{(\Id+\eps v)}{\al}$; the derivative involves the spatial gradient $\nabla\frac{\delta F}{\delta\al}$, the Wasserstein gradient.

In the course OT4ML Def. 15.2, p. 356; Def. 15.5 and Prop. 15.7, p. 358

A function $\frac{\delta F}{\delta\al}(\al)$ is the first variation of $F$ at $\al$ if, for $\al_\eps=(1-\eps)\al+\eps\be$,

$$F(\al_\eps)=F(\al)+\eps\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta\al}(\al)\,\dd(\be-\al)+o(\eps).$$

It is defined up to an additive constant ($\be-\al$ has zero mass). The Wasserstein gradient is $\nabla_x\frac{\delta F}{\delta\al}(\al)$, and $\frac{\dd}{\dd\tau}F\big(\pf{(\Id+\tau v)}{\al}\big)\big|_{\tau=0}=\int\ps{\nabla\frac{\delta F}{\delta\al}}{v}\,\dd\al$. The gradient flow is $\partial_t\al_t-\dv\big(\al_t\nabla\frac{\delta F}{\delta\al}(\al_t)\big)=0$.

Worked example — vertical and horizontal derivatives of an interaction energy

$\al=\frac12\delta_0+\frac12\delta_2$ and $F(\al)=\iint\abs{x-y}^2\dd\al\,\dd\al$, so $F(\al)=2\cdot\frac14\cdot4=2$ and $\frac{\delta F}{\delta\al}(x)=2\int\abs{x-y}^2\dd\al(y)=x^2+(x-2)^2$.

  • Vertical, towards $\be=\delta_1$: the formula predicts $F(\al_\eps)-F(\al)\approx\eps\big(\frac{\delta F}{\delta\al}(1)-\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta\al}\dd\al\big)=\eps(2-4)=-2\eps$. Direct computation: $F(\al_\eps)=2(1-\eps)$, exactly.
  • Horizontal, moving the two atoms towards each other ($v(0)=1$, $v(2)=-1$): $F=2(1-\eps)^2$, derivative $-4$ at $\eps=0$. Formula: $\nabla\frac{\delta F}{\delta\al}(x)=4x-4$, so $\int\ps{\nabla\frac{\delta F}{\delta\al}}{v}\dd\al=\frac12(-4)(1)+\frac12(4)(-1)=-4$.
  • Non-symmetric kernel $W(x,y)=xy^2$: $F(\al)=\E[X]\,\E[X^2]$ and $\frac{\delta F}{\delta\al}(x)=x\,\E[X^2]+\E[X]\,x^2$; the derivative towards $\delta_1$ is $-1$, both by the formula and numerically.
From the vertical to the horizontal derivative

Moving the mass by $\Id+\tau v$ changes the measure, to first order, by $-\tau\,\dv(\al v)$ (the continuity equation, §4). Plug this zero-mass perturbation into the first variation and integrate by parts:

$$F\big(\pf{(\Id+\tau v)}{\al}\big)=F(\al)-\tau\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta\al}\,\dv(\al v)+o(\tau)=F(\al)+\tau\int\ps{\nabla\frac{\delta F}{\delta\al}}{v}\,\dd\al+o(\tau).$$

The steepest descent direction for the metric $\int\norm v^2\dd\al$ is $v=-\nabla\frac{\delta F}{\delta\al}$, which gives the gradient flow (OT4ML Prop. 15.7, p. 358).

Pitfalls
  • The first variation is defined only up to a constant: never compare two of them pointwise without fixing it.
  • Vertical and horizontal derivatives are different objects: for $\int V\dd\al$ the first is $V$, the second $\nabla V$.
  • For particles $\al=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$, the ordinary gradient of $F$ with respect to $x_i$ is $\frac1n\nabla\frac{\delta F}{\delta\al}(x_i)$: the Wasserstein gradient flow moves each particle with velocity $-n\,\nabla_{x_i}F$ (OT4ML eq. (15.5), p. 360).
In machine learning — gradients of losses between distributions

To train a model with a loss between distributions, you need its derivative with respect to the model’s samples or weights. The table gives it: for an MMD loss, each generated sample moves along $-\nabla_x\int k(x,y)\,\dd(\al-\be)(y)$; for an OT loss, the derivative with respect to the weights of $\al$ is a dual potential, which Sinkhorn computes for free (Lesson 7).
