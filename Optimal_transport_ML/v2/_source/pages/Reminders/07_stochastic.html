8. Stochastic processes

Brownian motion is the continuous random walk; adding it to an ODE gives a stochastic differential equation, whose law evolves by the Fokker–Planck equation — a continuity equation whose velocity contains the score $\nabla\log\rho$. Only what the course needs is recalled here.

Brownian motion and stochastic differential equations

Used in Lesson 6 (stochastic interpolants, Schrödinger bridges, diffusion models), Lesson 7 (entropic OT is a static Schrödinger problem; its Gibbs kernel is a Gaussian heat kernel), Lesson 8 (Langevin dynamics, diffusion models, noisy particle systems).

A Brownian motion is the limit of a random walk with many tiny independent steps. Its displacement over a time $t$ is Gaussian with variance $t$: it spreads like $\sqrt t$, not like $t$. A stochastic differential equation adds such noise to an ODE: a deterministic drift plus random kicks.

Definitions and rules (standard, informal)
  • Brownian motion $(B_t)_{t\ge0}$ in $\R^d$: $B_0=0$, continuous paths, independent increments, $B_t-B_s\sim\N\big(0,(t-s)\mathrm I\big)$. Its paths are nowhere differentiable: “$\dd B_t/\dd t$” does not exist.
  • SDE $\dd X_t=b(X_t)\,\dd t+\sigma\,\dd B_t$, made rigorous as an Itô integral equation and simulated by Euler–Maruyama: $X_{k+1}=X_k+h\,b(X_k)+\sigma\sqrt h\,\xi_k$, $\xi_k\sim\N(0,\mathrm I)$ independent.
  • Itô’s formula (1D): $\dd f(X_t)=f'(X_t)\,\dd X_t+\frac12\sigma^2f''(X_t)\,\dd t$. The extra second-order term comes from $(\dd B_t)^2=\dd t$. Taking expectations: $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\E f(X_t)=\E\big[b(X_t)f'(X_t)+\frac{\sigma^2}2f''(X_t)\big]$.
  • Langevin dynamics $\dd X_t=-\nabla V(X_t)\,\dd t+\sqrt2\,\dd B_t$: its law converges (under mild conditions) to the density $\propto e^{-V}$.
Worked example
  • $\P(\abs{B_1}\le1)\approx0.683$ and $\P(\abs{B_1}\le2)\approx0.954$: the envelopes of the figure.
  • Itô with $f(x)=x^2$ and $X=B$ ($b=0$, $\sigma=1$): $\dd(B_t^2)=2B_t\,\dd B_t+\dd t$, so $\E B_t^2=t$. The ordinary chain rule would miss the $\dd t$.
  • Euler–Maruyama for $\dd X=-X\,\dd t+\sqrt2\,\dd B$ (an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, stationary law $\N(0,1)$): $X_{k+1}=(1-h)X_k+\sqrt{2h}\,\xi_k$. Its stationary variance $v$ solves $v=(1-h)^2v+2h$, so $v=\frac2{2-h}$: $1.053$ for $h=0.1$ (six simulations with $200\,000$ chains each give values within $0.005$ of it), $1.005$ for $h=0.01$. The discretisation bias vanishes like $h$.
Where the $\frac12\sigma^2f''$ comes from

Over a small time step $h$, $\Delta X\approx b\,h+\sigma\,\Delta B$ with $\Delta B\sim\N(0,h)$. Taylor: $f(X+\Delta X)-f(X)\approx f'(X)\Delta X+\frac12f''(X)(\Delta X)^2$. Now $(\Delta X)^2\approx\sigma^2(\Delta B)^2$ has mean $\sigma^2h$ — the same order as the drift term, not smaller — while its fluctuations are of order $h$ times a centred variable and average out over many steps. Summing over steps gives $\int\frac12\sigma^2f''\,\dd t$. For a smooth path, $(\Delta X)^2=O(h^2)$ and the term disappears: this is the difference between stochastic and ordinary calculus.

Pitfalls
  • Noise scales like $\sqrt{h}$, drift like $h$: in Euler–Maruyama the noise term is $\sigma\sqrt h\,\xi$, not $\sigma h\,\xi$.
  • Conventions for the noise level differ: $\sqrt2\,\dd B$ (this page), $\sigma\,\dd B$, or $\sqrt{2\sigma}\,\dd B$. With noise $\sqrt2\,s\,\dd B$ the Laplacian term of Fokker–Planck is $s^2\Delta$ (OT4ML p. 363 writes it this way). Check before reusing a formula.
  • Itô calculus is not ordinary calculus (previous example); another convention (Stratonovich) keeps the ordinary chain rule but changes the drift.
In machine learning — Langevin sampling and diffusion models

The unadjusted Langevin algorithm $x_{k+1}=x_k+h\nabla\log p(x_k)+\sqrt{2h}\,\xi_k$ samples approximately from $p$ using only its score $\nabla\log p$ (no normalising constant). Score-based diffusion models learn the score of noised data with a network and run a reverse-time SDE (or an ODE with the same marginals, next) from noise to data (OT4ML §16.1, p. 407; Remark 16.5, p. 410). Small-step SGD is sometimes modelled by such an SDE, although its noise can be heavy-tailed (OT4ML Remark 15.60, p. 397).

The heat equation, Fokker–Planck and Ornstein–Uhlenbeck

Used in Lesson 8 (Fokker–Planck is the Wasserstein gradient flow of a free energy; the heat equation that of the entropy; convergence rates), Lesson 6 (probability-flow ODEs of diffusion models), Lesson 7 (heat kernels and Gibbs kernels).

Follow not one noisy particle but the density $\rho_t$ of a whole cloud. The drift transports it (a continuity equation, §4), the noise spreads it (a Laplacian). The resulting PDE is the Fokker–Planck equation.

In the course OT4ML p. 363 and Example 15.15; Example 15.13, p. 361

If $\dd X_t=b(X_t)\,\dd t+\sqrt2\,s\,\dd B_t$, the density of $X_t$ solves $\ \partial_t\rho_t=-\dv(b\rho_t)+s^2\Delta\rho_t$. For $b=-\nabla V$ this is the $W_2$ gradient flow of the free energy $\rho\mapsto\int V\rho\,\dd x+s^2\int\rho\log\rho\,\dd x$. Without drift ($V=0$, $s=1$), the entropy flow is the heat equation $\partial_t\rho=\Delta\rho$.

Three ways to read Fokker–Planck ($s=1$, standard)
  • As a continuity equation: $\partial_t\rho+\dv(\rho v)=0$ with $v=b-\nabla\log\rho$ (because $\Delta\rho=\dv(\rho\nabla\log\rho)$). Particles moving with this deterministic velocity have the same laws $\rho_t$ as the SDE.
  • As relaxation to equilibrium: for $b=-\nabla V$ and $\rho_\infty\propto e^{-V}$, $\partial_t\rho=\dv\big(\rho\,\nabla\log\frac{\rho}{\rho_\infty}\big)$, and $\KL(\rho_t|\rho_\infty)$ decreases; under a logarithmic Sobolev inequality with constant $\lambda$ it decays like $e^{-2\lambda t}$ (OT4ML Prop. 15.42, p. 381).
  • Heat equation ($b=0$): $\rho_t=\rho_0*\N(0,2t\,\mathrm I)$, convolution with a Gaussian of variance $2t$.

Ornstein–Uhlenbeck ($V(x)=\frac12\norm x^2$): $X_t=e^{-t}X_0+\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}\,Z$ with $Z\sim\N(0,\mathrm I)$ independent of $X_0$. Gaussians stay Gaussian: $\N(m,\sigma^2)\mapsto\N\big(e^{-t}m,\ e^{-2t}\sigma^2+1-e^{-2t}\big)\to\N(0,1)$. Here $\lambda=1$.

Worked example — OU from $\N(3,0.25)$
  • At $t=1$: mean $3e^{-1}\approx1.104$, variance $0.25e^{-2}+1-e^{-2}\approx0.898$.
  • $\KL(\rho_t|\N(0,1))=\frac12\big(v+m^2-1-\log v\big)$: $4.818$ at $t=0$, $0.612$ at $t=1$, $0.082$ at $t=2$ — below the log-Sobolev bound $4.818\,e^{-2t}$, which gives $0.652$ and $0.088$.
  • The heat equation is checked by finite differences on $\rho_t=\N(0,0.5+2t)$ at $(t,x)=(0.4,0.7)$: $\partial_t\rho=-0.13890=\partial_{xx}\rho$.
From Itô’s formula to Fokker–Planck
  1. $$\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\int f\rho_t=\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\E f(X_t)=\E\big[\ps{b(X_t)}{\nabla f(X_t)}+\Delta f(X_t)\big]=\int\big(\ps{b}{\nabla f}+\Delta f\big)\rho_t .$$Itô’s formula in $\R^d$ with noise $\sqrt2\,\dd B$: the second-order term is $\frac12\cdot2\,\Delta f$.
  2. $$=\int f\,\big(-\dv(b\rho_t)+\Delta\rho_t\big).$$Integrate by parts once in the first term, twice in the second (§4).
  3. This holds for every test function $f$, hence $\partial_t\rho_t=-\dv(b\rho_t)+\Delta\rho_t$.Two densities with the same integrals against all test functions are equal.
Pitfalls
  • The factor in front of $\Delta$ depends on the noise convention (previous reminder).
  • The stationary density is $\propto e^{-V}$ for gradient drifts $b=-\nabla V$. For a general drift there is no such formula: the equilibrium solves $\dv(b\rho)=\Delta\rho$, which is rarely explicit.
  • The equation is linear in $\rho$, but its velocity $b-\nabla\log\rho$ depends on $\rho$: the deterministic particle picture needs the score of the current density.
In machine learning — diffusion models run an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process backwards

The forward noising process of a variance-preserving diffusion model is an OU process: $X_t=e^{-t}X_0+\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}\,Z$ turns any data distribution into nearly $\N(0,\mathrm I)$. A network $s_\theta(t,x)$ is trained to approximate the score $\nabla\log\rho_t$ (denoising score matching). Generation integrates the reverse-time SDE, or the probability-flow ODE $\dot x=-x-\nabla\log\rho_t(x)$ backwards from noise: it is the velocity $b-\nabla\log\rho$ above, so its particles have the same marginals $\rho_t$ (OT4ML Remark 16.5, p. 410–411).

Start the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process $\dd X=-X\,\dd t+\sqrt2\,\dd B$ at the point $X_0=3$. What is the law of $X_1$, and its $W_2$ distance to $\N(0,1)$?

Answer

$X_1=3e^{-1}+\sqrt{1-e^{-2}}\,Z\sim\N(1.104,\,0.930^2)$. By the 1D Gaussian formula (§9), $W_2^2=1.104^2+(0.930-1)^2\approx1.223$, so $W_2\approx1.106$: after one unit of time the Dirac has become a nearly standard Gaussian, only shifted.
