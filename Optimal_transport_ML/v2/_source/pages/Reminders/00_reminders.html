1. Probability measures

A probability measure spreads one unit of mass over a space; you can integrate functions against it, move it with a map, couple it with another one, and say when a sequence of them converges.

Measures, Diracs, densities, integrals

Used in every lesson: the objects that optimal transport compares (data sets, densities, model distributions) are probability measures. See also the refresher of Lesson 3.

Think of a pile of sand of total mass $1$ spread over a space $\X$. A probability measure $\al$ tells you how much sand lies in each region: $\al(A)$ is the mass in the set $A$. You can also weigh a function with it: $\int f\,\dd\al$ is the average of $f$ when each point counts with its share of sand.

In the course OT4ML Def. 2.1–2.2, p. 20; Def. 2.5, p. 21; Def. 2.6, p. 22; Def. 2.9, p. 23

A probability measure $\al\in\M(\X)$ is a positive measure of total mass $\al(\X)=1$. The three kinds used all the time:

  • Dirac $\delta_x$: $\delta_x(A)=1$ if $x\in A$, $0$ otherwise. Then $\int f\,\dd\delta_x=f(x)$.
  • Discrete measure $\al=\sum_{i=1}^na_i\delta_{x_i}$ with weights in the probability simplex $\Sigma_n=\{a\in\R_+^n:\ \sum_ia_i=1\}$ (a histogram). Then $\al(A)=\sum_{x_i\in A}a_i$ and $\int f\,\dd\al=\sum_ia_if(x_i)$. The empirical measure of a point cloud is $\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$.
  • Density: $\dd\al(x)=\rho_\al(x)\,\dd x$, so $\al(A)=\int_A\rho_\al$ and $\int f\,\dd\al=\int f\rho_\al\,\dd x$. More generally, if $\al$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $\lambda$ ($\al\ll\lambda$: $\lambda(A)=0\Rightarrow\al(A)=0$), its relative density $\frac{\dd\al}{\dd\lambda}$ (Radon–Nikodym derivative) satisfies $\int h\,\dd\al=\int h\frac{\dd\al}{\dd\lambda}\,\dd\lambda$.

Probabilistic reading: a random variable $X$ has law $\al$ (written $X\sim\al$) if $\P(X\in A)=\al(A)$; then $\E[f(X)]=\int f\,\dd\al$.

$\supp(\al)$
The support: the smallest closed set carrying all the mass (for $\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i}$ with $a_i>0$: the points $x_i$).
$\al\perp\be$
Mutually singular: they live on disjoint sets (a discrete measure and a density are always mutually singular).
$\Pc_p(\R^d)$
Measures with a finite $p$-th moment, $\int\norm{x}^p\dd\al(x)<\infty$. This is where $W_p$ is finite.
Worked example — a mixed measure

Let $\al=\frac12\delta_0+\frac12\,\mathcal U[0,2]$: half of the mass sits at $0$, the other half is spread uniformly on $[0,2]$ (density $\frac12\cdot\frac12=\frac14$ there).

  • $\al(\{0\})=\frac12$ (an atom), $\al(\{1\})=0$ (the density part gives no mass to a point), $\al\big((0,1]\big)=\frac12\cdot\frac12=\frac14$, $\al\big([0,1]\big)=\frac12+\frac14=\frac34$.
  • Mean: $\int x\,\dd\al=\frac12\cdot0+\frac12\cdot1=\frac12$. Second moment: $\frac12\cdot0+\frac12\cdot\frac43=\frac23$. Variance: $\frac23-\frac14=\frac5{12}$.
Pitfall — a density is not a probability

$\mathcal U[0,\frac12]$ has density $2$ on $[0,\frac12]$: densities can exceed $1$; only their integral is $1$. The “probability of a point” is $0$ for a measure with a density. And $\delta_x$ has no density with respect to $\dd x$: the “Dirac function” of physics is a measure, not a function.

In machine learning — data sets and models are measures

A data set of $n$ images with $d$ pixels is the empirical measure $\hat\al_n=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$ on $\R^d$; a generative model is a measure $\al_\theta$ with a density, or a push-forward of noise (next reminder). Training means making $\al_\theta$ close to $\hat\al_n$, so we need discrepancies that make sense between a discrete measure and a continuous one. The total variation between them is always $2$ (Lesson 3) and the KL divergence is $+\infty$ (§6): this is the first argument for transport distances.

Push-forward of a measure, change of variables

Used in Lesson 1 (Monge’s constraint $\pf{T}{\al}=\be$), Lessons 2–3 (marginals of a plan are push-forwards by the projections; displacement interpolation §6 of Lesson 3), Lesson 5 (projections $x\mapsto\ps{\theta}{x}$), Lesson 6 (flows), and every generative model.

Move each grain of sand from $x$ to $T(x)$: the new pile is the push-forward $\pf{T}{\al}$. The mass that lands in a set $B$ is the mass that came from the points sent into $B$.

In the course OT4ML Def. 2.10, p. 23; Remark 2.13, p. 25

For a measurable map $T:\X\to\Y$ and $\al\in\M(\X)$, the push-forward $\be=\pf{T}{\al}$ is defined, for every measurable $B\subset\Y$, by

$$\be(B)=\al\big(\{x\in\X:\ T(x)\in B\}\big),\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad\int_\Y h(y)\,\dd\be(y)=\int_\X h\big(T(x)\big)\,\dd\al(x)$$

for every bounded measurable $h$. For a discrete measure, $\pf{T}{\Big(\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i}\Big)}=\sum_ia_i\delta_{T(x_i)}$: the weights travel with their points. If $X\sim\al$ then $T(X)\sim\pf{T}{\al}$.

Push-forward of a density OT4ML Prop. 2.12, p. 24

If $T:U\to V$ is a $C^1$ diffeomorphism between open sets of $\R^d$, $\al=\rho_\al\,\dd x$ and $\be=\pf{T}{\al}=\rho_\be\,\dd y$, then

$$\rho_\al(x)=\abs{\det T'(x)}\,\rho_\be\big(T(x)\big),\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad\rho_\be(y)=\frac{\rho_\al\big(T^{-1}(y)\big)}{\abs{\det T'\big(T^{-1}(y)\big)}}.$$

On the line with $T$ increasing: $\rho_\be\big(T(x)\big)\,T'(x)=\rho_\al(x)$. Where $T$ compresses ($T'<1$) the density goes up; where it stretches ($T'>1$) it goes down.

Worked example — $T(x)=x^2/2$ on $\mathcal U[0,2]$
  1. $$\be\big([0,y]\big)=\al\big(\{x\in[0,2]:\ x^2/2\le y\}\big)=\al\big([0,\sqrt{2y}]\big)=\frac{\sqrt{2y}}2,\qquad 0\le y\le 2.$$Definition of the push-forward with $B=[0,y]$; the density of $\al$ is $\frac12$.
  2. $$\rho_\be(y)=\frac{\dd}{\dd y}\frac{\sqrt{2y}}2=\frac1{2\sqrt{2y}}\quad\text{on }(0,2].$$Differentiate the CDF. Check with the formula: $T^{-1}(y)=\sqrt{2y}$, $T'(x)=x$, so $\rho_\be(y)=\frac{1/2}{\sqrt{2y}}$. Same thing.
  3. Mean of $\be$ in two ways: $\int y\,\dd\be=\int_0^2\frac{y}{2\sqrt{2y}}\,\dd y=\frac23$, and $\int T\,\dd\al=\frac12\E[X^2]=\frac12\cdot\frac43=\frac23$.The second formula of the definition, with $h(y)=y$: you never need the density of $\be$ to integrate against it.
Proof of the density formula

Take a test function $h$ and change variables $y=T(x)$, $\dd y=\abs{\det T'(x)}\,\dd x$ (§4):

$$\int h(y)\rho_\be(y)\,\dd y=\int h\big(T(x)\big)\rho_\al(x)\,\dd x=\int h(y)\,\frac{\rho_\al\big(T^{-1}(y)\big)}{\abs{\det T'\big(T^{-1}(y)\big)}}\,\dd y.$$

The first equality is the definition of $\pf{T}{\al}$, the second the change of variables. Two densities with the same integral against every $h$ are equal almost everywhere.

Pitfalls
  • $\rho_\be\ne\rho_\al\circ T^{-1}$: forgetting the Jacobian factor gives a function that does not integrate to $1$.
  • If $T$ is not injective, add the contributions of all the preimages: $\rho_\be(y)=\sum_{x:\,T(x)=y}\rho_\al(x)/\abs{T'(x)}$ in 1D (Exercise 12 of the Lesson 1 sheet is of this kind).
  • A map cannot split mass: $\pf{T}{\delta_x}=\delta_{T(x)}$ is always a single Dirac. This is why no map sends $\delta_0$ to $\frac12\delta_{-1}+\frac12\delta_1$, and why Monge’s problem can have no solution (§9).
In machine learning — generative models are push-forwards

A GAN generator, a VAE decoder, a normalizing flow and a diffusion or flow-matching sampler all define the model as $\al_\theta=\pf{(G_\theta)}{\zeta}$, with $\zeta=\N(0,\mathrm I)$ and $G_\theta$ a network: to sample, draw $z\sim\zeta$ and output $G_\theta(z)$. The density of $\al_\theta$ is usually unknown (or does not exist when the latent dimension is smaller than the data dimension), so the training loss must be computable from samples — transport distances and MMD are (OT4ML §7.4, p. 134). Normalizing flows instead keep $G_\theta$ invertible and use the density formula above (§4).

Joint laws, marginals, independence

Used in Lesson 2 (transport plans are matrices with prescribed row and column sums), Lesson 3 (general couplings, gluing), Lesson 4 (duality), Lesson 7 (the entropy of a plan measures its dependence).

A joint law describes a pair $(X,Y)$; forgetting one coordinate gives a marginal. In the discrete case it is a table: the marginals are its row sums and its column sums. A coupling of $\al$ and $\be$ is any table with these row and column sums — any way of pairing the mass of $\al$ with the mass of $\be$.

In the course OT4ML Def. 3.25, p. 63; Def. 3.26, Remark 3.27, Def. 3.28, p. 64

For $\pi\in\M(\X\times\Y)$ with projections $P_\X(x,y)=x$, $P_\Y(x,y)=y$, the marginals are $\pi_1=\pf{(P_\X)}{\pi}$ and $\pi_2=\pf{(P_\Y)}{\pi}$. The set of couplings is

$$\U(\al,\be)=\big\{\pi\in\M(\X\times\Y):\ \pi_1=\al,\ \pi_2=\be\big\}.$$

If $(X,Y)\sim\pi\in\U(\al,\be)$ then $X\sim\al$ and $Y\sim\be$; the coupling encodes their dependence. The product $\al\otimes\be$ (independence) is always a coupling; $\pf{(\Id,T)}{\al}$ encodes the deterministic relation $Y=T(X)$. Discrete case: $\U(a,b)=\{P\in\R_+^{n\times m}:\ P\ind_m=a,\ P\T\ind_n=b\}$.

Independence. $X$ and $Y$ are independent iff $\pi=\al\otimes\be$ iff $\E[f(X)g(Y)]=\E[f(X)]\,\E[g(Y)]$ for all bounded $f,g$. Independence implies $\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=0$, not conversely.

Worked example — all the couplings of two histograms on $\{0,1\}$

Take $a=(\frac12,\frac12)$ and $b=(\frac14,\frac34)$, both on the points $\{0,1\}$. The row and column constraints leave one free parameter $p=P_{11}$:

$$P(p)=\begin{pmatrix}p&\frac12-p\\[2pt]\frac14-p&\frac14+p\end{pmatrix},\qquad 0\le p\le\tfrac14 .$$
  • The set of couplings is a segment. Its middle point $p=\frac18=a_1b_1$ is the independent coupling ($\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=0$); its ends $p=0$ and $p=\frac14$ have covariances $-\frac18$ and $+\frac18$.
  • With the cost $\abs{x-y}$, moving mass costs $P_{12}+P_{21}=\frac34-2p$, smallest at $p=\frac14$: cost $\frac14$. Only a quarter of the mass has to move from $0$ to $1$. (The minimum is at an end of the segment: §5, linear programming.)
Pitfalls
  • The marginals do not determine the joint law: $\U(\al,\be)$ is usually huge (dimension $(n-1)(m-1)$ for histograms).
  • Zero covariance is not independence: $X$ uniform on $\{-1,0,1\}$ and $Y=X^2$ have $\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=\E[X^3]-\E[X]\E[X^2]=0$, but $\P(X=0,Y=1)=0\ne\P(X=0)\P(Y=1)=\frac29$.
  • A coupling is not a map: a row of $P$ can have several nonzero entries (mass is split).
In machine learning — choosing a coupling between noise and data

Flow matching (Lesson 6) draws pairs $(X_0,X_1)$ from a coupling of the noise $\N(0,\mathrm I)$ and the data, and learns to move $X_0$ to $X_1$. With the independent coupling the straight paths cross; with an optimal-transport coupling computed on each minibatch they are shorter and straighter, which makes sampling cheaper (OT4ML Example 16.2 and Fig. 16.1, p. 408). In domain adaptation or single-cell biology the coupling between two unpaired data sets is precisely the unknown matching to infer (OT4ML Examples 3.22 and 3.24, p. 61).

Conditional laws and disintegration

Used in Lesson 3 (the gluing lemma), Lesson 4 (barycentric projection of a plan), Lesson 6 (the flow-matching velocity is a conditional expectation), Lesson 7 (conditional laws of entropic plans).

Slice the joint law at $X=x$ and renormalise: you get the law of $Y$ given $X=x$. For a transport plan, it says where the mass located at $x$ is sent.

Definition (standard)
  • Discrete: $\P(Y=y_j\mid X=x_i)=\dfrac{P_{ij}}{a_i}$ for $a_i>0$. Densities: $\rho(y\mid x)=\rho(x,y)/\rho_X(x)$.
  • Disintegration (on $\R^d$ and Polish spaces): for $\pi\in\U(\al,\be)$ there is a family $(\pi_x)_x$ of probability measures, unique for $\al$-almost every $x$, with $\int f\,\dd\pi=\int\Big(\int f(x,y)\,\dd\pi_x(y)\Big)\dd\al(x)$: “draw $X\sim\al$, then $Y\sim\pi_X$”.
  • Conditional expectation: $\E[Y\mid X]=m(X)$ with $m(x)=\int y\,\dd\pi_x(y)$. It is a random variable (a function of $X$), with $\E\big[\E[Y\mid X]\big]=\E[Y]$ (tower rule) and $\mathrm{Var}(Y)=\E\big[\mathrm{Var}(Y\mid X)\big]+\mathrm{Var}\big(\E[Y\mid X]\big)$.
  • Best prediction: $m$ minimises $\E\norm{Y-h(X)}^2$ over all functions $h$.
Worked example — the glued plan of Lesson 3

In the gluing example the glued plan between $x\in\{0,2\}$ and $z\in\{1,3\}$ is $\pi=\begin{pmatrix}3/8&1/8\\1/8&3/8\end{pmatrix}$ (rows $x$, columns $z$).

  • Given $X=0$: $Z=1$ with probability $\frac{3/8}{1/2}=\frac34$, $Z=3$ with probability $\frac14$, so $\E[Z\mid X=0]=1.5$. Given $X=2$: $\E[Z\mid X=2]=\frac14+\frac94=2.5$.
  • Tower rule: $\frac12\cdot1.5+\frac12\cdot2.5=2=\E[Z]$. Variances: $\mathrm{Var}(Z)=1=\underbrace{0.75}_{\E\,\mathrm{Var}(Z|X)}+\underbrace{0.25}_{\mathrm{Var}\,\E[Z|X]}$.
  • The map $x\mapsto\E[Z\mid X=x]$ ($0\mapsto1.5$, $2\mapsto2.5$) is the barycentric projection of the plan: a map built from a plan by averaging where each point sends its mass (OT4ML Def. 12.32, p. 281).
Why the conditional expectation is the best prediction

Write $Y-h(X)=\big(Y-m(X)\big)+\big(m(X)-h(X)\big)$ and expand the square. The cross term vanishes: conditioning on $X$, $\E\big[\ps{Y-m(X)}{m(X)-h(X)}\mid X\big]=\ps{\E[Y\mid X]-m(X)}{m(X)-h(X)}=0$. Hence

$$\E\norm{Y-h(X)}^2=\E\norm{Y-m(X)}^2+\E\norm{m(X)-h(X)}^2\ \ge\ \E\norm{Y-m(X)}^2,$$

with equality iff $h=m$ almost surely: $m$ is the orthogonal projection of $Y$ on the functions of $X$ (Pythagoras in $L^2$).

Pitfall — conditioning on an event of probability zero

When $X$ has a density, $\P(X=x)=0$ and “$\P(Y\in B\mid X=x)=\P(Y\in B,X=x)/\P(X=x)$” is $0/0$. The conditional law is then defined through densities or disintegration, and only for almost every $x$. Also, $\P(Y\mid X)$ and $\P(X\mid Y)$ are different objects: for a plan, “where does $x$ send its mass” versus “where does the mass arriving at $y$ come from”.

In machine learning — regression with the squared loss learns a conditional expectation

A network trained with the loss $\E\norm{f_\theta(X)-Y}^2$ tends to $\E[Y\mid X]$, the best predictor above. Flow matching uses exactly this: the velocity field that transports noise to data is $v_t(z)=\E\big[X_1-X_0\mid X_t=z\big]$ for $X_t=(1-t)X_0+tX_1$, the average velocity of all straight paths through $z$, learned by least squares (OT4ML Prop. 16.3, p. 408–409). Denoisers are conditional expectations too: Tweedie’s identity $\E[W\mid W+\sigma\eps=z]=z+\sigma^2\nabla\log\rho_\sigma(z)$ links them to the score used by diffusion models (OT4ML Prop. 16.6, p. 411).

For the optimal plan $P^\star=\begin{pmatrix}1/4&1/4\\0&1/2\end{pmatrix}$ between $a=(\frac12,\frac12)$ and $b=(\frac14,\frac34)$ on $\{0,1\}$ (above), what is the law of $Y$ given $X=0$, and the barycentric projection $x\mapsto\E[Y\mid X=x]$?

Answer

Row $X=0$ has mass $\frac12$, split equally: given $X=0$, $Y=0$ or $Y=1$ with probability $\frac12$ each, so $\E[Y\mid X=0]=\frac12$. Row $X=1$ sends everything to $1$: $\E[Y\mid X=1]=1$. The plan splits the mass at $0$ (it is not a map), and its barycentric projection $0\mapsto\frac12$, $1\mapsto1$ averages the destinations.

Convergence of random variables and of measures; law of large numbers, central limit theorem

Used in Lesson 3 (weak convergence, CLT, total variation), Lesson 5 (Monte Carlo over directions), Lesson 7 (sample complexity of OT), Lesson 8 (particle approximations).

Random variables can be close in several senses: realisation by realisation, with high probability, on average, or only through their laws. For measures, the useful notion compares averages of bounded continuous functions.

Definitions weak convergence: OT4ML Def. 4.18, p. 81
  • Almost surely: $X_n(\omega)\to X(\omega)$ for all $\omega$ outside a null set. In probability: $\P(\norm{X_n-X}>\eps)\to0$ for every $\eps>0$. In $L^p$: $\E\norm{X_n-X}^p\to0$.
  • Weak convergence of measures: $\al_n\wto\al$ iff $\int\varphi\,\dd\al_n\to\int\varphi\,\dd\al$ for every bounded continuous $\varphi$. In law: $X_n\to X$ in law iff their laws converge weakly. Only the laws matter: the variables need not live on the same probability space.
  • Implications: almost surely $\Rightarrow$ in probability $\Rightarrow$ in law, and $L^p\Rightarrow$ in probability (Markov’s inequality).
Law of large numbers and central limit theorem OT4ML Prop. 10.1, p. 195; Remark 4.23, p. 82

Let $X_1,X_2,\dots$ be i.i.d. with law $\al$, mean $m$ and covariance $\Sigma$, and $\bar X_n=\frac1n\sum_{i\le n}X_i$.

  • LLN: $\bar X_n\to m$ almost surely (finite mean suffices). For measures: the empirical measure $\hat\al_n=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{X_i}$ converges weakly to $\al$ almost surely, and $W_p(\hat\al_n,\al)\to0$ if $\al\in\Pc_p$.
  • CLT: $\sqrt n\,(\bar X_n-m)\to\N(0,\Sigma)$ in law (finite variance needed). So the error of an average of $n$ samples is of order $\sigma/\sqrt n$, in any dimension.
Worked example — one hundred dice

A die has mean $3.5$ and variance $\frac{35}{12}$. The mean $\bar X_{100}$ of $100$ dice has standard deviation $\sqrt{35/12}/10\approx0.171$. The CLT predicts $\P(\abs{\bar X_{100}-3.5}\le0.34)\approx\P(\abs{Z}\le1.99)\approx0.954$. The exact value, obtained by convolving the law of one die $100$ times, is $0.957$ (the normal approximation with the usual continuity correction also gives $0.957$). The error of an average scales like $1/\sqrt n$: four times more dice, half the error.

In law but not in probability: if $X\sim\N(0,1)$ and $X_n=-X$, then $X_n$ has the law of $X$ for every $n$ (convergence in law is trivial), but $\abs{X_n-X}=2\abs{X}$ does not go to $0$.

Pitfalls
  • Weak convergence does not imply convergence of moments: mass can escape to infinity (Lesson 3). That is why $W_p$-convergence on $\R^d$ is “weak convergence + convergence of $p$-th moments”.
  • The CLT needs a finite variance: averages of Cauchy variables do not concentrate at all.
  • The $\frac1{\sqrt n}$ rate is for averages of a fixed function. Distances between an empirical measure and its law are slower: $W_p(\hat\al_n,\al)$ decays like $n^{-1/d}$ in dimension $d>2p$ (the curse of dimensionality, OT4ML p. 198), while MMD and sliced distances keep $n^{-1/2}$ (OT4ML Prop. 10.11, p. 203; Thm. 10.16, p. 207).
In machine learning — minibatches are Monte Carlo averages

The loss on a minibatch of size $B$ is an empirical average of per-example losses: it is an unbiased estimate of the population loss, and by the CLT its fluctuation is of order $\sigma/\sqrt B$. Going from $B=64$ to $B=256$ halves the gradient noise. The same reasoning explains why OT losses computed between minibatches are biased and noisy, and why Lesson 7 studies entropic and debiased versions with better sample complexity.
