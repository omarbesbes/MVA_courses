Exercise 1 — A hexagon ★

The vertices of a regular hexagon of side $1$ are numbered $1,\dots,6$ around it; the odd ones are sources, the even ones targets. (a) List the $3!=6$ pairings, their cost for $c=\norm{x-y}$, and whether they have crossing segments. (b) Check the Catalan count of §2. (c) Which pairings are optimal for $\norm{x-y}$? For $\norm{x-y}^2$?

Hint

Two vertices of the hexagon are at distance $1$ (neighbours), $\sqrt3$ or $2$ (opposite). A source and a target are never at distance $\sqrt3$: why?

Solution

A source (odd) and a target (even) are either neighbours (distance $1$) or opposite (distance $2$); vertices two steps apart have the same parity. Pairings, written as source–target pairs:

pairingcost $\norm{x-y}$cost $\norm{x-y}^2$crossings
1–2, 3–4, 5–6330
1–6, 3–2, 5–4330
1–2, 3–6, 5–4460
1–4, 3–2, 5–6460
1–6, 3–4, 5–2460
1–4, 3–6, 5–26123

(b) Five pairings have no crossing: $C_3=\frac14\binom63=5$. (c) For both costs, the two pairings along the sides are optimal (cost $3$). The non-crossing pairings with a diameter pass the test of Proposition 1.5 but are not optimal: non-crossing is necessary, not sufficient.

Exercise 2 — Monge maps between discrete measures ★

On $\R$, $\al=\frac14\delta_0+\frac14\delta_1+\frac12\delta_2$ and $\be=\frac12\delta_{0.5}+\frac12\delta_2$, cost $\abs{x-y}$. (a) Find all Monge maps and the optimal one. (b) Same question with $\be=0.4\,\delta_{0.5}+0.6\,\delta_2$.

Hint

Group the sources by destination; each group must weigh exactly the mass of its target (§6).

Solution

(a) The groups must weigh $\frac12$: $\{0,1\}$ and $\{2\}$. Two maps. Map A: $0,1\mapsto0.5$, $2\mapsto2$, cost $\frac14\cdot0.5+\frac14\cdot0.5+\frac12\cdot0=0.25$. Map B: $2\mapsto0.5$, $0,1\mapsto2$, cost $\frac12\cdot1.5+\frac14\cdot2+\frac14\cdot1=1.5$. Optimal: A, value $0.25$ (it is also the sorted pairing). (b) The possible group masses are $\frac14,\frac12,\frac34,1$: none equals $0.4$. No Monge map: the value is $+\infty$. Kantorovich (Lesson 2) would split the atom at $2$.

Exercise 3 — A Gaussian computation ★

Compute the optimal map and $W_2^2$ between $\N\big((0,0),\mathrm{diag}(1,9)\big)$ and $\N\big((3,4),\mathrm{diag}(4,1)\big)$.

Hint

Diagonal covariances commute: work axis by axis with standard deviations.

Solution

Standard deviations: $(1,3)$ and $(2,1)$. $A=\mathrm{diag}(\frac21,\frac13)$, so $T(x)=\big(3+2x_1,\ 4+\frac{x_2}3\big)$: stretch the first axis, squeeze the second, translate. $\norm{\Delta m}^2=9+16=25$ and $\B^2=(1-2)^2+(3-1)^2=5$, so $W_2^2=30$ and $W_2=\sqrt{30}\approx5.48$.

Exercise 4 — Box–Muller through the Jacobian formula ★★

Let $(U_1,U_2)$ be uniform on $(0,1)^2$ and $T(u_1,u_2)=\big(r\cos2\pi u_2,\ r\sin2\pi u_2\big)$ with $r=\sqrt{-2\log u_1}$. Show that $T(U_1,U_2)\sim\N(0,I_2)$.

Hint

Invert $T$ in polar coordinates $y=(\rho\cos\theta,\rho\sin\theta)$: $u_1=e^{-\rho^2/2}$, $u_2=\theta/2\pi$. The density of $\pf{T}{\mathrm{Leb}}$ at $y$ is $\abs{\det(T^{-1})'(y)}$ (§5 with $\rho_\al=1$).

Solution
  1. $T$ is a $C^1$ bijection from $(0,1)^2$ onto $\R^2$ minus the half-line $\{(x,0):x\ge0\}$ (a null set), with inverse $u_1=e^{-\norm y^2/2}$, $u_2=\theta(y)/2\pi$ ($\theta\in(0,2\pi)$ the polar angle).$r$ runs over $(0,\infty)$ as $u_1$ runs over $(0,1)$; $2\pi u_2$ runs over the angles.
  2. In polar coordinates, $\dd y=\rho\,\dd\rho\,\dd\theta$, and $\dd u_1\,\dd u_2=\rho e^{-\rho^2/2}\dd\rho\cdot\frac{\dd\theta}{2\pi}$ (in absolute value).$\abs{\frac{\dd u_1}{\dd\rho}}=\rho e^{-\rho^2/2}$ and $\frac{\dd u_2}{\dd\theta}=\frac1{2\pi}$.
  3. So $\dd u_1\,\dd u_2=\frac1{2\pi}e^{-\norm y^2/2}\,\dd y$: the density of $T(U_1,U_2)$ is $\frac1{2\pi}e^{-\norm y^2/2}$, that of $\N(0,I_2)$. $\square$Proposition 2.12: the pushed density is $\rho_\al(T^{-1}y)\,\abs{\det(T^{-1})'(y)}$ with $\rho_\al=1$.

A Monte Carlo run ($10^6$ pairs) gives mean $(-0.0005,\,0.0013)$ and covariance within $0.002$ of $I_2$. Box–Muller is a push-forward generative model with an exact density — the 2D ancestor of normalizing flows.

Exercise 5 — A rotation is not a transport plan to be proud of ★★

$\al=\N\big(0,\mathrm{diag}(4,1)\big)$ and $\be=\N\big(0,\mathrm{diag}(1,4)\big)$. (a) Check that the rotation $R$ by $90°$ pushes $\al$ to $\be$ and compute its cost. (b) Compute the Brenier map and $W_2^2$. (c) Explain the difference with §7.

Hint

(a) $\pf{R}{\al}=\N(0,R\Sigma_\al R\T)$; cost $\tr\big((I-R)\Sigma_\al(I-R)\T\big)$. (b) Commuting covariances.

Solution

(a) $R=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, $R\,\mathrm{diag}(4,1)R\T=\mathrm{diag}(1,4)$: admissible. $I-R=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $(I-R)\T(I-R)=2I$, so the cost is $\tr\big(\Sigma_\al(I-R)\T(I-R)\big)=2\tr\Sigma_\al=10$. (b) $A=\mathrm{diag}(\frac12,2)$ (standard deviations $2\to1$ and $1\to2$), $\B^2=(2-1)^2+(1-2)^2=2$: $W_2^2=2$, five times less. (c) The rotation is monotone ($\ps{Rv}v=0\ge0$) but not symmetric: not a convex gradient, hence not optimal. The optimal map squeezes the long axis and stretches the short one, instead of turning the whole ellipse.

Exercise 6 — Swaps are not enough: cyclical monotonicity ★★

Sources $x_1=(0,0)$, $x_2=(0,3)$, $x_3=(3,0)$; targets $y_1=(0,2)$, $y_2=(3,3)$, $y_3=(1,0)$; squared cost. (a) Show that no swap improves the identity pairing $x_i\to y_i$. (b) Find the optimal pairing. (c) Show that the set $\{(x_i,y_i)\}$ is not cyclically monotone (StatOT Def. 1.9, §7).

Hint

(c) Compute $\sum_i\ps{x_i-x_{i+1}}{y_i}$ along the cycle $1\to2\to3\to1$, and relate it to costs.

Solution

The cost matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}4&18&1\\1&9&10\\13&9&4\end{pmatrix}$ (rows $x_i$, columns $y_j$). (a) Identity: $4+9+4=17$. The three swaps cost $18+1+4=23$, $1+9+13=23$ and $4+10+9=23$: no swap helps. (b) The six pairings cost $17,23,23,41,11,23$; the optimum is the 3-cycle $x_1\to y_3$, $x_2\to y_1$, $x_3\to y_2$, cost $1+1+9=11$. (c) Along $1\to2\to3\to1$: $\ps{x_1-x_2}{y_1}+\ps{x_2-x_3}{y_2}+\ps{x_3-x_1}{y_3}=\ps{(0,-3)}{(0,2)}+\ps{(-3,3)}{(3,3)}+\ps{(3,0)}{(1,0)}=-6+0+3=-3<0$: condition (1.11) fails. In cost terms, passing the targets around this cycle changes the cost by $2\times(-3)=-6$: $17\to11$. Two-point monotonicity holds, cyclical monotonicity does not — and only the latter characterises optimality.

Exercise 7 — Checking the Monge–Ampère equation ★★

(a) On $\R$, check (2.7) for the Brenier map from $\N(0,1)$ to $\N(m,\sigma^2)$. (b) In $\R^d$, check it for the dilation from the uniform law on the unit ball to the uniform law on the ball of radius $k$. (c) Why is it $\det\nabla^2\varphi$ and not $\abs{\det\nabla^2\varphi}$ in (2.7)?

Hint

Write the potential: $\varphi(x)=\frac\sigma2x^2+mx$ in (a), $\varphi(x)=\frac k2\norm x^2$ in (b).

Solution

(a) $\varphi'(x)=\sigma x+m$, $\varphi''=\sigma$, and $\rho_\be(\sigma x+m)=\frac1{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/2}$; the product is $\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/2}=\rho_\al(x)$. (b) $\nabla^2\varphi=kI$, $\det=k^d$; $\rho_\be=\frac1{k^d\,\abs{B_1}}$ on $B_k$ (volume scales like $k^d$), so $\det\nabla^2\varphi\cdot\rho_\be(kx)=\frac1{\abs{B_1}}=\rho_\al(x)$. (c) The Hessian of a convex function is positive semi-definite, so its determinant is $\ge0$: $\abs{\det}=\det$. Conversely, solutions of the equation with $\det>0$ but non-convex $\varphi$ (two negative eigenvalues, as $-\frac12\norm x^2$) are not Brenier potentials (§10).

Exercise 8 — Quantile normalization is a barycenter ★★

Two “arrays” measure three genes: $A=(1,4,6)$ and $B=(2,3,10)$. Quantile normalization replaces the $k$-th smallest value of each array by the average of the $k$-th smallest values. (a) Normalise $A$ and $B$. (b) Let $\nu$ be the common empirical law obtained, and $\al_A,\al_B$ the empirical laws of $A$ and $B$ (mass $\frac13$ per value). Compute $W_2^2(\nu,\al_A)$, $W_2^2(\nu,\al_B)$ and $W_2^2(\al_A,\al_B)$, and show that $\nu$ is the $W_2$ barycenter of $\al_A,\al_B$ with weights $\frac12,\frac12$.

Hint

On the line, the barycenter averages quantile functions (Lesson 3, §8), and its cost is $\frac14W_2^2(\al_A,\al_B)$ (Lesson 3, §6).

Solution

(a) Sorted values $(1,4,6)$ and $(2,3,10)$; averages $(1.5,\,3.5,\,8)$. So $A\mapsto(1.5,3.5,8)$ and $B\mapsto(1.5,3.5,8)$ (each value replaced according to its rank). (b) With sorted pairings (§3): $W_2^2(\nu,\al_A)=\frac13(0.5^2+0.5^2+2^2)=1.5$, $W_2^2(\nu,\al_B)=\frac13(0.5^2+0.5^2+2^2)=1.5$ and $W_2^2(\al_A,\al_B)=\frac13(1^2+1^2+4^2)=6$. The quantile function of $\nu$ is the average of those of $\al_A$ and $\al_B$ (step functions with steps at $\frac13,\frac23$), which characterises the 1D barycenter; consistently, $\frac12\cdot1.5+\frac12\cdot1.5=1.5=\frac14\cdot6$, the minimal value. Quantile normalization sends each sample, by its 1D Monge map, onto the barycenter of all samples.

Exercise 9 — Sorting fails for concave costs, also between densities ★★

$\al=\mathcal U_{[0,2]}$, $\be=\mathcal U_{[1,3]}$ (as in Exercise 5 of the sheet), cost $\abs{x-y}^{1/2}$. Compute the cost of the translation $x\mapsto x+1$, of the book-shifting map, and of the map equal to the identity on $(1,2]$ and to $x\mapsto3-x$ on $[0,1]$. Conclude.

Hint

The density of $\al$ is $\frac12$. For the last map, $\frac12\int_0^1\sqrt{3-2x}\,\dd x$.

Solution

Translation: every point moves by $1$, cost $1$. Book-shifting: half of the mass moves by $2$, the rest by $0$: $\frac12\sqrt2\approx0.707$. Reversal: $\frac12\int_0^1\sqrt{3-2x}\,\dd x=\frac12\cdot\frac{3^{3/2}-1}3=\frac{3\sqrt3-1}6\approx0.699$. The monotone map $C_\be^{-1}\circ C_\al$ (the translation) is the worst of the three: Theorem 2.41 needs a convex cost. This is the continuous version of the example of §3, and it contradicts a sentence of the 2019 notes (see §13).

Exercise 10 — The Brenier potential is not unique up to a constant ★★★

$\al=\frac12\,\mathcal U_{[0,1]}+\frac12\,\mathcal U_{[2,3]}$ and $\be=\frac12\,\mathcal U_{[0,1]}+\frac12\,\mathcal U_{[5,6]}$ on $\R$ (each $\mathcal U$ is the uniform probability on its interval). (a) Find the optimal map. (b) Find all convex $\varphi$ with $\varphi'=T$ on the support of $\al$, normalised by $\varphi(0)=0$. Are they equal up to an additive constant?

Hint

(a) Sorting. (b) On $[0,1]$ and on $[2,3]$, $\varphi$ is determined up to a constant each; between $1$ and $2$, convexity only constrains the slopes.

Solution

(a) The first half of the mass of $\al$ is $\mathcal U_{[0,1]}$ (to the left), the first half of $\be$ too: $T(x)=x$ on $[0,1]$ and $T(x)=x+3$ on $[2,3]$, nondecreasing, hence optimal (Theorem 2.41). (b) $\varphi(x)=\frac{x^2}2$ on $[0,1]$ and $\varphi(x)=\frac{x^2}2+3x+c$ on $[2,3]$. On $[1,2]$, $\varphi$ must be convex with slopes between $\varphi'(1)=1$ and $\varphi'(2)=5$, so $1\le\varphi(2)-\varphi(1)\le5$, i.e. $1\le\big(8+c\big)-\frac12\le5$: every $c\in[-6.5,-2.5]$ works (join the two pieces by a convex curve, for instance with slopes $1$ then $5$). For $c=-6.5$ and $c=-2.5$ the potentials agree on $[0,1]$ and differ by $4$ on $[2,3]$: they do not differ by a constant. What Brenier's theorem determines is $\nabla\varphi$, $\al$-a.e.; the potential is unique up to a constant only on each connected piece of the support. (This corrects a sentence of Peyré–Cuturi, Theorem 2.1; see §13.)

Exercise 11 — The displacement midpoint of two Gaussians is narrower than the averaged covariance ★★★

Let $\Sigma_{1/2}=\frac14(I+A)\Sigma_\al(I+A)$ be the covariance of McCann's midpoint between $\N(m_\al,\Sigma_\al)$ and $\N(m_\be,\Sigma_\be)$ (§10), with $A$ the Brenier matrix. Show that $\frac12(\Sigma_\al+\Sigma_\be)-\Sigma_{1/2}=\frac14(I-A)\Sigma_\al(I-A)\succeq0$. Check on the non-commuting example of §8, and interpret.

Hint

Expand both sides and use $A\Sigma_\al A=\Sigma_\be$ and $A=A\T$.

Solution
  1. $\Sigma_{1/2}=\frac14\big(\Sigma_\al+A\Sigma_\al+\Sigma_\al A+A\Sigma_\al A\big)=\frac14\big(\Sigma_\al+A\Sigma_\al+\Sigma_\al A+\Sigma_\be\big)$.Expand; covariance equation.
  2. $\frac12(\Sigma_\al+\Sigma_\be)-\Sigma_{1/2}=\frac14\big(\Sigma_\al+\Sigma_\be-A\Sigma_\al-\Sigma_\al A\big)=\frac14\big(\Sigma_\al-A\Sigma_\al-\Sigma_\al A+A\Sigma_\al A\big)$.Replace $\Sigma_\be$ by $A\Sigma_\al A$.
  3. $=\frac14(I-A)\Sigma_\al(I-A)\succeq0$.Factor; $\ps{(I-A)\Sigma_\al(I-A)v}{v}=\ps{\Sigma_\al w}{w}\ge0$ with $w=(I-A)v$, using $A=A\T$.

Check. On the example of §8 the difference is $\begin{pmatrix}0.086&-0.054\\-0.054&0.107\end{pmatrix}$, eigenvalues $0.042$ and $0.151$ (positive), equal to $\frac14(I-A)\Sigma_\al(I-A)$; and $\det\Sigma_{1/2}=3.784<4.25=\det\frac12(\Sigma_\al+\Sigma_\be)$. Interpretation. $\frac12(\Sigma_\al+\Sigma_\be)$ is the covariance of the mixture $\frac12\N(0,\Sigma_\al)+\frac12\N(0,\Sigma_\be)$, which superimposes the two shapes; the displacement midpoint is a single Gaussian of intermediate shape, never wider in any direction (in the Loewner order).
