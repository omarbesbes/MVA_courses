10. Previews: displacement interpolation and the Monge–Ampère equation OT4ML §2.4, pp. 31–34

Moving every particle along the straight line from $x$ to $T(x)$ gives McCann's displacement interpolation, a constant-speed path from $\al$ to $\be$; writing the Jacobian formula for $T=\nabla\varphi$ gives the Monge–Ampère equation, a nonlinear partial differential equation for the Brenier potential.

Displacement interpolation

An optimal map does not only compare $\al$ and $\be$: it tells each bit of mass where to go. Moving it there at constant speed produces a whole path of distributions between $\al$ and $\be$.

In the course OT4ML Def. 2.29 and Prop. 2.30, pp. 31–32

Definition 2.29 (Monge and McCann displacement interpolation). If $\pf{T}{\al}=\be$, the Monge interpolation generated by $T$ is the curve

$$T_t(x)=(1-t)x+tT(x),\qquad\al_t=\pf{(T_t)}{\al},\qquad t\in[0,1].$$

For the quadratic cost, when $T$ is the optimal Brenier map, this curve is called McCann's displacement interpolation.

Proposition 2.30 (Directed Monge displacement geodesics), in short: if $T$ is optimal for $\widetilde{\mathcal W}_p(\al,\be)$ and $T_t$ is one-to-one on a set of full $\al$-measure for every $t<1$, then $\widetilde{\mathcal W}_p(\al_s,\al_t)=(t-s)\,\widetilde{\mathcal W}_p(\al,\be)$ for $0\le s\le t\le1$. Thus $t\mapsto\al_t$ is an oriented constant-speed geodesic for the directed Monge distance. In particular, for $p=2$, this applies to the Brenier map under the hypotheses of Theorem 2.23.

$T_t(x)$
Where the particle that starts at $x$ is at time $t$: on the segment from $x$ to $T(x)$, a fraction $t$ of the way. $T_0=\Id$, $T_1=T$.
$\al_t=\pf{(T_t)}{\al}$
The distribution of all the particles at time $t$: $\al_0=\al$, $\al_1=\be$. If $X\sim\al$, then $\al_t$ is the law of $(1-t)X+tT(X)$.
constant-speed geodesic
The distance covered between times $s$ and $t$ is proportional to $t-s$: a shortest path, travelled at constant speed. Lesson 3 proves it for $W_2$ (§6 there, with any optimal coupling).
one-to-one
Particles never collide before $t=1$: two particles are never at the same place at the same time. For a Brenier map, $T_t=\nabla\big((1-t)\frac{\norm x^2}2+t\varphi\big)$ is the gradient of a $(1-t)$-strongly convex function, which is one-to-one. (Two straight paths may still intersect at different times: in the crossing example of §2, the two particles pass through the point $(2,\frac23)$ at $t=\frac23$ and $t=\frac13$.)
Intuition — move the mass, don't fade it

The obvious way to go from $\al$ to $\be$ is the mixture $(1-t)\al+t\be$: nothing moves, the mass of $\al$ fades out while that of $\be$ fades in (an image cross-fade, with ghosting). The displacement interpolation moves the mass (a morphing). Between two Diracs: the mixture is $(1-t)\delta_x+t\delta_y$, two fixed atoms with changing weights; the displacement interpolation is $\delta_{(1-t)x+ty}$, one atom sliding from $x$ to $y$.

Worked examples — interpolations you can write down
  • On the line (§9): the quantile function of $\al_t$ is $(1-t)C_\al^{-1}+tC_\be^{-1}$.
  • Gaussians (§8): $T_t(x)=m_t+\big((1-t)\Id+tA\big)(x-m_\al)$ with $m_t=(1-t)m_\al+tm_\be$, an affine map, so $\al_t$ is Gaussian: $$\al_t=\N(m_t,\Sigma_t),\qquad\Sigma_t=\big((1-t)\Id+tA\big)\,\Sigma_\al\,\big((1-t)\Id+tA\big).$$ On the non-commuting example of §8, $\Sigma_{1/2}=\begin{pmatrix}1.414&0.554\\0.554&2.893\end{pmatrix}$, with determinant $3.784$, while the averaged covariance $\frac12(\Sigma_\al+\Sigma_\be)$ has determinant $4.25$: the displacement midpoint is a narrower ellipse (extra Exercise 11 shows that this always happens).
  • 1D Gaussians: $\al_t=\N\big((1-t)m_\al+tm_\be,\ ((1-t)\sigma_\al+t\sigma_\be)^2\big)$ — the straight segment of the $(m,\sigma)$ half-plane of §8.

The Monge–Ampère equation

When $\al$ and $\be$ have densities and the Brenier potential is smooth, combine the Jacobian formula of §5 with $T=\nabla\varphi$. The Jacobian matrix of $\nabla\varphi$ is the Hessian $\nabla^2\varphi$, which is symmetric positive semi-definite (convexity), so $\abs{\det}=\det$:

In the course OT4ML eq. (2.7), p. 33

For sufficiently smooth positive densities and a classical Brenier map, the change-of-variables formula (2.4) gives the Monge–Ampère equation

$$\det\big(\nabla^2\varphi(x)\big)\,\rho_\be\big(\nabla\varphi(x)\big)=\rho_\al(x).\qquad(2.7)$$

With suitable boundary conditions, this characterizes the Brenier potential up to an additive constant among convex solutions. The identity holds pointwise in the classical regime; for nonsmooth convex potentials it is interpreted through the Alexandrov Monge–Ampère measure. The convexity constraint selects the degenerate-elliptic branch and forces $\det(\nabla^2\varphi(x))\ge0$ wherever the Hessian exists.

$\det\nabla^2\varphi(x)$
The volume factor of the map $\nabla\varphi$ at $x$ (§5).
(2.7)
“Mass in = mass out” in every tiny box, written for the unknown potential. It is nonlinear (a determinant of second derivatives) and the unknown appears inside $\rho_\be$ too.
boundary conditions
The “second boundary condition” $\nabla\varphi(\Omega)=\Lambda$: the support of $\al$ must be sent onto the support of $\be$. Without it, the local equation does not see the global constraint $\pf{T}{\al}=\be$.
among convex solutions
Convexity is part of the problem. $\varphi(x)=-\frac12\norm x^2$ in $\R^2$ has $\det\nabla^2\varphi=\det(-\Id)=1$ and solves (2.7) for $\al=\be=$ the uniform law on a disk, but $\nabla\varphi(x)=-x$ is not optimal: it costs $\E\norm{2X}^2=4\cdot\frac12=2$, against $0$ for the identity. The same sign game as for the Gaussian equation of §8.
Worked example — two checks of (2.7)
  • On the line, (2.7) reads $\varphi''(x)\,\rho_\be(\varphi'(x))=\rho_\al(x)$, i.e. $T'(x)\,\rho_\be(T(x))=\rho_\al(x)$. From $\mathcal U[0,1]$ to the density $2y$ on $[0,1]$ (Exercise 10), $T(x)=\sqrt x$: $T'(x)\rho_\be(T(x))=\frac1{2\sqrt x}\cdot2\sqrt x=1=\rho_\al(x)$.
  • Gaussians. $\nabla^2\varphi=A$ is constant, and taking determinants in $A\Sigma_\al A=\Sigma_\be$ gives $\det A=\sqrt{\det\Sigma_\be/\det\Sigma_\al}$ ($=\sqrt{4/3}\approx1.1547$ on the example of §8). At the point $x_0=(0.7,-0.3)$ of that example, $\det A\cdot\rho_\be(T(x_0))$ and $\rho_\al(x_0)$ both equal $0.07061$.
In the course OT4ML Prop. 2.34, p. 34

Proposition 2.34 (Linearization of the Monge–Ampère equation). Let $\rho_\eps=\rho_0+\eps r+o(\eps)$ be a smooth perturbation of a positive reference density $\rho_0$ on a smooth bounded domain $\Omega$, with $\int_\Omega r\,\dd x=0$. […] If $T_\eps(x)=x+\eps\nabla u(x)+o(\eps)$ transports $\rho_0\,\dd x$ to $\rho_\eps\,\dd x$, then, to first order, $-\nabla\cdot(\rho_0\nabla u)=r$. In particular, when $\rho_0$ is constant, the linearized equation is $-\Delta u=r/\rho_0$.

Decoding. Near the identity, the potential is $\varphi=\frac12\norm x^2+\eps u$, and $\det(\Id+\eps\nabla^2u)=1+\eps\Delta u+o(\eps)$ (the trace of $\nabla^2u$ is the Laplacian). So for small deformations Monge–Ampère becomes a Poisson equation: Monge–Ampère is a nonlinear, degenerate cousin of the Laplacian, which is why it is hard to solve numerically (OT4ML Remark 2.33). The divergence $\nabla\cdot$ and the Laplacian are recalled in Reminders — gradient, divergence, Laplacian.

In machine learning — straight paths in flow matching

Flow matching and rectified flows (OT4ML ch. 16, Example 16.2, p. 408) train a generative model by drawing a noise sample $X_0\sim\N(0,I)$ and a data sample $X_1$, forming the straight interpolation $X_t=(1-t)X_0+tX_1$, and regressing a velocity field $v_\theta(t,x)$ on $X_1-X_0$; samples are then generated by integrating $\dot x=v_\theta(t,x)$ from noise. With independent pairs $(X_0,X_1)$, two straight paths often go through the same point at the same time with different directions; a velocity field can only take one value there, so the learned field averages them, which curves the generated trajectories and requires many integration steps. If the pairs follow an optimal coupling, $X_t$ is McCann's interpolation: since $T_t$ is one-to-one, no two paths meet at the same time, a velocity field can follow them exactly, and the flow is straight. In practice, couplings computed by optimal assignment inside each mini-batch (Tong et al., 2023; Pooladian et al., 2023) make trajectories straighter. Dynamic transport is the subject of Lesson 6.

Take $\al=\delta_0$ and $\be=\delta_2$ on $\R$. Compute the displacement midpoint and the mixture midpoint, and their $W_2$ distances to $\al$.

Answer

Displacement: $\al_{1/2}=\delta_1$, at distance $1=\frac12W_2(\al,\be)$ from $\al$ — half way. Mixture: $\frac12\delta_0+\frac12\delta_2$; moving it to $\delta_0$ moves half of the mass by $2$, so $W_2^2=\frac12\cdot4=2$ and $W_2=\sqrt2\approx1.414$: further from $\al$ than $\be$'s half-way point, and not on a shortest path (Lesson 3).
