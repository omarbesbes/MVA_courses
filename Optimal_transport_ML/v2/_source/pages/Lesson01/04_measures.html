4. Probability measures: one language for points and densities OT4ML §2.1, pp. 20–23

A probability measure says how much mass sits in each region of space; Diracs, histograms, densities and their mixtures are all probability measures, handled by the same two operations — the mass of a set and the integral of a function.

Why measures?

The assignment problem only handles $n$ points against $n$ points with equal masses. Real data come in many forms: point clouds with unequal weights, histograms on a grid, continuous densities, mass concentrated on a curve, mixtures of these. We need one object that covers them all, so that “move the mass of $\al$ onto $\be$” has a meaning in every case.

In the course OT4ML §2.1, p. 20

Measures are the language that lets point clouds, densities and singular objects be handled uniformly. We only recall the facts needed later: integration, total variation, densities and probabilistic laws.

Diracs, histograms, discrete measures

In the course OT4ML Defs. 2.1–2.2, p. 20

Definition 2.1 (Probability simplex). The probability simplex of length $n$ is $\Sigma_n=\{a\in\R_+^n:\ \sum_{i=1}^na_i=1\}$. Its elements are also called probability vectors or histograms.

Definition 2.2 (Discrete measure). A discrete measure with weights $a$ and locations $x_1,\dots,x_n\in\X$ is $\al=\sum_{i=1}^na_i\delta_{x_i}$ (2.1), where $\delta_x$ is the Dirac mass at position $x$. It is a probability measure if $a\in\Sigma_n$, and a positive measure if all weights $a_i$ are nonnegative.

The Dirac mass should be thought of as a unit of mass infinitely concentrated at one location. The empirical probability distribution of a point cloud is $\al=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$. Many applications manipulate both the positions $x_i$ (a Lagrangian discretization) and the weights $a_i$ (an Eulerian discretization). Moving the positions gives an adaptive representation, but usually destroys convexity; changing only the weights often preserves it. […] A Dirac measure $\delta_x$ is then defined as $\delta_x(A)=1$ if $x\in A$ and $0$ otherwise, and this extends by linearity for discrete measures of the form (2.1) as $\al(A)=\sum_{x_i\in A}a_i$.

$a\in\Sigma_n$
A list of $n$ nonnegative weights adding up to $1$ — a histogram.
$\delta_x$
“All the mass at $x$.” As a function of sets: $\delta_x(A)=1$ if the set $A$ contains $x$, $0$ otherwise.
$\al=\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i}$
Mass $a_i$ at the point $x_i$. The mass of a set $A$ is the total weight of the points that lie in $A$.
empirical distribution
A data set $x_1,\dots,x_n$ seen as a measure: each point gets mass $\frac1n$.
Lagrangian / Eulerian
Two ways to change a discrete measure: move the points and keep the weights (Lagrangian, like following particles — what a map does, §5), or keep the points and change the weights (Eulerian, like a histogram on a fixed grid). Monge's problem is Lagrangian.

General measures: mass of a set, integral of a function

In the course OT4ML Def. 2.5 and the pairing, p. 21

Definition 2.5 (Radon and probability measures). A locally finite positive Borel measure $\al$ on $\X$ is Radon if $\al(A)=\sup\{\al(K):K\subset A\text{ compact}\}$ for every Borel set $A$. We write $\mathcal M_+(\X)$ for finite positive Radon measures and $\M(\X)=\{\al\in\mathcal M_+(\X):\al(\X)=1\}$ for probability measures.

Every finite Borel measure on a Polish space is Radon. For an integrable function $f$, we use the pairing notation $\ps{f}{\al}=\int_\X f(x)\,\dd\al(x)$. Integration of such a measurable $f$ against a discrete measure $\al$ computes a sum $\int_\X f(x)\,\dd\al(x)=\sum_{i=1}^na_if(x_i)$.

positive measure
A rule $A\mapsto\al(A)\in[0,+\infty]$ giving a mass to each (reasonable) set, which is additive: the mass of a union of disjoint sets is the sum of their masses (also for countably many sets).
Borel set
The “reasonable” sets: those obtained from open sets by complements and countable unions. Every set you will meet in this course is Borel.
Radon, Polish
Regularity conditions needed for the existence theorems of later lessons. On $\R^d$, on a closed subset of it, or on a finite set, they hold automatically: you can forget them for now.
$\M(\X)$
Probability measures: positive, with total mass $\al(\X)=1$. The book writes $\mathcal M_+^1(\X)$.
$\ps{f}{\al}=\int f\,\dd\al$
The average of $f$ under $\al$: for a discrete measure a weighted sum, for a density an ordinary integral (below). The mass of a set is the special case $f=\ind_A$: $\al(A)=\int\ind_A\,\dd\al$.
Reminder — measures and integrals

Two facts are used all along: integrals are linear in $f$ and in $\al$ ($\int(f+g)\,\dd\al=\int f\,\dd\al+\int g\,\dd\al$, $\int f\,\dd(\al+\be)=\int f\,\dd\al+\int f\,\dd\be$), and a measure is determined by its integrals: if $\int f\,\dd\al=\int f\,\dd\be$ for every bounded continuous $f$, then $\al=\be$. Densities, atoms, and the difference between them

In the course OT4ML Def. 2.6, p. 22

Definition 2.6 (Relative density). If $\al$ is absolutely continuous with respect to a positive reference measure $\lambda$, its relative density is the Radon–Nikodym derivative $\rho_\al=\frac{\dd\al}{\dd\lambda}$, $\dd\al(x)=\rho_\al(x)\,\dd\lambda(x)$. Equivalently, for every bounded measurable function $h$, […] $\int_\X h(x)\,\dd\al(x)=\int_\X h(x)\rho_\al(x)\,\dd\lambda(x)$. […] On $\R^d$ the reference $\lambda$ is often Lebesgue measure $\dd x$.

density $\rho_\al$
A function $\ge0$ with $\int\rho_\al=1$; then $\al(A)=\int_A\rho_\al(x)\,\dd x$: the mass of a set is the area under the density. Example: the uniform law $\mathcal U[a,b]$ has density $\frac1{b-a}$ on $[a,b]$.
absolutely continuous
“Has a density” (with respect to Lebesgue measure, unless said otherwise). Equivalently: $\al(A)=0$ for every set $A$ of zero volume.

Two more words are needed in §6–§9, and the difference between them matters:

Definition — atoms

A point $x$ is an atom of $\al$ if $\al(\{x\})>0$. A measure with no atom is atomless (or diffuse).

  • A density implies atomless: a single point has zero volume, so $\al(\{x\})=\int_{\{x\}}\rho_\al=0$.
  • Atomless does not imply a density. The uniform measure on the segment $\{0\}\times[0,1]$ of $\R^2$ has no atom, but it lives on a set of zero area, so it has no density in $\R^2$. (On the line, the Cantor distribution is another example.)

The 1D Monge map (§9) needs a source without atoms; Brenier's theorem (§7) needs a source with a density. The segment above is exactly the source of the counter-example of §6.

Pitfall — a density is not a probability

$\rho_\al(x)$ is a mass per unit length (or volume), not the probability of the point $x$ (which is $0$ for a density). It can exceed $1$: $\mathcal U[0,\frac12]$ has density $2$. And a Dirac has no density at all — not even a very large one.

Mixtures, and the probabilistic reading

In the course OT4ML Def. 2.9, p. 23

Definition 2.9 (Random variable and law). Let $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\P)$ be a probability space and let $\X$ be Polish. An $\X$-valued random variable is a measurable map $X:(\Omega,\mathcal F)\to(\X,\mathcal B(\X))$. Its law is $\mathrm{Law}(X)=\pf{X}{\P}$, $\mathrm{Law}(X)(A)=\P(X\in A)$ for every Borel set $A\subset\X$.

For every integrable function $f$, integration with respect to this law is expectation: $\int_\X f(x)\,\dd\,\mathrm{Law}(X)(x)=\E[f(X)]$.

So “$X\sim\al$” and “$\al$ is the law of $X$” say the same thing, and $\int f\,\dd\al=\E[f(X)]$. The notation $\pf{X}{\P}$ is a push-forward, the subject of §5.

Worked example — a measure that is neither discrete nor a density

Let $\al=\frac12\delta_0+\frac12\,\mathcal U[0,1]$ on $\R$. As a random variable: flip a fair coin; on heads $X=0$, on tails $X$ is uniform on $[0,1]$.

  • Mass of a set: $\al([0,\frac12])=\frac12\cdot1+\frac12\cdot\frac12=\frac34$ (the atom, plus half of the uniform part); $\al(\{0\})=\frac12$; $\al((0,\frac12])=\frac14$.
  • Integral: $\int x^2\,\dd\al=\frac12\cdot0^2+\frac12\int_0^1x^2\,\dd x=\frac16$, and the mean is $\int x\,\dd\al=\frac14$. (A Monte Carlo run with $10^6$ draws gives $0.7500$ and $0.1665$.)
  • It has an atom at $0$, hence no density; it is not discrete either. Its CDF (§9) jumps by $\frac12$ at $0$, then grows linearly.
In machine learning — data sets and documents are measures
  • A data set is a discrete measure. A mini-batch of $n=64$ colour images of size $32\times32$ is the empirical measure $\frac1{64}\sum_{i=1}^{64}\delta_{x_i}$ on $\R^{3072}$ ($32\times32\times3=3072$ pixel values). The data distribution itself is unknown; a generative model must approach it from such samples. A discrepancy that works for discrete measures (unlike KL or $L^2$, §1) can be computed between two mini-batches.
  • A document is a histogram on a geometric space. In the word mover's distance (Kusner et al., 2015; OT4ML Example 4.11, p. 78), a document is the measure $\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i}$ where $a_i$ is the normalised frequency of word $i$ and $x_i\in\R^{300}$ its word2vec embedding. Two documents with no word in common (“Obama speaks to the media in Illinois” / “The President greets the press in Chicago”) are far for any bag-of-words count, but close for the transport cost, because their words are close in the embedding space.
  • Lagrangian or Eulerian. Particle methods and generative models move the positions of samples (Lagrangian); image histograms or occupancy grids change the weights on fixed bins (Eulerian). Monge's problem and its maps are the Lagrangian side.

For $\al=0.2\,\delta_{0.5}+0.5\,\delta_{1.5}+0.3\,\delta_{2.5}$, compute $\al([1,3])$ and $\int x\,\dd\al$.

Answer

$\al([1,3])=0.5+0.3=0.8$ (the points $1.5$ and $2.5$ are in $[1,3]$, the point $0.5$ is not). $\int x\,\dd\al=0.2\cdot0.5+0.5\cdot1.5+0.3\cdot2.5=0.1+0.75+0.75=1.6$.

True or false: “if every point has mass zero, the measure has a density”.

Answer

False. The uniform measure on a segment in the plane gives zero mass to every point but has no density with respect to area: it gives mass $1$ to the segment, a set of area $0$. Atomless is weaker than absolutely continuous.
