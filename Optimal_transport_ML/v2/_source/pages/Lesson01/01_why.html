1. Why transport? Comparing distributions with geometry OT4ML ch. 1–2, pp. 9, 20

Machine learning keeps comparing probability distributions; optimal transport compares them by the cost of moving one onto the other, so that the comparison inherits the geometry of the space where the data live.

The problem: machine learning compares distributions

Many learning problems are, at their core, a comparison between two probability distributions:

  • a generative model produces samples (images, molecules, sentences); training asks that the distribution of its samples be close to the distribution of the data;
  • in domain adaptation, a classifier is trained on a labelled source domain (say, synthetic images) and must work on a target domain (real photos) whose feature distribution is shifted;
  • a 3D scanner returns a point cloud; to compare it with a reference shape, you compare two clouds of points;
  • two images are compared through their colour histograms, two documents through their word histograms.

In each case we want a number $D(\al,\be)\ge0$ that says how different two distributions $\al$ and $\be$ are, so that learning becomes $\min_\theta D(\al_\theta,\be)$ over the parameters $\theta$ of a model $\al_\theta$. Two requirements are natural: $D$ should make sense for point clouds (finite samples) as well as for densities, and it should be small when the mass is moved a little: shifting every point by $\eps$ should change $D$ by about $\eps$.

Pointwise comparisons are blind to geometry

The classical ways of comparing distributions compare them point by point — the density of $\al$ at $x$ against the density of $\be$ at the same $x$. They do not know how far apart two points are.

Reminder — two classical discrepancies

Kullback–Leibler divergence: $\KL(\al\,|\,\be)=\int\log\frac{\dd\al}{\dd\be}\,\dd\al$ if $\al$ has a density with respect to $\be$, and $+\infty$ otherwise (in particular as soon as $\al$ puts mass where $\be$ puts none). Total variation: the total mass by which the two distributions differ, $\sum_i\abs{a_i-b_i}$ for histograms on the same points, $\int\abs{\rho_\al-\rho_\be}$ for densities. Both are recalled in Reminders — entropy and KL and f-divergences; total variation is studied in Lesson 3.

Worked example — moving mass by $\eps$, or by $1000$
  • Two Diracs. $\al=\delta_0$ and $\be=\delta_\eps$ on $\R$, with $\eps\ne0$. The total variation is $\abs{1}+\abs{-1}=2$, the largest possible value, whatever $\eps$; $\KL(\delta_0\,|\,\delta_\eps)=+\infty$ because $\delta_0$ charges the point $0$ that $\delta_\eps$ ignores; neither has a density, so $\norm{\rho_\al-\rho_\be}_{L^2}$ is not even defined. Moving the unit of mass from $0$ to $\eps$ costs $\abs{\eps}$ for the cost $c(x,y)=\abs{x-y}$: it goes to $0$ with $\eps$, as we want.
  • Two bumps. $\al=\mathcal U[0,1]$ and $\be=\mathcal U[a,a+1]$ (uniform laws, density $1$ on their interval) with $a\ge1$. The supports do not overlap, so $\norm{\rho_\al-\rho_\be}_{L^2}=\sqrt{1+1}=\sqrt2\approx1.414$ for $a=1$, $a=10$ and $a=1000$ alike. Moving all the mass by $a$ costs $a$: $1$, $10$, $1000$.

The second example is typical of machine learning: a model whose samples land in the wrong place gets the same pointwise score whether it is slightly or badly wrong, so the score gives no direction in which to improve.

The idea: lift a ground cost to distributions

Fix a ground cost $c(x,y)\ge0$: the price of moving one unit of mass from the point $x$ to the point $y$ (for instance $\norm{x-y}$ or $\norm{x-y}^2$). The transport cost between $\al$ and $\be$ is the price of the cheapest way to move all the mass of $\al$ onto $\be$. For two Diracs there is only one way, so the cost of $\delta_x\to\delta_y$ is $c(x,y)$: the ground geometry is kept, without distortion, inside the space of distributions. This is what “lifting a ground distance to a distance between distributions” means.

There are two ways to make “move the mass” precise. In Monge's formulation (1781, this lesson) each point is sent to one place: the unknown is a map $T$. In Kantorovich's formulation (1942, Lesson 2) the mass at a point may be split between several destinations: the unknown is a coupling. With $c=d^p$ the second one gives the Wasserstein distance $W_p$ (Lesson 3). The book starts with the simplest case of all:

In machine learning — transport costs as losses between distributions
  • Generative models. A generator $g_\theta$ maps a Gaussian noise vector $z\in\R^{k}$ to an image $g_\theta(z)\in\R^d$. Its distribution has no density you can evaluate (§5), but you can sample it and compare the samples with data samples. Wasserstein GANs (Arjovsky, Chintala, Bottou, 2017) train $g_\theta$ by minimising an estimate of the transport cost $W_1$ between generated and real images (OT4ML §7.4 “GANs via duality”, p. 134; Lesson 4 explains the dual form they use).
  • Point clouds. To train a network that outputs a 3D point cloud of $n$ points, a classical loss is the cost of the optimal assignment between the predicted points and the $n$ ground-truth points — exactly the problem of §2 (the “Earth Mover's distance” loss of Fan, Su and Guibas, 2017).
  • Domain adaptation. Transporting the labelled source features onto the target features lets a classifier trained on the source be used on the target (Courty, Flamary, Tuia, Rakotomamonjy, 2017; OT4ML Example 3.22, p. 61). We return to it with Brenier's theorem in §7.

In all three, the point is the one of the worked example above: the loss sees how far the mass is from where it should be, so its gradient tells the model where to move it.

A short history

The outline of the course traces the following line; the dates are added here for orientation, and each name comes back in a later lesson.

  • Monge (1781), Mémoire sur la théorie des déblais et des remblais: move the earth dug from cuts to fill embankments, each particle going to one destination, at minimal total distance. This lesson.
  • Kantorovich (1942) lets mass split and turns the problem into a linear program with a dual; Dantzig's simplex method (1947) solves linear programs; Birkhoff (1946) and von Neumann (1953) explain why, for equal weights, splitting gains nothing (permutations are the extreme points of the doubly stochastic matrices). Lesson 2.
  • Schrödinger (1931) asks for the most likely evolution of a cloud of particles — an entropic transport problem; Sinkhorn (1964) studies the matrix scaling that solves it. Lesson 7.
  • Brenier (1987, 1991): for the squared distance the optimal map is the gradient of a convex function (§7). McCann (1997): displacement interpolation (§10). Jordan, Kinderlehrer and Otto (1998): diffusion equations are gradient flows for $W_2$ (Lesson 8). Villani's books (2003, 2009) and Figalli's regularity theory (§7) made the field central in analysis (Fields Medals 2010 and 2018).
  • Cuturi (2013) computes entropic transport with Sinkhorn's algorithm on GPUs, fast enough for learning (Lesson 7); Wasserstein GANs (2017) bring transport to generative modelling.

Why does every reasonable transport cost give $\mathrm{cost}(\delta_x,\delta_y)=c(x,y)$?

Answer

All the mass of $\delta_x$ sits at $x$ and all of it must end at $y$: there is only one way to move it, sending the unit of mass from $x$ to $y$, at price $c(x,y)$. Diracs are the first test case of every statement of this course.

In the two-bump example, what is the transport cost for $c(x,y)=\abs{x-y}^2$, and why can no plan do better than moving everything by $a$?

Answer

The translation $x\mapsto x+a$ moves each unit of mass by $a$, so it costs $a^2$ for the squared cost. No plan does better: whatever the plan, the mean position goes from $\frac12$ to $a+\frac12$, so the average displacement $\E[Y-X]$ is $a$, and $\E[(Y-X)^2]\ge\big(\E[Y-X]\big)^2=a^2$ (the variance is $\ge0$). We prove the general version in §8 (means separate from the rest).
