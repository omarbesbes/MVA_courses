9. One dimension: quantile functions solve Monge's problem OT4ML §2.6, pp. 37–40

On the line, send each point $x$ to the point of $\be$ that has the same fraction of mass to its left: $T=C_\be^{-1}\circ C_\al$. This “continuous sorting” is an optimal Monge map for every convex cost $h(x-y)$ as soon as $\al$ has no atoms, and its cost is an integral of differences of quantiles.

Cumulative and quantile functions

In the course OT4ML Def. 2.39, p. 37

Definition 2.39 (Cumulative and quantile functions). For $\al\in\M(\R)$, its cumulative distribution function is

$$C_\al(x)=\al\big((-\infty,x]\big).\qquad(2.8)$$

Its generalized inverse, or quantile function, is

$$C_\al^{-1}(r)=\inf\{x\in\R:\ C_\al(x)\ge r\},\qquad r\in(0,1).\qquad(2.9)$$
$C_\al(x)$
The fraction of the mass of $\al$ lying at or to the left of $x$. It is nondecreasing, goes from $0$ (at $-\infty$) to $1$ (at $+\infty$), and is right-continuous. It jumps by $a$ at an atom of mass $a$, and is flat where $\al$ has no mass.
$C_\al^{-1}(r)$
“The point below which a fraction $r$ of the mass lies”: the first $x$ where the CDF reaches the level $r$. For $r=\frac12$ it is the median. If $C_\al$ is a continuous increasing bijection, $C_\al^{-1}$ is its ordinary inverse.
generalized
When $C_\al$ jumps (atom), several levels $r$ give the same point: $C_\al^{-1}$ has a flat step. When $C_\al$ is flat (gap in the support), $C_\al^{-1}$ jumps over the gap.
Reminder — the one property of the quantile function you need

For $r\in(0,1)$ and $x\in\R$ (StatOT eq. (1.5), p. 27):

$$C_\al^{-1}(r)\le x\quad\iff\quad r\le C_\al(x).$$

Proof. ($\Leftarrow$) If $r\le C_\al(x)$, then $x$ belongs to the set $\{x':C_\al(x')\ge r\}$, so its infimum is $\le x$. ($\Rightarrow$) That set is an interval $[q,+\infty)$ containing its left end $q=C_\al^{-1}(r)$: take $x'_n\downarrow q$ in the set; right-continuity gives $C_\al(q)=\lim C_\al(x'_n)\ge r$. So if $q\le x$, monotonicity gives $C_\al(x)\ge C_\al(q)\ge r$. $\square$ More: Reminders — CDF and quantiles.

Worked examples — three CDFs and their quantile functions
  • $\al=\frac12\delta_0+\frac12\delta_1$: $C_\al=0$ on $(-\infty,0)$, $\frac12$ on $[0,1)$, $1$ on $[1,\infty)$; $C_\al^{-1}(r)=0$ for $r\le\frac12$ and $1$ for $r>\frac12$. Two jumps of the CDF, two flat steps of the quantile function.
  • $\al=\mathcal U[0,2]$: $C_\al(x)=\frac x2$ on $[0,2]$, $C_\al^{-1}(r)=2r$.
  • $\al=\frac12\delta_0+\frac12\,\mathcal U[0,1]$ (§4): $C_\al(x)=\frac12+\frac x2$ on $[0,1]$ (jump of $\frac12$ at $0$); $C_\al^{-1}(r)=0$ for $r\le\frac12$ and $2r-1$ for $r>\frac12$.
In the course OT4ML Prop. 2.40, p. 37

Proposition 2.40 (Quantile push-forward). One has $\pf{(C_\al^{-1})}{\mathrm{Leb}_{(0,1)}}=\al$. If $\al$ has no atoms, then $\pf{(C_\al)}{\al}=\mathrm{Leb}_{(0,1)}$.

$\mathrm{Leb}_{(0,1)}$
The uniform law on $(0,1)$.
first claim
If $U$ is uniform, $C_\al^{-1}(U)\sim\al$: inverse transform sampling, the simplest generative model (§5).
second claim
If $X\sim\al$ and $\al$ has no atom, $C_\al(X)$ is uniform: the probability integral transform (why p-values of continuous tests are uniform under the null). It fails with atoms (Exercise 11).

The one-dimensional Monge map

In the course OT4ML Thm. 2.41, p. 38

The quantile construction becomes a deterministic Monge map only when the source has no atoms, because the cumulative distribution function can then be used as a genuine change of variable.

Theorem 2.41 (One-dimensional Monge solution). Let $\al,\be\in\M(\R)$, assume that $\al$ has no atoms, and let $h:\R\to[0,+\infty)$ be convex. Consider the cost $c(x,y)=h(x-y)$. Write $q_\al=C_\al^{-1}$ and $q_\be=C_\be^{-1}$, and assume that $h(q_\al-q_\be)\in L^1(0,1)$. Then

$$T=q_\be\circ C_\al=C_\be^{-1}\circ C_\al\qquad(2.10)$$

satisfies $\pf{T}{\al}=\be$ and minimizes $\min_{\pf{S}{\al}=\be}\int h(x-S(x))\,\dd\al(x)$. In particular, for any $1\le p<+\infty$, if $\al$ and $\be$ have finite $p$th moments and $\al$ has no atoms, then $h(t)=\abs t^p$ gives the usual one-dimensional Monge optimizer.

$T(x)=C_\be^{-1}(C_\al(x))$
Read it right to left: $C_\al(x)$ is the fraction $r$ of $\al$'s mass to the left of $x$; $C_\be^{-1}(r)$ is the point of $\be$ with the same fraction to its left. Equal ranks to equal ranks: sorting, for a continuum of points (§3).
$\al$ has no atoms
So that $C_\al$ is continuous and $C_\al(X)$ uniform (Proposition 2.40); an atom would have to be split.
$h$ convex
As in §3: $\abs t^p$ with $p\ge1$, but not $\abs t^{1/2}$.
$h(q_\al-q_\be)\in L^1$
The optimal cost $\int_0^1h(q_\al(r)-q_\be(r))\,\dd r$ is finite.
Intuition — a continuum of sorted points

Cut both distributions into many thin slices of equal mass, from left to right. The $k$-th slice of $\al$ goes to the $k$-th slice of $\be$: this is the sorted matching of §3 between two clouds of equal-mass points, and $T$ is its limit. $T$ is nondecreasing, steep where $\be$ is spread out and flat where $\be$ is concentrated: $T'(x)=\rho_\al(x)/\rho_\be(T(x))$ (the Jacobian formula of §5).

Idea of the proof

Admissibility: $C_\al$ turns $\al$ into a uniform law, $C_\be^{-1}$ turns the uniform law into $\be$. Optimality, for the quadratic cost: $T$ is nondecreasing, hence a “subgradient of a convex function”, and the easy half of Brenier's theorem (§7) applies. For a general convex $h$ the book uses the one-dimensional Kantorovich theorem (Theorem 3.39, Lesson 2).

Full proof (admissibility; optimality for the quadratic cost)

Admissibility.

  2. $\pf{T}{\al}=\pf{(C_\be^{-1})}{\big(\pf{(C_\al)}{\al}\big)}=\pf{(C_\be^{-1})}{\mathrm{Leb}_{(0,1)}}=\be$.Composition rule of push-forwards (§5). ($T$ is defined $\al$-almost everywhere: the points where $C_\al(x)\in\{0,1\}$ carry no mass.)

For a general convex $h$, OT4ML proves optimality with the one-dimensional Kantorovich theorem (Theorem 3.39): the quantile coupling $\pf{(q_\al,q_\be)}{\mathrm{Leb}_{(0,1)}}$ is optimal among all couplings, and when $\al$ has no atoms it is the coupling $\pf{(\Id,T)}{\al}$ of the map $T$. For $h=\abs\cdot$, StatOT Thm. 1.7 (p. 27) gives a complete proof through CDFs: the quantile coupling is optimal for $W_1$.

Pitfall — with an atom in the source, $C_\be^{-1}\circ C_\al$ is not admissible

$\al=\delta_0$ and $\be=\mathcal U[0,1]$: $C_\al(0)=1$, so $T(0)=C_\be^{-1}(1)=1$ and $\pf{T}{\al}=\delta_1\ne\be$. Of course: no map splits an atom (§6). The quantile coupling $\pf{(C_\al^{-1},C_\be^{-1})}{\mathrm{Leb}_{(0,1)}}$ still describes the optimal way to move the mass (Lesson 2), but it is not a map. Peyré–Cuturi state (2.39) without the hypothesis (see the notes on the sources in §13).

Worked examples — the formula on familiar laws
  • Uniform to uniform. $\mathcal U[a,b]\to\mathcal U[c,d]$: $C_\al(x)=\frac{x-a}{b-a}$, $C_\be^{-1}(r)=c+(d-c)r$, so $T(x)=c+\frac{d-c}{b-a}(x-a)$, affine (Exercise 4 of the sheet).
  • Gaussians. With $\Phi$ the CDF of $\N(0,1)$: $C_\al(x)=\Phi\big(\frac{x-m_\al}{\sigma_\al}\big)$ and $C_\be^{-1}(r)=m_\be+\sigma_\be\Phi^{-1}(r)$, so $T(x)=m_\be+\frac{\sigma_\be}{\sigma_\al}(x-m_\al)$: the map of §8, recovered.
  • Uniform to exponential. $\mathcal U[0,1]\to\mathrm{Exp}(1)$: $C_\al(x)=x$, $C_\be^{-1}(r)=-\log(1-r)$, so $T(x)=-\log(1-x)$ — the inverse transform sampler of §5 is an optimal map.

The cost: an integral of quantiles

In the course OT4ML Prop. 2.44, pp. 38–39

Proposition 2.44 (One-dimensional Wasserstein formulas). Let $1\le p<+\infty$ and let $\al,\be\in\M(\R)$ have finite $p$-th moments. Then

$$\mathcal W_p^p(\al,\be)=\int_0^1\abs{C_\al^{-1}(r)-C_\be^{-1}(r)}^p\,\dd r=\norm{C_\al^{-1}-C_\be^{-1}}^p_{L^p([0,1])}.\qquad(2.11)$$

For $p=1$, this is equivalently

$$\mathcal W_1(\al,\be)=\norm{C_\al-C_\be}_{L^1(\R)}=\int_\R\abs{C_\al(x)-C_\be(x)}\,\dd x .\qquad(2.12)$$
$\mathcal W_p$
The Wasserstein distance of Lesson 3 (defined with couplings, so (2.11) holds even when $\al$ has atoms). When $\al$ has no atoms it is also the optimal Monge cost of Theorem 2.41.
(2.11)
Pair the levels: the mass at level $r$ goes from $C_\al^{-1}(r)$ to $C_\be^{-1}(r)$, and the costs are averaged over $r$. Plugging the map into the cost: with $X=C_\al^{-1}(U)$, $T(X)=C_\be^{-1}(C_\al(C_\al^{-1}(U)))=C_\be^{-1}(U)$ (step 3 of the proof of Prop. 2.40), so $\int h(x-T(x))\,\dd\al=\int_0^1h\big(C_\al^{-1}(r)-C_\be^{-1}(r)\big)\dd r$.
(2.12)
For $p=1$, the area between the two CDFs — the same area as between the two quantile functions, measured horizontally instead of vertically.
Why the two $p=1$ formulas agree (layer cake)
  1. For two numbers $a,b$: $\abs{a-b}=\int_\R\abs{\ind_{a\le x}-\ind_{b\le x}}\,\dd x$.The integrand is $1$ exactly for $x$ between $a$ and $b$ (one end included).
  2. Take $a=C_\al^{-1}(r)$, $b=C_\be^{-1}(r)$ and integrate over $r\in(0,1)$; exchange the integrals (Fubini, everything is $\ge0$).The left side becomes $\int_0^1\abs{C_\al^{-1}-C_\be^{-1}}$.
  3. $\ind_{C_\al^{-1}(r)\le x}=\ind_{r\le C_\al(x)}$, so the inner integral is $\int_0^1\abs{\ind_{r\le C_\al(x)}-\ind_{r\le C_\be(x)}}\,\dd r=\abs{C_\al(x)-C_\be(x)}$. $\square$The key equivalence of the reminder; then the integrand is $1$ for $r$ between $C_\al(x)$ and $C_\be(x)$.
Worked example — uniform to exponential

$\al=\mathcal U[0,1]$, $\be=\mathrm{Exp}(1)$, $T(x)=-\log(1-x)$.

  • $\mathcal W_1=\int_0^1\big(-\log(1-x)-x\big)\,\dd x=1-\frac12=\frac12$ (note $-\log(1-x)\ge x$, so no absolute value is needed). With CDFs: $\int_0^1\big(x-(1-e^{-x})\big)\dd x+\int_1^\infty e^{-x}\,\dd x=\big(\frac12-e^{-1}\big)+e^{-1}=\frac12$. Same value.
  • $\mathcal W_2^2=\E\big[(U+\log(1-U))^2\big]=\E U^2+\E[E^2]-2\E[UE]$ with $E=-\log(1-U)\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1)$: $\frac13+2-2\cdot\frac34=\frac56\approx0.833$, using $\E[E^2]=2$ and $\E[UE]=\int_0^1-u\log(1-u)\,\dd u=\frac34$. (Quadrature: $0.83333$.)

Geometry on the line: composition, flatness, interpolation

In the course OT4ML Remark 2.42 and Example 2.43, p. 38; Remark 2.46, p. 39

Remark 2.42 (Composition is one-dimensional). In dimension one, optimal maps compose. Assume that $\al$ and $\be$ have no atoms; no atomlessness assumption is needed on $\ga$. Then the monotone rearrangements $T_{\al\to\be}=C_\be^{-1}\circ C_\al$, $T_{\be\to\ga}=C_\ga^{-1}\circ C_\be$ are well defined $\al$- and $\be$-almost everywhere. Then $T_{\be\to\ga}\circ T_{\al\to\be}=T_{\al\to\ga}$ $\al$-a.e. […]

Example 2.43 (Linear obstruction to composing Brenier maps), in short: if $T_1(x)=A_1x$ and $T_2(x)=A_2x$ with $A_1,A_2$ symmetric positive definite, $T_2\circ T_1$ has matrix $A_2A_1$, a gradient field only when $A_1A_2=A_2A_1$.

Remark 2.46 (Quantiles linearize one-dimensional Wasserstein geometry). Formula (2.11) means that the map $\al\mapsto C_\al^{-1}$ embeds one-dimensional Wasserstein geometry isometrically into $L^p(0,1)$. Its image is the closed convex cone of almost-everywhere nondecreasing functions with finite $p$-th moment, not the whole linear space. For $p=2$, the Wasserstein metric on probability measures over the real line is therefore Hilbertian in the precise sense of being isometric to a convex subset of a Hilbert space.

  • Composition. On the line, the optimal map from $\al$ to $\ga$ can be computed through any intermediate $\be$. Not in $\R^2$: $A_1=\mathrm{diag}(2,1)$ and $A_2=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ are symmetric positive definite, but $A_2A_1=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\2&2\end{pmatrix}$ is not symmetric — the composition of two Brenier maps is not a Brenier map.
  • Flatness. Through quantile functions, measures on the line become points of a convex set in $L^2(0,1)$, with the $L^2$ distance. Averages are easy there: the $W_2$ barycenter of measures on the line averages their quantile functions (Lesson 3, §8); and every projection of sliced transport (Lesson 5) is such a 1D problem.
  • Interpolation. The quantile function of McCann's interpolation (§10) is $C_{\al_t}^{-1}=(1-t)C_\al^{-1}+tC_\be^{-1}$: a straight line in the flat picture (OT4ML Fig. 2.11, p. 40).

In machine learning — one-dimensional transport everywhere
  • Colour transfer, channel by channel. The cheapest colour transfer applies Theorem 2.41 to each colour channel separately: the red values of the source image are mapped by $C_{\be,R}^{-1}\circ C_{\al,R}$ onto those of the target image, and the same for green and blue (this is what skimage.exposure.match_histograms does on colour images). It ignores the correlations between channels, so it can create colours absent from the target palette; the 3D map of §7, the Gaussian map of §8, or sliced transport (Lesson 5, which iterates 1D maps along random directions) correct this.
  • Generators and flows in 1D. Inverse transform sampling $C_\be^{-1}(U)$ is a generator, and in 1D every invertible normalizing flow is a monotone map. In higher dimension, triangular (Knothe–Rosenblatt) maps transport one coordinate at a time with conditional 1D quantile maps (OT4ML Prop. 2.51, p. 42, beyond this course): this triangular structure is the one of autoregressive normalizing flows.
  • Cheap, differentiable losses. Because $W_p$ on the line only needs a sort, it is computed in $O(n\log n)$ on mini-batches and differentiated through the sort; the sliced Wasserstein distance (Lesson 5) averages it over many random 1D projections of high-dimensional data.
Reflexes
  • A transport problem on the line → write the CDFs and the quantile functions; the map is $C_\be^{-1}\circ C_\al$ (source without atoms), the cost $\int_0^1h(C_\al^{-1}-C_\be^{-1})$.
  • $W_1$ on the line → area between the CDFs, often easier than the quantiles.
  • To check a 1D map → nondecreasing, and $T'(x)\,\rho_\be(T(x))=\rho_\al(x)$.

Give the optimal map from $\mathcal U[0,1]$ to $\frac12\delta_0+\frac12\,\mathcal U[0,1]$ and its $W_1$ cost.

Answer

$C_\al(x)=x$ and $C_\be^{-1}(r)=0$ for $r\le\frac12$, $2r-1$ for $r>\frac12$ (worked example above). So $T(x)=0$ on $[0,\frac12]$ — the left half is crushed onto the atom — and $T(x)=2x-1$ on $(\frac12,1]$ — the right half is stretched over $[0,1]$. $W_1=\int_0^{1/2}x\,\dd x+\int_{1/2}^1\abs{x-(2x-1)}\,\dd x=\frac18+\int_{1/2}^1(1-x)\,\dd x=\frac18+\frac18=\frac14$.
