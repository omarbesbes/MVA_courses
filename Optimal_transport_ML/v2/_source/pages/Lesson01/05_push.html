5. Push-forward: how a map moves a measure OT4ML §2.2, pp. 23–25

A map $T$ moves each bit of mass from $x$ to $T(x)$; the resulting measure $\pf{T}{\al}$ gives to a set $B$ the mass of the points that land in $B$ — it is the law of $T(X)$ when $X\sim\al$ — and for densities a Jacobian determinant accounts for the stretching of space.

Moving points: the discrete case

Monge's unknown is a map $T$: “the mass at $x$ goes to $T(x)$”. We need to say which measure we obtain after moving all the mass of $\al$ this way. For points, it is obvious: move the points, keep their weights.

In the course OT4ML §2.2, p. 23

For a measurable map $T:\X\to\Y$, the push-forward operator $T_\#:\mathcal M(\X)\to\mathcal M(\Y)$ records the distribution of the image points. For a Dirac mass, one has $\pf{T}{\delta_x}=\delta_{T(x)}$. The operation $\al\mapsto\pf{T}{\al}$ is linear in the measure, although its definition for a general measure uses inverse images rather than a decomposition into Dirac masses. In this sense, moving from $T$ to $T_\#$ linearizes the action of a map at the price of passing from a possibly finite-dimensional space $\X$ to the infinite-dimensional space $\mathcal M(\X)$, an idea that underlies many convex relaxations. For discrete measures (2.1), the push-forward operation consists simply in moving the positions of all the points in the support of the measure: $\pf{T}{\al}=\sum_ia_i\delta_{T(x_i)}$.

Worked example — when two points land at the same place

$\al=\frac13(\delta_{-1}+\delta_0+\delta_1)$ and $T(x)=x^2$. The points move to $1$, $0$, $1$, so

$$\pf{T}{\al}=\tfrac13\delta_1+\tfrac13\delta_0+\tfrac13\delta_1=\tfrac13\delta_0+\tfrac23\delta_1 .$$

Weights of points sent to the same place add up. The reverse is impossible: a map sends one point to one point, so it can merge atoms but never split one. This simple fact is behind every failure of Monge's problem (§6).

The definition, and why it works for every measure

In the course OT4ML Def. 2.10, p. 23

Definition 2.10 (Push-forward). For a measurable map $T:\X\to\Y$ and $\al\in\mathcal M(\X)$, the push-forward measure $\be=\pf{T}{\al}\in\mathcal M(\Y)$ is defined, for every measurable set $B\subset\Y$, by

$$\be(B)=\al\big(\{x\in\X:\ T(x)\in B\}\big).\qquad(2.2)$$

Equivalently, for every bounded measurable function $h:\Y\to\R$,

$$\int_\Y h(y)\,\dd\be(y)=\int_\X h\big(T(x)\big)\,\dd\al(x).\qquad(2.3)$$

Note that $T_\#$ preserves positivity and total mass, so that if $\al\in\M(\X)$ then $\pf{T}{\al}\in\M(\Y)$.

The preimage $T^{-1}(B)$: all the starting points whose mass lands in $B$. So (2.2) says: the mass that ends up in $B$ is the mass that started in $T^{-1}(B)$. Nothing is created or lost.

The same with averages: averaging $h$ over the arrival positions is averaging $h\circ T$ over the starting positions. Take $h=\ind_B$ to get back (2.2).

The preimages of measurable sets are measurable, so that (2.2) makes sense. Continuous maps, piecewise continuous maps, monotone maps on $\R$: all measurable.

$\be(\Y)=\al(T^{-1}(\Y))=\al(\X)$: take $B=\Y$ in (2.2).
In the course OT4ML Remark 2.13, p. 25

Remark 2.13 (Probabilistic interpretation). Let $X:\Omega\to\X$ be a random variable defined on an abstract probability space $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\P)$. Its law, or probability distribution, is the push-forward of $\P$ by $X$, namely $\al=\pf{X}{\P}$. Applying another push-forward $\be=\pf{T}{\al}$ for $T:\X\to\Y$, following (2.3), is equivalent to defining another random variable $Y=T(X)$, namely $\omega\in\Omega\mapsto T(X(\omega))\in\Y$. Indeed $\be=\pf{T}{\al}=\pf{T}{(\pf{X}{\P})}=\pf{(T\circ X)}{\P}$, so $\be$ is the law of $Y$. Thus, if $x$ is sampled according to the law $\al$, then $y=T(x)$ is sampled according to the law $\be$.

This is the sentence to remember: $X\sim\al\ \Rightarrow\ T(X)\sim\pf{T}{\al}$. Sampling a push-forward is easy — sample $\al$, apply $T$ — even when its density is unknown.

Properties (each one line from (2.2) or (2.3))
  • Diracs: $\pf{T}{\delta_x}=\delta_{T(x)}$, since $\delta_x(T^{-1}(B))=1$ iff $x\in T^{-1}(B)$ iff $T(x)\in B$.
  • Linearity: $\pf{T}{(\lambda\al+\mu\al')}=\lambda\,\pf{T}{\al}+\mu\,\pf{T}{\al'}$, because the right side of (2.2) is linear in $\al$ — even when $T$ is not linear.
  • Composition: $\pf{(S\circ T)}{\al}=\pf{S}{(\pf{T}{\al})}$, since $(S\circ T)^{-1}(B)=T^{-1}(S^{-1}(B))$.
  • Push-forward and pull-back (OT4ML Remark 2.11, p. 23): (2.3) reads $\ps{h\circ T}{\al}=\ps{h}{\pf{T}{\al}}$. Measures are pushed forward by $T$, test functions are pulled back ($h\mapsto h\circ T$): the two operations are adjoint.
Worked examples — two push-forwards of the uniform law
  • An affine map. $\al=\mathcal U[0,1]$, $T(x)=2x+1$. For $y\in[1,3]$: $\pf{T}{\al}([1,y])=\al(\{x:2x+1\le y\})=\al([0,\frac{y-1}2])=\frac{y-1}2$. That is the CDF of $\mathcal U[1,3]$: $\pf{T}{\al}=\mathcal U[1,3]$, with density $\frac12$ — the interval was stretched by $2$, so the density was divided by $2$.
  • Inverse transform sampling. $U\sim\mathcal U[0,1)$ and $T(u)=-\log(1-u)$. For $y\ge0$: $\P(T(U)\le y)=\P(U\le1-e^{-y})=1-e^{-y}$: $T(U)$ follows the exponential law $\mathrm{Exp}(1)$. (Simulated: $10^6$ draws give $\P(T(U)\le1)\approx0.6324$ against $1-e^{-1}\approx0.6321$.) A one-dimensional generative model: uniform noise in, exponential samples out. §9 shows that $T$ here is the quantile function of $\mathrm{Exp}(1)$.

Densities: the Jacobian formula

When $\al$ and $\pf{T}{\al}$ have densities, how are they related? The naive guess “the density travels with the points”, $\rho_\be(T(x))=\rho_\al(x)$, is wrong: the first example above shows that stretching an interval by $2$ divides the density by $2$.

In the course OT4ML Prop. 2.12, p. 24

Proposition 2.12 (Push-forward formula for densities). Let $U,V\subset\R^d$ be open, let $\al$ and $\be$ have Lebesgue densities $\rho_\al$ and $\rho_\be$ on $U$ and $V$, and let $T:U\to V$ be a $C^1$ diffeomorphism such that $\be=\pf{T}{\al}$. Then, for Lebesgue-almost every $x\in U$,

$$\rho_\al(x)=\abs{\det(T'(x))}\,\rho_\be\big(T(x)\big).\qquad(2.4)$$

Equivalently, for Lebesgue-almost every $y\in V$,

$$\rho_\be(y)=\rho_\al(T^{-1}y)\,\frac1{\abs{\det\big(T'(T^{-1}y)\big)}}.$$
$C^1$ diffeomorphism
$T$ is a bijection from $U$ onto $V$, continuously differentiable, with a continuously differentiable inverse. No folding, no tearing.
$T'(x)$
The Jacobian matrix of $T$ at $x$ ($d\times d$); in 1D, the derivative.
$\abs{\det T'(x)}$
The factor by which $T$ multiplies small volumes near $x$. Above $1$: space is expanded, the density decreases; below $1$: space is compressed, the density increases.
(2.4)
Mass balance in a small box: the mass $\rho_\al(x)\,\dd x$ near $x$ equals the mass $\rho_\be(T(x))\times\abs{\det T'(x)}\,\dd x$ in the image box.
“almost every”
Densities are only defined up to sets of zero volume (changing $\rho$ at a few points changes no integral).
Idea of the proof

Write the definition (2.3) with densities, $\int h\,\rho_\be\,\dd y=\int h(T(x))\,\rho_\al(x)\,\dd x$, and change variables $y=T(x)$ in the right-hand side: two integrals of $h$ against two densities, equal for every $h$ — so the densities are equal.

Full proof
  1. For every continuous compactly supported $h$ on $V$: $\int_Vh(y)\rho_\be(y)\,\dd y=\int_Vh\,\dd\be=\int_Uh(T(x))\,\dd\al(x)=\int_Uh(T(x))\,\rho_\al(x)\,\dd x$.Density of $\be$; definition (2.3) of $\be=\pf{T}{\al}$; density of $\al$.
  2. $$\int_Uh(T(x))\,\rho_\al(x)\,\dd x=\int_Vh(y)\,\rho_\al(T^{-1}y)\,\frac{\dd y}{\abs{\det T'(T^{-1}y)}} .$$Change of variables $x=T^{-1}(y)$: the Jacobian of $T^{-1}$ at $y$ is $T'(T^{-1}y)^{-1}$, whose determinant is $1/\det T'(T^{-1}y)$ (refresher).
  3. So $\int_Vh\,\big(\rho_\be-g\big)\,\dd y=0$ for every such $h$, with $g(y)=\rho_\al(T^{-1}y)/\abs{\det T'(T^{-1}y)}$.Steps 1 and 2.
  4. Hence $\rho_\be=g$ almost everywhere, which is the second formula; substituting $y=T(x)$ gives (2.4). $\square$Two locally integrable functions with the same integrals against all continuous compactly supported $h$ agree almost everywhere.
Worked examples — the formula at work
  • $T(x)=2x+1$ on $\mathcal U[0,1]$: $T'=2$, so $\rho_\be(y)=\rho_\al(\frac{y-1}2)/2=\frac12$ on $[1,3]$ — as computed by hand above. The naive $\rho_\al\circ T^{-1}$ would be $1$ on $[1,3]$, of total mass $2$: not even a probability density.
  • $T(x)=x^2$ on $\mathcal U(0,1)$: $T'(x)=2x$, $T^{-1}(y)=\sqrt y$, so $\rho_\be(y)=\frac1{2\sqrt y}$ on $(0,1)$, which integrates to $[\sqrt y]_0^1=1$. It is infinite at $0$, where $T'(0)=0$ crushes a neighbourhood of $0$ into a tiny interval.
  • In $\R^2$, $T(x)=Ax$ with $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ ($\det A=2$) pushes the uniform law on the unit square to the uniform law on the parallelogram $A([0,1]^2)$, of area $2$, with density $\frac12$.
Pitfall — maps that are not one-to-one

Proposition 2.12 assumes a bijection. If several points have the same image, their contributions add up: $\rho_\be(y)=\sum_{x:\,T(x)=y}\rho_\al(x)/\abs{\det T'(x)}$. Exercise 12 of the sheet (§11) does it for $T(x)=x^2$ on $\mathcal U[-1,1]$: two preimages $\pm\sqrt y$; using only one of them gives half of the mass.

In machine learning — generative models are push-forwards
  • Push-forward generative models. A GAN generator or a VAE decoder is a neural network $G_\theta:\R^k\to\R^d$ applied to a latent noise $Z\sim\N(0,I_k)$. The model is the measure $\al_\theta=\pf{(G_\theta)}{\N(0,I_k)}$: sampling is one forward pass. Its density is usually unavailable — when $k
  • Normalizing flows make the Jacobian formula the training objective. $T_\theta:\R^d\to\R^d$ is a diffeomorphism built from simple invertible layers, and by Proposition 2.12, with $z=T_\theta^{-1}(x)$, $$\log\rho_\theta(x)=\log\rho_Z(z)-\log\abs{\det T_\theta'(z)} .$$ The parameters are fitted by maximising this log-likelihood on the data. RealNVP and Glow use “coupling layers” whose Jacobian matrices are triangular, so that the determinant is the product of the diagonal entries: $O(d)$ operations instead of $O(d^3)$.
  • The reparameterization trick of variational auto-encoders writes the latent code as $z=\mu_\phi(x)+\sigma_\phi(x)\odot\eps$ with $\eps\sim\N(0,I)$: the encoder's distribution is the push-forward of a fixed noise by a map depending on $\phi$, so that $\nabla_\phi\E[f(z)]=\E\big[\nabla_\phi f(\mu_\phi+\sigma_\phi\odot\eps)\big]$ can be estimated by sampling $\eps$.

Compute $\pf{T}{\al}$ for $\al=\mathcal U[-1,1]$ and $T(x)=\abs{x}$.

Answer

For $y\in[0,1]$: $\pf{T}{\al}([0,y])=\al([-y,y])=\frac{2y}2=y$, the CDF of $\mathcal U[0,1]$. So $\pf{T}{\al}=\mathcal U[0,1]$. With densities: two preimages $\pm y$, each contributing $\frac12/1$, total $1$.

Can a map push $\frac12\delta_0+\frac12\delta_1$ onto $\mathcal U[0,1]$? Onto $\delta_5$? Onto $\frac12\delta_3+\frac12\delta_{-3}$?

Answer

Not onto $\mathcal U[0,1]$: $\pf{T}{\al}=\frac12\delta_{T(0)}+\frac12\delta_{T(1)}$ is always carried by at most two points. Onto $\delta_5$: yes, $T\equiv5$. Onto $\frac12\delta_3+\frac12\delta_{-3}$: yes, $T(0)=3$, $T(1)=-3$ (or the other way round) — two maps, and Monge's problem would choose the cheaper one.
