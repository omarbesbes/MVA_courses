What you will be able to do after this page
  1. Explain what it means to compare two distributions geometrically — by the cost of moving one onto the other — and why pointwise comparisons of densities miss it.
  2. State the optimal assignment problem, explain why trying the $n!$ permutations is hopeless, solve it on the line by sorting, and certify an optimum with dual potentials (the idea behind the Hungarian method).
  3. Work with probability measures (Diracs, densities, mixtures) and with the push-forward $\pf{T}{\al}$, including the Jacobian formula for densities.
  4. State Monge's problem between measures and exhibit its three failures: no admissible map, an infimum that is not attained, several optimal maps.
  5. State Brenier's theorem with all its hypotheses, prove its “easy half”, and explain why gradients of convex functions are the right multi-dimensional version of nondecreasing maps.
  6. Compute the optimal map and the transport cost between Gaussians (affine map, Bures metric) and between measures on the line (quantile functions).
  7. Describe McCann's displacement interpolation and derive the Monge–Ampère equation.
  8. Solve the 13 exercises of the sheet Monge formulation, and recognise these objects in machine learning: assignment losses, histogram equalization, generative models, colour transfer, domain adaptation, the FID score.

The story of the lecture in seven steps

To compare two distributions, measure the cheapest way to move one onto the other; Monge asks for a map that does it, and for the squared distance the best map is the gradient of a convex function.

  1. Why (§1). Machine learning compares distributions all the time: a model with data, a source domain with a target domain, two point clouds. Optimal transport compares them through the geometry of the space where the data live, by lifting a ground cost $c(x,y)$ between points to a cost between distributions.
  2. Points (§2–§3). $n$ sources and $n$ targets of equal mass: choose the cheapest pairing, a permutation. There are $n!$ of them; the Hungarian method finds the best one in $O(n^3)$ operations, with a certificate; on the line, sorting solves it in $O(n\log n)$.
  3. Measures and push-forward (§4–§5). One language for point clouds, densities and mixtures. A map $T$ moves a measure $\al$ to $\pf{T}{\al}$; for densities, the Jacobian determinant appears.
  4. Monge's problem (§6). Minimise $\int c(x,T(x))\,\dd\al(x)$ over maps $T$ with $\pf{T}{\al}=\be$. It can have no admissible map, no minimiser, or many minimisers — maps cannot split mass.
  5. Brenier (§7). For $c(x,y)=\norm{x-y}^2$ and a source with a density, there is a unique optimal map, the gradient of a convex function — the multi-dimensional version of “nondecreasing”.
  6. Closed forms (§8–§9). Between Gaussians the optimal map is affine and the cost involves the Bures metric; on the line it is the quantile map $C_\be^{-1}\circ C_\al$.
  7. Previews (§10). Moving each particle along a straight line gives McCann's interpolation (developed in Lesson 3); the change of variables gives the Monge–Ampère equation.

What you need

This is the first lesson: it reuses no earlier lesson, only undergraduate mathematics. Open only the refreshers you need; each one says exactly what is used on this page. The Reminders page goes deeper.

Refresher — random variables, laws, expectations

A random variable $X$ with values in $\R^d$ has a law (distribution) $\al$: $\al(A)=\P(X\in A)$ is the probability that $X$ falls in the set $A$. We write $X\sim\al$. Expectations are integrals against the law: $\E[f(X)]=\int f\,\dd\al$. Two random variables with the same law have the same expectations of all functions, and conversely. Used here in §4 (laws), §5 (“if $X\sim\al$ then $T(X)\sim\pf{T}{\al}$”) and in every cost $\E\norm{X-T(X)}^2$. More: Reminders — measures.

Refresher — convex functions, gradients, subgradients

A function $\varphi:\R^d\to\R\cup\{+\infty\}$ is convex if $\varphi\big((1-t)x+tx'\big)\le(1-t)\varphi(x)+t\varphi(x')$ for $t\in[0,1]$. If $\varphi$ is differentiable, convexity is equivalent to the tangent inequality

$$\varphi(x')\ \ge\ \varphi(x)+\ps{\nabla\varphi(x)}{x'-x}\qquad\text{for all }x,x' :$$

the graph lies above each tangent plane. A vector $v$ with $\varphi(x')\ge\varphi(x)+\ps{v}{x'-x}$ for all $x'$ is a subgradient at $x$; the set of them is $\partial\varphi(x)$ (it is $\{\nabla\varphi(x)\}$ where $\varphi$ is differentiable). On $\R$, a differentiable $\varphi$ is convex iff $\varphi'$ is nondecreasing; for a $C^2$ function on $\R^d$, iff the Hessian $\nabla^2\varphi(x)$ is positive semi-definite everywhere. Used here in §7 (Brenier), §8 (quadratic potentials) and §9 (nondecreasing maps are derivatives of convex functions). More: Reminders — convex functions.

Refresher — change of variables and the Jacobian determinant

If $T:U\to V$ is a $C^1$ bijection between open sets of $\R^d$ with a $C^1$ inverse (a diffeomorphism), and $T'(x)$ is its Jacobian matrix (row $k$ = gradient of the $k$-th coordinate of $T$), then for every integrable $g$

$$\int_V g(y)\,\dd y=\int_U g\big(T(x)\big)\,\abs{\det T'(x)}\,\dd x .$$

Meaning: a tiny box of volume $\dd x$ around $x$ is sent to a tiny parallelepiped of volume $\abs{\det T'(x)}\,\dd x$ around $T(x)$. In 1D with $T$ increasing: $\int g(y)\,\dd y=\int g(T(x))\,T'(x)\,\dd x$, the usual substitution $y=T(x)$. Used here in §5 (density of a push-forward) and §10 (Monge–Ampère). More: Reminders — Jacobian.

Refresher — symmetric positive matrices, square roots, trace

A symmetric matrix $S$ is diagonalisable in an orthonormal basis: $S=UDU\T$ with $U$ orthogonal and $D$ diagonal (spectral theorem). It is positive semi-definite ($S\succeq0$) if $\ps{Sx}{x}\ge0$ for all $x$, i.e. if its eigenvalues are $\ge0$; positive definite ($S\succ0$) if they are $>0$. Its square root $S^{1/2}=UD^{1/2}U\T$ is the unique symmetric positive semi-definite matrix whose square is $S$. Two trace facts: $\tr(AB)=\tr(BA)$, and similar matrices ($B=PAP^{-1}$) have the same trace and eigenvalues. Used here in §8 (Gaussian map and Bures metric). More: symmetric matrices, square roots.

Refresher — Gaussian vectors

$X\sim\N(m,\Sigma)$ with mean $m\in\R^d$ and covariance $\Sigma\succeq0$. If $\Sigma\succ0$ it has the density $\rho(x)=\det(2\pi\Sigma)^{-1/2}\exp\big(-\frac12\ps{x-m}{\Sigma^{-1}(x-m)}\big)$. Two facts are used: an affine image of a Gaussian vector is Gaussian, $AX+b\sim\N(Am+b,\,A\Sigma A\T)$; and a Gaussian law is determined by its mean and covariance. The level set at one standard deviation, $\{x:\ps{x-m}{\Sigma^{-1}(x-m)}=1\}$, is an ellipse with axes along the eigenvectors of $\Sigma$ and half-lengths the square roots of its eigenvalues. Used here in §8. More: Reminders — Gaussians.

Refresher — permutations

A permutation of $[\![n]\!]=\{1,\dots,n\}$ is a bijection $\sigma:[\![n]\!]\to[\![n]\!]$; there are $n!$ of them. We write it as the list $(\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(n))$. A transposition (swap) exchanges two values; a cycle $(i_1\to i_2\to\dots\to i_k\to i_1)$ moves values around a loop; every permutation is a product of disjoint cycles. If $\sigma$ is a permutation, $\sum_i v_{\sigma(i)}=\sum_j v_j$ for any vector $v$ (each index appears exactly once). Used here in §2, §3 and §7. More: Reminders — permutations.

$\mathrm{Perm}(n)$; $\sigma$“permutations”Bijections of $\{1,\dots,n\}$; source $i$ goes to target $\sigma(i)$ (§2).
$C_{i,j}$“cost matrix”$c(x_i,y_j)$, the cost of pairing source $i$ with target $j$.
$\delta_x$; $\sum_ia_i\delta_{x_i}$“Dirac at $x$”; “discrete measure”All the mass at $x$; mass $a_i$ at $x_i$ (§4).
$\M(\X)$“probability measures on $\X$”Positive measures of total mass $1$ (the book's $\mathcal M_+^1(\X)$; StatOT writes $\mathcal P(\R^d)$).
$\rho_\al$“density of $\al$”$\dd\al(x)=\rho_\al(x)\,\dd x$ (§4).
$\pf{T}{\al}$“$T$ push-forward $\al$”The measure obtained by moving each bit of mass of $\al$ from $x$ to $T(x)$; the law of $T(X)$ when $X\sim\al$ (§5). The book writes $T_\sharp\al$.
$\mathcal M_c(\al,\be)$“Monge value”$\inf\{\int c(x,T(x))\,\dd\al(x):\pf{T}{\al}=\be\}$ (§6). Not to be confused with $\M(\X)$, the set of probability measures.
$\widetilde{\mathcal W}_p$“directed Monge distance”$\mathcal M_{d^p}^{1/p}$, the Monge value for the cost $d(x,y)^p$, to the power $1/p$ (§6).
$W_p$“Wasserstein distance”The same with couplings instead of maps (Lessons 2–3); it coincides with $\widetilde{\mathcal W}_p$ in the cases computed here (§8, §9).
$\nabla\varphi$, $\partial\varphi$, $\nabla^2\varphi$“gradient, subdifferential, Hessian”Of a convex potential $\varphi$; Brenier's map is $T=\nabla\varphi$ (§7).
$\N(m,\Sigma)$; $\Sigma^{1/2}$; $\B$“Gaussian”; “square root”; “Bures”Mean $m$, covariance $\Sigma$; the matrix square root; the Bures metric between covariances (§8).
$C_\al$, $C_\al^{-1}$“CDF”, “quantile function”$C_\al(x)=\al((-\infty,x])$ and its generalised inverse (§9). The book writes $\mathcal C_\al$; the sheet $C_\al$ or $F_\mu$; StatOT $F_\mu$ and $F_\mu^\dagger$.
$T_t$, $\al_t$“McCann interpolation”$T_t=(1-t)\Id+tT$, $\al_t=\pf{(T_t)}{\al}$ (§10).

The quadratic cost is $\norm{x-y}^2$ (without a factor $\frac12$) throughout this lesson, as in OT4ML chapter 2; later lessons sometimes use $\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$, which changes the cost by a factor 2 but not the optimal map.
