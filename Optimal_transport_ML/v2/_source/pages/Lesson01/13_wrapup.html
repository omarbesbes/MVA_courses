13. Summary of the lecture

The lecture on one screen
  1. Why (§1): to compare distributions, measure the cheapest way to move one onto the other for a ground cost $c(x,y)$. It sees how far mass moves — unlike KL, total variation or $L^2$ — and $\mathrm{cost}(\delta_x,\delta_y)=c(x,y)$.
  2. Assignment (§2): $\min_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Perm}(n)}\frac1n\sum_iC_{i,\sigma(i)}$. $n!$ candidates; swaps give a necessary test (for $\norm{x-y}^2$: $\ps{x_i-x_j}{y_{\sigma(i)}-y_{\sigma(j)}}\ge0$); for $\norm{x-y}$ in the plane optimal segments do not cross; prices $f_i+g_j\le C_{i,j}$ tight on $\sigma$ certify optimality; the Hungarian method finds them in $O(n^3)$.
  3. The line (§3): for $h(x-y)$ with $h$ convex, sort both clouds and match equal ranks ($O(n\log n)$); unique for strictly convex $h$, ties for $\abs{x-y}$ (book-shifting), false for concave costs. Histogram equalization is 1D transport (ties split atoms).
  4. Measures (§4): $\al(A)$ and $\int f\,\dd\al$ for Diracs, histograms, densities and mixtures; atomless is weaker than “has a density”.
  5. Push-forward (§5): $\pf{T}{\al}(B)=\al(T^{-1}(B))$, $\int h\,\dd(\pf{T}{\al})=\int h\circ T\,\dd\al$; $X\sim\al\Rightarrow T(X)\sim\pf{T}{\al}$; densities: $\rho_\al(x)=\abs{\det T'(x)}\,\rho_\be(T(x))$.
  6. Monge's problem (§6): $\mathcal M_c(\al,\be)=\inf\{\int c(x,T(x))\,\dd\al:\pf{T}{\al}=\be\}$. Same as the assignment for uniform clouds; no map from an atom to two atoms or from discrete to continuous (asymmetric); maps always exist from atomless sources; the segment example has no optimal map ($1+\frac1{12n^2}\to1$); $\abs{x-y}$ has many optimal maps. $\widetilde{\mathcal W}_p$ is a directed extended distance.
  7. Brenier (§7): $c=\norm{x-y}^2$, finite second moments, $\al$ with a density $\Rightarrow$ unique optimal map $T=\nabla\varphi$, $\varphi$ convex; no splitting (Monge = Kantorovich). Easy half by Fenchel–Young; convex gradients are monotone, not conversely (rotations). Smoothness needs convex supports (Caffarelli).
  8. Gaussians (§8): $T(x)=m_\be+A(x-m_\al)$, $A=\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}(\Sigma_\al^{1/2}\Sigma_\be\Sigma_\al^{1/2})^{1/2}\Sigma_\al^{-1/2}$; $W_2^2=\norm{m_\al-m_\be}^2+\B(\Sigma_\al,\Sigma_\be)^2$; in 1D $(m_\al-m_\be)^2+(\sigma_\al-\sigma_\be)^2$. The FID is this formula on neural features.
  9. The line, in general (§9): $T=C_\be^{-1}\circ C_\al$ for atomless $\al$ and convex $h$; $W_p^p=\int_0^1\abs{C_\al^{-1}-C_\be^{-1}}^p$; $W_1=\int\abs{C_\al-C_\be}$; quantiles make 1D transport flat.
  10. Previews (§10): McCann's interpolation $\al_t=\pf{\big((1-t)\Id+tT\big)}{\al}$, a constant-speed geodesic along which particles never collide; Monge–Ampère $\det\nabla^2\varphi\,\rho_\be(\nabla\varphi)=\rho_\al$, a Poisson equation to first order.

The reflexes to acquire

  • Any new claim → test it on Diracs and on two-point examples first.
  • Uniform point clouds of the same size → Monge = assignment; to certify an assignment → exhibit prices tight on it.
  • Points on a line, convex cost → sort; concave cost → do not sort.
  • A push-forward → think “move the points, keep the weights”; for densities, do not forget the Jacobian (and all the preimages).
  • Monge's problem → first ask whether an admissible map exists (atoms!), then whether the infimum is attained.
  • Quadratic cost, source with a density → look for a convex potential; a candidate map is optimal as soon as it is a convex gradient that does the transport.
  • Linear map candidate → symmetric positive semi-definite matrix, or not optimal.
  • Gaussians → split means and covariances; check $A\Sigma_\al A=\Sigma_\be$ and the signs of the eigenvalues of $A$.
  • Measures on the line → CDFs and quantile functions; $W_1$ = area between the CDFs.
  • Interpolating distributions → move mass along the optimal map, do not mix.

What comes next

  • Lesson 2 (discrete Kantorovich). Let mass split: couplings instead of maps. This repairs the three failures of §6 (the relaxed problem always has a solution, is symmetric, and is a linear program). The Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem explains why, for uniform clouds of the same size, an optimal coupling can be taken to be a permutation — so nothing was lost in §2.
  • Lesson 3 (Wasserstein distances). $W_p$ is a distance; McCann's interpolation is a geodesic; barycenters of Gaussians and of measures on the line (geodesics, closed forms).
  • Lesson 4 (duality). Dual potentials, $c$-transforms and the proof of Brenier's theorem; semi-discrete transport and Laguerre cells (the cells of Example 2.19); $W_1$ and Wasserstein GANs.
  • Lesson 5 (sliced transport). Average the 1D transport of §9 over random directions: sorting in high dimension.
  • Lesson 6 (dynamic transport, flow matching). McCann's interpolation as the motion of particles with minimal kinetic energy; generative models built on it.
  • Lessons 7–8. Entropic regularization and Sinkhorn (Schrödinger's problem of §1); gradient flows in the Wasserstein space (Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto).

Notes on the sources (things that could confuse you), each with a check

The exercise sheet (details in §11):

  • Ex. 11(1) says “$\al$ has a density (equivalently, $C_\al$ is continuous)”. Not equivalent: the Cantor distribution has a continuous CDF and no density. The result only needs $\al$ atomless (§11).
  • Ex. 9(a) needs $u\in L^1(\al)$, $v\in L^1(\be)$: with $\al=\be$ the Cauchy law, $c=\abs{x-y}$, $u=\abs x$, every map is a minimiser of $\tilde c$ (all costs are $+\infty$) but only the identity minimises $c$ (§11).
  • Ex. 13, solution: an axial symmetry is a reflection, not a rotation (harmless, radial laws are invariant under all orthogonal maps); an almost-everywhere identity is evaluated at a fixed point chosen after the null set; and (ii) as an equality fails when $\be$ charges a sphere — disk to circle: $t\equiv1$, $F_\al(r)=r^2\ne1=F_\be(t(r))$, while $t=F_\be^{-1}\circ F_\al$ is right (§11).
  • Typos: “Higer dimension” (Ex. 6), the title of Ex. 7, “insantiate” (Ex. 13), and the garbled “its density $f$ has a radial density satisfies” in the statement of Ex. 13.

Peyré–Cuturi, Computational Optimal Transport:

  • Thm. 2.1, p. 31 takes $c=\norm{x-y}^2$ but relates the convex potential to the dual potential by $\varphi=\frac{\norm x^2}2-f$; for this cost it should be $\varphi=\frac{\norm x^2-f}2$ (the printed relation is the one for $\frac12\norm{x-y}^2$). Check: $f(x)=1-2x$, $g(y)=2y-2$ are feasible for $\abs{x-y}^2$ on $\R$ (the slack is $(x-y+1)^2\ge0$) and optimal between $\delta_0$ and $\delta_1$; the printed $\varphi,\psi$ give $\varphi(0)+\psi(1)=-\frac12<\ps01=0$, violating the constraint $\varphi(x)+\psi(y)\ge\ps xy$, while $\frac{x^2-f}2$, $\frac{y^2-g}2$ give exactly $0$. The same theorem says $\varphi$ is “unique (up to an additive constant)”: only $\nabla\varphi$ is unique $\al$-a.e. (extra Exercise 10: potentials with $c\in[-6.5,-2.5]$).
  • Remark 2.24, p. 33 (and the 2019 notes, p. 7): the density condition is weakened to not charging sets “having Hausdorff dimension smaller than $d-1$ (e.g. hypersurfaces)”; hypersurfaces have dimension exactly $d-1$, and the right condition is not charging $(d-1)$-rectifiable sets (OT4ML Def. 2.25). Check: the segment of §6, of dimension $1=d-1$ in $\R^2$, is a source for which no optimal map exists.
  • Remark 2.25, p. 33 linearises Monge–Ampère with “$\varphi=\Id+\eps\psi$”; $\varphi$ is a scalar potential, so it should be $\varphi=\frac12\norm x^2+\eps\psi$, i.e. $\nabla\varphi=\Id+\eps\nabla\psi$ (as in the 2019 notes, p. 7, and OT4ML Prop. 2.34).
  • Eq. (2.39), p. 36 gives $T=C_\be^{-1}\circ C_\al$ as the optimal map without assuming that $\al$ has no atoms. Check: $\al=\delta_0$, $\be=\mathcal U[0,1]$ gives $\pf{T}{\al}=\delta_1\ne\be$ (§9).
  • After (2.36), p. 36: “isometric to a linear space […] or, equivalently, […] a Hilbertian metric”. The image of $\al\mapsto C_\al^{-1}$ is a convex cone, not a linear space ($-C_\al^{-1}$ is decreasing, hence not a quantile function), and the metric is Hilbertian only for $p=2$ (OT4ML Remark 2.46 is precise). The 2019 notes (p. 8) also say “linear space”, but restrict “Hilbertian” to $p=2$.
  • Remark 2.28, p. 34: after $W_p(\al,\be)^p=\frac1n\sum_i\abs{x_i-y_i}^p$ (2.33), “$W_p(\al,\be)$ is the $\ell^p$ norm between two vectors of ordered values”. Up to the factor $n^{-1/p}$: $W_p=n^{-1/p}\norm{x_{\mathrm{sorted}}-y_{\mathrm{sorted}}}_p$. Check: $\{0,1\}$ to $\{1,2\}$ gives $W_1=1$, while the $\ell^1$ norm of $(1,1)$ is $2$.
  • Remark 2.31, p. 37 writes the Gaussian density as $\det(2\pi\Sigma)^{-1/2}\exp(-\ps{x-m}{\Sigma^{-1}(x-m)})$, without the factor $\frac12$ in the exponent (in 1D it would integrate to $1/\sqrt2$), and “$\pf{T}{\rho_\al}=\rho_\be$” for $\pf{T}{\al}=\be$. The computation is unaffected (the factor is missing on both sides).
  • Remark 2.4, eq. (2.5), p. 14 writes the discrete Monge objective as $\sum_ic(x_i,T(x_i))$; with weights it must be $\sum_ia_ic(x_i,T(x_i))=\int c(x,T(x))\,\dd\al$. Check: sources $1,0,2$ with masses $\frac12,\frac14,\frac14$, targets $1,6$ with masses $\frac12,\frac12$, cost $\abs{x-y}$: the two admissible maps cost $2.5$ and $3$ with weights, but $10$ and $7$ without — the unweighted objective picks the wrong map.

  • p. 8: “This closed form formula is also optimal for any cost of the form $h(\abs{x-y})$ for increasing $h$.” False for concave $h$: for $\abs{x-y}^{1/2}$ between $\mathcal U_{[0,2]}$ and $\mathcal U_{[1,3]}$, the monotone map costs $1$ and another map $0.699$ (extra Exercise 9).
