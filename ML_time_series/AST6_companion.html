Multivariate time series

Every earlier lecture worked on one channel at a time. This one asks what changes when \(D\) channels are recorded together, and answers with tools the course already has: least squares becomes vector autoregression, sparse coding becomes joint sparse coding, and filtering moves from the time axis to a graph that links the channels.

A question that comes back

Joint or separate? Slide 10 asks "do we really need a multivariate model?", and the conclusion leaves it open: "a key question remains as to whether or not these dimensions should be modeled together" (slide 91). A joint model can see what no channel shows alone, but it pays for it: pairwise interactions cost \(O(D^2)\) parameters against \(O(D)\) (slide 8), and it "needs a huge amount of data to learn and understand the dependencies" (slide 91). It is Lecture 3's "how many parameters?" asked across channels. Each experiment below ends with its own answer, and they differ. A change of correlation is invisible to any separate model. Two of the six ECG limb leads carry everything, because the other four are computed from them. On the foot sensor, a VAR model wins in sample at equal parameter count, and three separate AR models win on held-out walking. A graph repairs a broken electrode when it is told which one is broken, and then a plain regression does as well.

§1

Problem statement

AST6 · slides 3–8

Multivariate time series are everywhere (slides 4–5):

  • correlated quantities recorded simultaneously: hospitalizations, patients in intensive care and deaths during the COVID-19 pandemic;
  • 2D and 3D trajectories: motion capture, mouse tracking, eye tracking;
  • sensor networks: environmental monitoring, wireless networks, social networks.

Two solutions (slide 6): process each dimension independently, with the tools of the previous lectures, or use dedicated models that take the links between dimensions into account.

Notations (slide 7). A time series of length \(N\) with \(D\) dimensions is stored in a matrix

\[ \bX = \begin{pmatrix} x_1[1] & \cdots & x_D[1] \\ \vdots & & \vdots \\ x_1[N] & \cdots & x_D[N] \end{pmatrix} \in \R^{N \times D}. \]

A row is a multivariate sample \(\bx[n] \in \R^D\), all channels at one instant. A column \(\bx_d \in \R^N\) is one channel over time. Every method of this lecture is a choice of which of the two it treats as the unit: PCA mixes columns, a VAR model predicts rows from rows, a graph lives on the \(D\) entries of each row.

Impact of the multivariate setting (slide 8)

  • The information can be contained in the relations between channels, and not in the channels themselves: the phenomenon may be invisible on each channel taken separately, and clear in their joint behaviour. Examples: two sensors whose correlation drops, two brain regions that desynchronize.
  • The number of parameters becomes much larger: a model with pairwise interactions has \(O(D^2)\) parameters, against \(O(D)\) for \(D\) independent univariate models.
  • The dimensions may have a geometry or a structure: sensors on a scalp, stations in a network, regions in an atlas. The channels are not exchangeable, and their arrangement carries information.

Three possible answers: bypass the problem, extend the models, or change the object. They organize the rest of the lecture.

Watch out

Three D's. The slides use \(D\) for the number of dimensions (slide 7), \(\bD\) for the dictionary (slide 37) and \(\bD\) again for the degree matrix of a graph (slide 50). On this page, \(D\) is always the number of channels; the dictionary and the degree matrix are written in bold, and the context says which is meant.

Key idea

A univariate method sees the marginal distribution of one channel. A change in the relation between channels leaves every marginal unchanged, so no amount of univariate processing can find it: it takes a statistic of several channels at once, here a covariance matrix. This is the one case where a joint model is not optional. For everything else, the question is whether the relations are strong enough to be worth their parameters.

First considerations

AST6 · slides 9–15

2.1 · Do we really need a multivariate model?

slide 10

Before choosing a model, check whether the dimensions interact at all.

  • Correlation matrix: the instantaneous linear dependence between channels, \(\corr(x_i[n], x_j[n])\). A matrix close to \(\bI_D\) means a joint treatment will be neither useful nor relevant.
  • Cross-covariance: the lagged dependence,
    \[ \gamma_{i,j}[m] = \Cov\big(x_i[n],\, x_j[n+m]\big). \]
    The position of the peak indicates which channel leads, which is useful if the sensors are desynchronized.

If the relationships between dimensions are not strong, it is better to process each dimension separately, then aggregate.

Beyond the slides

Reading a cross-correlation. With the slide's definition, a peak at \(m^\star \gt 0\) means that \(x_j\) repeats \(x_i\) with a delay of \(m^\star\) samples: \(x_i\) leads. The experiments normalize it, \(\rho_{i,j}[m] = \gamma_{i,j}[m] / (\sigma_i \sigma_j)\), so that it lies in \([-1, 1]\). Three cautions:

  • The band \(\pm 2/\sqrt N\) assumes white series. For two independent series, the variance of \(\hat\rho_{i,j}[m]\) is about \(\frac1N \sum_k \rho_{i,i}[k]\,\rho_{j,j}[k]\) (Bartlett's formula), which is \(1/N\) only if one of them is white. Strongly autocorrelated or non-stationary series produce large, meaningless cross-correlations: Yule's "nonsense correlations" (1926). The cure is prewhitening: fit an AR model to each channel and cross-correlate the residuals, the innovations of Lecture 3.
  • A periodic signal gives a lag modulo its period. If both channels repeat every \(T\) samples, the peaks recur every \(T\), and "who leads" only makes sense within one period.
  • Correlation is linear. A correlation close to 0 does not prove independence: \(x_j = x_i^2\) with \(x_i\) symmetric has zero correlation and total dependence. Mutual information (Lecture 2) catches it.

2.2 · How to bypass the problem: PCA

slides 11–15

A common trick in the literature (slide 11):

  • use PCA to compute the principal components and select those that contribute the most to the global variance;
  • since the PCs are uncorrelated, they can be processed individually with univariate models and techniques;
  • then the results on all PCs are merged to perform the desired task.

With the centred data \(\bX_c\) and its covariance \(\bSigma = \frac1N \bX_c^\top \bX_c = \bV \bLambda \bV^\top\), the components are the columns of \(\bX_c \bV\), with variances \(\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_D\) and explained variance \(\lambda_k / \sum_j \lambda_j\). This is the PCA of Lecture 2 applied to channels instead of features.

The example of slides 12–15: five ECG channels over 13 seconds. Only the first two principal components contribute to the variance; components 3 to 5 are of order \(10^{-3}\). Each of the two is modelled by a univariate AR(50) model, and the two prediction errors are summed to build an anomaly detector: the detector of Lecture 5 §3.3, run on a two-dimensional summary of the recording.

The slides do not name the recording. The experiment below uses a public 12-lead ECG with one premature ventricular beat, so that the detector has something to find.

Beyond the slides

Why exactly two components. A standard 12-lead ECG records the potentials of a few electrodes and computes the six limb leads from two of them: \(\mathrm{III} = \mathrm{II} - \mathrm{I}\) (Einthoven), and \(\mathrm{aVR} = -(\mathrm I + \mathrm{II})/2\), \(\mathrm{aVL} = \mathrm I - \mathrm{II}/2\), \(\mathrm{aVF} = \mathrm{II} - \mathrm I/2\) (Goldberger). Six limb leads therefore have rank 2 by construction, and the remaining principal components are the rounding of the stored values: in the record below, 1 µV, which is the \(10^{-3}\) (in mV) of slide 13. With the six chest leads V1–V6, which are measured, the rank rises to 8. PCA found the structure of the recording system, not of the heart. The heart shows up one level down: it behaves roughly as a single electric dipole rotating in 3-D, so three components carry most of the 12-lead variance.

Key idea

The bypass turns a multivariate problem into a few univariate ones, and any univariate tool can then be used. It works when a few components carry the variance and the task lives in them. Two things are lost on the way. PCA decorrelates the channels at lag 0 only, so the components can still be related with a delay, and processing them one by one ignores it. And the components are ranked by variance, not by relevance: a small-variance direction may be exactly where the anomaly is.

§3

Models for multivariate time series

AST6 · slides 16–46

Almost all models and approaches seen in the course have been extended to multivariate time series (slide 17). The extension is sometimes straightforward, sometimes it requires more complex data structures, and some notions such as sparsity have to be redefined to take an adequate notion of structure into account. Two examples: autoregressive models become vector autoregressive models, and dictionary learning becomes multivariate dictionary learning.

3.1 · Vector autoregressive models

slides 18–30 · Zivot 2006, Lütkepohl 2013

The VAR(\(p\)) model (slide 18):

\[ \bx[n] = \bc + \sum_{i=1}^{p} \bA_i\, \bx[n-i] + \bb[n], \]
  • \(p\): the order of the model; \(\bc\): an intercept of size \(D\);
  • \(\bA_1, \dots, \bA_p\): the VAR matrices, each of size \(D \times D\);
  • \(\bb[n]\): white noise, often called the innovation.

Careful: the sign convention is the opposite of the univariate AR(\(p\)) of Lecture 3, which wrote \(x[n] = -\sum_i a_i x[n-i] + b[n]\). Here the coefficients enter with a plus sign, as in statsmodels.

Noise structure (slide 19):

  • \(\Esp[b_d[n]] = 0\) for every \(d\): zero mean;
  • \(\Esp[\bb[n]\bb[n]^\top] = \bSigma_b\): a \(D \times D\) covariance matrix;
  • \(\Esp[\bb[n]\bb[n-k]^\top] = \bzero\) for \(k \neq 0\): uncorrelated samples at different times.

So the innovations may be correlated across channels at the same instant, through the off-diagonal entries of \(\bSigma_b\), but not across time. Two channels can be linked in two ways: with a delay, through the \(\bA_i\), or instantaneously, through \(\bSigma_b\).

Example: 2-D VAR(1) (slide 20). For \(D = 2\) and \(p = 1\),

\[ \begin{cases} x_1[n] = c_1 + a_{1,1}\, x_1[n-1] + a_{1,2}\, x_2[n-1] + b_1[n] \\ x_2[n] = c_2 + a_{2,1}\, x_1[n-1] + a_{2,2}\, x_2[n-1] + b_2[n]. \end{cases} \]

Each channel depends on its own past and on the past of the other; this takes the possible correlations between dimensions into account. Compared with \(D\) univariate AR models, it costs \(D^2 p\) parameters instead of \(Dp\) (plus \(D\) for the intercept).

Beyond the slides

When is a VAR model stationary? Iterating \(\bx[n] = \bA\bx[n-1] + \bb[n]\) gives \(\bx[n] = \sum_{k \ge 0} \bA^k \bb[n-k]\), which converges when every eigenvalue of \(\bA\) has modulus below 1. For a VAR(\(p\)), stack \(p\) consecutive samples into one vector of size \(pD\): the model becomes a VAR(1) with the companion matrix \(\begin{pmatrix} \bA_1 & \cdots & \bA_{p-1} & \bA_p \\ \bI & & & \bzero \\ & \ddots & & \vdots \\ & & \bI & \bzero \end{pmatrix}\), and the condition applies to its \(pD\) eigenvalues. For \(D = 1\), these are the poles of the AR model, and the condition is Lecture 3's "poles inside the unit circle". Complex eigenvalues make the pair oscillate, with a period set by their angle.

Matrix form (slides 21–22)

Writing the model for \(n = p+1, \dots, N\) and stacking the rows gives \(\bY = \bZ\underline{\bA} + \bB\), with

\[ \bY = \begin{pmatrix} \bx[p+1]^\top \\ \vdots \\ \bx[N]^\top \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \underline{\bA} = \begin{pmatrix} \bc^\top \\ \bA_1^\top \\ \vdots \\ \bA_p^\top \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \bB = \begin{pmatrix} \bb[p+1]^\top \\ \vdots \\ \bb[N]^\top \end{pmatrix}, \]
\[ \bZ = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \bx[p]^\top & \cdots & \bx[1]^\top \\ 1 & \bx[p+1]^\top & \cdots & \bx[2]^\top \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 1 & \bx[N-1]^\top & \cdots & \bx[N-p]^\top \end{pmatrix}, \]

so \(\bY, \bB \in \R^{(N-p) \times D}\), \(\bZ \in \R^{(N-p) \times (pD+1)}\) and \(\underline{\bA} \in \R^{(pD+1) \times D}\). The slides write \(\bx[n]\) as the rows of \(\bY\) and \(\bA_i\) as the blocks of \(\underline{\bA}\); with rows as samples, the blocks are the transposes \(\bA_i^\top\), which is what makes \(\bZ\underline{\bA}\) reproduce \(\sum_i \bA_i\bx[n-i]\) row by row.

Estimation (slide 23)

All the parameters are estimated by minimizing \(\|\bY - \bZ\underline{\bA}\|_F^2\), with the closed-form solution already met in the earlier lectures, and the noise covariance is the empirical covariance of the residuals:

\[ \hat{\underline{\bA}} = (\bZ^\top\bZ)^{-1}\bZ^\top\bY, \qquad \hat\bSigma_b = \frac{1}{N-p}\,(\bY - \bZ\hat{\underline{\bA}})^\top(\bY - \bZ\hat{\underline{\bA}}). \]
Beyond the slides

\(D\) regressions sharing one design. The Frobenius norm is a sum over the columns of \(\bY\), so the problem splits into \(D\) ordinary least-squares problems, one per channel, all with the same matrix \(\bZ\): column \(d\) of \(\hat{\underline{\bA}}\) regresses \(x_d[n]\) on the past of every channel. Because every equation has the same regressors, this equation-by-equation estimate is also the maximum-likelihood estimate for Gaussian innovations, whatever \(\bSigma_b\) is (Zellner's result on "seemingly unrelated regressions"). The page's engine solves the normal equations by a Cholesky factorization, and matches statsmodels' VAR on the coefficients, on \(\hat\bSigma_b\) and on the Granger statistics.

Prediction and order selection (slide 28)

As for univariate AR models, prediction uses the estimates: \(\hat\bx[n] = \hat\bc + \sum_{i=1}^p \hat\bA_i\,\bx[n-i]\). The order \(p\) can be selected with BIC or AIC (Lecture 5), or by plotting the reconstruction error for \(p = 1, \dots, p_{\max}\) with

\[ p_{\max} = \left\lfloor \frac{N-1}{D+1} \right\rfloor \]

and searching for an elbow [Hurvich & Tsai, 1993]. All variants of AR models have multivariate counterparts, VMA, VARMA, VARIMA, straightforward extensions based on the same principle.

Where \(p_{\max}\) comes from

The least-squares problem has \(N - p\) equations per channel and \(pD + 1\) unknowns. It is determined only if \(N - p \ge pD + 1\), that is \(p(D+1) \le N - 1\). For the foot recording, \(N = 500\) and \(D = 3\) give \(p_{\max} = 124\). At that order each channel has 373 coefficients for 376 equations, and the fit is almost exact whatever the data. The elbow must therefore be read long before \(p_{\max}\), or replaced by an error on data that were not used for the fit. Slide 29 does that: it predicts the last 100 samples, from 5 to 6 s, with a VAR(100) model.

Granger causality

slide 30 · Granger 1969

Beyond prediction, the matrices \(\bA_1, \dots, \bA_p\) describe who influences whom. In the 2-D example, \(a_{1,2}\) quantifies the influence of dimension 2 on dimension 1. More generally, dimension \(d'\) does not help predict dimension \(d\), given all the others, if

\[ (\bA_i)_{d,d'} = 0 \quad \text{for every lag } i. \]

This can be tested with a Fisher test on the residuals, and doing it for all pairs produces a directed graph of influences between the dimensions. This notion is Granger causality [Granger, 1969]. Careful: it only reflects predictivity.

The test. Fit the equation of channel \(d\) twice on the same samples: with all the lags (residual sum of squares \(\RSS_u\)), and without the \(p\) lags of \(d'\) (\(\RSS_r\)). Under the hypothesis of no influence,

\[ F = \frac{(\RSS_r - \RSS_u)/p}{\RSS_u/(N - p - 1 - pD)} \sim F\big(p,\ N - p - 1 - pD\big). \]

A small p-value means that the past of \(d'\) improves the prediction of \(d\) more than chance would. statsmodels' test_causality gives the same \(F\) statistic with a system-wide denominator of \(D(N - p - 1 - pD)\) degrees of freedom, hence slightly different p-values.

Watch out

What Granger causality is not. It tests whether one past helps predict another present, given the variables in the model and nothing else. A channel that is not in the model can create a link: a common driver that reaches \(x_1\) before \(x_2\) makes \(x_1\) "predict" \(x_2\). Testing pairs one at a time turns every chain \(x_1 \to x_2 \to x_3\) into an extra \(x_1 \to x_3\). A purely instantaneous relation, in \(\bSigma_b\), is invisible to the test, whatever its strength. And subsampling or aggregating in time can create or hide links. Key idea

A VAR model is \(D\) linear regressions of each channel on the past of all channels. It captures lagged relations that separate AR models cannot see, at the price of \(D^2\) coefficients per lag. The fit on the training data always improves with the order, until \(p_{\max}\) where it is nearly perfect and useless. The order must be chosen by a penalty or by held-out data, and at equal parameter budget the question becomes whether cross-channel lags are worth more than longer own lags. The zero pattern of the fitted matrices is a Granger graph: a statement about prediction within the recorded channels, not about mechanism.

3.2 · Multivariate dictionary learning: convolutional

slides 31–35 · Garcia-Cardona 2018, Moreau 2018

Two strategies for dictionary learning were introduced in the course (slide 31):

  • convolutional dictionary learning (Lecture 1): \(\displaystyle\min_{(\bd_k),(\bz_k)} \Big\|\bx - \sum_{k=1}^K \bz_k * \bd_k\Big\|_2^2 + \lambda \sum_{k=1}^K \|\bz_k\|_1\) with \(\|\bd_k\|_2^2 \le 1\);
  • dictionary learning (Lecture 3): \(\displaystyle\min_{(\bd_k),(\bz_k)} \|\bX - \bD\bZ\|_F^2 + \lambda \sum_{k=1}^K \|\bz_k\|_1\) with \(\|\bd_k\|_2 \le 1\).

How can we extend this to multivariate data?

Convolutional (slides 32–33): the extension is straightforward. The input \(\bX\) is \(N \times D\), each atom \(\bD_k\) becomes an \(L \times D\) matrix, a pattern of length \(L\) across all channels, and the activation \(\bz_k\) stays a single vector of length \(N - L + 1\). We search for multivariate patterns instead of univariate ones:

\[ \min_{(\bD_k),(\bz_k)} \Big\|\bX - \sum_{k=1}^K \bz_k * \bD_k\Big\|_F^2 + \lambda\sum_{k=1}^K \|\bz_k\|_1, \quad \|\bD_k\|_F^2 \le 1, \]

where \(\bz * \bD = [\bz * \bD^{(1)}, \dots, \bz * \bD^{(D)}]\) is taken independently on each dimension. The model extends to a collection of \(M\) signals \(\bX^{(m)}\) with shared atoms and their own activations \(\bz_k^{(m)}\).

Resolution (slide 35): exactly the same solvers as in the univariate case, FISTA or ADMM (Lectures 1 and 3), mostly variants of gradient descent, alternating convolutional sparse coding (CSC, the \(\bz_k\)) and convolutional dictionary learning (CDL, the \(\bD_k\)). The gradients may become prohibitive to compute because of the size of the objects [Garcia-Cardona & Wohlberg, 2018]; recent solutions use parallel computing and a locally greedy coordinate descent that updates the coefficients \(z_k^{(m)}[n]\) on several cores [Moreau et al., 2018].

Beyond the slides

Why one shared activation helps. The gradient of the data term with respect to \(\bz_k\) is \(-\sum_{d=1}^{D} \big(\br^{(d)} \star \bD_k^{(d)}\big)\): the correlation of each channel's residual with the matching channel of the atom, summed over the channels. This is the sliding dot product of Lecture 1, pooled. If a pattern appears in every channel and the noise is independent across channels, the pooled correlation grows like \(D\) while its noise grows like \(\sqrt D\): a weak occurrence that no single channel can separate from the noise can pass the threshold jointly. The price is the assumption itself, that the pattern's channels always occur together with the same shape. A popular constraint makes atoms rank one, \(\bD_k = \bu_k \bv_k^\top\), a spatial pattern \(\bu_k\) times a waveform \(\bv_k\): the model of alphacsc for brain signals (slide 94; La Tour et al. 2018, mini-project 1.14).

3.2 · Multivariate dictionary learning: joint sparsity

slides 36–46 · Tropp 2006, Cotter 2005

For non-convolutional dictionary learning, the multivariate problem can be posed in several ways, depending on the assumptions (slide 36):

  • Solution 1 (easy): one dictionary per dimension. Solve \(D\) independent standard problems, as if each dimension were a different signal. No new method is needed.
  • Solution 2 (more complex): one shared dictionary for all dimensions. The sparsity constraint is reformulated, and the sparse coding step becomes a joint sparse representation.

The shapes (slide 37): \(\bX\) is \(N \times D\); the \(K\) atoms \(\bd_k \in \R^N\) are the columns of \(\bD \in \R^{N \times K}\); the activations form \(\bZ \in \R^{K \times D}\), whose row \(\bz_k\) holds the coefficients of atom \(k\) in every dimension, \(z_{k,d}\):

\[ \min_{\bZ}\ \|\bX - \bD\bZ\|_F^2 + \lambda\, \Omega(\bZ). \]

What is good sparsity for a joint representation? (slide 38)

  • each column \(\bz_{:,d}\) should be sparse: the signal of dimension \(d\) is a combination of a few atoms;
  • but each row \(\bz_k\) should not be sparse: to learn a joint representation, an atom should be used by as many dimensions as possible.

Both are encoded by a mixed norm,

\[ \Omega(\bZ) = \sum_{k=1}^K \Big(\sum_{d=1}^D |z_{k,d}|^q\Big)^{p/q}, \]

with \(p \le 1\) for sparsity across atoms (few atoms per dimension) and \(q \ge 1\) for no sparsity within a row (the atom is shared across dimensions).

Algorithms (slide 39)

  • \(p = q = 1\): the problem separates into independent sparse approximation problems, one per dimension (Lecture 3).
  • \(p = q = 2\): a regularized least-squares problem with closed-form solution,
    \[ \begin{aligned} \bZ &= (\bD^\top\bD + \lambda\bI_K)^{-1}\bD^\top\bX \\ &= \bD^\top(\bD\bD^\top + \lambda\bI_N)^{-1}\bX. \end{aligned} \]
  • \(p = 1\), \(q = 2\): the \(L_{2,1}\) norm of \(\bZ\), solved by iteratively reweighted least squares (M-FOCUSS). See [Tropp et al., 2006] for a review.

The two forms of the ridge solution are equal by the "push-through" identity \((\bD^\top\bD + \lambda\bI)^{-1}\bD^\top = \bD^\top(\bD\bD^\top + \lambda\bI)^{-1}\): the first inverts a \(K \times K\) matrix, the second an \(N \times N\) one.

\(L_{2,1}\) regularization (slides 40–42)

With \(p = 1\), \(q = 2\), the problem is \(\min_\bZ \|\bX - \bD\bZ\|_F^2 + \lambda\sum_k \|\bz_k\|_2\). The idea is a weight matrix that forces rows with small norms to zero, \(\bW = \diag\big(\sqrt{\|\bz_1\|_2}, \dots, \sqrt{\|\bz_K\|_2}\big)\). Since

\[ \sum_{k=1}^K\sum_{d=1}^D \frac{|z_{k,d}|^2}{\|\bz_k\|_2} = \sum_{k=1}^K \|\bz_k\|_2, \]

the penalty equals \(\|\bW^{-1}\bZ\|_F^2\) when \(\bW\) is computed from \(\bZ\) itself. Freezing \(\bW\) at the current estimate gives a weighted least-squares problem, \(\min_\bZ \|\bX - \bD\bZ\|_F^2 + \lambda\|\bW^{-1}\bZ\|_F^2\), solved in closed form. Iterating is the IRLS of [Cotter et al., 2005].

Beyond the slides

Three facts about M-FOCUSS. (1) The update. Substitute \(\bZ = \bW\bQ\): the weighted problem becomes the ridge problem \(\min_\bQ \|\bX - \bD\bW\bQ\|_F^2 + \lambda\|\bQ\|_F^2\), whose solution by the push-through form is \(\bQ = \bW\bD^\top(\bD\bW^2\bD^\top + \lambda\bI_N)^{-1}\bX\), hence the update. (2) Which problem it solves. At a fixed point, \(\bX - \bD\bZ = \lambda\bR\) with \(\bR = (\bD\bW^2\bD^\top + \lambda\bI)^{-1}\bX\), so \(\bD^\top(\bX - \bD\bZ) = \lambda\bW^{-2}\bZ\): row \(k\) reads \(\bd_k^\top(\bX - \bD\bZ) = \lambda\,\bz_k/\|\bz_k\|_2\). This is the optimality condition of \(\|\bX - \bD\bZ\|_F^2 + 2\lambda\sum_k\|\bz_k\|_2\): the factor 2 comes from differentiating the squared error. On a random 10 × 16 dictionary, the page's engine finds the M-FOCUSS fixed point with \(\lambda = 0.3\) within \(1.3 \cdot 10^{-7}\) of the group-lasso solution with penalty 0.6. (3) No exact zeros. Reweighting shrinks a row geometrically but never sets it to 0, so "activated atoms" must be counted with a threshold. With an orthonormal dictionary, everything is explicit: with \(\bC = \bD^\top\bX\), the three problems of slide 39 are solved by entrywise soft thresholding at \(\lambda/2\) (\(L_1\)), a uniform shrinkage by \(1/(1+\lambda)\) (\(L_2\)), and a row soft thresholding, \(\bz_k = \bc_k(1 - \lambda/(2\|\bc_k\|_2))_+\) (\(L_{2,1}\)).

Key idea

Joint sparsity changes what "sparse" means: few atoms, but the same atoms for every channel. It is expressed by a mixed norm, sparse across rows and dense within a row, and it pays off when the channels really share their structure. The algorithms are the ones of Lectures 1 and 3 with a group of coefficients in place of a single one: soft thresholding becomes row thresholding, a Lasso becomes a group Lasso, and M-FOCUSS reaches it by reweighted least squares.

§4

Graph signal processing

AST6 · slides 47–89 · Ortega et al. 2018

In most practical applications, the dimensions of a multivariate signal are linked (slide 48): by correlation between the recorded variables (pressure, temperature and precipitation), or by spatial proximity and interactions (sensor networks, body sensors, social networks). These links can be modelled explicitly by a graph: that is graph signal processing, and each multivariate sample \(\bx[n]\) is assumed to be carried by the graph.

4.1 · Concepts and definitions

slides 49–61

A graph \(\cG\) is a triplet \((\cV, \cE, \cW)\) (slide 49): \(\cV\) is a finite set of \(D\) nodes (usually \(\{1, \dots, D\}\)), \(\cE \subset \cV \times \cV\) a set of edges, and \(\cW: \cE \to \R\) maps each edge to a scalar value. Here the graph is undirected with positive weights, and \(\cW(i, j)\) encodes the strength of the relationship between dimensions \(i\) and \(j\).

The Laplacian (slide 50) is \(\bL = \bD - \bW\), with the weight (or adjacency) matrix \(W_{i,j} = \cW(i, j)\) if \((i, j) \in \cE\) and 0 otherwise, and the diagonal degree matrix \(D_{i,i} = \sum_j W_{i,j}\). It is symmetric and positive semi-definite.

By construction (slide 51):

  • for every \(\bx \in \R^D\), \(\displaystyle \bx^\top\bL\bx = \frac12\sum_{(i,j) \in \cE} W_{i,j}(x_i - x_j)^2 \ge 0\);
  • the constant vector \(\bone_D\) is an eigenvector of \(\bL\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda_1 = 0\), since every row of \(\bL\) sums to zero;
  • the number of connected components of the graph is the number of eigenvalues equal to zero.

The sum in the first formula runs over ordered pairs, each edge counted twice, hence the \(\frac12\).

Beyond the slides

Why zero eigenvalues count components. \(\bL\) is positive semi-definite, so \(\bL\bx = \bzero\) exactly when \(\bx^\top\bL\bx = 0\), that is when \(x_i = x_j\) across every edge with a positive weight: \(\bx\) is constant on each connected component. The null space is spanned by the indicator vectors of the components, one per component, which gives the multiplicity of the eigenvalue 0. The second eigenvalue \(\lambda_2\) measures how far the graph is from splitting into two (Fiedler's algebraic connectivity), and the sign pattern of its eigenvector suggests where to cut. That is the idea behind spectral clustering.

A graph signal (slide 52) associates an element of \(\Omega\) with each of the \(D\) nodes. With \(\Omega = \R\) it is a simple graph signal: each node carries one sample \(x_d[n]\). With \(\Omega = \R^N\) it is a time-vertex signal: each node carries one time series \(\bx_d\). The lecture considers simple graph signals, one sample \(\bx[n]\) at a time. Slides 53–57 build the running example: one node per signal dimension, edges for the links between dimensions, weights for their strengths, and one multivariate sample drawn on the nodes. Graph signals are shown as stems over the nodes or as node colours (slides 58–59). They come from sensor networks, interaction networks, economic dependencies, images and point clouds (slide 60), and most learning tasks extend to them (slide 61): sampling and compression (choose the most relevant nodes), graph inference (learn the graph), denoising and filtering, interpolation of missing data, classification, event and anomaly detection, prediction.

4.2 · Graph Fourier transform

slides 62–65

For a graph with one connected component, diagonalize the Laplacian (slide 62):

\[ \bL = \bU \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & \lambda_D \end{pmatrix} \bU^\top, \qquad 0 = \lambda_1 \lt \lambda_2 \le \dots \le \lambda_D. \]
  • The \(\lambda_i\) are interpreted as spatial frequencies; \(\lambda_1 = 0\) is the DC component.
  • \(\bu_i\) is the eigenvector of frequency \(\lambda_i\); \(\bu_1\) is constant.

The graph Fourier transform of a graph signal \(\bx \in \R^D\) is

\[ \hx = \bU^\top\bx, \qquad \bx = \bU\hx. \]

Low frequencies describe global phenomena, high frequencies local ones (slide 63). The eigenvectors can model symmetries, anti-symmetries and spatial phenomena (slide 64): \(\bu_2\) typically splits the graph into two halves of opposite signs. The graph spectrum of a signal is \(|\hat x_i|^2\) plotted against \(\lambda_i\) (slide 65). \(\bU\) is orthogonal, so the transform preserves the energy: \(\|\hx\|_2 = \|\bx\|_2\).

Beyond the slides

The DFT is the GFT of a ring. On a cycle of \(D\) nodes with unit weights, \((\bL\bx)_i = 2x_i - x_{i-1} - x_{i+1}\) with indices modulo \(D\). \(\bL\) is circulant, so its eigenvectors are the complex exponentials \(e^{2\pi \mathrm{j} k i/D}\) of the DFT (in real form, cosines and sines), with eigenvalues \(\lambda = 2 - 2\cos(2\pi k/D)\), increasing with the frequency \(|k|\). The graph Fourier transform of a ring is the DFT of Lecture 2. "Frequency" on a graph is the same idea, how fast the signal varies between neighbours, on a structure that has no FFT: computing \(\bU\) costs an eigendecomposition, \(O(D^3)\).

4.3 · Bandlimitedness and smoothness

slides 66–72

Bandlimitedness (slide 66). A common assumption in signal processing is the sparsity of the spectrum (Lecture 3): bandlimited signals (baseband, wideband) underlie sampling and denoising. GSP uses the same notion on the graph spectrum:

\[ \bx \text{ is } K\text{-bandlimited} \iff \|\hx\|_0 = K. \]

Slide 67 filters the example by removing all frequencies except the four most dominant ones, and slide 68 shows the resulting 4-bandlimited approximation.

Smoothness (slide 69). Intuitively, values on adjacent nodes should be similar. The smoothness of a graph signal is

\[ S(\bx) = \bx^\top\bL\bx = \frac12\sum_{(i,j)\in\cE} W_{i,j}(x_i - x_j)^2, \]

small when \((x_i - x_j)^2\) is small wherever \(W_{i,j}\) is large. Careful: the name is counterintuitive, since a large "smoothness" is achieved by non-smooth signals and vice versa (slide 70). For the eigenvectors, \(S(\bu_i) = \bu_i^\top\bL\bu_i = \lambda_i\) (slide 71): each eigenvalue is the smoothness of its eigenvector, and the constant \(\bu_1\) is perfectly smooth.

Watch out

Two meanings of "\(K\)-bandlimited". Slide 66 defines it as \(\|\hx\|_0 = K\), any \(K\) frequencies, and slide 67 keeps "the four most dominant": the best \(K\)-term approximation of Lecture 3, in the graph Fourier basis. Most of the GSP literature, and the sampling theorems of project 6.7 (Puy et al., 2018), call a signal \(K\)-bandlimited when \(\hat x_i = 0\) for \(i \gt K\): its spectrum sits on the \(K\) lowest frequencies. The first is a sparsity assumption, the second a smoothness assumption, and they keep different coefficients. The experiment below computes both.

Two identities connect the two notions. Writing \(\bx = \bU\hx\), \(S(\bx) = \sum_i \lambda_i \hat x_i^2\): smoothness is the spectrum weighted by the frequencies, so a smooth signal has its energy at low \(\lambda_i\). And the parallels with the earlier lectures (slide 72): smoothness goes with low-frequency approximation, useful for denoising and interpolation (§4.4); sparsity goes with bandlimitedness, useful for subsampling and reconstruction.

4.4 · Graph filtering

slides 73–83 · Stanković et al. 2019

As in standard signal processing, a filter can be defined in the graph (vertex) domain or in the graph frequency domain (slide 73). For a graph signal \(\bx\), the filtered signal is

\[ \by = \Big(\sum_{i=0}^p h_i \bL^i\Big)\bx \qquad \text{or} \qquad \hy = \Big(\sum_{i=0}^p h_i \bLambda^i\Big)\hx. \]

The two agree because \(\bL^i = \bU\bLambda^i\bU^\top\) (slide 74). Since \(\bLambda\) is diagonal, filtering in the frequency domain multiplies each coefficient, \(\hat y_j = \tilde\lambda_j\hat x_j\) with \(\tilde\lambda_j = \sum_i h_i\lambda_j^i\). Any function \(h\) of the eigenvalues defines a filter, \(\tilde\lambda_j = h(\lambda_j)\).

Low-pass filtering (slide 75). Lecture 4 used low-pass filters for denoising and interpolation. On a graph, search for a smooth approximation of the signal [Stanković et al., 2019]:

\[ \min_\by\ \|\bx - \by\|_2^2 + \alpha\,\by^\top\bL\by \]

has the closed-form solution of a low-pass filter,

\[ \by = (\bI_D + \alpha\bL)^{-1}\bx, \qquad h(\lambda) = \frac{1}{1 + \alpha\lambda}. \]

(Set the gradient \(2(\by - \bx) + 2\alpha\bL\by\) to zero.) The filter is applied to each multivariate sample \(\bx[n]\) independently: it uses the graph to smooth the signal along the dimensions, not along time.

Beyond the slides

Polynomial filters are local. \((\bL^i)_{j,k} = 0\) whenever nodes \(j\) and \(k\) are more than \(i\) edges apart, so a polynomial filter of order \(p\) mixes each node only with its \(p\)-hop neighbourhood. It needs no eigendecomposition, only \(p\) matrix-vector products, which is why large graphs use polynomial (Chebyshev) approximations of \(h\) (Hammond, Vandergheynst & Gribonval, 2011). Graph convolutional networks learn exactly such filters (ChebNet 2016, GCN 2017), the tool of project 6.8. The Tikhonov filter \((\bI + \alpha\bL)^{-1}\) is not polynomial: every node influences every other node of its component.

A probabilistic reading. If the clean signal has the Gaussian density \(\propto \exp(-\alpha\,\by^\top\bL\by)\), a Gaussian Markov random field whose precision matrix is a scaled Laplacian, and the noise is white, then \((\bI + \alpha\bL)^{-1}\bx\) is the posterior mean of \(\by\). The Laplacian is an inverse covariance, and its zeros say which channels are conditionally independent given the others. scikit-learn's GraphicalLasso (slide 94) learns a sparse precision matrix from data, a close cousin of §4.5.

Key idea

A graph filter smooths across channels the way a low-pass filter smooths across time: \(h(\lambda)\) damps the frequencies where neighbouring channels disagree. It works as a prior: the signal is assumed smooth on the graph, and the graph decides what "neighbouring" means. Used blindly, it spreads the damage of a bad channel and blurs the good ones. Used with what is known, the location of the missing values, it becomes interpolation. That is Lecture 4's lesson: repair only what you know is broken, and measure the result against data you did not use.

4.5 · Graph learning

slides 84–89 · Dong et al. 2016, Mateos et al. 2019

Given a multivariate time series \(\bX\), seen as \(N\) graph signals \(\bx[1], \dots, \bx[N]\), how do we learn the graph (slide 84)? An important assumption is graph-stationarity: all the samples are carried by the same graph, and the graph does not change. Two strategies: ad hoc computation, or learning algorithms based on constraints such as smoothness or bandlimitedness [Mateos et al., 2019; Dong et al., 2016].

Ad hoc graphs (slide 85):

  • correlation: \(W_{i,j} = \corr(\bx_i, \bx_j)\) if it exceeds a threshold \(\lambda\), 0 otherwise;
  • distance between sensor coordinates \(\bc_i\), \(\bc_j\): \(W_{i,j} = e^{-\gamma\|\bc_i - \bc_j\|_2^2}\) if it exceeds \(\lambda\), 0 otherwise, so distant sensors get small weights.

Graph inference under a smoothness assumption (slides 86–87). Aim: learn the graph that best explains the structure of the signal, assuming that the graph signals are smooth on it. Input \(\bX \in \R^{N \times D}\), output a Laplacian \(\bL = \bU\bLambda\bU^\top\):

\[ \min_{\bY, \bL}\ \|\bX - \bY\|_F^2 + \alpha\,\tr(\bY^\top\bL\bY) + \beta\,\|\bL\|_F^2 \]
\[ \text{s.t.}\quad \tr(\bL) = D, \qquad L_{i,j} = L_{j,i} \le 0\ \ (i \ne j), \qquad \bL\bone_D = \bzero_D. \]
  • \(\bY\) is a smooth approximation of the signals \(\bX\);
  • the constraints impose a Laplacian structure;
  • the problem is not convex in \((\bY, \bL)\) jointly: minimize alternately in \(\bY\) and \(\bL\). In \(\bL\) it is a quadratic program; in \(\bY\) it has a closed form.
Watch out

Rows or columns. With \(\bX \in \R^{N \times D}\), one sample per row, the smoothness term must be \(\tr(\bY\bL\bY^\top) = \sum_n \by[n]^\top\bL\,\by[n]\): the slide's \(\tr(\bY^\top\bL\bY)\) is written in the convention of Dong et al., where the signals are the columns of a \(D \times N\) matrix. Each constraint has a job. \(\tr(\bL) = D\) rules out the trivial \(\bL = \bzero\), which would make any signal perfectly smooth. The sign and row-sum constraints make \(\bL\) a Laplacian. \(\beta\|\bL\|_F^2\) controls the sparsity: a small \(\beta\) concentrates the weight on a few edges, a large one spreads it over all pairs.

Beyond the slides

What the two steps do. With the edge weights \(w_{i,j} = -L_{i,j}\), \(\tr(\bY\bL\bY^\top) = \sum_{i \lt j} w_{i,j}\|\by_i - \by_j\|_2^2\), where \(\by_i\) is node \(i\)'s column of values across the samples. The \(\bL\)-step therefore puts weight on the pairs of nodes whose signals are close, and the Frobenius term and the trace constraint decide how many. It is a quadratic program over the \(D(D-1)/2\) weights, on a scaled simplex \(\{w \ge 0, \sum w = D/2\}\); the page solves it by projected gradient, and matches scipy's SLSQP to \(3.5 \cdot 10^{-8}\). The \(\bY\)-step is \(\bY = \bX(\bI + \alpha\bL)^{-1}\): the low-pass filter of slide 75. Kalofolias (2016, project 6.9) replaced the trace constraint by a logarithmic barrier on the degrees, which avoids isolated nodes. The page divides the data and smoothness terms by \(N\), so that \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) do not depend on the number of samples.

Where things stand (slide 91)

  • Multivariate models. "A key question remains as to whether or not these dimensions should be modeled together." In several cases, channel-independent models outperform channel-mixing ones, and a joint model needs a huge amount of data to learn and understand the dependencies.
  • Multimodality is the next challenge: time series with different physical properties, and the alignment, synchronization and sampling frequencies of the modalities.
  • Graph signal processing was launched about ten years ago. Since then theories have abounded, but "its practical implementation remains fragile, and there has been no revolution in its applications". Graph learning is a hot topic, in particular for time-varying graphs, and higher-order structures, simplicial complexes and hypergraphs, can encode interactions between more than two vertices.

What the benchmarks conclude (slide 92)

Classification, on the 30 datasets of the multivariate archive [Ruiz et al., 2021]: the best performers are the same families as in the univariate case, adapted to several dimensions; methods treating the dimensions independently remain competitive on many datasets; the gain from modelling the dependencies is real, but smaller than expected.

Anomaly detection, on the univariate and multivariate tracks of the same benchmark [Liu & Paparrizos, 2024]: the ranking changes between the two tracks; statistical methods dominate the univariate track, and neural networks come back on the multivariate one; part of the univariate track is in fact made of multivariate series split dimension by dimension.

In both cases, a multivariate method should be compared to the same method applied dimension by dimension. This page's experiments are small instances of the same finding: the joint model is essential for a change of correlation, helps a little for short-memory prediction of the foot sensor, and loses to separate long-memory models on held-out data.

Key idea

The lecture in one sentence: a multivariate method is a hypothesis about how the channels are related, and it has to earn its parameters. PCA assumes that a few directions carry the information, a VAR model that each channel is predicted by the past of the others, joint sparsity that the channels share their atoms, a graph that neighbouring channels carry similar values. When the hypothesis is right, the joint method sees what no separate method can. When it is not, the separate baseline wins, and only that comparison tells the two cases apart.

import numpy as np

def var_fit(X, p, eval_from=None):
    """VAR(p) with intercept by least squares (slides 21-23). X: N x D.
    Returns B = [c; A_1^T; ...; A_p^T], the residuals and the RMSE of each channel."""
    N, D = X.shape
    Z = np.hstack([np.ones((N - p, 1))] + [X[p - i:N - i] for i in range(1, p + 1)])  # rows n = p..N-1
    B = np.linalg.solve(Z.T @ Z, Z.T @ X[p:])
    R = X[p:] - Z @ B
    e = R[(eval_from or p) - p:]
    return B, R, np.sqrt((e ** 2).mean(axis=0))

The Granger F test (slide 30) 
from scipy.stats import f as fisher

def granger(X, p, tgt, src):
    """F test of slide 30: does the past of channel src help predict channel tgt, given the others?"""
    N, D = X.shape
    Z = np.hstack([np.ones((N - p, 1))] + [X[p - i:N - i] for i in range(1, p + 1)])
    y = X[p:, tgt]
    keep = [0] + [1 + i * D + e for i in range(p) for e in range(D) if e != src]
    rss = lambda Zs: np.sum((y - Zs @ np.linalg.lstsq(Zs, y, rcond=None)[0]) ** 2)
    rss_u, rss_r = rss(Z), rss(Z[:, keep])
    df2 = (N - p) - Z.shape[1]
    F = ((rss_r - rss_u) / p) / (rss_u / df2)
    return F, fisher.sf(F, p, df2)

F, pval = granger(foot, 4, tgt=0, src=1)      # does Y help predict X?

def xcorr(x, y, M):
    """rho[m] = corr(x[n], y[n + m]), m = -M..M, biased estimate"""
    x, y, N = x - x.mean(), y - y.mean(), len(x)
    den = np.sqrt((x ** 2).sum() * (y ** 2).sum())
    return np.array([x[max(0, -m):N - max(0, m)] @ y[max(0, m):N - max(0, -m)] for m in range(-M, M + 1)]) / den

def csc_multi(X, atoms, lam, iters=250, joint=True):
    """Multichannel convolutional sparse coding (slide 33) with known atoms, by nonnegative FISTA.
    X: D x N; atoms: K x D x L. joint: one activation per atom; otherwise one per atom and channel."""
    D, N = X.shape
    K, _, L = atoms.shape
    M, nf = N - L + 1, 1 << int(np.ceil(np.log2(N + L)))
    Af = np.fft.fft(atoms, nf)                                 # K x D x nf
    if joint:
        lip = (np.abs(Af) ** 2).sum(axis=(0, 1)).max()
    else:
        lip = (np.abs(Af) ** 2).sum(axis=0).max()
    shape = (K, 1 if joint else D, M)
    Z, Y, t = np.zeros(shape), np.zeros(shape), 1.0
    def recon(Z):
        Zf = np.fft.fft(Z, nf)                                 # K x (1 or D) x nf
        return np.real(np.fft.ifft((Zf * Af).sum(axis=0), nf))[:, :N]
    for _ in range(iters):
        Rf = np.fft.fft(recon(Y) - X, nf)                      # D x nf

Graph learning under smoothness (slides 86–87) 
def learn_graph(X, alpha=1.0, beta=0.05, outer=10, inner=1500):
    """Smoothness-based graph learning (slides 86-87, Dong et al. 2016), rows of X = graph signals.
    Terms divided by N; L-step by projected gradient on the simplex {w >= 0, sum w = n/2}."""
    N, n = X.shape
    iu = np.triu_indices(n, 1)
    def proj(v, s):                                            # Euclidean projection on the scaled simplex
        u = np.sort(v)[::-1]; css = np.cumsum(u) - s
        r = np.nonzero(u - css / np.arange(1, len(u) + 1) > 0)[0][-1]
        return np.maximum(v - css[r] / (r + 1), 0)
    def deg(w):
        Wm = np.zeros((n, n)); Wm[iu] = w; Wm += Wm.T
        return Wm, Wm.sum(axis=1)
    Y, w = X.copy(), np.full(len(iu[0]), n / 2 / len(iu[0]))
    for _ in range(outer):
        z = (((Y[:, :, None] - Y[:, None, :]) ** 2).sum(axis=0) / N)[iu]   # ||y_i - y_j||^2 / N
        v, t, step = w.copy(), 1.0, 1 / (4 * beta * n)
        for _ in range(inner):                                 # FISTA on alpha z.w + beta ||L(w)||_F^2
            _, d = deg(v)
            gr = alpha * z + beta * (2 * d[iu[0]] + 2 * d[iu[1]] + 4 * v)
            wn = proj(v - step * gr, n / 2)
            tn = (1 + np.sqrt(1 + 4 * t * t)) / 2
            v, w, t = wn + (t - 1) / tn * (wn - w), wn, tn
        Wm, _ = deg(w)
        Y = np.linalg.solve(np.eye(n) + alpha * laplacian(Wm), X.T).T  # Y-step: the low-pass filter
    return Wm

Checked: on 15 samples of the 30 EEG electrodes, the learned weight matrix agrees with the page to \(2 \cdot 10^{-15}\) (35 edges at \(\beta = 0.05\)).

Six lectures, one object: a signal indexed by time, recorded to be understood, compared, predicted or monitored. Each lecture answers one question about it, and each answer rests on a choice made before looking at the data. This section puts the six side by side, with links into every page.

the notes of each page's §0
L1 · §2.0
What counts as the same?

Before comparing two series, decide which differences do not matter: an offset, a scale, a shift, a warping. The invariance chooses the distance, and the distance chooses the answer.

L2 · synthesis
What does a feature keep?

Every feature forgets something, the order of the samples, the phase, the time itself. A feature is useful when it forgets what does not matter for the task. And selecting features with the test labels quietly lets the answer into the question.

L3 · §0
How many parameters?

The order of an AR model, the number of harmonics, of atoms, of non-zero coefficients: the fit always improves with them, so the fit alone can never choose.

L4 · §0
How much should be removed?

Every cleaning method has a knob, and the clean signal that would set it is exactly what is missing. Calibrate on noise, estimate the error, corrupt clean data on purpose, or judge the whole pipeline.

L5 · §0
Who defines the event?

A cost, a model, a penalty, an annotator and a metric each decide part of what a change or an anomaly is. The data never decide alone.

L6 · §0
Joint or separate?

A joint model sees relations no single channel shows, and pays \(O(D^2)\) parameters for them. It has to beat the same method applied channel by channel.

a decision guide
Key idea

The course in one sentence: every method is a decision about what to ignore, and the data you fitted cannot tell you whether it was right. An invariance ignores a deformation, a feature ignores the order of the samples, a model ignores what it cannot express, a filter ignores a band, a penalty ignores small changes, a separate model ignores the other channels. Each lecture found the same way out: decide with data that the method did not use (a validation set, held-out samples, a clean recording corrupted on purpose, annotations), or with a penalty that prices every parameter. And compare with the simplest method that could have worked.

Check yourself

try first, then open
1 · Give an example where the information is in the relation between channels, and a method that detects it.

Two channels whose correlation drops while each keeps its mean, variance and spectrum. Every univariate cost sees nothing (in the lab, cost drops of 5.6 and 8.3 against BIC's 19.2); the Gaussian cost with a covariance matrix finds the change at the right sample (a drop of 320). Any method that looks at one marginal at a time is blind to it.

2 · What does a correlation matrix close to the identity tell you, and what does it not?

That there is no instantaneous linear dependence, so a joint treatment of the samples at the same instant is useless (slide 10). It says nothing about lagged dependence (look at the cross-covariance), nor about non-linear dependence (correlation 0 does not mean independence).

3 · The cross-covariance γ₁,₂[m] = Cov(x₁[n], x₂[n + m]) peaks at m = +12. Which channel leads? And if both channels are periodic of period 100?

\(x_1\) leads: \(x_2\) repeats it 12 samples later. With a period of 100, peaks also appear at \(12 - 100 = -88\), and "leads by 12" and "lags by 88" cannot be told apart: the lag is only defined modulo the period (the two feet peak at −51 and +51).

4 · Two independent random walks show a correlation of −0.43. Why, and what should be done?

Non-stationary series with trends produce large sample correlations by chance (Yule's nonsense correlations): the band \(\pm 2/\sqrt N\) assumes white series, and 75% of the lags fall outside it. Prewhiten, by fitting an AR model to each series and cross-correlating the residuals: 3% of the lags remain outside, as expected by chance.

5 · Why do the ECG channels of slide 13 have only two principal components?

The limb leads are computed from two measured ones: \(\mathrm{III} = \mathrm{II} - \mathrm I\) and the three augmented leads are combinations of I and II. Their rank is 2 by construction, and the other components are the rounding of the stored values (1 µV, the \(10^{-3}\) mV of slide 13). The chest leads are measured, so with all twelve leads the rank is 8.

6 · The principal components are uncorrelated. Can they be processed independently without loss?

Not always. PCA decorrelates at lag 0 only: in the ECG, PC1 and PC2 correlate at −0.39 with a delay of 28 ms, and separate models ignore it. And PCA ranks directions by variance, which is not relevance for the task.

7 · The AR-error detector of slides 14–15 does not flag the premature beat. Why, and what does?

An AR model scores the predictability of the next sample: it is worst at the sharp onset of every normal QRS, and the wide ectopic beat is locally smooth (9th of 13 beats). A discord score compares whole windows with their nearest neighbours, and ranks the premature beat first for every window from 0.3 to 1 s. The bypass is fine; the univariate tool defines the anomaly.

8 · Write the VAR(p) model and count its parameters. Compare with D separate AR models.

\(\bx[n] = \bc + \sum_{i=1}^p \bA_i\bx[n-i] + \bb[n]\): \(D^2p\) coefficients and \(D\) intercepts, plus \(D(D+1)/2\) for \(\bSigma_b\). \(D\) AR(\(p\)) models have \(Dp + D\). For the foot sensor (\(D = 3\), \(p = 50\)): 450 against 150.

9 · Why does VAR estimation split into D separate regressions?

\(\|\bY - \bZ\underline{\bA}\|_F^2\) is a sum over the columns of \(\bY\), and column \(d\) of \(\underline{\bA}\) only enters column \(d\): each channel is regressed on the past of all channels, with the same design \(\bZ\). With identical regressors, this is also the Gaussian maximum-likelihood estimate.

10 · When is a VAR(1) stable? A VAR(p)?

VAR(1): every eigenvalue of \(\bA\) has modulus below 1, so that \(\sum_k \bA^k\bb[n-k]\) converges. VAR(\(p\)): the same condition on the \(pD \times pD\) companion matrix. For \(D = 1\), these are the AR poles of Lecture 3. Complex eigenvalues make the channels oscillate.

11 · Derive p_max = ⌊(N − 1)/(D + 1)⌋. What happens there?

There are \(N - p\) equations and \(pD + 1\) unknowns per channel; the system is determined iff \(N - p \ge pD + 1\), i.e. \(p \le (N-1)/(D+1)\). At \(p_{\max}\) (124 for the foot) the fit is almost exact (RMSE 0.018) and the held-out error explodes (6.4): the elbow must be read much earlier.

12 · Slides 25–27 show VAR(50) and VAR(16) beating three AR(50). Is the joint model better?

In sample, yes, even at equal budget (VAR(16), 144 parameters, against 150). On held-out walking, the best separate model (AR(101), 303 parameters, mean RMSE 0.382) slightly beats the best joint one (VAR(35), 315 parameters, 0.392): a memory of one stride matters more than the other axes. The comparison has to be made out of sample.

13 · Name three traps of Granger causality.

A hidden common driver creates a link between its two targets; pairwise tests turn a chain \(x_1 \to x_2 \to x_3\) into an extra \(x_1 \to x_3\); a purely instantaneous relation (in \(\bSigma_b\)) is invisible. Plus the 5% of chance links per null pair (17% of draws with at least one false link among four null pairs), and the effects of sampling and aggregation.

14 · What changes in multichannel CDL, and why can a shared activation help?

Atoms become \(L \times D\) patterns and each keeps a single activation. The gradient sums the correlations of all channels, so the evidence is pooled, and the channel where two patterns differ decides which one occurred. Coded separately, channel 1 of slide 34 never uses atom 1 and confuses the patterns.

15 · What is good sparsity for a joint representation? Write the mixed norm and the role of p and q.

Each column (a dimension) sparse, each row (an atom) dense: \(\Omega(\bZ) = \sum_k(\sum_d|z_{k,d}|^q)^{p/q}\), with \(p \le 1\) for few atoms and \(q \ge 1\) for no sparsity within a row. \(p = 1\), \(q = 2\) is the \(L_{2,1}\) norm.

16 · Derive the M-FOCUSS update, and say which problem its fixed point solves.

With \(\bW\) frozen, substitute \(\bZ = \bW\bQ\): \(\min_\bQ \|\bX - \bD\bW\bQ\|_F^2 + \lambda\|\bQ\|_F^2\), so \(\bQ = \bW\bD^\top(\bD\bW^2\bD^\top + \lambda\bI)^{-1}\bX\) and \(\bZ = \bW^2\bD^\top(\dots)^{-1}\bX\). At a fixed point, \(\bd_k^\top(\bX - \bD\bZ) = \lambda\bz_k/\|\bz_k\|_2\): the optimality condition of the \(L_{2,1}\) problem with penalty \(2\lambda\).

17 · With an orthonormal dictionary, give the closed forms for L1, L2 and L2,1.

With \(\bC = \bD^\top\bX\): \(L_1\), soft thresholding of each entry at \(\lambda/2\); \(L_2\), \(\bC/(1+\lambda)\); \(L_{2,1}\), \(\bz_k = \bc_k(1 - \lambda/(2\|\bc_k\|_2))_+\), a whole row kept or dropped.

18 · Prove that the number of zero eigenvalues of L is the number of connected components.

\(\bL\) is positive semi-definite, so \(\bL\bx = \bzero\) iff \(\bx^\top\bL\bx = \frac12\sum W_{i,j}(x_i - x_j)^2 = 0\), iff \(\bx\) is constant on each component. The null space is spanned by the component indicators, one per component.

19 · Why are the Laplacian eigenvalues called frequencies?

\(S(\bu_i) = \bu_i^\top\bL\bu_i = \lambda_i\): the eigenvalue measures how much its eigenvector varies across edges, 0 for the constant. On a ring graph, the eigenvectors are the DFT's sinusoids and \(\lambda = 2 - 2\cos(2\pi k/D)\) grows with the frequency.

20 · Slide 66 defines K-bandlimited as ‖x̂‖₀ = K. What is the usual definition, and why does it matter?

Usually, \(\hat x_i = 0\) for \(i \gt K\): the spectrum sits on the \(K\) lowest frequencies, a smoothness assumption that sampling theorems rely on. Slide 66's is a sparsity assumption (any \(K\) frequencies, the best \(K\)-term approximation of slide 67). On slide 57's sample, with \(K = 4\), the two keep different coefficients: 40.5% against 52.7% error.

21 · Why is a polynomial graph filter local, and why does it matter?

\((\bL^i)_{j,k} = 0\) if \(j\) and \(k\) are more than \(i\) edges apart: an order-\(p\) filter mixes \(p\)-hop neighbourhoods and needs only \(p\) matrix-vector products, no eigendecomposition. It scales to large graphs, and it is what graph neural networks learn.

22 · Derive the Tikhonov graph filter and its frequency response.

\(\min_\by \|\bx - \by\|^2 + \alpha\by^\top\bL\by\): the gradient \(2(\by - \bx) + 2\alpha\bL\by = 0\) gives \(\by = (\bI + \alpha\bL)^{-1}\bx\), and in the eigenbasis \(h(\lambda) = 1/(1 + \alpha\lambda)\). It assumes the signal smooth on the graph; its strength must be read against \(\lambda_{\max}\).

23 · Two EEG electrodes are broken, and you know which. What do you do?

Not a blind low-pass filter on every channel: it reduces the noise (correlation 0.27 → 0.45) without recovering the signal, and alters the healthy channels. Interpolate the broken nodes from the others (harmonic interpolation: 0.92 and 0.97 on the distance graph), or regress them on the others with a covariance from clean data. Build any correlation graph on clean data.

24 · In the graph-learning problem of slide 87, what does each term and constraint do?

\(\|\bX - \bY\|_F^2\): \(\bY\) stays close to the data; \(\alpha\tr(\bY\bL\bY^\top) = \alpha\sum w_{i,j}\|\by_i - \by_j\|^2\): weight goes to pairs with close signals; \(\beta\|\bL\|_F^2\): the sparsity; \(\tr\bL = D\): rules out \(\bL = \bzero\); \(L_{i,j} \le 0\), \(\bL\bone = \bzero\): a valid Laplacian.

25 · What should every multivariate method be compared with?

The same method applied dimension by dimension (slide 92): benchmarks find independent methods competitive, and on this page the separate AR models beat the joint VAR on held-out walking. A joint model is justified by that comparison, not by its in-sample fit.
