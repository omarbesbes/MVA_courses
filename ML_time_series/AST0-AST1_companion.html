Introduction: why time series need their own machine learning

The intro argues one thing: with time series, understanding the data comes before any algorithm, and most off-the-shelf ML ignores what makes the data special, which is time.

I.1

What a time series is

AST0 · slides 8–11

A time series is a sequence of data points indexed in time order. In practice it is an array of real numbers of size \(D \times N\): \(D\) dimensions (channels) and \(N\) samples. Three objects travel together: the sample number \(n\), the values \(x[n] \in \R^D\), and the timestamps \(t[n]\).

One object, three names

Time series in mathematics, statistics, economics and finance. Signals in signal processing, physics and engineering. Sequences in computer science, bioinformatics and data mining. The course uses the three words interchangeably. The working definition: real-valued, or at least ordered, sequential data.

Why this matters to you: the same idea is often published under different names by different communities. Lecture 1 is full of examples. Sliding a template along a signal and computing inner products is a matched filter for a signal-processing engineer, a cross-correlation for a statistician, and the core of similarity search for a data miner. Pattern extraction is called motif discovery in data mining.

Univariate or not

Many real series are multivariate: 2D or 3D trajectories (motion capture, eye tracking), several sensors recorded together, multimodal sensor networks, signals living on a graph. Everything in Lecture 1 is written for one dimension; the multivariate case is Lecture 6, and the conclusion of Lecture 1 names it as the open challenge for motif discovery.

I.2

Why time series are hard

AST0 · slides 12–13

Size. Audio at 44.1 kHz gives 2.6 million samples per minute. Algorithms must scale, which is why Lecture 1 spends so much effort on complexity (FFT tricks, lower bounds).

Structure. Multivariate, multimodal, heterogeneous: different units, rates and noise levels in the same dataset.

Quality. Noise, missing data, trends, mixtures of sources. Each of these returns in Lecture 1 as a deformation that a distance must tolerate or not.

Labels. Nobody needs training to label a cat. Labeling an ECG or a machine vibration takes expertise. The typical project looks like this:

  • noisy, dirty data;
  • a few annotated signals with blurry labels that cannot be turned into clean classes;
  • an expert with years of experience who cannot translate it into ML-ready annotations.
Key idea

When labels are scarce, you cannot train your way out of the problem. That is the reason this course is built on unsupervised, interpretable methods: compare, model and explain, then show the result to the expert.

I.3

Time is information

AST0 · slides 14–16

What separates a time series from ordinary tabular data is sequence and chronology. Each sample measures a phenomenon at a given instant, and time lets you study how the phenomenon evolves. Slide 15 makes the point with airline passenger counts: plot the same values with the times mixed up and all structure disappears. Try it.

Why ML ignores it

Slide 16: a speech recording or an eye-tracking heatmap becomes a vector of numbers, the vector goes into a classifier, out comes "male, young, British accent" or "likely to buy a car". Most ML algorithms do not care about time. Give an SVM or a random forest vectors in which coordinates 3 and 17 are swapped in every example and it learns exactly as well. It has no idea that coordinate 3 comes just before coordinate 4.

So the temporal structure has to be put in by us, in one of three places: in the similarity measure (Lecture 1: DTW knows that time can stretch), in the features (Lecture 2), or in a model or representation (Lecture 3).

I.4

Two ways of looking at a series: physics and statistics

AST0 · slide 17

Physics

\(x[1:N]\) is the digitization of a physical phenomenon \(x(t)\), for instance \(x[n] = x(nT_s)\) with sampling period \(T_s\). Properties of the phenomenon (frequencies, energy, dynamics) can be read from the samples, and the other way round. Tools: Fourier transform, filtering, differential equations. Time has been modeled this way since the 18th century.

Statistics

\(x[1:N]\) is one realization of a stochastic process \(X[1:N]\). Randomness lets you model a wider class of signals. Statistical properties (moments, correlations, distributions) are estimated from the one realization you have. Tools: autocorrelation, stationarity, AR models, likelihoods.

In Lecture 1

Most methods combine both. You will see all three flavours in Lecture 1:

  • Physics toolbox, used for speed. The FFT and the convolution theorem make pattern search \(O(N \log N)\).
  • Statistical model. Convolutional dictionary learning writes \(x = \sum_k z_k * d_k + e\): a generative model with a noise term.
  • Neither: geometry. Euclidean distance, DTW and the matrix profile just compare shapes.
Link

"One realization is enough to learn about the process" needs assumptions: stationarity and ergodicity, formalized at the start of L2 §2.1. AR models come in L3 §2.3.

I.5

Is deep learning the answer?

AST0 · slides 18–24

Deep learning reaches state-of-the-art results on some tasks, but:

  • Good performance is not good understanding of the data.
  • It is a black box: practitioners in the field cannot interpret the results.
  • Long contexts. Early models (LSTM) handled a few hundred samples at most. Long-context architectures exist, but really using distant information is still open.
  • Data-hungry: many labeled examples are needed to avoid overfitting, which is exactly what we lack (I.2).
  • Sensitive to distribution shift, and time series are inherently non-stationary.
  • Easy to fool: shortcut learning, adversarial examples.

And the benchmarks of slides 19–20 show that simpler ("shallow") methods hold their ground on every task:

  • Forecasting. A one-layer linear model (LTSF-Linear) beats Transformer forecasters on long-horizon benchmarks (Zeng et al., 2022; see also TFB, 2024).
  • Classification. The 2024 "bake off redux" finds no deep model that dominates, no "AlexNet moment" for time series.
  • Clustering. k-Shape, a shallow method, stays competitive with deep and foundation models (2025).
  • Anomaly detection. Simple methods perform almost as well as sophisticated ones (2022, 2025).

The LSTM that learned nothing (slides 21–24)

A blog post trains an LSTM on the first 250 days of a stock index and predicts the rest. The predicted curve sits on the real one, the scatter plot of predicted against real values gives \(R^2 = 0.89\), the RMSE is low. Then you zoom in: the prediction is the real curve shifted by one day. The network learned to repeat the last value. Differencing the series, \(x[n] - x[n-1]\), gives white noise: the index is a random walk, and a random walk cannot be predicted. A careful look before modeling would have shown it.

Key idea

Beyond the slides

For a random walk \(x[n] = x[n-1] + \varepsilon[n]\) with independent zero-mean \(\varepsilon\), the best possible forecast in mean square is \(\mathbb{E}[x[n] \mid x[1:n-1]] = x[n-1]\). No model, deep or not, can do better. An LSTM that outputs \(x[n-1]\) has found the optimum of a problem with no signal in it.

Lecture 1: pattern recognition and detection

How to tell that two pieces of signal are "the same pattern", how to find a known pattern fast in a very long series, and how to discover patterns nobody told you about.

§1

Two problems, one building block

AST1 · slides 4–10

The motivating examples of slides 4–5 share one feature: the meaningful events are shapes that repeat. In a household's power consumption over one day (peak 7.18 kW, mean 0.49 kW, 11.0 kWh), the refrigerator, kettle, toaster, washing machine, cooker and oven each leave a recognizable signature. In ECGs, normal sinus rhythm, atrial fibrillation, ventricular bigeminy and left bundle branch block differ by the shape and the repetition of the beats.

Problem 1 · Pattern detection

Given a dictionary of patterns, retrieve these patterns in an input time series.

  • patterns and series may be multivariate;
  • patterns may have different lengths;
  • patterns may be annotated (linked to a phenomenon), and then detection annotates the series automatically.

Slides 6–7: a decaying bump and a rectangular pulse are found in a long signal and colored in place.

Problem 2 · Pattern extraction

Given an input time series (or a set of series), learn a dictionary of patterns.

  • a pattern is a shape that appears repetitively, a somewhat blurry notion;
  • for simplicity all patterns have the same length;
  • the extracted patterns characterize the series, or are studied on their own.

Slide 9: the same signal, and the two shapes are now the output.

Everything in the lecture hangs on one question: when are two pieces of signal the same pattern? Answer it with a similarity measure (§2), and detection becomes "slide the pattern along the series and compute the measure everywhere" (§3). Extraction then becomes "compare the series with itself" (§4.1), or fitting a model that builds the series from a few patterns (§4.2).

§2

Comparing time series

AST1 · slides 12–39

2.0 · Invariances: decide what "the same" means

slide 12

To decide whether a pattern repeats, you need to compare subsequences with a similarity measure. The difficulty: occurrences of the same pattern are never identical. They can differ by:

Key idea

A similarity measure is useful to the extent that it is blind to the deformations deemed irrelevant, and sensitive to everything else.

There is no universally correct choice. It is an application-level decision. In gesture recognition, speed varies from person to person, so you want invariance to warping. In cardiology, the amplitude of some ECG waves is itself diagnostic, so normalizing it away would destroy information.

2.1 · Euclidean distance

slides 13–16
\[ d_{EUC}(\bx, \by) = \sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N} \big(x[n] - y[n]\big)^2} \]

It compares sample \(n\) with sample \(n\), and only with it (the vertical lines of slide 13). This lock-step matching is its strength (simple, fast, a true metric) and its weakness: it needs a perfect match between the two timelines and equal lengths. On the example of slides 14–16, starting from a baseline distance of 3.5 between two occurrences:

Why these numbers
  • Offset: \(\|\bx - (\bx + \beta \mathbf 1)\| = |\beta| \sqrt N\). It grows with the offset and with the length, whatever the shape.
  • Time shift: a shift of only 1.6% of the length raises the distance by half, because every sample on a steep edge is now compared with a different level.
  • Outliers: one spike of height \(A\) adds \(A^2\) to \(d^2\). Squaring lets a single bad sample dominate.
  • Low white noise is roughly fine: if \(\by = \bx' + \be\) with independent noise of variance \(\sigma^2\), then \(\mathbb E\, d^2(\bx, \by) = d^2(\bx, \bx') + N\sigma^2\). Noise adds about the same amount to every comparison, so the ranking of candidates mostly survives.

2.2 · Normalized Euclidean distance

slides 17–22

To become robust to offset and amplitude, z-normalize both series before comparing them (\(\mu_x\), \(\sigma_x\) are the mean and standard deviation of \(\bx\)):

\[ d_{nEUC}(\bx, \by) = \sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N} \big(\tilde x[n] - \tilde y[n]\big)^2}, \qquad \tilde x[n] = \frac{x[n] - \mu_x}{\sigma_x} \]

Why it is invariant. If \(\by = \alpha \bx + \beta \mathbf 1\) with \(\alpha \gt 0\), then \(\mu_y = \alpha \mu_x + \beta\) and \(\sigma_y = \alpha \sigma_x\), hence \(\tilde y = \tilde x\) exactly. (With \(\alpha \lt 0\), \(\tilde y = -\tilde x\): a mirrored shape, at maximal distance.) On the slide example the distance stays at 0.8 for the baseline, the offset and the amplitude change, and rises to 1.9 (shift), 1.9 (noise) and 2.7 (outliers). It is still sensitive to shifts, dilatation and contraction, outliers and misaligned timelines.

Key result

Expanding the square gives the link with the Pearson correlation coefficient \(\rho\):

\[ d_{nEUC}^2(\bx, \by) = 2N\,(1 - \rho), \qquad \rho = \frac{\sum_n x[n]y[n] - N\mu_x\mu_y}{N \sigma_x \sigma_y} \]
  • Ranking by z-normalized distance is ranking by correlation.
  • The distance depends on the data only through one inner product and the first two moments. This is what makes the fast search of §3 possible.
  • Reading scale: \(d = 0\) same shape; \(d = \sqrt{2N}\) uncorrelated shapes; \(d = 2\sqrt N\) mirror image. You will see \(\sqrt{2N}\) again as the "flat level" of distance profiles.
Derivation of \(d^2 = 2N(1-\rho)\) in three lines

With population standard deviations, \(\sum_n \tilde x[n] = 0\) and \(\|\tilde{\bx}\|^2 = \sum_n (x[n] - \mu_x)^2 / \sigma_x^2 = N\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \|\tilde\bx - \tilde\by\|^2 &= \|\tilde\bx\|^2 + \|\tilde\by\|^2 - 2\langle \tilde\bx, \tilde\by\rangle = 2N - 2 \sum_n \frac{(x[n] - \mu_x)(y[n] - \mu_y)}{\sigma_x \sigma_y} \\ &= 2N - 2\,\frac{\sum_n x[n]y[n] - N\mu_x\mu_y}{\sigma_x\sigma_y} = 2N(1 - \rho). \end{aligned} \]

Since \(\rho \in [-1, 1]\), \(d^2 \in [0, 4N]\).

Watch out

Flat segments. Dividing by \(\sigma_x\) amplifies noise when a subsequence is nearly constant: a flat piece with a little noise becomes pure amplified noise after normalization, and pure noise can match arbitrary shapes. Slide 17 also warns that normalization may increase the sensitivity to additive noise; compare the ratio of noisy to clean, ×2.0 for \(d_{EUC}\) (3.5 to 6.9) and ×2.4 for \(d_{nEUC}\) (0.8 to 1.9).

Beyond the slides: common practical fixes are to skip windows whose \(\sigma\) is below a floor, or to add a small constant to \(\sigma\).

LT-normalization: also remove a linear trend

z-normalization removes a constant offset, not a linear drift. Remove the projection onto the subspace \(\mathcal A = \mathrm{span}\{\mathbf 1, (1, 2, \dots, N)^\top\}\), then normalize:

\[ \bx^{LT} = \frac{(I - \Pi_{\mathcal A})\,\bx}{\|(I - \Pi_{\mathcal A})\,\bx\|_2}, \qquad d_{LT}(\bx, \by) = \big\|\bx^{LT} - \by^{LT}\big\|_2 \]

\(\Pi_{\mathcal A}\bx\) is the least-squares straight line fitted to \(\bx\), so \((I - \Pi_{\mathcal A})\bx\) is the detrended series (the residual of a linear regression). It is as cheap as z-normalization. z-normalization is the special case \(\mathcal A = \mathrm{span}\{\mathbf 1\}\): then \((I - \Pi)\bx = \bx - \mu_x \mathbf 1\), whose norm is \(\sqrt N \sigma_x\), so \(\bx^{LT} = \tilde\bx / \sqrt N\) and \(d_{LT} = d_{nEUC}/\sqrt N\), the same ranking. The same principle extends to larger invariance groups: remove any subspace of nuisance directions (Germain et al., 2024), and there is a large literature on invariances and deformations.

Link

Least-squares detrending is L4 §3.1. Lecture 2 notes that convolutional features are offset-invariant, "the convolutional counterpart of subtracting the mean in z-normalization", and that choosing a loss or a data augmentation is choosing an invariance, "the same modelling decision as choosing an invariance for a distance" L2.

2.3 · Dynamic Time Warping (DTW)

slides 23–38

Three questions remain open (slide 23): how to become insensitive to changes of the timeline (contraction, dilatation, shifts), how to compare series of different lengths, and how to extend the Euclidean distance to nonlinear timeline modifications. DTW answers all three by letting the matching bend: sample \(x[i_k]\) is associated with sample \(y[j_k]\), and the pairs \((i_k, j_k)\) are chosen by optimization. DTW comes from speech recognition (Sakoe and Chiba, 1978); Berndt and Clifford (1994) brought it to pattern search in data mining.

\[ P = \big((i_1, j_1), \dots, (i_{K_P}, j_{K_P})\big), \qquad w(P) = \sum_{k=1}^{K_P} \big(x[i_k] - y[j_k]\big)^2, \qquad d_{DTW}(\bx, \by) = \sqrt{\min_{P \in \mathcal P} w(P)} \]

A path \(P\) is a list of matched pairs: \((i_k, j_k) \in P\) means \(y[j_k]\) is matched with \(x[i_k]\). For series of lengths \(M\) and \(N\), an admissible path is:

  • continuous, \(i_k - i_{k-1} \le 1\) and \(j_k - j_{k-1} \le 1\): no sample is skipped, like a king on a chessboard;
  • monotonic, \(i_{k-1} \le i_k\) and \(j_{k-1} \le j_k\): time never runs backwards, matching lines never cross;
  • bounded, \((i_1, j_1) = (1, 1)\) and \((i_{K_P}, j_{K_P}) = (M, N)\): both series are compared from start to end.

If \(M = N\) and the path is the diagonal (\(K_P = N\), \(i_k = j_k = k\)), \(w(P)\) is exactly the squared Euclidean distance. Since the diagonal is one admissible path among many and DTW keeps the cheapest, \(d_{DTW} \le d_{EUC}\) for equal lengths.

Why dynamic programming, and how it works

The number of admissible paths explodes: 13 for two series of length 3, 1,462,563 for length 10, about \(3.5 \times 10^{74}\) for length 100 (these are the Delannoy numbers; beyond the slides). Enumerating them is hopeless. But the problem has optimal substructure: the best path to \((i, j)\) arrives from one of the three neighbours, and its beginning must itself be the best path to that neighbour, otherwise swapping in a better beginning would lower the total. Define \(C(i, j)\) as the minimum cost of an admissible path from \((1, 1)\) to \((i, j)\); then \(d_{DTW}(\bx, \by) = \sqrt{C(M, N)}\) and

\[ C(i, j) = \big(x[i] - y[j]\big)^2 + \min\big\{ C(i-1, j-1),\ C(i-1, j),\ C(i, j-1) \big\} \]
Algorithm 1 · Dynamic Time Warping (slide 31)
Input  x ∈ ℝ^M, y ∈ ℝ^N
for 1 ≤ i ≤ M, 1 ≤ j ≤ N:  D(i,j) = (x[i] − y[j])²   # step 1: distance matrix
C(1,j) = Σ_{ℓ≤j} D(1,ℓ)                          # first row: only moves right
C(i,1) = Σ_{ℓ≤i} D(ℓ,1)                          # first column: only moves up
for 2 ≤ i ≤ M, 2 ≤ j ≤ N:                        # step 2: cumulative matrix
    C(i,j) = D(i,j) + min{C(i−1,j−1), C(i−1,j), C(i,j−1)}
return d_DTW = √C(M,N)                           # O(MN) time

Step 1 (slide 32): the matrix \(D(i,j)\) of all pairwise squared differences. Bright bands appear where one series is far from the other.

Step 2 (slides 33–34): the cumulative matrix \(C\) is a landscape with a valley, and the optimal path runs along its floor. Filling the table costs \(O(MN)\). To get the path itself, backtrack from \((M, N)\), each time stepping to the predecessor that achieved the minimum.

Worked example: fill the table yourself

Take \(\bx = (1, 3, 4, 2)\) and \(\by = (1, 2, 4, 4, 2)\): different lengths, which the Euclidean distance cannot even handle. Compute \(D\), then \(C\) row by row, then read the path. Answer below.

Show the matrices and the path

For instance \(C(2,3) = D(2,3) + \min\{C(1,2), C(1,3), C(2,2)\} = 1 + \min\{1, 10, 1\} = 2\). Constraining the path: Sakoe-Chiba band, Itakura parallelogram

If the two timelines are roughly similar, there is no need (and no reason) to compute all of \(C\). The Sakoe-Chiba band computes \(C(i,j)\) only for \(|i - j| \lt \lambda\) (Sakoe et al., 1978); the Itakura parallelogram (Itakura, 1975) restricts the slope instead. Which one fits depends on what you assume about the timeline variations. Two benefits:

  • speed: \(O(N\lambda)\) cells instead of \(O(N^2)\) (9 ms against 2 ms in slide 36);
  • sanity: it forbids pathological warpings where one sample is matched with a huge stretch of the other series. This is the "nor advisable" of slide 35.

The band will also be the key to a good lower bound in §3.2.

What DTW is robust to

By construction, timeline changes. But the path must still align the first and last samples, which hurts under time shifts (variants exist that relax the endpoints). DTW is sensitive to scale (normalize beforehand), to noise and to outliers (slide 37).

Not a metric

\(d_{DTW}\) is symmetric and zero on identical inputs, but it violates the triangle inequality. Smallest example (beyond the slides): \(a = (0)\), \(b = (2)\), \(c = (2, 2)\). Then \(d(a,b) = 2\), \(d(b,c) = 0\) and \(d(a,c) = \sqrt 8 \approx 2.83 \gt 2 + 0\). Note that \(d(b,c) = 0\) although \(b \ne c\).

Consequences (slide 38). Not being a metric forbids metric indexing structures, which prune a search with the triangle inequality. It complicates barycenters (what is the "average" of series under DTW?) and degrades k-means-type clustering, which alternates assignment and averaging. Elastic distances built on edit operations are true metrics: TWED (Time Warp Edit Distance) and MSM (Move-Split-Merge). DTW is also not differentiable because of the min, so it cannot serve as a training loss for a neural network. Soft variants replace the min by a soft-min: soft-DTW, DILATE, soft-MSM (mini-projects 1.1–1.4).

Beyond the slides

The soft-min used by soft-DTW is \(\min^{\gamma}\{a_1, \dots, a_n\} = -\gamma \log \sum_i e^{-a_i/\gamma}\). It is smooth for \(\gamma \gt 0\) and tends to the ordinary min as \(\gamma \to 0\). Plugging it into the recursion for \(C\) gives a differentiable DTW.

§3

Detecting patterns: search a long series fast

AST1 · slides 41–59

To find a known pattern \(\bp\) of length \(N_p\) in a series \(\bx\) of length \(N\), compute its distance to every subsequence:

\[ d[n] = d\big(\bp,\ x[n : n + N_p - 1]\big), \qquad 1 \le n \le N - N_p + 1 \]

This sequence is the distance profile. Occurrences of the pattern are its deep local minima. Done naively it costs about \(N\) distance computations of cost \(N_p\) each: \(O(N N_p)\) for the Euclidean distance, and \(O(N N_p^2)\) for full DTW. With \(N = 10^8\) samples that is too slow, so the question of the section is: can we compute this with a good complexity? Two different answers, because the two distances have different structures.

3.1 · Euclidean distances: the whole profile in O(N log N)

slides 42–50 · Mueen et al., 2015

Step 1: expand the square (slide 43). Each term has a different status:

\[ d_{EUC}^2\big(\bp, x[n:n+N_p-1]\big) = \underbrace{\sum_{i=n}^{n+N_p-1} x[i]^2}_{s_x[n]\text{: sliding energy}} + \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{N_p} p[i]^2}_{\text{constant}} - 2\,\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{N_p} x[n+i-1]\,p[i]}_{r[n]\text{: sliding dot product}} \]
\[ d_{nEUC}^2\big(\bp, x[n:n+N_p-1]\big) = 2N_p\left(1 - \frac{r[n] - N_p\,\mu_x[n]\,\mu_p}{N_p\,\sigma_x[n]\,\sigma_p}\right) \]

This is §2.2 at work: the normalized distance only needs the inner product \(r[n]\) and the moments. Everything about \(\bp\) is computed once. What changes with \(n\) are the sliding statistics \(s_x[n], \mu_x[n], \sigma_x[n]\) and the sliding dot product \(r[n]\).

Step 2: sliding statistics by cumulative sums (slides 44–45). Precompute, in linear time, \(c_x[n] = \sum_{i \le n} x[i]\) and \(c^{(2)}_x[n] = \sum_{i \le n} x[i]^2\). Any window sum is then a difference of two entries:

\[ s_x[n] = c^{(2)}_x[n+N_p-1] - c^{(2)}_x[n-1], \quad \mu_x[n] = \frac{c_x[n+N_p-1] - c_x[n-1]}{N_p}, \quad \sigma_x[n] = \sqrt{\frac{s_x[n]}{N_p} - \mu_x[n]^2} \]

\(O(N)\) for all windows together, independently of \(N_p\).

Step 3: the sliding dot product is a convolution (slides 46–47). Reverse the pattern, \(\overleftarrow{p}[k] = p[N_p - k + 1]\). Sliding a template along a signal and summing products is exactly convolving the signal with the reversed template:

\[ r[n] = \sum_{i=1}^{N_p} x[n+i-1]\,p[i] = \big(\bx * \overleftarrow{\bp}\big)[n + N_p - 1], \qquad \bx * \by = \mathrm{iFFT}\big\{\mathrm{FFT}\{\bx\} \times \mathrm{FFT}\{\by\}\big\} \]

By the convolution theorem, a convolution in time is a term-by-term product in frequency, and the FFT computes a DFT in \(O(N \log N)\). So all \(N - N_p + 1\) dot products cost three FFTs: \(O(N \log N)\), whatever the pattern length.

The index bookkeeping, and why padding to length N is enough

With the convention \((\bx * \mathbf q)[m] = \sum_k x[m - k + 1]\,q[k]\) and \(q = \overleftarrow{p}\), substitute \(i = N_p - k + 1\):

\[ (\bx * \overleftarrow{\bp})[m] = \sum_{i=1}^{N_p} x[m - N_p + i]\,p[i] = r[m - N_p + 1]. \]

So \(r[1 : N - N_p + 1]\) is the convolution read at indices \(m = N_p, \dots, N\), which is the \([N_p : N]\) of slide 47.

The FFT computes a circular convolution: indices wrap around modulo the FFT length. Slide 47 pads \(\overleftarrow{\bp}\) with \(N - N_p\) zeros, so the circular convolution has length \(N\). For \(m \ge N_p\), every index \(m - N_p + i\) lies between 1 and \(N\), so nothing wraps. Wrap-around only corrupts the first \(N_p - 1\) outputs, and those are exactly the ones we throw away.

Algorithm 2 · Normalized Euclidean distance profile (slide 49)
Input  x ∈ ℝ^N, pattern p ∈ ℝ^Np
p ← (p − μp) / σp ;  p⃖ = p[Np] p[Np−1] … p[1] 0 … 0     # z-normalize, reverse, pad to length N
c1 = 0_{N+1} ;  c2 = 0_{N+1}
for 2 ≤ i ≤ N+1:  c1(i) = c1(i−1) + x[i−1] ;  c2(i) = c2(i−1) + x[i−1]²
r = iFFT{ FFT{x} × FFT{p⃖} }                              # every sliding dot product at once
s = c2(Np+1 : N+1) − c2(1 : N−Np+1)                       # sliding energies
μ = ( c1(Np+1 : N+1) − c1(1 : N−Np+1) ) / Np              # sliding means
σ = √( s/Np − μ² )                                        # sliding standard deviations
d = √( 2 (Np − r(Np : N) / σ) )                           # the profile

The last line is the formula of step 1 with \(\mu_p = 0\) and \(\sigma_p = 1\) (the pattern was normalized first): \(2N_p(1 - r/(N_p \sigma)) = 2(N_p - r/\sigma)\). The normalized version is not more expensive than the plain one; the only extra work is the linear-time \(\mu_x[n]\). With \(N_p = 10^2\) and \(N = 10^8\) the whole profile takes about 16 seconds on a standard computer (slide 48).

3.2 · DTW: prune instead of computing

slides 51–59 · Rakthanmanon et al., 2012

DTW has no FFT shortcut: the min inside the recursion breaks the algebra. So the problem is reformulated: instead of the whole profile, we look for the best match only,

\[ d^* = \min_{1 \le n \le N - N_p + 1} d_{DTW}\big(\bp,\ x[n : n+N_p-1]\big). \]

This changes everything, because now any candidate that is provably worse than the best so far can be skipped without computing its DTW. Windows are z-normalized with the same cumulative-sum trick as before, to be robust to amplitude and offset. The UCR suite of Rakthanmanon et al. combines several tricks, and the first one is a lower bound.

Key idea

A cheap lower bound is a certificate of rejection. If \(d_{DTW}(\bx, \by) \ge \LB(\bx, \by)\) and \(\LB\) exceeds the best distance found so far, then the DTW exceeds it too, and the candidate cannot be the best match. A tighter bound prunes more but costs more, which is why bounds are used in cascade, cheapest first.

\(\LB_{KimFL}\): the endpoints (Kim et al., 2001)

Every admissible path contains \((1, 1)\) and \((M, N)\) (boundary condition), and all other terms are non-negative, so

\[ d_{DTW}^2(\bx, \by) = \min_{P \in \mathcal P} \sum_{k=1}^{K_P} \big(x[i_k] - y[j_k]\big)^2 \ \ge\ \big(x[1] - y[1]\big)^2 + \big(x[M] - y[N]\big)^2 \]
\[ \LB_{KimFL}(\bx, \by) = \sqrt{\big(x[1]-y[1]\big)^2 + \big(x[M]-y[N]\big)^2} \]

It costs \(O(1)\) and is rough: two samples out of \(N_p\). How it is used (slide 54):

  1. Compute \(\LB_{KimFL}(\bp, x[n:n+N_p-1])\) for every \(n\). It is very cheap.
  2. Initialize the set \(\mathcal N\) of candidates to all of \(1 \dots N - N_p + 1\), and the best distance \(d^* = +\infty\).
  3. Pick one or a few candidates in \(\mathcal N\), compute their true DTW, update \(d^*\).
  4. Remove from \(\mathcal N\) every \(n\) with \(\LB(\bp, x[n:n+N_p-1]) \gt d^*\).
  5. Repeat steps 3–4 until \(\mathcal N\) is empty.

In slide 55, after one single DTW computation, \(\LB_{KimFL}\) already removes 10% of the candidates.

\(\LB_{Keogh}\): an envelope (Keogh et al., 2004)

Assume the DTW is constrained by a band of half-width \(\lambda\): \(x[i_k]\) can only be matched with \(y[j_k]\) if \(|i_k - j_k| \le \lambda\). Build the upper and lower envelopes of \(\bx\) over the window of possible matches:

\[ u[j] = \max_{j - \lambda \le i \le j + \lambda} x[i], \qquad l[j] = \min_{j - \lambda \le i \le j + \lambda} x[i] \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad l[j_k] \le x[i_k] \le u[j_k]. \]

So each term of the path is at least the squared distance from \(y[j_k]\) to the interval \([l[j_k], u[j_k]]\): \((l[j_k] - y[j_k])^2\) if \(y[j_k]\) is below it, \((y[j_k] - u[j_k])^2\) if above, and 0 inside. The bound depends only on \(j_k\):

\[ \LB_{Keogh}(\bx, \by) = \sqrt{\sum_{j:\ y[j] \lt l[j]} \big(l[j] - y[j]\big)^2 + \sum_{j:\ y[j] \gt u[j]} \big(y[j] - u[j]\big)^2} \]
The step slide 58 skips

Why may we sum over every \(j\)? Because every index \(j\) of \(\by\) appears at least once in any admissible path (continuity plus boundary conditions: the path cannot jump over a column). Keep one term per \(j\), bound it from below by its envelope distance, and drop the other terms, which are all \(\ge 0\). What is left is still below \(d_{DTW}^2\). The bound is only valid for DTW computed with the same band (or a narrower one): without a band, the envelope becomes \([\min \bx, \max \bx]\) and the bound is nearly always 0.

\(\LB_{Keogh}\) costs \(O(N_p)\) per candidate once the query's envelope is built, against \(O(N_p \lambda)\) for the banded DTW. In slide 59, with \(\lambda = 50\), computing only the first 50 DTWs avoids 80% of the computations.

Second trick, early abandoning. While summing the terms of a bound, or filling the DTW matrix, stop as soon as the partial sum exceeds \(d^{*2}\). Other tricks (pruned normalization, reordering the samples, cascades of bounds) together avoid more than 99.9999% of the DTW computations, which is how DTW search scales to trillions of subsequences.

Beyond the slides

Why early abandoning is safe for DTW: every admissible path crosses every row \(i\) of the matrix, and costs only accumulate along a path. So if all cells of row \(i\) already exceed \(d^{*2}\), the final \(C(M, N)\) will too.

In step 3 of the procedure, picking candidates in increasing order of their lower bound (best first) maximizes pruning: the moment the smallest remaining bound exceeds \(d^*\), you can stop.

§4

Learning patterns: when nobody gives you the templates

AST1 · slides 61–87

§3 needs a dictionary of templates. Often you have none, and the reverse question is the interesting one: which patterns does this series contain? It is an unsupervised task, with no prior knowledge except the average duration \(L\) of the patterns. And "pattern" is hard to define: repeated shapes, periodicity, and so on. Two routes: reuse distances (§4.1), or fit a model (§4.2).

4.1 · Distance-based extraction: the matrix profile

slides 62–67 · Yeh et al., 2016

Working definition: a pattern is a subsequence likely to be found several times in the series. Test a candidate by computing its distance profile against the whole series. In slide 63 the candidate (a bump) produces three deep minima: it appears three times, so it might be a pattern. In slide 64 the candidate (a piece of noise) produces a single zero at its own position and nothing else: unlikely to be a pattern. Doing this for every candidate by brute force means \(N\) distance profiles.

In fact only the minimum of each profile matters: does this subsequence have a near-copy somewhere else? That minimum, for every position, is the matrix profile:

\[ m[n] = \min_{i \gt n + L\ \text{or}\ i \lt n - L} d\big(x[n : n+L-1],\ x[i : i+L-1]\big) \]
Trivial matches

The window starting at \(n + 1\) overlaps the one starting at \(n\) on \(L - 1\) samples, so it is almost the same subsequence and its distance is tiny. Without excluding overlapping positions, every subsequence would have a near-copy (its own neighbour) and every \(m[n]\) would be close to 0. Hence the condition \(|i - n| \gt L\), the exclusion zone. (Beyond the slides: libraries often use a smaller zone, around \(L/4\).)

  • Small \(m[n]\): the subsequence was found elsewhere, it could be a pattern. The global minimum and its nearest neighbour form the motif pair: the two most similar non-overlapping subsequences (slide 66, red and green).
  • Large \(m[n]\): the subsequence looks like nothing else in the series. That is the starting point of anomaly detection in Lecture 5.
  • It is computed efficiently with the tools of §3: each \(m[n]\) is the minimum of one distance profile.
Beyond the slides

Complexity: \(N\) profiles by FFT give \(O(N^2 \log N)\). The STOMP algorithm reaches \(O(N^2)\) by noticing that the dot products of consecutive rows are related, \(QT_{n+1}[i+1] = QT_n[i] - x[n]\,x[i] + x[n+L]\,x[i+L]\), and memory stays \(O(N)\) because only the running minimum is stored. The experiment below uses exactly this update. The stumpy library implements it, with GPU, streaming and multidimensional versions.

From the motif pair to motif sets (slide 67)

The matrix profile solves the motif pair problem. Applications need all occurrences of a recurring pattern: the motif set problem. One procedure: threshold the distance profile of the motif pair to collect every sufficiently close subsequence, remove them, and repeat until the desired number of motif sets is found. The two parameters (threshold, number of sets) strongly affect the result and are hard to set, which is what several mini-projects attack.

4.2 · Dictionary-based extraction: convolutional dictionary learning

slides 68–87 · Grosse et al., 2007; Wohlberg, 2014

A different route: write down how the series could have been generated from a few patterns, and fit that model. Learn a dictionary of \(K\) patterns (atoms) \(\bd_k\) of length \(L\), and activation signals \(\bz_k\) of length \(N - L + 1\), with \(z_k[n] \ne 0\) when pattern \(k\) is activated at time \(n\):

\[ x[n] = \sum_{k=1}^{K} (\bz_k * \bd_k)[n] + e[n] \]

Why a convolution. Convolving an atom with a sparse spike train places a copy of the atom at each spike, scaled by the spike's height. So the model says: the series is a sum of copies of a few shapes, placed anywhere, at any amplitude, plus noise. The model is shift-invariant by construction, which is what a pattern that "can occur anywhere" requires.

Slide 69 shows it on an accelerometer signal: atom \(D_1\) fires at almost every step, atom \(D_2\) twice.

The optimization problem (slide 70):

\[ \min_{(\bd_k), (\bz_k)\ :\ \forall k,\ \|\bd_k\|_2^2 \le 1}\ \underbrace{\Big\| \bx - \sum_{k=1}^{K} \bz_k * \bd_k \Big\|_2^2}_{f(Z, D)\text{: explain the data}} + \underbrace{\lambda \sum_{k=1}^{K} \|\bz_k\|_1}_{\psi(Z)\text{: few activations}} \]
  • The \(\ell_1\) penalty makes activations sparse: each occurrence is explained by one placement of one atom, which is what makes the learned patterns interpretable (sparsity is developed in Lecture 3).
  • The norm constraint removes a scale ambiguity: \((\alpha \bd_k, \bz_k/\alpha)\) gives the same reconstruction for any \(\alpha \gt 0\). With the \(\ell_1\) penalty this is worse than an instability: the penalty becomes \(\lambda\|\bz_k\|_1/\alpha\), so letting \(\alpha \to \infty\) sends it to 0 without changing the fit. Without the constraint the minimum is not attained; atoms blow up and activations vanish.
  • Not convex in the pair \((D, Z)\), because \(\bz_k * \bd_k\) is a product of unknowns. But for fixed \(D\) the problem in \(Z\) is a convex LASSO, and for fixed \(Z\) the problem in \(D\) is a convex constrained least squares. Hence alternated resolution (slide 71):
\[ \underbrace{D^* = \argmin_{D\ :\ \|\bd_k\|_2^2 \le 1} \Big\|\bx - \sum_k \bz_k * \bd_k\Big\|_2^2}_{\text{dictionary learning}} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{Z^* = \argmin_{Z} \Big\|\bx - \sum_k \bz_k * \bd_k\Big\|_2^2 + \lambda\sum_k \|\bz_k\|_1}_{\text{convolutional sparse coding (CSC)}} \]

Proximal gradient descent

Both subproblems have the form "smooth \(f\) plus a simple non-smooth part", solved by proximal gradient descent (slide 72): (1) a gradient step on \(f\), (2) a proximal step that pulls the result back onto the constraint set or towards sparsity. The two proximal operators (slide 74):

\[ \mathrm{proj}_{\|\cdot\|_2 \le 1}(\by) = \frac{\by}{\max(\|\by\|_2, 1)} \qquad\qquad \mathcal S_\gamma(\by)[n] = \mathrm{sign}(y[n]) \times \max\big(|y[n]| - \gamma, 0\big) \]
Where soft thresholding comes from (1D derivation)

The proximal operator of \(\gamma|\cdot|\) at \(v\) is the minimizer of \(\tfrac12 (u - v)^2 + \gamma |u|\). For \(u \gt 0\) the derivative is \(u - v + \gamma\), zero at \(u = v - \gamma\), valid if \(v \gt \gamma\). Symmetrically \(u = v + \gamma\) if \(v \lt -\gamma\). If \(|v| \le \gamma\), the subdifferential at 0, \([-\gamma, \gamma] - v\), contains 0, so \(u = 0\). Together: \(u = \mathrm{sign}(v)\max(|v| - \gamma, 0)\). For the indicator of a convex set, the same definition gives the Euclidean projection onto the set.

Computing the gradients (slide 73)

By the convolution theorem and Parseval's theorem (energy is preserved by the DFT, up to a constant), the data term becomes a sum of term-by-term products in frequency, which is linear in each block:

\[ f(Z, D) \propto \Big\| \hat\bx - \sum_{k=1}^{K} \hat\bz_k \odot \hat\bd_k \Big\|_2^2 = \big\| \hat\bx - \widehat{D}\,\hat\bz \big\|_2^2, \qquad \text{since } \mathbf u \odot \mathbf v = \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf u)\,\mathbf v \]

Gradients with respect to \(\widehat D\) and \(\hat\bz\) are those of an ordinary least-squares problem; the updates are brought back to the time domain by an inverse DFT.

A link worth seeing

Written in the time domain (beyond the slides, with the convention \(f = \tfrac12\|\mathbf r\|^2\) and residual \(\mathbf r = \bx - \sum_k \bz_k * \bd_k\)):

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial z_k[n]} = -\sum_{\ell=1}^{L} r[n + \ell - 1]\, d_k[\ell], \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial d_k[\ell]} = -\sum_{n} z_k[n]\, r[n + \ell - 1]. \]

The first one is the sliding dot product of §3.1, between the residual and atom \(k\): a CSC step slides each atom along what is still unexplained, raises the activations where it fits, and soft thresholding keeps only the strong responses. That is template detection, repeated. The second one pulls each atom towards the average of the residual segments where it is active.

Dictionary step, Z fixed (slide 75)

Projected gradient descent:

\[ D \leftarrow D - \alpha \nabla_D f(Z, D), \qquad \bd_k \leftarrow \frac{\bd_k}{\max(\|\bd_k\|_2, 1)} \]

Alternatives: ADMM, K-SVD (Mairal et al., 2010; Lecture 3).

Sparse coding step, D fixed (slide 76)

ISTA, the Iterative Soft-Thresholding Algorithm (Daubechies et al., 2004):

\[ Z \leftarrow Z - \alpha \nabla_Z f(Z, D), \qquad Z \leftarrow \mathcal S_{\lambda\alpha}(Z) \]

Alternatives: ADMM, FISTA (accelerated ISTA), coordinate descent. The step \(\alpha\) must be at most \(1/L_f\), the inverse Lipschitz constant of \(\nabla f\): one of the hyperparameters slide 87 mentions.

Summary
  • Unlike distance-based extraction, CDL handles mixtures of patterns, thanks to the additive model: two overlapping occurrences look like neither pattern, so no template matches them, but their sum is explained exactly.
  • CDL is sensitive to initialization (normalized Gaussian noise, random data frames) and to hyperparameters (\(\lambda\), \(K\), \(L\), the step size through the Lipschitz constant).
  • With a fixed dictionary of templates, run only the CSC step: it solves the detection problem of §3 (try the second mode above).

The core algorithms in NumPy

Short reference implementations for the tutorials, checked against brute force (MASS agrees to \(10^{-12}\); the DTW gives 1.0 on the worked example). The library equivalents are stumpy.mass, stumpy.stump and tslearn.metrics.dtw.

Distance profile by FFT (MASS) 
import numpy as np

def mass(x, p):
    """z-normalized Euclidean distance profile of pattern p along series x, in O(N log N)."""
    N, m = len(x), len(p)
    p = (p - p.mean()) / p.std()
    # sliding dot product r[n] = sum_i x[n+i] p[i]: FFT convolution with the reversed pattern
    r = np.fft.irfft(np.fft.rfft(x) * np.fft.rfft(p[::-1], N), N)[m - 1:]
    # sliding mean and standard deviation from cumulative sums
    c1 = np.concatenate(([0.0], np.cumsum(x)))
    c2 = np.concatenate(([0.0], np.cumsum(x ** 2)))
    mu = (c1[m:] - c1[:-m]) / m
    sig = np.sqrt(np.maximum((c2[m:] - c2[:-m]) / m - mu ** 2, 1e-24))
    return np.sqrt(np.maximum(2 * (m - r / sig), 0))
DTW with a Sakoe-Chiba band 
def dtw(x, y, band=None):
    """DTW distance; band = Sakoe-Chiba half-width (None = unconstrained)."""
    M, N = len(x), len(y)
    C = np.full((M + 1, N + 1), np.inf)      # padded border = the initialization of slide 30
    C[0, 0] = 0.0
    for i in range(1, M + 1):
        lo, hi = (1, N) if band is None else (max(1, i - band), min(N, i + band))
        for j in range(lo, hi + 1):
            cost = (x[i - 1] - y[j - 1]) ** 2
            C[i, j] = cost + min(C[i - 1, j - 1], C[i - 1, j], C[i, j - 1])
    return np.sqrt(C[M, N])
Matrix profile (naive, reusing MASS) 
def matrix_profile(x, L):
    """Naive z-normalized matrix profile, O(N^2 log N)."""
    n = len(x) - L + 1
    mp = np.empty(n)
    for i in range(n):
        d = mass(x, x[i:i + L])
        d[max(0, i - L):i + L + 1] = np.inf      # exclusion zone: no trivial matches
        mp[i] = d.min()
    return mp
Convolutional sparse coding by ISTA 
def csc_ista(x, D, lam, n_iter=300):
    """ISTA for 1/2 ||x - sum_k z_k * d_k||^2 + lam * sum_k ||z_k||_1.
    D has shape (K, L); returns Z with shape (K, N - L + 1)."""
    K, L = D.shape
    N = len(x)
    Z = np.zeros((K, N - L + 1))
    lip = np.max(np.sum(np.abs(np.fft.rfft(D, N)) ** 2, axis=0))   # Lipschitz constant of the gradient
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        resid = x - sum(np.convolve(Z[k], D[k]) for k in range(K))
        grad = -np.array([np.correlate(resid, D[k], mode="valid") for k in range(K)])
        Z = Z - grad / lip                                            # gradient step
        Z = np.sign(Z) * np.maximum(np.abs(Z) - lam / lip, 0)         # soft thresholding
    return Z

The ½ in front of the data term only rescales \(\lambda\) compared with the slides. np.correlate(resid, D[k], "valid") is exactly the sliding dot product of §3.1.

Four identities to know by heart

  • \(d_{nEUC}^2 = 2N(1 - \rho)\); flat level \(\sqrt{2N}\), maximum \(2\sqrt N\).
  • \(r[n] = (\bx * \overleftarrow{\bp})[n + N_p - 1]\), all of them by one FFT product.
  • \(C(i,j) = D(i,j) + \min\{C(i-1,j-1), C(i-1,j), C(i,j-1)\}\).
  • \(\LB \le d_{DTW} \le d_{EUC}\) (equal lengths, band containing the diagonal).

Two operators

  • Soft thresholding \(\mathcal S_\gamma(v) = \mathrm{sign}(v)\max(|v| - \gamma, 0)\): the prox of \(\gamma\|\cdot\|_1\).
  • Unit-ball projection \(\by / \max(\|\by\|_2, 1)\): the prox of the constraint \(\|\bd\|_2 \le 1\).

R.2

Check yourself

Answer out loud or on paper before opening. The questions follow the page, and several ask for a small computation.

1 · Why won't a random forest fed with raw windows exploit time?

It treats the coordinates as an unordered set of features: permuting them the same way in every example changes nothing for it, so it cannot know that coordinate \(t\) sits next to \(t+1\). It also needs aligned, fixed-length inputs, and a shifted occurrence puts the same shape on different coordinates. Time has to be put in through the similarity measure, the features or the model.

2 · An LSTM forecasts a stock index with R² = 0.9. What do you check first?

Compare with the persistence baseline \(\hat x[n] = x[n-1]\); compute the metric on the changes \(x[n] - x[n-1]\) rather than on levels; look at the autocorrelation of the changes. White-noise changes mean a random walk, and then the best forecast is the last value, so R² on levels says nothing.

3 · Prove that z-normalized distance ignores y = αx + β1 with α > 0. What if α < 0?

\(\mu_y = \alpha\mu_x + \beta\) and \(\sigma_y = |\alpha|\sigma_x\), so \(\tilde y = \mathrm{sign}(\alpha)\,\tilde x\). For \(\alpha \gt 0\) the normalized series are equal and the distance is 0. For \(\alpha \lt 0\), \(\tilde y = -\tilde x\), \(\rho = -1\) and the distance is maximal, \(2\sqrt N\).

4 · Two z-normalized windows of length 200 are uncorrelated. Their distance?

\(\sqrt{2 \cdot 200 \cdot (1 - 0)} = 20\). That is the level of the flat parts of the distance profile in slide 50 and in the experiment of §3.1.

5 · Why is DTW at most the Euclidean distance for equal lengths?

The diagonal path \(i_k = j_k = k\) is admissible and its cost is the squared Euclidean distance. DTW minimizes over all admissible paths, so it can only be smaller or equal.

6 · Compute DTW between x = (0, 1, 2) and y = (0, 2).

\(D\) rows: \((0, 4)\), \((1, 1)\), \((4, 0)\). \(C\) rows: \((0, 4)\), \((1, 1)\), \((5, 1)\). So \(d_{DTW} = \sqrt 1 = 1\). There is a tie: the middle value 1 can be matched with the 0 or with the 2 of \(y\), each at cost 1.

7 · What does the exclusion zone of the matrix profile prevent?

Trivial matches: the window starting at \(n \pm 1\) overlaps the one at \(n\) almost completely, so its distance is tiny. Without the zone, every \(m[n]\) would be near 0 and every subsequence would look like a motif.

8 · Best DTW so far is 3.1. A candidate has LB_Keogh = 3.4, another 2.0. What do you do?

Skip the first: its DTW is at least 3.4, so it cannot beat 3.1. For the second the bound proves nothing; compute its DTW, with early abandoning as soon as the partial cost passes \(3.1^2\).

9 · Why does LB_Keogh need a band-constrained DTW?

The envelope at \(j\) covers the samples that \(y[j]\) is allowed to be matched with, \(|i - j| \le \lambda\). The bound is valid for DTW with that band (or a narrower one). Without a band, the envelope is \([\min \bx, \max \bx]\) everywhere and the bound is almost always 0.

10 · In CDL, what goes wrong without the constraint ‖d_k‖ ≤ 1?

Replacing \((\bd_k, \bz_k)\) by \((\alpha\bd_k, \bz_k/\alpha)\) keeps the reconstruction and divides the \(\ell_1\) penalty by \(\alpha\). Sending \(\alpha \to \infty\) drives the penalty to 0: the minimum is not attained, atoms blow up and activations vanish. The constraint fixes the scale.

11 · Compute S_0.5 of (−1.2, 0.3, 0.8).

\((-0.7,\ 0,\ 0.3)\). Values within \(\pm 0.5\) become exactly 0; the others move 0.5 towards 0.

12 · Why is CDL non-convex when each subproblem is convex?

The reconstruction \(\sum_k \bz_k * \bd_k\) is bilinear: linear in \(Z\) for fixed \(D\) (LASSO, convex) and linear in \(D\) for fixed \(Z\) (constrained least squares, convex), but it multiplies unknowns together. The family \((\alpha\bd, \bz/\alpha)\) shows the set of equivalent solutions is not convex.

13 · How does CSC with a fixed dictionary relate to pattern detection, and what does it add?

With the known templates as atoms, the activations say where each pattern occurs and with which amplitude: that is detection. Its gradient is the sliding dot product of the residual with each atom. It adds the additive model: overlapping occurrences, which no single template matches, are explained as a sum.

14 · One subsequence has a very large matrix-profile value. Meaning, and where does it return?

It has no near-copy anywhere in the series: a discord, a candidate anomaly. Lecture 5 (§3.4, distance-based anomaly detection) uses exactly this.

15 · Give a case where you should not z-normalize.

When level or amplitude carries meaning. In power monitoring, a kettle and a toaster both draw rectangular pulses; what tells them apart is the power level, which z-normalization erases. Same for diagnostic ECG amplitudes, or absolute temperature thresholds. Also be careful with nearly flat segments, which normalization turns into amplified noise.

16 · DTW is not a metric. One concrete consequence for clustering?

k-means alternates assignment and averaging, and averaging under DTW is ill-defined: the arithmetic mean of misaligned series blurs the shape, so you need heuristics like DBA. Triangle-inequality pruning is also unavailable. Metric elastic distances (MSM, TWE) avoid part of this.
