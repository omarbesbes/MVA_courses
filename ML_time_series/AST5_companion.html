Change-point and anomaly detection

Lectures 1 to 4 looked for patterns, extracted features, fitted models and cleaned the data. This lecture looks for events: the instants where the model changes, and the samples that the model does not explain. Its tools come from the earlier lectures: costs derived from maximum likelihood, dynamic programming, penalties for model selection, AR residuals and the matrix profile.

A question that comes back

Who defines the event? Slide 4 calls both problems "ill-posed", and every method answers the question with a choice made beforehand. The cost function "conditions the type of change-points that we want to detect" (slide 13). The model or the distance defines normal, so an anomaly is only a departure from it (slide 72). The penalty or the annotator decides which changes are significant (slides 42, 51). The metric decides what a good detection is (slide 88). Lecture 3's question, "how many parameters?", returns as the number of change points, and Lecture 4's, "how much should be removed?", as the detection threshold. The experiments below show each of these choices changing the answer on the same data: four costs, four segmentations; one threshold rule, from 1 to 67 anomalies; one detection, excellent or mediocre depending on the metric.

§1

Problem statement

AST5 · slides 3–6

Time series are widely used for monitoring: economy, industry, healthcare, meteorology. When recorded for hours, days or weeks, the data is likely to be redundant (slide 4). Two fundamental questions:

  • were there significant changes in my data across time?
  • was there something new or unusual in my data?

Both are ill-posed: what is a significant change? What is new or unusual?

Problem 1 · Change-point detection (slide 5)

Given a time series \(\bx\), retrieve the times \((t_1, \dots, t_K)\) where a significant change occurs. It requires estimating both the change points and their number \(K\), and it depends on the meaning given to "change".

Problem 2 · Anomaly detection (slide 6)

Given a time series \(\bx\), retrieve the set of samples \(\cT\) that correspond to unusual phenomena. They may be isolated or contiguous samples (the outliers of Lecture 4), and the answer depends on the meaning given to "usual" and "unusual".

Key idea

Both problems measure a departure from a model. Change-point detection asks where the parameters of the model change, and fits one model per segment; anomaly detection asks where the data depart from one model of normal behaviour. Which of the two you get depends on the scale: the same event is an anomaly for a detector that looks at months and a change of regime for one that looks at years.

§2

Change-point detection

AST5 · slides 7–53

A change-point detection method has three ingredients, and the slides treat them in order: a cost function that says what a homogeneous segment is (§2.3), a search method that finds the best segmentation for this cost (§2.4), and a constraint on the number of changes, either fixed or penalized (§2.5). This is the typology of Truong, Oudre and Vayatis (2020), the review behind the ruptures library. The slides' figures were made with ruptures, and the experiments below reproduce them on the same data.

2.1 · Dealing with non-stationary time series

slides 8–11

As seen in Lecture 2, stationarity (in the wide sense, for example) is a fundamental assumption: the DFT, the autocorrelation function and feature extraction all need it (slide 8, L2 §2.1). Observed for a long time, a monitored system is likely to change, smoothly or abruptly (slide 9). And in some contexts, knowing where the abrupt changes are also carries relevant information on the system. To a first approximation, three strategies bypass the problem (slide 10):

  • if the changes are smooth, treat it as a detrending task: remove the slow phenomenon and keep the seasonality, which may be more stationary (L4 §3);
  • divide the signal into small frames on which it is assumed stationary, as the spectrogram does (L2);
  • work on the derivative \(x'[n] = x[n] - x[n-1]\), which "in general has nicer stationarity properties" (slide 11). "Careful! This can imply to re-integrate the signal after processing, which can be a source of errors!"

Key idea

Differencing removes a change in level by concentrating it into a single sample. That is why it helps against slow drifts and why it hurts change-point detection: the evidence for a change comes from averaging many samples on each side, which is exactly what the costs of §2.3 do. A change point in \(x\) is an outlier in \(x'\), the first link between the two halves of the lecture.

2.2 · Problem formulation

slides 12–14

When the changes are abrupt, or when their instants are relevant in the context, use change-point detection (slide 12). Assume that \(x[n]\) undergoes abrupt changes at the times \(\cT^* = (t_1^*, \dots, t_{K^*}^*)\). The goal is to retrieve both the number of change points \(K^*\) and their times \(\cT^*\). One assumption: offline segmentation, where the whole series is available, "but can easily be adapted to online setting". With a cost function \(c(\cdot)\), the problem is (slide 13)

\[ (\hat t_1, \dots, \hat t_K) = \argmin_{(t_1, \dots, t_K)} \sum_{k=0}^{K} c\big(\bx[t_k : t_{k+1}]\big), \qquad t_0 = 0, \quad t_{K+1} = N, \]

where \(\bx[a:b] = (x[a+1], \dots, x[b])\) is the segment of length \(b - a\). A change point at \(t\) means that the regime ends at \(t\) and the next one starts at \(t+1\) (slide 14). The cost measures the homogeneity of a segment; choosing it "conditions the type of change-points that we want to detect", and it is often based on a probabilistic model of the data. The minimization is solved exactly by dynamic programming, or approximately with sliding windows.

Watch out

Indices. The slides count samples from 1 and write \(\bx[a:b] = (x[a+1], \dots, x[b])\). In Python, which counts from 0, x[a:b] is the same set of samples. ruptures returns the list \([t_1, \dots, t_K, N]\): the last entry is always \(N\), and \(t_k\) is the 0-based index of the first sample of the new regime. The experiments print a change point as the month where the new regime starts.

2.3 · Cost functions

slides 14–28

The cost function characterizes the notion of homogeneity (slide 14). The most common ones come from parametric probabilistic models: a change point is then a change in the parameters of a probability density function (Basseville and Nikiforov, 1993). Non-parametric costs can be used when no model is available.

Maximum likelihood (slide 15). Given a parametric family of densities \(f(\cdot \mid \theta)\), \(\theta \in \Theta\),

\[ c_{ML}\big(\bx[a:b]\big) = -\sup_\theta \sum_{n=a+1}^{b} \log f\big(x[n] \mid \theta\big). \]

It assumes that, on a regime, the samples are i.i.d. from one density, and it estimates the parameters of each regime by maximum likelihood. It adapts to a change in mean, in variance, or in both.

Change in mean · \(c_{L_2}\)

\(\displaystyle \sum_{n=a+1}^{b} \|x[n] - \mu_{a:b}\|_2^2\)

"The most popular is indubitably the L2 norm" (Page, 1955): \(c_{ML}\) for a Gaussian with fixed variance (slide 16). The residual sum of squares around the segment mean.

Mean and variance · \(c_\Sigma\)

\(\displaystyle (b-a)\log\sigma_{a:b}^2 + \frac{1}{\sigma^2_{a:b}}\sum_{n=a+1}^{b}\|x[n]-\mu_{a:b}\|_2^2\)

\(c_{ML}\) for a Gaussian with unknown mean and variance (slide 20). With a covariance matrix instead of \(\sigma^2\), it detects "changes of correlations between dimensions" (Lavielle, 1999).

Slope and intercept · \(c_{\text{linear}}\)

\(\displaystyle \min_{\balpha}\sum_{n=a+1}^{b}\Big(x[n] - \sum_{i=1}^{M}\alpha_i\beta_i[n]\Big)^2\)

Piecewise linear regression on covariate functions \(\beta_i[n]\): changes in trend or seasonality (Bai and Perron, 1998). For slope and intercept, \(\beta_1[n] = 1\), \(\beta_2[n] = n\) (slide 23).

Rank-based · \(c_{\text{rank}}\)

\(\displaystyle \sum_{n=a+1}^{b} \|r[n] - \mu^r_{a:b}\|_2^2\)

With \(r[n]\) the number of \(i\) such that \(x[i] \le x[n]\), the rank of \(x[n]\) in the series: "robust and invariant with respect to amplitude changes" (Lung-Yut-Fong et al., 2015; slide 26).

Beyond the slides

Where the costs come from. For \(\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) with \(\sigma\) fixed, \(-\log f(x \mid \mu) = (x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2) + \text{const}\), and the supremum over \(\mu\) is reached at the segment mean: \(c_{ML} = c_{L_2}/(2\sigma^2) + \text{const}\). With \(\sigma\) unknown, the supremum is reached at the segment variance, and the second term of \(c_\Sigma\) becomes \(\sum (x - \mu_{a:b})^2/\sigma_{a:b}^2 = b - a\): so \(c_\Sigma = (b-a)(\log\sigma_{a:b}^2 + 1)\), and only the log-variance of each segment matters. ruptures computes \((b-a)\log(\sigma^2_{a:b} + 10^{-6})\): the constant does not change the argmin, and the \(10^{-6}\) keeps a constant segment from costing \(-\infty\). For the rank cost it computes \(-(b-a)(\mu^r_{a:b})^2/\operatorname{var}(r)\), which for a fixed number of segments has the same minimizer as the slide's formula (the two differ by the constant \(\sum r^2\) and a positive factor).

Key idea

A change point is wherever that model has to be refitted. Two costs disagree whenever the data move in a way one of them ignores, so the choice of cost is the definition of "change".

Link

The rank cost is an invariance in the sense of Lecture 1: an increasing transform changes the values but not their order, so the segmentation is the same, as z-normalization makes a distance blind to offset and scale (L1 §2.1). The linear cost is Lecture 3's regression on covariates (L3 §2.2) fitted on each segment, and with sines and cosines as covariates it detects changes in seasonality (slide 23).

2.4 · Search method

slides 29–38

With the cost fixed and \(K\) known, the problem of slide 13 can be solved in two ways (slide 29): the optimal resolution with dynamic programming finds the true solution, at a cost; the approximate resolution tests for a single change point on a sliding window.

Optimal resolution (slide 30). Write \(\cV(\cT, \bx) = \sum_{k=0}^{K} c(\bx[t_k : t_{k+1}])\). Conditioning on the first change point,

\[ \min_{|\cT| = K} \cV(\cT, \bx) = \min_{1 \le t \le N-K} \Big[ c\big(\bx[0:t]\big) + \min_{|\cT| = K-1} \cV\big(\cT, \bx[t:N]\big) \Big]. \]

The best segmentation with \(K\) changes is a first segment followed by the best segmentation of the rest with \(K-1\) changes. It is a recursive problem, "just like DTW in Lecture 1", solved by dynamic programming (Bai and Perron, 2003) in two steps: compute the cumulative costs, then backtrack to the change points. Complexity \(\mathcal O(KN^2)\) (slide 31).

Algorithm 1 · Opt (slide 31)
Input   signal {y_t}, t = 1..T; cost function c(·); number of regimes K ≥ 2
for all (u, v), 1 ≤ u < v ≤ T:   C₁(u, v) ← c({y_t}, t = u..v)
for k = 2, …, K − 1:
    for all u, v with v − u ≥ k:  C_k(u, v) ← min over u + k − 1 ≤ t < v of C_{k−1}(u, t) + C₁(t + 1, v)
L ← a list of K elements; L[K] ← T; k ← K
while k > 1:                                     # backtracking
    s ← L(k)
    t* ← argmin over k − 1 ≤ t < s of C_{k−1}(1, t) + C₁(t + 1, s)
    L(k − 1) ← t*;  k ← k − 1
Output  L without T: the estimated breakpoint indexes
Watch out

Algorithm 1 counts regimes: its \(K\) is the number of segments, so it returns \(K - 1\) change points, while slides 13 and 29 call \(K\) the number of change points. It also uses inclusive, 1-based segments \(\{y_t\}_{t=u}^{v}\). Only the full table \(C_{K-1}(1, t)\) for \(t \le T\) is needed for the answer: the implementation of the experiment below, like ruptures, fills one row per number of segments and never stores \(C_k(u, v)\) for \(u \gt 1\).

Approximate resolution (slides 32–35). The complexity of the optimal resolution is prohibitive for long series. Approximate methods rely on detecting a single change point, which is far easier: slide a window of length \(2w\), and at each position decide whether there is a change. Given a window centred on sample \(n\), the discrepancy function is

\[ d[n] = c\big(\bx[n-w : n+w]\big) - c\big(\bx[n : n+w]\big) - c\big(\bx[n-w : n]\big). \]

It compares the homogeneity of the whole window with that of its two halves. If a change occurs at \(n\) and \(w\) is well adapted, both halves are homogeneous (small costs) and the whole window is not (large cost): large values of \(d[n]\) suggest a change point at \(n\) (slide 33). Compute the discrepancy curve, then search for its \(K\) largest peaks: complexity \(\mathcal O(N)\) (slide 35).

Algorithm 2 · Win (slide 35)
Input   signal {y_t}, t = 1..T; cost function c(·); half-window width w; peak search procedure PKSearch
Z ← [0, 0, …], a T-long array filled with 0                    # score list

    p ← (t − w)..t;   q ← t..(t + w);   r ← (t − w)..(t + w)
    Z[t] ← c(y_r) − [c(y_p) + c(y_q)]
L ← PKSearch(Z)                                              # peak search procedure
Output  set L of estimated breakpoint indexes
Beyond the slides

For the L2 cost, the discrepancy is a difference of means. Cutting a set of samples into two groups of sizes \(n_1, n_2\) splits its sum of squares into within-group and between-group parts, so \(c(\bx_{1 \cup 2}) - c(\bx_1) - c(\bx_2) = \frac{n_1 n_2}{n_1 + n_2}(\bar x_1 - \bar x_2)^2\). With two halves of \(w\) samples, \(d[n] = \frac{w}{2}\,(\bar x_{\text{left}} - \bar x_{\text{right}})^2\): the square of the difference between the mean of the \(w\) samples before \(n\) and the mean of the \(w\) samples after. Divided by \(\sigma^2\) it is the square of the two-sample \(z\)-statistic, which is slide 38's "other vision": a statistical test between two sets of \(w\) samples. The experiment below checks the identity to \(4 \cdot 10^{-12}\).

How to set \(w\) (slide 38). It must be smaller than the shortest regime, because the discrepancy only makes sense if the left and right halves are each homogeneous. But it must be large enough for the costs to be estimated from enough samples: seen as a statistical test between two sets of \(w\) samples, a good estimation requires a sufficient number of them.

2.5 · Finding the number of change points

slides 39–53

So far \(K\) was known. In practice it is hard to set, and the total cost \(\cV(\cT, \bx)\) always decreases when \(K\) increases (slide 39). Three solutions: heuristics testing several values of \(K\); a penalized formulation that seeks a compromise between reconstruction error and complexity; supervised approaches learned from annotated signals.

Heuristics (slide 40): compute the optimal \(\cV\) for \(K = 1, \dots, K_{\max}\) and look for an elbow on the curve. Penalized change-point detection (slides 41–42): the best \(K\) is a compromise between the sum of costs and the number of ruptures \(|\cT|\). "We actually had the same problem in various tasks": the order of an AR model, the number of atoms of a dictionary. In all cases, more parameters give a better reconstruction. It is a model selection problem:

\[ (\hat t_1, \dots, \hat t_{\hat K}) = \argmin_{(t_1, \dots, t_K),\, K} \ \sum_{k=0}^{K} c\big(\bx[t_k : t_{k+1}]\big) + \beta K. \]

The change points and their number are estimated jointly. An additional change point must decrease the sum of costs by at least \(\beta\). And with the pruning strategy below, this problem is even cheaper to solve than the one with fixed \(K\).

PELT (slides 43–45). Let \(F(t) = \min_\cT \big[\cV(\cT, \bx[0:t]) + \beta|\cT|\big]\) be the optimal penalized cost of the first \(t\) samples. Conditioning on the position \(t\) of the last change point before \(N\),

\[ F(N) = \min_{t \lt N} \Big[ F(t) + c\big(\bx[t:N]\big) + \beta \Big], \]

which costs \(\mathcal O(N)\) operations per time step, \(\mathcal O(N^2)\) in total. The idea: if a candidate \(t\) can be shown to be useless for every future time, remove it for good. Pruning criterion: if there exists \(s\) with \(t \lt s \lt N\) such that

\[ F(t) + c\big(\bx[t:s]\big) \ \ge\ F(s), \]

then \(t\) can be discarded. Why (slide 45): add \(c(\bx[s:N]) + \beta\) to both sides. Adding a change point cannot increase the sum of costs, so \(c(\bx[t:s]) + c(\bx[s:N]) \le c(\bx[t:N])\), and therefore \(F(t) + c(\bx[t:N]) + \beta \ge F(s) + c(\bx[s:N]) + \beta\): in the recursion, the term of \(t\) is never smaller than the term of \(s\), whatever happens after \(s\) (slide 44). The solution remains exact, and if the regime lengths are drawn uniformly at random, the complexity becomes \(\mathcal O(N)\) (Killick et al., 2012).

Beyond the slides

When is the pruning valid? The proof needs \(c(\bx[t:s]) + c(\bx[s:N]) \le c(\bx[t:N])\). Every \(c_{ML}\) satisfies it, because two segments can each take their own best parameter while the whole must share one: so do \(c_{L_2}\), \(c_\Sigma\) and \(c_{\text{linear}}\). The rank cost satisfies it too (a weighted mean of squares exceeds the square of the weighted mean). Killick et al. state the general case with a constant: prune when \(F(t) + c(\bx[t:s]) + C \ge F(s)\), where \(C\) makes the inequality hold. L[T] in Algorithm 3 also contains the index 0 from the first step, which is not a change point. And the \(Z[0] = -\beta\) of the first line cancels the penalty of the first segment, so that \(Z[T] = \cV + \beta K\).

How to choose \(\beta\)? There is no explicit formula between \(\beta\) and \(K\) (slide 47). Two model selection criteria estimate the relevance of a model with \(k\) parameters fitted on \(N\) samples, \(\hat L\) being the maximized likelihood (slide 48):

\[ \BIC = k\log N - 2\log\hat L \ \ (\text{Schwarz, 1978}), \qquad \AIC = 2k - 2\log\hat L \ \ (\text{Akaike, 1974}). \]

For change-point detection with the L2 cost they give (slide 49) \(\beta = 2\sigma^2\log N\) for BIC and \(\beta = 4\sigma^2\) for AIC, with \(\sigma^2\) the noise variance. Slide 50 shows the result "obtained with the BIC criterion".

Beyond the slides

Where \(2\sigma^2\log N\) comes from. With Gaussian noise of known variance, \(-2\log\hat L = c_{L_2}/\sigma^2 + \text{const}\). Each change point adds two parameters, its position and the mean of the new segment, so BIC adds \(2\log N\) per change and AIC adds 4. Multiply by \(\sigma^2\) to express the penalty in the units of \(c_{L_2}\): \(\beta = 2\sigma^2\log N\) and \(\beta = 4\sigma^2\). The derivation assumes i.i.d. noise of variance \(\sigma^2\) within each regime, and \(\sigma^2\) itself must be estimated: the experiment shows how much that choice weighs.

Supervised change-point detection (slides 51–52). \(\beta\) can also be learned from a collection of annotated signals \(\bx^{(1)}, \dots, \bx^{(M)}\) with annotations \(\cT_*^{(1)}, \dots, \cT_*^{(M)}\) (Truong et al., 2017). With \(V_\beta(\cT, \bx) = \sum_{k=0}^{K} c(\bx[t_k : t_{k+1}]) + \beta K\), the relevance of a value of \(\beta\) is measured by the excess penalized risk

\[ E\big(\bx^{(\ell)}, \beta\big) = V_\beta\big(\cT_*^{(\ell)}, \bx^{(\ell)}\big) - \min_\cT V_\beta\big(\cT, \bx^{(\ell)}\big), \]

which says how well the annotated segmentation can be approached with this \(\beta\). The function \(\beta \mapsto E(\bx^{(\ell)}, \beta)\) is convex and easily optimized:

\[ \beta_{\text{opt}} = \argmin_{\beta \gt 0} \ \frac{1}{M}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M} E\big(\bx^{(\ell)}, \beta\big). \]

"With only a few annotated examples, it is possible to find an adequate range for \(\beta\)." Slide 53 shows one signal annotated with two protocols: Protocol I marks six changes, Protocol II only three, and the learned penalty (dashed) follows each protocol.

Beyond the slides

Why convex. For each segmentation \(\cT\), \(V_\beta(\cT, \bx)\) is an affine function of \(\beta\), with slope \(|\cT|\). Their minimum over \(\cT\) is a minimum of affine functions, hence concave and piecewise linear, and it equals \(\min_K (\cV^*_K + \beta K)\), the lower envelope drawn by the staircase above. \(E\) is an affine function minus a concave one: convex, piecewise linear, non-negative, and zero exactly for the values of \(\beta\) at which the annotation is itself an optimal segmentation. The experiment computes \(E\) exactly from the optimal costs \(\cV^*_K\) of the dynamic program.

Anomaly detection

AST5 · slides 54–77

Four introductory examples raise the difficulty step by step (slides 55–58). An anomaly can be too small or too large a value, an outlier (slide 55). In a machine temperature recording, some small values are anomalies and others are not (slide 56). In the search volume for the term "puppy", the anomalies depend on the previous values: a peak is abnormal for its time of year, not for the series (slide 57). And in the airline series, the anomalies are unusual events (slide 58). Chandola et al. (2009) distinguish three forms (slide 59):

slides 55, 60 · values
Outliers

Isolated samples with exceptionally large or low values. A threshold on the values finds them.

slides 56, 60 · values in context
Bursts of outliers

Segments that do not match what is usually observed in terms of values. A local statistic or a model finds them.

slides 57–58, 72 · behaviour
Unusual events

Stretches that break the regularity of the series without any extreme value. Only a model of normal behaviour, or a comparison with the rest of the series, finds them.

The three families of methods that follow match these three forms: statistics of the values (§3.2), residuals of a model (§3.3) and distances between subsequences (§3.4). Each defines normal differently, and each is blind to what its definition leaves out.

3.1 · Outlier detection

slide 60

Simple anomalies, isolated or contiguous samples, can be detected with techniques already described in Lecture 4 (slide 60):

  • statistical methods: global, a histogram of the values to spot aberrant ones (L4 §5.1); adaptive, thresholds on sliding windows, with the mean and standard deviation or the median;
  • model-based methods: the residual or prediction error of a trend + seasonality, sinusoidal or AR model (L4 §5.2, L3 §2).

Lecture 4 detected outliers in order to remove them. Here the detection is the result: the flagged samples are what the user wants to see.

3.2 · Statistical methods

slides 61–66

Global (slides 61–63). On the histogram of the airline series, "one outlier can be considered as an anomaly", and there is "only one detected anomaly": September 2001.

Adaptive (slide 64). Use a sliding window and perform a statistical test for outlier detection. Contrary to the histogram, these methods take the local context into account, "but careful, time information is lost! Only the distribution of values is used for detection." A multitude of tests exists; the most common are

\[ \text{Mu-Sigma (Roberts, 2000):} \quad |x[n] - \mu_n| \gt \lambda\sigma_n, \qquad \text{Med-Mad (Leys et al., 2013):} \quad |x[n] - \med_n| \gt \lambda\mad_n, \]

where \(\mu_n, \sigma_n\) are the local mean and standard deviation around sample \(n\), \(\med_n, \mad_n\) the local median and median absolute deviation, and \(\lambda\) a threshold. Under an i.i.d. Gaussian assumption, \(\lambda = 1 \to 68\%\), \(\lambda = 2 \to 95\%\), \(\lambda = 3 \to 99.7\%\). Slides 65–66 use \(\lambda = 1.5\) and a window of 12 samples.

A sliding window compares a value with its neighbours' values, but not with its neighbours' times. It cannot tell a seasonal trough from a collapse, and a window that straddles a change mixes two regimes into one inflated spread. The threshold, meanwhile, fixes the false alarm rate in advance: with λ = 1.5 and the raw MAD, about a third of any Gaussian series is "anomalous".

Idea: use a time series model to detect anomalies (Yamanishi and Takeuchi, 2002; Hill and Minsker, 2010). Advantage: it truly takes the temporal aspects into account. Three steps (slide 67):

  1. choose an adequate model and learn its parameters;
  2. compute the prediction or the reconstruction of the signal;

Trend + seasonality (slides 68–69): a polynomial trend of degree 4, and cosines and sines with frequencies multiple of \(1/12\) (L3 §2.2). 3.4 · Distance-based methods

slides 72–77

Some anomalies are not characterized by aberrant values but by a new behaviour, not seen before in the series. They can only be defined as a divergence from a normal behaviour (slide 72). "This task is the dual of the task already seen in Lecture 1 (Pattern Detection/Extraction), and the same techniques can therefore be used": instead of searching for repetitive patterns, search for non-repetitive events.

Matrix profile (Yeh et al., 2016; slide 73). Given a pattern length \(L\),

\[ m[n] = \min_{i \gt n+L \text{ or } i \lt n-L} d\big(\bx[n : n+L-1],\ \bx[i : i+L-1]\big), \]

the distance from each subsequence to its nearest non-overlapping neighbour, computed efficiently with the z-normalized Euclidean distance (L1 §4.1). Small values mean the subsequence occurs elsewhere: a candidate pattern. Large values mean it is far from every other subsequence: a candidate anomaly, likely a new behaviour (slide 75). The advantage: no parametric model. The requirement: a rough idea of the scale of the anomaly, the length \(L\).

Clustering (slide 76): divide the signal into (possibly overlapping) subsequences, cluster them (k-means, spectral clustering), and flag the subsequences far from their centroid or medoid (Schmidl et al., 2022; Boniol et al., 2022).

A word on supervised approaches (slide 77)

  • Semi-supervised: use knowledge on normality. Learn a model of normal behaviour and detect anomalies in the residual or as deviations from it.   • Supervised: supervised classification techniques can also be used, such as SVM, random forests or neural networks. The benchmarks of §5 explain why this is harder than it looks: "labelled anomalies describe the ones already known, and a detector trained on them tends to miss the new ones" (slide 94).
§4

Evaluation of event detection methods

AST5 · slides 78–91

Several time series tasks are event detection tasks: pattern recognition (Lecture 1), change-point detection, anomaly detection. To benchmark methods you need an annotated dataset in which the events of interest have been highlighted, and relevant metrics. And a question the metrics must answer: what does a good detection mean (slide 79)?

4.1 · Point-based and range-based metrics

slides 80–88

When the events are single points, the standard metrics apply (slide 80):

\[ \text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}, \qquad \text{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}, \]

with \(TP\), \(FP\), \(FN\) the numbers of true positives, false positives and false negatives. Both lie between 0 and 1, and a precision/recall curve compares methods. But what if the events are ranges? Slides 81–83 show three detections that are "correct" and still flawed: one is delayed ("would not be suitable for e.g. anomaly detection in industrial monitoring"), one gets the duration badly wrong, and one detects two annotated events as a single one. Tatbul et al. (2018) list what a range-based metric can reward (slide 84):

  • existence: catching the event at all, even with a single point, may be valuable by itself;
  • size: the larger the correctly predicted portion of the event, the higher the recall;
  • position: in some cases the relative position of that portion matters too;
  • cardinality: one prediction range may be worth more than several fragments.

Formulation (slides 85–87). With predicted intervals \(P = \{P_1, \dots, P_{N_P}\}\) and real ones \(R = \{R_1, \dots, R_{N_R}\}\),

\[ \text{recall} = \frac{1}{N_R}\sum_{i=1}^{N_R}\text{recall}(R_i, P), \qquad \text{precision} = \frac{1}{N_P}\sum_{i=1}^{N_P}\text{precision}(R, P_i), \]
\[ \text{recall}(R_i, P) = \alpha\,\text{existence}(R_i, P) + (1-\alpha)\,\text{cardinality}(R_i, P) \times \text{size_position}(R_i, P), \]
\[ \text{precision}(R, P_i) = \text{cardinality}(R, P_i) \times \text{size_position}(R, P_i), \]

where existence is 1 if \(R_i\) overlaps some \(P_j\) (only used for recall), \(\text{size_position}(R_i, P) = \sum_j w(R_i, R_i \cap P_j)\) with \(w\) an overlap score between 0 and 1 against the desirable portion of \(R_i\), and cardinality is 1 if \(R_i\) overlaps at most one \(P_j\), and a penalty \(\gamma(R_i, P)\) otherwise. The functions \(w\) and \(\gamma\) may differ for precision and recall. With single-point events, \(\alpha = 0\), \(\gamma = 1\) and \(w\) the percentage of common points, these definitions reduce to the standard ones.

How to choose the parameters (slide 88). They define what a good detection is for the user. \(\alpha\) is the weight given to the mere existence of a detection. \(w\) says which part of the event matters: a uniform weight favours covering the whole event, a weight biased towards the beginning favours early detection. \(\gamma\) penalizes a fragmented detection. "The right one depends on the use case."

Beyond the slides

The functions of Tatbul et al. For a range \(R\) of length \(\ell\) and its overlap \(O\) with a prediction, \(w(R, O) = \sum_{i \in O}\delta(i) / \sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\delta(i)\), where the positional bias \(\delta(i)\), for the \(i\)-th sample of the range, is 1 (flat), \(\ell - i + 1\) (front: early samples weigh more), \(i\) (back), or rises then falls (middle). The cardinality penalty is \(\gamma = 1\) or \(\gamma = 1/x\), with \(x\) the number of distinct ranges overlapped. 4.2 · Simpler matching criteria

slide 89

The exact boundaries of an event are rarely known, and the annotation is itself imprecise: a criterion requiring an exact match is too severe (slide 89). Simpler alternatives:

  • tolerance margin: a predicted point is counted as correct if it falls within \(\pm\tau\) samples of a true event;
  • overlap criterion: a predicted interval \(P_i\) and a true interval \(R_j\) match if their Intersection over Union exceeds a threshold, \(\IoU = |P_i \cap R_j| / |P_i \cup R_j|\), with results often reported for several thresholds (25%, 50%, 75%);
  • distance to the closest event: the average temporal distance between each predicted point and the closest true event, compared to what a random detector would give.
Beyond the slides

For change points, the tolerance margin is the usual choice: ruptures.metrics.precision_recall(true, predicted, margin) counts a true change as found if an unused predicted change lies within the margin, which is how the penalty-learning experiment of §2.5 scores its test signals (±5 samples). The same module offers the Hausdorff distance, the largest distance from an estimated change to the nearest true one or the reverse, and the Rand index, the share of pairs of samples that both segmentations put in the same or in different segments.

4.3 · From a score to a detection

slides 90–91

So far the method output a set of detected events. In practice most methods output an anomaly score \(s[n]\), and a threshold turns it into events (slide 90). The measured performance then depends on two things: the quality of the score, and the choice of the threshold. To assess the score alone, summarize the performance over the whole range of thresholds:

  • AUC-ROC: the area under the curve of the true positive rate against the false positive rate;
  • AUC-PR: the area under the precision/recall curve.

When events are rare, AUC-ROC is optimistic: the false positive rate is normalized by the number of normal points, which is very large, so many false alarms barely move the curve. Precision/recall curves are therefore preferred. The range-based definitions of §4.1 can replace the standard precision and recall, which gives range-based versions of these curves.

Watch out

The big debate about event detection evaluation (slide 91). "Always check the metrics used in a paper before drawing conclusions about the performance of an algorithm: the same detector can appear excellent or mediocre depending on how it is scored." "The detection threshold should not be chosen on the test set! Reporting the best score over all only provides an upper bound." And evaluation practices still vary widely across papers, even in benchmarks, which makes results hard to compare. The experiment above shows all three: AUC-ROC against AUC-PR, the test-set threshold, and a protocol under which a random detector competes with a real one.

AST5 · slides 92–100

Two problems, two communities (slide 93)

  • Change-point detection comes from statistics: "a well-posed problem, few methods, theoretical guarantees (and most works deal with single change-point)".
  • Anomaly detection comes from data mining: "a large and fast-growing literature, and no agreed definition of what an anomaly is".

Active topics

  • Change points: high-dimensional and non-parametric settings, with kernel-based and distribution-free procedures where no model of the segments is available; robustness to outliers and to heavy-tailed noise; post-detection inference, confidence intervals and p-values for the estimated locations rather than point estimates alone.
  • Anomalies: foundation models, used as encoder, as zero-shot detector, or as interpreter of the detected anomaly; deep methods dominate the recent publications, "but simple statistical baselines sometimes still obtain the best scores (the community is deeply divided on the matter!)".
  • The problem of evaluation: metrics, benchmarks and annotated datasets have become research contributions in their own right. "How do you assess methods if your annotations and data are uncertain, questionable, unreliable?"

What the large benchmarks conclude (slide 94)

Two large-scale evaluations of anomaly detection, Schmidl et al. (2022; 71 algorithms) and Paparrizos et al. (2025):

  • simple methods remain competitive: statistical and distance-based approaches often match, and sometimes beat, deep learning methods;
  • supervision is hard to exploit: labelled anomalies describe the ones already known, and a detector trained on them tends to miss the new ones;
  • no method dominates: the ranking depends on the type of anomaly (point, contextual, collective) and on the dataset, "still the open problem of setting a definition for anomaly".

This page's experiments are a small version of the same findings: the AR(12) residual and the matrix profile both put September 2001 first, the window statistics mostly flag the calendar, and the metric can make a random detector look good.

Key idea

The lecture in one sentence: an event is a departure from a model, and every choice of the method is a choice of model. The cost says what stays constant inside a regime; the penalty, or the annotator, says which changes are worth reporting; the statistic, the model or the distance says what is normal; the metric says which detections are good. Change points are where the model has to be refitted, anomalies are where the data refuse it, and evaluation decides whether the answer was useful.

def cumsums(x):
    return np.concatenate([[0.0], np.cumsum(x)]), np.concatenate([[0.0], np.cumsum(x ** 2)])

def l2_cost(x):
    """c(x[a:b]) = sum of squares around the segment mean, O(1) per segment (a, b may be arrays)."""
    s1, s2 = cumsums(x)
    return lambda a, b: np.maximum(0, s2[b] - s2[a] - (s1[b] - s1[a]) ** 2 / (b - a))

def normal_cost(x):
    """(b - a) log(variance + 1e-6): changes in mean and variance, as ruptures' "normal"."""
    s1, s2 = cumsums(x)
    def c(a, b):
        n = b - a; m = (s1[b] - s1[a]) / n
        return n * np.log(np.maximum(0, (s2[b] - s2[a]) / n - m ** 2) + 1e-6)
    return c

rank_cost = lambda x: l2_cost(rankdata(x))    # slide 26: the L2 cost of the ranks

def opt(x, K, cost, min_size=2):
    """Exact segmentation with K change points (slides 30-31); F[k, t] = best cost of x[0:t] with k changes."""
    N, c = len(x), cost(x)
    F = np.full((K + 1, N + 1), np.inf); A = np.zeros((K + 1, N + 1), int)
    t0 = np.arange(min_size, N + 1); F[0, t0] = c(np.zeros_like(t0), t0)
    for k in range(1, K + 1):
        for t in range((k + 1) * min_size, N + 1):
            s = np.arange(k * min_size, t - min_size + 1)   # last change before t
            v = F[k - 1, s] + c(s, t)
            A[k, t], F[k, t] = s[np.argmin(v)], v.min()
    bkps, t = [N], N
    for k in range(K, 0, -1):                           # backtracking
        t = A[k, t]; bkps.insert(0, int(t))
    return bkps, F[K, N]

def pelt(x, beta, cost):
    """Penalized segmentation (slide 46), exact thanks to the pruning of slide 43."""
    N, c = len(x), cost(x)
    Z = np.zeros(N + 1); Z[0] = -beta
    last = np.zeros(N + 1, int); adm = np.array([0])
    for t in range(1, N + 1):
        v = Z[adm] + c(adm, t) + beta
        i = np.argmin(v); Z[t], last[t] = v[i], adm[i]
        adm = np.append(adm[v - beta <= Z[t]], t)          # keep s if Z[s] + c(x[s:t]) <= Z[t]
    bkps, t = [N], N
    while last[t] > 0:
        t = last[t]; bkps.insert(0, int(t))
    return bkps

from scipy.signal import argrelmax

def window(x, w, K, cost):
    """Discrepancy d[n] (slide 33) and its K largest peaks, more than w samples apart (slide 35)."""
    N, c = len(x), cost(x)
    n = np.arange(w, N - w)
    d = c(n - w, n + w) - c(n - w, n) - c(n, n + w)       # for L2: (w/2)(mean before - mean after)^2
    peaks = argrelmax(d, order=w, mode="wrap")[0]
    top = peaks[np.argsort(d[peaks], kind="stable")[::-1][:K]]
    return sorted(n[top].tolist()) + [N], d

def mu_sigma(x, W=12, lam=1.5):
    """|x - mu_n| > lam sigma_n on a centred window of W samples (slide 64)."""
    s = pd.Series(x); r = s.rolling(W, center=True)
    return np.flatnonzero((s - r.mean()).abs() > lam * r.std(ddof=0))

def med_mad(x, W=12, lam=1.5, scale=1.0):
    """|x - med_n| > lam mad_n; slide 66 uses the raw MAD (scale = 1), a Hampel filter scale = 1.4826."""
    s = pd.Series(x); r = s.rolling(W, center=True)
    mad = r.apply(lambda v: np.median(np.abs(v - np.median(v))), raw=True)
    return np.flatnonzero((s - r.median()).abs() > lam * scale * mad)

def ar_residual(x, p):
    """One-step prediction error of an AR(p) with intercept, fitted by least squares (slides 70-71)."""
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(x) - p)] + [x[p - k:len(x) - k] for k in range(1, p + 1)])
    beta = np.linalg.lstsq(X, x[p:], rcond=None)[0]
    e = np.full(len(x), np.nan); e[p:] = x[p:] - X @ beta
    return e, beta

def matrix_profile(x, L):
    """z-normalized distance of each subsequence to its nearest neighbour with |i - n| > L (slide 73)."""
    S = np.lib.stride_tricks.sliding_window_view(x, L)
    Z = (S - S.mean(1, keepdims=True)) / S.std(1, keepdims=True)
    D = np.sqrt(np.maximum(2 * L * (1 - Z @ Z.T / L), 0))   # d^2 = 2L(1 - correlation)
    i = np.arange(len(S)); D[np.abs(i[:, None] - i[None, :]) <= L] = np.inf
    return D.min(1)

from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score, average_precision_score

def range_pr(R, P, alpha=0.0, bias="flat", gamma="one"):
    """Range-based recall and precision (slides 85-87); R and P are lists of inclusive (start, end)."""
    def delta(i, l):
        return {"flat": 1, "front": l - i + 1, "back": i, "middle": i if i <= l / 2 else l - i + 1}[bias]
    def w(r, lo, hi):                                       # share of the weight of r inside [lo, hi]
        j = np.arange(r[0], r[1] + 1); d = np.array([delta(i, len(j)) for i in range(1, len(j) + 1)], float)
        return d[(j >= lo) & (j <= hi)].sum() / d.sum()
    def side(A, B, alpha):
        tot = 0.0
        for r in A:
            ov = [(max(r[0], b[0]), min(r[1], b[1])) for b in B if not (r[1] < b[0] or r[0] > b[1])]
            g = 1 / len(ov) if gamma == "reciprocal" and len(ov) > 1 else 1
            tot += alpha * (len(ov) > 0) + (1 - alpha) * g * sum(w(r, lo, hi) for lo, hi in ov)
        return tot / len(A)
    return side(R, P, alpha), side(P, R, 0.0)             # (recall, precision)

def point_adjust(truth, pred):
    """Kim et al. (2022): one flagged point inside a true event marks the whole event as detected."""
    pred = pred.copy(); edges = np.flatnonzero(np.diff(np.r_[0, truth, 0]))
    for s, e in zip(edges[::2], edges[1::2]):
        if pred[s:e].any(): pred[s:e] = True
    return pred

Check yourself

try first, then open
1 · Why are change-point and anomaly detection "ill-posed", and what makes each of them well-posed in practice?

Neither "significant change" nor "unusual" is defined by the data. A cost function (what stays constant in a regime) and a constraint on the number of changes make change-point detection a well-defined optimization; a model, a statistic or a distance of normal behaviour, plus a threshold, make anomaly detection one. Change the choice and the answer changes.

2 · September 2001 in the airline series: change point or anomaly?

Both. As a single month it is the most unusual value (9.7 robust standard deviations below the AR(12) prediction); as the start of a regime it opens seven years on a lower trend line (the slope-and-intercept segmentation with two changes cuts there and in September 2008). The answer depends on the question and on the scale.

3 · What does differencing do to a change in mean, and why does it make the change harder to detect?

It turns it into a single sample of height equal to the jump, while the noise std grows by \(\sqrt2\). In x the change is supported by all the samples on each side; in x′ by one. In the lab, a change of 1 in noise of 0.5 is found on x, but its two samples rank 37th and 72nd among the 449 values of |x′|.

4 · Why can re-integrating a processed derivative produce large errors?

Every error on one sample of x′ becomes a permanent offset on all the later samples of x: re-integration is a cumulative sum. Thresholding x′ at 2.5 std in the lab keeps three noise samples and drops both changes: the level ends 1.63 away from the truth.

5 · Write the problem of slide 13 with its conventions. What does "a change point at t" mean?

\(\argmin_{(t_1,\dots,t_K)}\sum_{k=0}^{K} c(\bx[t_k:t_{k+1}])\), \(t_0 = 0\), \(t_{K+1} = N\), \(\bx[a:b] = (x[a+1], \dots, x[b])\). The regime ends at \(t\) and the next starts at \(t+1\) (1-based). In Python and ruptures, \(t\) is the 0-based index of the first sample of the new regime, and the list of breakpoints ends with \(N\).

6 · Derive c_L2 from c_ML. What does c_Σ reduce to?

Gaussian with fixed \(\sigma\): \(-\log f = (x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2) + \text{const}\), maximized at the segment mean, so \(c_{ML} = c_{L_2}/(2\sigma^2) + \text{const}\). With unknown \(\sigma\), the second term of \(c_\Sigma\) equals \(b - a\), so \(c_\Sigma = (b-a)(\log\sigma^2_{a:b} + 1)\): only the log-variance of each segment matters.

7 · Which cost gives the same segmentation for x and log x, and why?

The rank cost: an increasing transform keeps the order of the values, so the ranks, hence the cost of every segment, are unchanged. In the lab its seven change points stay put under log x and x³, while L2 moves three of its seven.

8 · Why is the optimal search a dynamic program, and what is its complexity?

The best segmentation with \(K\) changes is a first segment followed by the best segmentation of the rest with \(K - 1\) changes (slide 30): optimal substructure, as in DTW. Filling the table of best costs for every prefix and number of segments costs \(\mathcal O(KN^2)\), instead of enumerating \(\binom{N-1}{K}\) segmentations (about \(7 \cdot 10^{16}\) for 864 months and 7 changes).

9 · For the L2 cost, what is the discrepancy d[n] of the sliding window?

The between-group sum of squares of the two halves: \(d[n] = \frac{w}{2}(\bar x_{\text{left}} - \bar x_{\text{right}})^2\). Divided by \(\sigma^2\), the square of a two-sample z-statistic: the window method is a sequence of two-sample tests.

10 · How should w be set, and what happens when it is too large?

Below the shortest regime, so that both halves are homogeneous, but large enough to estimate the costs. Too large and close changes fall inside one window: the 1981 and 1984 changes, 30 months apart, are misplaced at w = 40; and since peaks must be more than w apart, from w = 60 the window cannot even propose 7 changes.

11 · Why can't the fit alone choose K, and what does β mean?

The optimal sum of costs always decreases with K (each extra change can only help). With \(\cV + \beta K\), a change is kept only if it lowers the sum of costs by at least \(\beta\): \(\beta\) is the price of one change.

12 · State and prove PELT's pruning rule.

If \(F(t) + c(\bx[t:s]) \ge F(s)\) for some \(s \gt t\), discard \(t\). For any later end \(N\), since splitting never increases the cost, \(F(t) + c(\bx[t:N]) + \beta \ge F(t) + c(\bx[t:s]) + c(\bx[s:N]) + \beta \ge F(s) + c(\bx[s:N]) + \beta\): the candidate \(s\) is always at least as good as \(t\), so no optimal solution is lost.

13 · When does pruning save the most, and why?

When changes are frequent: each change quickly makes the older candidates useless, so the admissible set stays small. In the lab, PELT evaluates 3.2% of the unpruned costs at β = 1 (77 changes) and 31% at β = 100 (4 changes).

14 · Where does β = 2σ² log N come from, and why did it fail on the unemployment series?

BIC with Gaussian noise of known variance: \(-2\log\hat L = c_{L_2}/\sigma^2\), two parameters per change (position and mean), so \(2\log N\) per change, times \(\sigma^2\). It needs \(\sigma^2\) and white noise. The residual within regimes has a lag-1 autocorrelation of 0.87, and \(\sigma^2\) is ambiguous: 10 changes with the variance of the series, 76 with the month-to-month noise.

15 · Why do some values of K never come out of a penalized method?

\(\min_\cT \cV + \beta|\cT| = \min_K (\cV^*_K + \beta K)\): only the \(K\) on the lower convex hull of the points \((K, \cV^*_K)\) are ever optimal. On the unemployment series (five-month grid), 3, 5, 7, 11, 14 and 18 are not on the hull.

16 · Why is the excess penalized risk convex in β, and what does learning β achieve?

\(V_\beta(\cT_*)\) is affine in \(\beta\), and \(\min_\cT V_\beta(\cT)\) is a minimum of affine functions, concave: their difference is convex and piecewise linear. Minimizing its mean over annotated signals finds the price per change that best reproduces the annotations: \(\beta \approx 11\) when every change is annotated, \(\approx 94\) when only large jumps are, which BIC cannot know.

17 · Why do Mu-Sigma and Med-Mad flag mostly Januaries, Februaries and Julys in the airline series?

A 12-month window holds one full seasonal cycle, and the window statistics ignore time: the seasonal swing counts as spread and its troughs and peaks as departures (25 of 27 and 51 of 67 flags). And \(\lambda\) fixes the rate: on Gaussian noise, 14% of the samples for Mu-Sigma at 1.5, 30% for the raw Med-Mad.

18 · Why is September 2001 only 2.1 MADs from its local median?

Its centred window, March 2001 to February 2002, holds six months before the drop and six after: two regimes. The median sits between them and the MAD is inflated by the gap. An event that changes the regime hides itself from local statistics, which is why the window should be shorter than the regimes.

19 · What is the "echo" of an anomaly in an AR model, and how can it be removed?

The anomalous values are regressors of the next \(p\) predictions; with a seasonal lag of 12, they spoil the predictions a year later (December 2002, predicted from a depressed December 2001, is flagged at 4.4). Replacing flagged samples by their predictions before reusing them (detect, then interpolate) removes it: the December 2002 error falls from 7,781 to 3,620.

20 · What does the matrix profile define as normal, and what are its two requirements?

Normal is "has a close neighbour elsewhere in the series", in z-normalized shape. It needs the scale \(L\) of the anomaly (the top discord moves from November 2009 at L = 3 to September 2001 at L = 6 and 12), and the normal behaviours must repeat; an anomaly that occurs twice is a motif.

21 · Give the four principles of Tatbul et al. and the parameter that controls each.

Existence (\(\alpha\), recall only), size and position (the overlap function \(w\) with a positional bias: flat, front, middle, back), cardinality (\(\gamma\): 1 or 1/number of overlaps). With single points, \(\alpha = 0\), \(\gamma = 1\) and a flat \(w\), they reduce to the standard precision and recall.

22 · Why is AUC-ROC optimistic for rare events, and what does point adjustment do?

The false positive rate divides by the number of normal points, which is huge: 1,900 false alarms among 19,480 normal points barely move it (0.930 to 0.888) while AUC-PR collapses (0.627 to 0.164). Point adjustment counts a whole event as detected if one of its points is flagged: long events are hit by chance, and a random detector goes from an F1 of 0.03 to 0.61.

23 · You report the best F1 over all thresholds. What is wrong?

The threshold is chosen with the test labels: it is an upper bound, not a performance. Choose the threshold on training or validation data, as any other parameter of the detector (slide 91; the same leak as selecting features on the whole data set in Lecture 2).
