Models and representation learning

What it reuses

  • The autocorrelation function: its first peak gives the fundamental frequency (slide 14), and its first \(p+1\) values are all an AR model needs (slide 30; L2 §2.2).
  • The periodogram and its noise: AR coefficients give a parametric estimate of the power spectral density instead (slide 23; L2 §2.3).
  • Stationarity: assumed by AR models, restored by differencing in ARIMA, and the premise of Wold's theorem (slides 23, 36, 38; L2 §2.1).
  • Symbolization: the observations of a hidden Markov model are taken discrete (slide 44; L2 §2.4).
  • The dynamic programming of DTW: the three HMM algorithms are solved the same way, "cf Lecture 1" (slides 48, 51; L1 §2.3).
  • ISTA and projected gradient: the two steps of convolutional dictionary learning, here without convolution (slides 81, 89; L1 §4.2).
  • The LASSO of embedded feature selection is the \(\ell_1\) sparse coding problem, with features in place of atoms (slide 74; L2 §3.2).

What it prepares

  • Denoising is sparse coding: "all previously seen algorithms can be used to that aim (see Lecture 3)", then dictionary learning on the frames of the signal, L4 §2.2 (AST4 slides 23, 30–32).
  • Detrending by least-squares regression on polynomials, or by differencing, L4 §3 (AST4 slides 46–51).
  • Filling long gaps with an AR model estimated by Levinson-Durbin, L4 §4.3 (AST4 slides 65–69).
  • Choosing a number of change points is the same problem as choosing an AR order or a number of atoms, solved with penalties such as BIC and AIC, L5 §2.5 (AST5 slides 41, 48).
  • Anomalies as large residuals of a trend + seasonality or AR(12) model, or large Kalman innovations, L5 §3.3 (AST5 slides 60, 67–71; slide 60 here).
  • Vector AR models ("the sign convention is the opposite of the univariate AR(p) of Lecture 3") and multivariate dictionary learning, where "no new method is needed", L6 §3 (AST6 slides 18, 36).
A question that comes back

How many parameters? This lecture asks it four times: the order \(p\) of an AR model (§2.3), the number of harmonics or functions in a regression (§2.1–2.2), the sparsity \(K_0\) or penalty \(\lambda\) of a sparse code (§3.3), and the number of atoms \(K\) of a dictionary (§3.4). Lecture 2 asked it about features, Lecture 5 asks it about change points, Lecture 6 about VAR orders. AST5 slide 41 names the common trap: "in all cases the higher the order (and the number of parameters), the better the reconstruction". The fit error alone can never choose. A penalty on the number of parameters or a check on held-out data must, and the experiments below show both.

§1

Two problems

AST3 · slides 4–9

Two questions open the lecture. The first is Lecture 2's: how do we represent a whole series with a small number of features (slide 4)? The second is new: how can we predict future values (slide 5)? Slide 5 shows two forecasts of the same monthly industrial index. A naive one repeats the last value with an uncertainty band that fans out. The other is built on a trend + seasonality decomposition and follows the seasonal pattern. Predicting needs more than a description: it needs an idea of how the series is produced.

Problem 1 · Models (slide 6)

Find a relevant model for a time series and infer its parameters.

  • the series is represented in a condensed form, its set of parameters;
  • these parameters can be used for clustering, classification…;
  • the model itself can predict future values or reconstruct missing ones.

Problem 2 · Representation learning (slide 7)

Learn an adaptive representation for a set of time series.

  • instead of a parametric model, learn an adequate one directly from the data;
  • useful for clustering, classification…;
  • avoids having to pick an off-the-shelf model.
Key idea

A model is a hypothesis about how the series was generated. Its parameters are features that come with a meaning (a frequency, a growth rate, a memory), and because the model can generate data, it can also predict and fill gaps. The price: when the hypothesis is wrong, the parameters faithfully describe a series that does not exist. The residual check of §2.3 is how you find out.

Why models?

Slide 9 argues for them. Before predicting, classifying or extracting features, you need to understand the data, and experimental science has always done it with (mostly statistical) models. They summarize the signal in a few parameters and give an idea of its future behaviour. They "may seem outdated but when well used, some of them often compare nicely to more recent and complex approaches (yes, including deep learning!)". They are also only approximations: "in real life, most of the underlying hypotheses are not satisfied".

§2

Standard models

AST3 · slides 8–60

The models of this section differ in one respect: what they allow to explain the current sample \(x[n]\). The slides make the organizing principle explicit twice. Slide 21 moves from deterministic functions of time to "the signal itself", and slide 43 introduces "another modelling principle", an unobserved state. Everything that is not explained is noise, \(b[n]\).

Known functions of time

\(x[n] = \sum_j \alpha_j \beta_j(nT_s) + b[n]\)

Sinusoids at multiples of \(f_0\), polynomial trends, seasonal cosines. Estimation is a linear regression. §2.1, §2.2

Its own past

\(x[n] = -\sum_{i=1}^{p} a_i x[n-i] + b[n]\)

Autoregressive: a regression on the signal itself. Estimated from the autocorrelation. §2.3

Past shocks

\(x[n] = b[n] + \sum_{j=1}^{q} m_j b[n-j]\)

Moving average: the memory is in the innovations. Combined with AR in ARMA and ARIMA. §2.3

A hidden state

\(s[n] \to s[n+1]\), \(x[n] \mid s[n]\)

An unobserved state with its own dynamics drives the observations: finite (HMM) or continuous (Kalman). §2.4

2.1 · The sinusoidal model

slides 10–14

"The first physical model for periodic signals" (slide 10):

\[ x[n] = \sum_{i=1}^{N_h} a_i \sin\!\Big(2\pi (i f_0) \frac{n}{F_s} + \phi_i\Big) + b[n] \]
  • \(F_s\): sampling frequency (Hz); \(f_0\): fundamental frequency (Hz); \(i f_0\): the \(i\)-th harmonic;
  • \(a_i\), \(\phi_i\): amplitude and phase of harmonic \(i\); \(N_h\): number of harmonics;
  • \(b[n]\): white noise, uncorrelated with the deterministic part.

The signal is fully characterized by \(f_0\) and the harmonic amplitudes (slide 12). This is useful for indexing, describing and predicting purely periodic phenomena. An important feature is the vector of normalized amplitudes \(a_2/a_1, a_3/a_1, a_4/a_1, \dots\), called the timbre by analogy with musical instruments: a flute and a violin playing the same note share \(f_0\) and differ by these ratios.

Estimation (slide 13) has two stages. First \(f_0\), either from the highest peak of the spectrum or, "often more robust", from the autocorrelation: its first peak for positive lags is at the period \(m_0\) (in samples), so \(\hat f_0 = F_s/m_0\) (slide 14: \(F_s = 1000\) Hz, \(m_0 = 125\), \(\hat f_0 = 8\) Hz). Then the harmonics, either from the DFT at the multiples of \(f_0\), or by regression on the functions \(\sin(2\pi i f_0 n/F_s)\) and \(\cos(2\pi i f_0 n/F_s)\). The regression is the trend + seasonality model of §2.2 with sinusoids as the known functions. The pair \((\alpha_i^{\sin}, \alpha_i^{\cos})\) gives \(a_i = \sqrt{(\alpha_i^{\sin})^2 + (\alpha_i^{\cos})^2}\).

Watch out

The spectral peak fails in two ways that the ACF avoids. Resolution: the bins are \(F_s/N = 1\) Hz apart, so at \(f_0 = 8.4\) Hz the peak reads 8 Hz. The regression on multiples of 8 Hz drifts out of phase within the second and explains about half of the variance (\(R^2 \approx 0.52\)), against 0.996 with the ACF estimate \(1000/119 = 8.40\) Hz. Octave errors: with a weak fundamental, the highest peak is the second harmonic and the spectral estimate doubles \(f_0\). The ACF adds up the contributions of all harmonics at their common period, so its main peak stays at \(m_0\).

"More robust" is not "always better". With very heavy noise (a noise std of 3 on the default signal) the ACF peak drifts to 133 samples (7.5 Hz) while the spectral peak still reads 8 Hz. The lab takes the highest local maximum after the first zero crossing: with a weak fundamental, a small local maximum near \(m_0/2\) comes first, and a literal "first peak" rule would halve \(m_0\).

Beyond the slides

Hearing behaves like the ACF. Remove the fundamental of a harmonic sound and you still hear its pitch at \(f_0\): this is the "missing fundamental" effect, and pitch-detection algorithms for speech and music are built on autocorrelation-like functions for this reason.

2.2 · Trend + seasonality

slides 15–20

The sinusoidal model used one family of functions, \(\beta(t) = \sin(2\pi f_0 t + \phi)\). Any known functions can play this role (slide 15). Some vary slowly and are called the trend: \(\beta(t) = 1\), \(t\), \(t^2\)… Others are periodic and are called the seasonality: \(\cos(2\pi f t)\), \(\sin(2\pi f t)\)… (slide 17):

\[ x[n] = \underbrace{\alpha_1 \beta_1(nT_s) + \dots + \alpha_j \beta_j(nT_s)}_{\text{trend}} + \underbrace{\alpha_{j+1}\beta_{j+1}(nT_s) + \dots + \alpha_d \beta_d(nT_s)}_{\text{seasonality}} + b[n] \]

The functions \(\beta_1, \dots, \beta_d\) are known and the \(\alpha_j\) are real parameters: estimating the model is a regression, approximating the series by a linear combination of known functions. Stack the samples in \(\bx = (x[0], \dots, x[N-1])^\top\) and the functions in the \(N \times d\) matrix \(\bbeta\) whose column \(j\) is \(\beta_j\) sampled at \(0, T_s, \dots, (N-1)T_s\). Least squares minimizes \(\|\bx - \bbeta\balpha\|_2^2\), with the closed form (slide 18)

\[ \hat\balpha = (\bbeta^\top \bbeta)^{-1} \bbeta^\top \bx. \]

Two examples. Monthly temperature anomalies since 1880 are fitted with \(1, t, t^2, t^3\) (slide 19): a smooth, accelerating trend. Monthly US airline passengers 2016–2018 (slide 20) are fitted with a linear trend plus three harmonics of the yearly frequency \(f_0 = 1/12\) per month: \(\cos\) and \(\sin\) at \(2\pi f_0 t\), \(4\pi f_0 t\), \(6\pi f_0 t\), so \(d = 8\) functions for 36 samples.

Key idea

The regression does not care what the functions are. Sinusoids at multiples of \(f_0\) (§2.1), polynomials, seasonal cosines, any family known in advance: choosing \(\bbeta\) is the whole modelling decision, and the least-squares machinery is the same. Section 3 takes this literally: the columns of \(\bbeta\) become the atoms of a dictionary, the \(\alpha_j\) its activations.

Beyond the slides

Conditioning. With raw years \(t = 1880, \dots, 2020\), the column \(t^3\) reaches \(8 \cdot 10^9\) while the constant column is 1: \(\bbeta^\top\bbeta\) is numerically almost singular and the formula of slide 18 loses many digits. Rescale time to \([-1, 1]\) (the lab does) or use a solver based on the QR decomposition, such as np.linalg.lstsq. The fitted curve does not change, because the rescaled polynomials span the same space.

Choosing the period. The seasonal functions need the period to be known (12 months, 365 days, 24 hours). When it is not, estimate it first, as for \(f_0\) in §2.1.

Link

Lecture 4 detrends with exactly this regression (AST4 slides 46–50) and notes that "least-square regression is itself a filter": \(\bx_{\text{trend}} = \bbeta(\bbeta^\top\bbeta)^{-1}\bbeta^\top\bx\) is a projection, a linear operation on \(\bx\) (slide 51). Lecture 5 detects anomalies as samples that diverge from a trend (polynomial of degree 4) + seasonality (harmonics of 1/12) model: "not only large/small values but also temporal progression" (AST5 slides 68–69).

2.3 · Autoregressive models and their variants

slides 21–42

It makes sense to think that what we observe at \(x[n]\) is linked to the previous values \(x[n-1], \dots, x[n-p]\), and the autocorrelation function shows these links (slide 21). Hence the idea: instead of regressing on off-the-shelf functions, regress on the signal itself. This is the autoregressive (AR) model of order \(p\) (slide 22):

\[ x[n] = -\sum_{i=1}^{p} a_i\, x[n-i] + b[n] \]
  • \(p\): order of the model; \(a_1, \dots, a_p\): AR coefficients;
  • \(b[n]\): white noise, called the innovation: the part of \(x[n]\) that the past cannot predict.

Properties (slide 23). Under reasonable assumptions an AR(p) process is stationary, which the lecture assumes from here on. The coefficients can be used for indexing and prediction. The model is a physical source/filter model: a linear filter excited by white noise, very common in speech and sound processing. The coefficients also give an approximation of the power spectral density: parametric PSD estimation, the alternative to the periodogram of Lecture 2. Slide 24 shows several AR(5) processes: five coefficients are enough to produce very different textures, smooth, oscillating or bursty.

Watch out

The minus sign is a convention. With it, the model reads \(x[n] + a_1 x[n-1] + \dots + a_p x[n-p] = b[n]\): a filter with coefficients \((1, a_1, \dots, a_p)\) turns the series into white noise. Many books and libraries (statsmodels among them) write \(x[n] = \sum_i \varphi_i x[n-i] + b[n]\), so \(\varphi_i = -a_i\), and so does Lecture 6: "the sign convention is the opposite of the univariate AR(p) of Lecture 3" (AST6 slide 18). An AR(1) with \(a_1 = -0.8\) is the process \(x[n] = 0.8\,x[n-1] + b[n]\).

Beyond the slides

The reasonable assumptions and the spectrum. Write \(A(z) = 1 + a_1 z^{-1} + \dots + a_p z^{-p}\). The process is \(b[n]\) filtered by \(1/A(z)\), and it is stationary when the roots of \(A\), the poles of the filter, lie strictly inside the unit circle. Its power spectral density is then

\[ S_x(f) = \frac{\sigma_b^2}{\big|1 + \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k e^{-j2\pi f k}\big|^2}, \qquad f \in [0, 1/2] \text{ cycles per sample.} \]

A pair of poles \(r e^{\pm j\theta}\) close to the circle (\(r \to 1\)) makes the denominator small near \(f = \theta/2\pi\): a resonance, a sharp spectral peak. This is the source/filter picture of speech: the vocal tract is an all-pole filter whose resonances are the formants, excited by noise or by a pulse train. Its estimation from short frames, linear predictive coding, is exactly the Yule-Walker estimation below.

Estimating the coefficients: the Yule-Walker equations (slides 29–30)

Start from \(x[n] + a_1 x[n-1] + \dots + a_p x[n-p] = b[n]\), multiply by \(x[n-1]\) and take expectations. The innovation \(b[n]\) is white and uncorrelated with the past, so \(\Esp[b[n]x[n-1]] = \Esp[b[n]]\,\Esp[x[n-1]] = 0\). If \(x\) is wide-sense stationary, \(\Esp[x[n_1]x[n_2]] = \gamma_x[|n_1 - n_2|]\), and the equation becomes \(\gamma_x[1] + a_1\gamma_x[0] + \dots + a_p\gamma_x[p-1] = 0\). Repeating with \(x[n-2], \dots, x[n-p]\) gives the Yule-Walker equations:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} \gamma_x[0] & \gamma_x[1] & \cdots & \gamma_x[p-1] \\ \gamma_x[1] & \gamma_x[0] & \cdots & \gamma_x[p-2] \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \vdots \\ \gamma_x[p-1] & \gamma_x[p-2] & \cdots & \gamma_x[0] \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \\ \vdots \\ a_p \end{pmatrix} = - \begin{pmatrix} \gamma_x[1] \\ \gamma_x[2] \\ \vdots \\ \gamma_x[p] \end{pmatrix} \]

Knowing \(\gamma_x[m]\) for \(m = 0, \dots, p\) is enough to estimate the parameters. In practice you plug in the empirical autocorrelation of Lecture 2.

Levinson-Durbin (slides 31–32). The matrix is Toeplitz (constant along its diagonals), so the system can be solved recursively in the order. From the coefficients \(\ba^k = (a_1^k, \dots, a_k^k)\) of the order-\(k\) model, compute the new coefficient \(a_{k+1}^{k+1}\), then update the others.

Algorithm 1 · Levinson-Durbin recursion (slide 32)
Input  order p, autocorrelations γ[0], …, γ[p] (or estimates)
a¹ = ( −γ[1]/γ[0] )
for k = 1 … p−1:
    c = (γ[1], …, γ[k])ᵀ ;  c′ = (γ[k], …, γ[1])ᵀ     # c′: c reversed
    α = γ[k+1] + (aᵏ)ᵀ c′
    β = γ[0]   + (aᵏ)ᵀ c                            # prediction error of order k
    κ = −α / β                                      # the new coefficient a_{k+1}^{k+1}
    aᵏ⁺¹ = ( aᵏ + κ · (aᵏ reversed) ,  κ )          # update the others, append κ
return aᵖ                                         # O(p²) operations, not O(p³)

The order (slide 33). Run the recursion up to some \(p_{\max}\): you get the models of every order at once. If \(x\) is AR(p), then \(a_{p+1}^{p+1}\) must be 0, since the best linear prediction of \(x[n]\) only involves \(x[n-1], \dots, x[n-p]\), and the same holds beyond. This defines the partial autocorrelation function

\[ \mathrm{PACF}[m] = -a_m^m, \]

the correlation between \(x[n]\) and \(x[n-m]\) once the linear dependence on the intermediate lags \(x[n-1], \dots, x[n-m+1]\) has been removed. Slides 34–35 show it on an AR(10): the ACF "does not provide much information on the order", while the PACF is 0 for \(m \gt p\).

Key idea

The ACF says what is correlated with \(x[n]\); the PACF says what is still useful. \(x[n-2]\) is correlated with \(x[n]\) in an AR(1) simply because both are correlated with \(x[n-1]\): once \(x[n-1]\) is known, \(x[n-2]\) adds nothing. The PACF at lag \(m\) is the weight that lag \(m\) receives when it is allowed in the predictor after all closer lags, so for an AR(p) it vanishes beyond \(p\).

Moving averages, ARMA, ARIMA (slides 36–39)

The moving average model MA(q) puts the memory in the innovations instead of the observations: a stationary weighted sum of recent shocks,

\[ x[n] = b[n] + \sum_{j=1}^{q} m_j\, b[n-j]. \]

Wold's theorem (1938): every wide-sense stationary process is the sum of a deterministic signal and an MA(q) process, possibly with \(q = \infty\). ARMA(p, q) combines both, "persistence in the phenomenon and in the innovations":

\[ x[n] = -\sum_{i=1}^{p} a_i\, x[n-i] + b[n] + \sum_{j=1}^{q} m_j\, b[n-j]. \]

ARIMA(p, d, q) applies the ARMA model to \(x^{(d)}\), the series differentiated \(d\) times: "ARMA model extended to possible non-stationary processes". One difference removes a linear trend, two a quadratic one (AST4 slide 51).

Experiment

Same innovations, same parameters (slide 39)

One white noise sequence feeds four models: an AR(10), an MA(4), the ARMA(10, 4) with these AR and MA parts, and the ARIMA(10, 1, 4), which is the running sum of the ARMA.

The first three are stationary and hover around 0: the MA(4) forgets a shock after 4 samples, the AR(10) remembers it through its own past, and the ARMA does both. Integration changes the nature of the process: the ARIMA wanders away and does not come back, because every innovation shifts its level permanently. Its empirical ACF would decay very slowly, the signature of non-stationarity from Lecture 2.

Beyond the slides

AR and MA are two views of the same thing. A stationary AR(p) is an MA(\(\infty\)): unrolling the recursion gives \(x[n] = \sum_{j \ge 0} \psi_j b[n-j]\), with \(\psi_0 = 1\) and \(\psi_j = -\sum_{i=1}^{\min(j, p)} a_i \psi_{j-i}\), which is Wold's theorem at work. The forecasting experiment below uses these weights for its uncertainty band. Conversely, an invertible MA(q) is an AR(\(\infty\)). This is why the two identification tools swap roles: the ACF of an MA(q) is exactly 0 beyond lag \(q\) while its PACF only decays, and the PACF of an AR(p) is 0 beyond \(p\) while its ACF only decays.

Orders, estimation and checking: the Box-Jenkins methodology (slides 40–42)

For ARMA models, setting the orders is "not as easy as for AR models": the ACF and PACF only give an indicative idea, and theoretical results "do not translate into practical do's and don'ts". Model selection criteria such as BIC or AIC help (Lecture 5). Estimating the parameters requires ad hoc optimization, maximum likelihood or non-linear least squares (slide 40). Box and Jenkins organize the work in three steps (slide 41):

  1. Identification and selection. Is the series stationary, and after how many differentiations (this gives \(d\))? Does it contain a seasonal component? Choose the orders from the ACF and PACF, or with selection criteria.
  2. Estimation of the parameters, for instance by maximum likelihood.
  3. Checking. Does the residual have the expected properties: is it stationary, is it white noise?

The residual of an AR model is \(\hat b[n] = x[n] + \sum_{i=1}^{p} \hat a_i x[n-i]\) (slide 42). It should behave like white noise: its estimated autocorrelation should be close to a Dirac, a statistical test such as Ljung-Box can confirm it, and the residual itself should show no remaining structure (periodicity, trend, time-varying variance). In the words of slide 42: "A good model is not one whose residual is small, it is one whose residual is unstructured."

Watch out

The band is per lag. \(\pm 1.96/\sqrt N\) contains a white-noise correlation with probability 95% at each lag. With 40 lags, one or two crossings are expected by chance (35 lags beyond the order × 5% ≈ 1.75). In 40 draws of the AR(5) with N = 1000, the PACF crossed the band somewhere beyond lag 5 in 30 draws, 1.4 times on average. Read the PACF for where it drops, not for its last crossing.

The fit error cannot choose the order. Levinson-Durbin gives \(E_k = E_{k-1}(1 - (a_k^k)^2) \le E_{k-1}\): a bigger model never predicts worse on the data it was fitted on. You need a penalty or a test.

Beyond the slides

AIC and BIC (defined in AST5 slide 48). For a Gaussian AR(k) fitted to \(N\) samples, up to constants, \(\mathrm{AIC}(k) = N \ln \hat\sigma_k^2 + 2k\) and \(\mathrm{BIC}(k) = N \ln \hat\sigma_k^2 + k \ln N\), with \(\hat\sigma_k^2 = E_k\) from Levinson-Durbin. The lab plots them divided by \(N\), so all three curves share one axis. In 40 draws of each AR preset at N = 1000, BIC found the true order 32 to 39 times; AIC 20 to 31 times, overestimating it otherwise. BIC's penalty grows with \(\ln N\), which makes it consistent (it finds the true order as \(N \to \infty\)), while AIC aims at the best predictor and tolerates extra terms.

Ljung-Box. \(Q = N(N+2)\sum_{m=1}^{M} \hat\rho_m^2/(N-m)\) on the residual autocorrelations \(\hat\rho_m\), compared with a \(\chi^2\) distribution with \(M - k\) degrees of freedom. In the same 40 draws, with \(M = 25\) lags, the test on an AR(p−1) fit rejected whiteness in 83 to 100% of the draws (the AR(10) is the hard case: its tenth coefficient is small), and on the AR(p) fit in 0 to 8% of them, close to the 5% expected by chance.

Forecasting with an AR model

slides 25–28

To predict \(h\) steps ahead (slide 26): learn the AR parameters on the first \(N - h\) samples, then reconstruct the last \(h\) samples by recursivity,

\[ \hat x[n] = -\sum_{i=1}^{p} \hat a_i\, \hat x[n-i], \]

where \(\hat x[n-i]\) is the observed value when it is available and an earlier prediction otherwise. Two warnings from the slide. Each predicted value is reused to predict the next one, so the innovations accumulate and the uncertainty grows with the horizon. And beyond a few decorrelation times, the model predicts nothing more than the mean: point predictions alone are misleading for large \(h\). 2.4 · Latent-variable models: hidden Markov models

slides 43–55

So far, models relate the observations to one another: \(x[n]\) is explained by \(x[n-1], \dots, x[n-p]\) or by functions of time. Another modelling principle (slide 43): the observations are driven by an unobserved state that follows its own dynamics. Temperature and precipitation are driven by the season, which we do not observe directly. Two questions arise, both of interest: estimate the state from the observations (segmentation, tracking), and estimate the parameters of the dynamics (modelling). Two models differ only by the nature of the state: finite for the hidden Markov model, continuous for the state-space model.

The hidden Markov model (slide 44). The state \(s[n]\) takes its values in a finite alphabet \(\{1, \dots, S\}\) and is unknown; only \(x[0], \dots, x[N-1]\) are observed. For simplicity the observations are also discrete, with values in \(\{1, \dots, Q\}\) (quantization and symbolization, Lecture 2). The model is characterized by three distributions:

  • initial state \(p_i^0 = P(s[0] = i)\), a vector of length \(S\);
  • transitions \(p_{i,j} = P(s[n+1] = j \mid s[n] = i)\), an \(S \times S\) matrix;
  • emissions \(e_{i,m} = P(x[n] = m \mid s[n] = i)\), an \(S \times Q\) matrix.

Two assumptions (slide 45): the state sequence is Markov of order one, \(P(s[n+1] \mid s[n], s[n-1], \dots) = P(s[n+1] \mid s[n])\), and each observation depends only on the current state, \(P(x[n] \mid s[0:N-1], x[0:n-1]) = P(x[n] \mid s[n])\). Together they give the joint distribution, and the model is time-homogeneous (\(\bP\) and \(\bE\) do not depend on \(n\)):

\[ P(\bx, \bs) = p^0_{s[0]}\, e_{s[0], x[0]} \prod_{n=1}^{N-1} p_{s[n-1], s[n]}\, e_{s[n], x[n]}. \]

The running example (slide 47): two hidden states, Rainy and Sunny; three observations, what someone did that day: Walk, Shop, Clean.

\[ \bp^0 = \begin{pmatrix} 0.6 & 0.4 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \bP = \begin{pmatrix} 0.7 & 0.3 \\ 0.4 & 0.6 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \bE = \begin{pmatrix} 0.1 & 0.4 & 0.5 \\ 0.6 & 0.3 & 0.1 \end{pmatrix} \]

Rows: Rainy, Sunny. Columns of \(\bE\): Walk, Shop, Clean. A rainy day tends to be followed by a rainy day (0.7), and on a rainy day people clean (0.5) more than they walk (0.1).

All three are solved by dynamic programming (cf Lecture 1) with the same complexity \(O(NS^2)\); a brute-force enumeration of the state sequences would cost \(O(S^N)\).

Forward recursion (slides 49–50). Summing the joint distribution over all \(S^N\) sequences is infeasible. Introduce the forward variable \(\alpha_i[n] = P(x[0:n], s[n] = i \mid \btheta)\). The Markov assumption gives a recursion in \(n\):

\[ \alpha_j[n+1] = \Big(\sum_{i=1}^{S} \alpha_i[n]\, p_{i,j}\Big)\, e_{j, x[n+1]}, \qquad \alpha_i[0] = p_i^0\, e_{i, x[0]}, \qquad P(\bx \mid \btheta) = \sum_{i=1}^{S} \alpha_i[N-1]. \]

Read it as: reach state \(j\) from any state \(i\), then emit the observed value. "Each node aggregates all the paths that reach it." A symmetric backward variable \(\beta_i[n] = P(x[n+1:N-1] \mid s[n] = i, \btheta)\) obeys the same kind of recursion, run from the end.

Viterbi (slides 51–52). To find the most likely sequence, replace the sum by a maximum:

\[ \delta_j[n+1] = \max_i \big(\delta_i[n]\, p_{i,j}\big)\, e_{j, x[n+1]}, \qquad \delta_i[0] = p_i^0\, e_{i, x[0]}. \]

\(\delta_i[n]\) is the probability of the most likely path ending in state \(i\) at time \(n\). At each step, store the argmax, then backtrack from \(\argmax_i \delta_i[N-1]\): "only the best path reaching each node is kept". This is the operation that turns an HMM into a segmentation tool: each sample receives a state label.

Baum-Welch (slide 53). The likelihood involves the unobserved states and cannot be maximized directly. Baum-Welch is the instance of Expectation-Maximization for this model. The E step computes, with the current \(\btheta\), the posteriors from \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\):

\[ \gamma_i[n] = P(s[n] = i \mid \bx, \btheta) = \frac{\alpha_i[n]\,\beta_i[n]}{P(\bx \mid \btheta)}, \qquad \xi_{i,j}[n] = P(s[n] = i, s[n+1] = j \mid \bx, \btheta). \]

The M step re-estimates the parameters as normalized counts, where the counts are replaced by their expected values:

\[ \hat p_{i,j} = \frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-2} \xi_{i,j}[n]}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-2} \gamma_i[n]}, \qquad \hat e_{i,m} = \frac{\sum_{n:\, x[n] = m} \gamma_i[n]}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \gamma_i[n]}, \qquad \hat p_i^0 = \gamma_i[0]. \]

If the states were observed, these would be plain frequencies: how often rain follows rain, how often people walk when it rains. "EM replaces the missing counts by their expectation" (Bilmes, 1998, for the full derivation).

Beyond the slides

The missing formula: \(\xi_{i,j}[n] = \alpha_i[n]\, p_{i,j}\, e_{j, x[n+1]}\, \beta_j[n+1] / P(\bx \mid \btheta)\), a path that is in \(i\) at \(n\), jumps to \(j\) and emits \(x[n+1]\). Underflow: \(\alpha_i[n]\) is a product of about \(2n\) probabilities and falls below the smallest double after a few hundred samples. Implementations normalize \(\alpha\) at each step and add the logarithms of the normalizers, or work in the log domain for Viterbi. Local maxima: each EM iteration increases the likelihood, but only up to a local maximum, and the result depends on the starting point.

On 2000 simulated days, the learned parameters usually explain the activities slightly better than the true ones (a higher likelihood) while being visibly different from them: activities say little about the weather, so many parameter sets fit almost equally well. With 10000 days they come close to the truth. Even with the true parameters, decoding recovers the true weather on only about 78% of the days.

Beyond discrete observations (slide 55). The quantization assumption can be dropped: only the emission model changes, and the three recursions are unchanged. With Gaussian emissions, each state has a mean and a variance, \(x[n] \mid s[n] = i \sim \mathcal N(\mu_i, \sigma_i^2)\); with Gaussian mixture emissions, each state can have a multimodal law. HMMs had a major impact on speech recognition and on the probabilistic modelling of genomic sequences. Slide 54 uses one to segment a signal into quiet, oscillating and noisy parts.

2.4 (continued) · Continuous hidden states: the Kalman filter

slides 56–60

What if the hidden state is continuous (slide 56)? The state-space (or linear dynamical system) formulation has exactly the same structure as the HMM, a hidden dynamic observed through a noisy measurement; only the state space has changed:

\[ \bs[n+1] = \bA\, \bs[n] + \bu[n] \quad \text{(state equation)}, \qquad x[n] = \bc^\top \bs[n] + v[n] \quad \text{(observation equation)}, \]

with \(\bs[n] \in \R^D\), a state transition matrix \(\bA\), and independent Gaussian noises \(\bu[n]\), \(v[n]\). Many models of this lecture can be rewritten in this form, in particular AR(p) models, with \(\bs[n] = (x[n], \dots, x[n-p+1])^\top\), and trend + seasonality models.

The Kalman filter (slide 57) estimates \(\bs[n]\) from \(x[0], \dots, x[n]\). Write \(\hat\bs[n_1 \mid n_2] = \Esp[\bs[n_1] \mid x[0:n_2]]\) for the estimate at time \(n_1\) given the observations up to \(n_2\). Under Gaussian assumptions the solution is in closed form and computed recursively, in two alternating steps:

  • prediction: propagate the current estimate through the state equation, \(\hat\bs[n+1 \mid n] = \bA\, \hat\bs[n \mid n]\);
  • update: correct it with the new observation, weighted by the Kalman gain \(\bk[n+1]\):
\[ \hat\bs[n+1 \mid n+1] = \hat\bs[n+1 \mid n] + \bk[n+1]\,\big(x[n+1] - \bc^\top \hat\bs[n+1 \mid n]\big). \]

The term in parentheses is the innovation, the part of the new observation that the model did not anticipate. The gain balances the confidence in the model and the confidence in the measurement. Missing samples are handled naturally: skip the update step, and the uncertainty grows accordingly. Nonlinear models call for extensions such as particle filters.

Beyond the slides

The rest of the recursion. The filter also propagates the error covariance \(\bP[n_1 \mid n_2]\) of its estimate. With \(\bQ\) the covariance of \(\bu\) and \(r\) the variance of \(v\):

\[ \begin{gathered} \bP[n+1 \mid n] = \bA\,\bP[n \mid n]\,\bA^\top + \bQ, \qquad \bk[n+1] = \frac{\bP[n+1 \mid n]\,\bc}{\bc^\top \bP[n+1 \mid n]\,\bc + r}, \\ \bP[n+1 \mid n+1] = (\bI - \bk[n+1]\,\bc^\top)\,\bP[n+1 \mid n]. \end{gathered} \]

The denominator of the gain is the variance of the innovation. For the local level model of slide 58 (\(D = 1\), \(A = c = 1\), a random walk seen through noise), the gain settles at \(k = P/(P + r)\) with \(P = \big(q + \sqrt{q^2 + 4qr}\big)/2\): it depends only on the ratio \(q/r\) of the state noise to the measurement noise. A large ratio means the state moves a lot, so trust the measurement (\(k \to 1\)); a small one means the state barely moves, so average many measurements (\(k \to 0\)).

An AR(2) as a state-space model: \(\bs[n] = (x[n], x[n-1])^\top\), \(\bA = \begin{pmatrix} -a_1 & -a_2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\), \(\bc = (1, 0)^\top\), \(\bu[n] = (b[n+1], 0)^\top\), and \(v = 0\) (the series is observed exactly).

Representation learning

AST3 · slides 61–101

To learn from time series, you need to study them in an adequate representation space (slide 62). Lecture 2 showed that the frequency domain is especially adapted to time signals, but it is not the only one. In fact, (almost) every model of §2 is a representation, a very condensed list of parameters. Beyond off-the-shelf models, can the representation be learned directly from the series? The slide's answer: "YES!"

3.1 · Every model is a dictionary

slides 63–68

Back to the DFT (slides 63–64). A series of \(N\) samples and its DFT are two descriptions of the same object:

\[ X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\, e^{-j2\pi \frac{kn}{N}}, \qquad x[n] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} X[k]\, e^{j2\pi\frac{kn}{N}}. \]

For a real signal, with \(a_k = \mathrm{Re}\{X[k]\}/N\) and \(b_k = -\mathrm{Im}\{X[k]\}/N\), the second formula reads \(x[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} a_k \cos(2\pi kn/N) + b_k \sin(2\pi kn/N)\): the series is a weighted sum of fixed cosines and sines, and the weights are the representation. The sinusoidal model is the same sum restricted to the harmonics of \(f_0\), and the trend + seasonality model uses its functions \(\beta_j\) the same way (slide 65).

The unified framework (slides 66–67). Most of these models represent \(x\) as a linear combination of functions stored in a dictionary: \(K\) functions \(\{d_k[n]\}_{1 \le k \le K}\), called atoms, weighted by activation coefficients \(z_k\):

\[ x[n] = \sum_{k=1}^{K} z_k\, d_k[n] \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \bx = \bD \bz, \qquad \bD \in \R^{N \times K} \text{ (atoms as columns)}, \ \bz \in \R^K. \]

Representing a signal with a known dictionary is "a simple regression task" (slide 68). The real question is how to know that the representation is good. A good one uses only a small subset of the atoms: this is the notion of sparsity.

3.2 · Sparsity

slides 69–73

If the reconstruction needs more than \(N\) activations, a dictionary is not worth using: the signal itself has only \(N\) values (slide 69). Yet the most common dictionaries (Fourier basis, wavelet bases, Gabor atoms…) contain \(K \approx N\) atoms, or even \(K \gg N\): they are redundant. Slide 70 shows the Fourier dictionary with \(N = K = 128\). The rescue is that for most signals, a large number of the activations \(z_k\) are close to 0 (slide 71). Keeping only the 50 largest Fourier coefficients of a 128-sample signal reconstructs it almost perfectly (slide 72): 50 numbers instead of 128.

Finding a good representation therefore means finding a sparse activation vector, and finding a sparse \(\bz\) from a signal \(\bx\) and a dictionary \(\bD\) is called sparse coding (slide 73).

Key idea

Sparsity is a property of the pair (signal, dictionary), not of the signal alone. Beyond the slides

For an orthonormal basis, keeping the \(K_0\) largest coefficients gives the exact best \(K_0\)-term approximation: by Parseval, the squared error is the energy of the discarded coefficients, so you discard the smallest. For a redundant dictionary, the coefficients are no longer unique, the best \(K_0\) atoms are not the \(K_0\) largest correlations, and finding them exactly is a combinatorial problem (NP-hard in general). Hence the algorithms of §3.3.

3.3 · Sparse coding

slides 74–85

Two formulations (slide 74). The \(\ell_0\) version fixes the number of nonzero coefficients; the \(\ell_1\) version, the LASSO (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator), penalizes their absolute sum:

\[ \bz^* = \argmin_{\|\bz\|_0 = K_0} \|\bx - \bD\bz\|_2^2 \qquad\text{or}\qquad \bz^* = \argmin_{\bz} \|\bx - \bD\bz\|_2^2 + \lambda\|\bz\|_1. \]

The \(\ell_0\) problem is solved approximately by Iterative Hard Thresholding and by Matching Pursuit and its orthogonal variant; the \(\ell_1\) problem is convex and solved by the Iterative Soft Thresholding Algorithm.

Watch out

MP can pick the same atom twice. The residual after a projection is orthogonal to the atom just used, not to the earlier ones, so an old atom can become the best match again. The coefficient must then be accumulated, \(z_{k^*} \leftarrow z_{k^*} + \langle \br, \bd_{k^*}\rangle\), as in the box above (slide 77 writes "="), and after \(K_0\) iterations the code may have fewer than \(K_0\) nonzeros: in the lab, 40 iterations of MP give 39 distinct atoms. OMP re-projects on all selected atoms, so its residual is orthogonal to all of them and no atom returns.

Step sizes are not a detail. The update of slide 81 has no factor 2 in front of the gradient: with threshold \(\lambda\mu\), it minimizes \(\tfrac12\|\bx - \bD\bz\|_2^2 + \lambda\|\bz\|_1\), which is the objective of slide 74 with \(\lambda\) halved. Both ISTA and IHT need \(\mu \le 1/\|\bD\|_2^2\) (IHT's condition \(\|\bD\|_2 \lt 1\) is the case \(\mu = 1\)). On this dictionary, \(\|\bD\|_2^2 = 2\): IHT with \(\mu = 1\) ends with a 90% error.

Beyond the slides

Two facts about the \(\ell_1\) solution. It is exactly 0 as soon as \(\lambda \ge \lambda_{\max} = \|\bD^\top\bx\|_\infty\) (1.86 here), so useful values of \(\lambda\) are fractions of \(\lambda_{\max}\), as in Lecture 1's CDL. And soft thresholding pulls every kept coefficient towards 0 by \(\lambda\mu\) at each step: the support is often good, the amplitudes are biased. The common fix is to keep the support and refit by least squares (at \(\lambda = 0.25\) here, the error drops from 39% to 18%).

Other \(\ell_1\) solvers (slide 84): FISTA adds a Nesterov momentum step after the soft thresholding, which accelerates the convergence; ADMM introduces auxiliary variables to split the problem into a data term and a penalty term optimized separately; LARS (least angle regression) adds the atom most correlated with the residual and moves all chosen activations in their joint least-squares direction; coordinate descent updates one activation at a time in closed form.

How to choose the dictionary (slide 85)? It depends on the phenomenon to retrieve. The Fourier basis (or the DCT for real signals) matches the DFT, suits bandlimited signals, and handles sinusoidal and seasonal components. Polynomial functions handle the trend. Wavelet dictionaries (Haar, Daubechies…) handle multi-scale and localized phenomena. Lecture 4 uses all three for denoising.

3.4 · Dictionary learning

slides 86–101

Could the dictionary that gives the best representation be learned automatically (slide 86)? With a single signal the task is trivial: choose the signal itself as the dictionary. So consider a dataset of \(M\) series \(\bx_1, \dots, \bx_M\), stacked as the columns of \(\bX \in \R^{N \times M}\). The dictionary \(\bD\) is still \(N \times K\), and the activation vector becomes an activation matrix \(\bZ \in \R^{K \times M}\), one column per series (slide 87). Dictionary learning with \(\bZ\) known (slide 88):

\[ \bD^* = \argmin_{\forall k,\ \|\bd_k\|_2 \le 1} \|\bX - \bD\bZ\|_F^2. \]

The norm constraint prevents \(\bD\) from being arbitrarily large (otherwise \(\bD\) could grow while \(\bZ\) shrinks). With \(\bX\) and \(\bZ\) fixed the problem is convex, and several solvers exist: projected gradient descent (below), block coordinate descent, K-SVD, ADMM, and online dictionary learning, which processes the data on the fly (Mairal et al., 2009).

The joint problem, with both \(\bD\) and a sparse \(\bZ\) unknown, "cannot be solved as such" (slide 90): alternate between the two subproblems until the residual is small enough. The matrix \(\bX\) can be formed from a single long signal by extracting overlapping frames (slide 92). Slides 93–95 show five atoms starting from noise, the smooth atoms they become, their activations over the frames, and the sparse reconstruction of the signal.

Uses (slide 96). A common dictionary for a set of series makes the activations usable as features for clustering or classification. For classification, one can also learn a dictionary per class and assign a new series to the class whose dictionary gives the sparsest representation. The sparse representation serves for denoising and interpolation (Lecture 4), and isolating atoms gives source separation. Other constraints fit other applications: with non-negative \(\bD\) and \(\bZ\), atoms combine but never cancel, and the purely additive representation is often more interpretable (non-negative matrix factorization).

An economic example (slides 97–101): the US unemployment rate, 72 years at one sample per month, cut into 72 non-overlapping frames of 12 samples. With \(K = 3\) atoms and \(\ell_0\) sparse coding with \(K_0 = 1\), all recession periods are captured by the first atom.

Key idea

With \(K_0 = 1\), dictionary learning is a clustering. Each frame is explained by a single atom times a gain, so sparse coding assigns every frame to one atom, and the dictionary step moves each atom towards the frames assigned to it. This is k-means on the shapes of the frames, with a free gain for each frame. On the yearly frames, the clusters are "years when the rate rises", "flat years" and "years when it falls": the recession years gather in the rising atom. Beyond the slides: K-SVD, one of the solvers of slide 88, was introduced exactly as a generalization of k-means (Aharon et al., 2006).

Watch out

Frames are not shift-invariant. On the third dataset, every learned atom is the same pattern at a different position in the frame: a frame dictionary needs one atom per shift, and wastes most of its atoms describing positions. Convolutional dictionary learning (L1 §4.2) slides each atom along the signal instead, so one atom covers all positions. Lecture 6 writes the two problems side by side (AST6 slide 31).

Dead atoms. An atom that no frame selects gets a zero gradient and never changes: slide 98's third atom is never used. On the yearly frames with noise initialization, two of the three atoms die and every year uses the same one. Beyond the slides: initializing the atoms with random frames, as K-SVD and scikit-learn do, or replacing unused atoms by the worst-represented frames, avoids it.

Foundation models

The usual methodology chooses a model, estimates its parameters, and checks its assumptions and its fit (slide 104). For a couple of years, another strategy, inspired by images, video and text, has been proposed: pretrain one large model on a huge and heterogeneous corpus of time series, then apply it to a new series without any estimation (zero-shot). Examples: Chronos, TimesFM, Moirai, MOMENT, Time-MoE. There is no model selection and no parameter estimation anymore: everything relies on the capacity of the model to transfer.

Reported results are encouraging on several benchmarks, but the comparison with well-tuned classical models is still being established. "It is not ready yet: will it be soon?" Open questions: access to the training data, possible overlap between training and test sets, behaviour on multivariate and long-horizon settings, and the absence of interpretable parameters, which is what §2 was about.

Levinson-Durbin and the PACF 
import numpy as np

def acf(x, max_lag):
    """Biased autocorrelation of the centered series, lags 0..max_lag."""
    x = np.asarray(x, float) - np.mean(x)
    return np.array([x[:len(x) - m] @ x[m:] for m in range(max_lag + 1)]) / len(x)

def levinson_durbin(g, p):
    """Slides' convention x[n] = -sum_i a_i x[n-i] + b[n]. Returns a (order p), PACF[0..p], E[0..p]."""
    a, E, pacf = np.zeros(0), [g[0]], [1.0]
    for k in range(p):
        kappa = -(g[k + 1] + a @ g[k:0:-1]) / E[-1]      # new coefficient a_{k+1}^{k+1}
        a = np.concatenate([a + kappa * a[::-1], [kappa]])
        E.append(E[-1] * (1 - kappa ** 2))                # prediction error of order k+1
        pacf.append(-kappa)
    return a, np.array(pacf), np.array(E)

AIC and BIC per sample: np.log(E / E[0]) + 2 * np.arange(len(E)) / N and np.log(E / E[0]) + np.arange(len(E)) * np.log(N) / N. In statsmodels, statsmodels.tsa.stattools.pacf and AutoReg use the opposite sign for the coefficients.

Recursive AR forecast with its uncertainty band 
def ar_forecast(x, p, h):
    """Yule-Walker AR(p) on x, recursive prediction of h steps and its +-1 std band."""
    mu = np.mean(x)
    a, _, E = levinson_durbin(acf(x, p), p)
    past = list(np.asarray(x, float)[-p:] - mu)
    pred = []
    for _ in range(h):
        v = -a @ np.array(past[-1:-p - 1:-1])             # -sum_i a_i x[n-i]
        pred.append(v); past.append(v)
    psi = np.zeros(h); psi[0] = 1                         # MA(infinity) weights
    for j in range(1, h):
        q = min(j, p); psi[j] = -a[:q] @ psi[j - 1::-1][:q]
    return np.array(pred) + mu, np.sqrt(E[-1] * np.cumsum(psi ** 2))
HMM: scaled forward-backward and Viterbi 
def hmm_forward_viterbi(p0, P, E, obs):
    """Scaled forward-backward (log-likelihood, posteriors) and Viterbi path, discrete observations."""
    p0, P, E = map(np.asarray, (p0, P, E))
    N, S = len(obs), len(p0)
    alpha, c = np.zeros((N, S)), np.zeros(N)
    alpha[0] = p0 * E[:, obs[0]]; c[0] = alpha[0].sum(); alpha[0] /= c[0]
    for n in range(1, N):
        alpha[n] = (alpha[n - 1] @ P) * E[:, obs[n]]      # reach j from any i, then emit
        c[n] = alpha[n].sum(); alpha[n] /= c[n]           # normalize to avoid underflow
    beta = np.ones((N, S))
    for n in range(N - 2, -1, -1):
        beta[n] = P @ (E[:, obs[n + 1]] * beta[n + 1]) / c[n + 1]
    gamma = alpha * beta                                  # P(s[n] = i | x)
    lP, lE = np.log(P), np.log(E)
    delta, back = np.log(p0) + lE[:, obs[0]], []
    for n in range(1, N):
        scores = delta[:, None] + lP                      # scores[i, j]: best path to i, then i -> j
        back.append(scores.argmax(axis=0)); delta = scores.max(axis=0) + lE[:, obs[n]]
    path = [int(delta.argmax())]
    for b in reversed(back): path.append(int(b[path[-1]]))
    return np.log(c).sum(), gamma, path[::-1]

Kalman filter for the local level model, with missing samples 
def local_level_kalman(x, q, r, P0=1.0):
    """Kalman filter for s[n+1] = s[n] + u (var q), x[n] = s[n] + v (var r). NaN = missing sample."""
    s, P, est, var = x[0], P0, [], []
    for xn in x:
        if not np.isnan(xn):                              # update: correct with the innovation
            k = P / (P + r)
            s, P = s + k * (xn - s), (1 - k) * P
        est.append(s); var.append(P)                      # s[n|n] and its variance
        P = P + q                                         # predict: s[n+1|n] = s[n|n]
    return np.array(est), np.array(var)
Orthogonal Matching Pursuit and (F)ISTA 
def omp(x, D, K0):
    """Orthogonal Matching Pursuit; atoms (columns of D) selected by normalized correlation."""
    norms, support, r = np.linalg.norm(D, axis=0), [], x.copy()
    for _ in range(K0):
        score = np.abs(D.T @ r) / norms; score[support] = -1
        support.append(int(np.argmax(score)))
        coef, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(D[:, support], x, rcond=None)
        r = x - D[:, support] @ coef                      # residual orthogonal to all chosen atoms
    z = np.zeros(D.shape[1]); z[support] = coef
    return z

def fista(x, D, lam, n_iter=500, fast=True):
    """(F)ISTA for 1/2 ||x - Dz||^2 + lam ||z||_1 with step 1/L, L = ||D||_2^2."""
    L = np.linalg.norm(D, 2) ** 2
    z = y = np.zeros(D.shape[1]); t = 1.0
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        base = y if fast else z
        v = base + D.T @ (x - D @ base) / L               # gradient step
        z_new = np.sign(v) * np.maximum(np.abs(v) - lam / L, 0)   # soft thresholding
        if fast:                                          # Nesterov momentum
            t_new = (1 + np.sqrt(1 + 4 * t * t)) / 2
            y = z_new + (t - 1) / t_new * (z_new - z); t = t_new
        z = z_new
    return z

fast=False is plain ISTA. With scikit-learn: sklearn.linear_model.OrthogonalMatchingPursuit and Lasso (whose objective divides the data term by the number of samples, so its alpha is \(\lambda/N\)).

Dictionary learning by alternated resolution 
def learn_dictionary(X, K, K0, n_iter=30, n_pgd=30, D=None, seed=0):
    """Alternated resolution (slide 91): OMP on every column of X, then projected gradient on D."""
    if D is None:
        D = np.random.default_rng(seed).standard_normal((X.shape[0], K))
        D /= np.linalg.norm(D, axis=0)                    # noise atoms, as in slide 93
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        Z = np.column_stack([omp(x, D, K0) for x in X.T]) # sparse coding step
        G, XZ = Z @ Z.T, X @ Z.T
        mu = 1 / max(np.linalg.norm(G, 2), 1e-12)
        for _ in range(n_pgd):                            # dictionary step (slide 89)
            D = D - mu * (D @ G - XZ)                     # gradient of 1/2 ||X - DZ||_F^2
            D = D / np.maximum(np.linalg.norm(D, axis=0), 1)   # projection on ||d_k|| <= 1
    Z = np.column_stack([omp(x, D, K0) for x in X.T])
    return D, Z

1 · Why is the ACF often more robust than the highest spectral peak for estimating f₀?

All harmonics add up in the ACF at the common period \(m_0\), so a weak or missing fundamental does not move its main peak, whereas the highest spectral peak can be a harmonic (octave error). For low \(f_0\) the ACF is also finer: its resolution is one sample of period, against \(F_s/N\) Hz for the spectrum. It is not universally better: under very heavy noise its peak can drift first.

2 · Why is fitting a trend + seasonality model a linear regression, even with cosines?

The functions \(\beta_j\) are known and the model is linear in the coefficients \(\alpha_j\): only the weights are estimated, so least squares gives \(\hat\balpha = (\bbeta^\top\bbeta)^{-1}\bbeta^\top\bx\). The nonlinearity in \(t\) is inside the known functions. (Estimating the frequency itself would be nonlinear, which is why \(f_0\) is estimated first.)

3 · Adding t³ lowered the training error but raised the error on the held-out year. How?

A bigger model always fits the training data at least as well (it contains the smaller one). The cubic term uses its extra freedom to follow the noise and the local curvature, and a polynomial keeps curving after the data end, so it extrapolates badly. Only held-out data or a penalty can reveal it.

4 · With the slides' convention, what is a₁ for x[n] = 0.8 x[n−1] + b[n]? And in statsmodels?

\(a_1 = -0.8\), since the model is written \(x[n] = -a_1 x[n-1] + b[n]\). In statsmodels (and in Lecture 6's VAR) the coefficient is \(+0.8\).

5 · Derive the first Yule-Walker equation. Which assumption makes E[b[n] x[n−1]] vanish?

Multiply \(x[n] + \sum_i a_i x[n-i] = b[n]\) by \(x[n-1]\) and take expectations: \(\gamma[1] + a_1\gamma[0] + \dots + a_p\gamma[p-1] = \Esp[b[n]x[n-1]]\). The innovation is white and uncorrelated with the past of the series (\(x[n-1]\) only depends on \(b[n-1], b[n-2], \dots\)), so the right side is 0. Stationarity turns \(\Esp[x[n-i]x[n-1]]\) into \(\gamma[|i-1|]\).

6 · What does Levinson-Durbin exploit, and what does it give for free?

The Toeplitz structure of the Yule-Walker matrix: the order-\((k+1)\) solution is obtained from the order-\(k\) one in \(O(k)\), \(O(p^2)\) in total. For free: the models of every order \(1..p\), their prediction errors \(E_k\) (for AIC/BIC) and the PACF \(-a_k^k\).

7 · Why does the PACF of an AR(p) vanish beyond p, while its ACF does not?

The ACF measures any correlation, including the indirect one that propagates through the intermediate samples, so it decays slowly. The PACF at lag \(m\) is the coefficient of \(x[n-m]\) in the best order-\(m\) predictor: for an AR(p) the best predictor only uses \(p\) lags, so lags beyond \(p\) get weight 0.

8 · You fit an AR(4) to data from an AR(5). What do the residual ACF and Ljung-Box say?

The residual still contains the part of the dynamics the AR(4) cannot capture, so its ACF has values outside the band and Ljung-Box rejects whiteness (in the lab's draws, essentially every time at N = 1000). The residual may even be small; what matters is that it is structured.

9 · Why can the training prediction error not choose the order, and what do AIC and BIC add?

\(E_k\) never increases with \(k\): a bigger model fits at least as well. AIC and BIC add a price per parameter (\(2k\) and \(k\ln N\) to \(N\ln E_k\)), so extra coefficients must buy enough error reduction to be kept. BIC's price grows with \(N\) and it tends to find the true order; AIC tolerates extra terms.

10 · What do the ACF and PACF of an MA(4) look like, and why does the AR order chosen for it grow with N?

The ACF is exactly 0 beyond lag 4; the PACF decays without cutting off. An invertible MA(4) is an AR(\(\infty\)): with more data, the small far coefficients become estimable and worth their penalty, so AIC and BIC keep adding lags.

11 · In a 365-day forecast, why does the band grow, what does it tend to, and why does p = 365 help?

Each prediction reuses earlier predictions, so the unknown innovations accumulate: the variance is \(\sigma_b^2\sum_{j \lt h}\psi_j^2\), which tends to the variance of the process. With \(p = 365\) the model can use the same day one year earlier, so the forecast keeps the seasonal cycle instead of relaxing to the mean.

12 · What explains x[n] in an HMM, compared with an AR model?

In an AR model, the past observations. In an HMM, only the current hidden state: given \(s[n]\), \(x[n]\) is independent of everything else. The memory lives in the Markov chain of the states.

13 · Why is P(x | θ) computable in O(NS²)? What is α_i[n]?

\(\alpha_i[n] = P(x[0:n], s[n] = i \mid \btheta)\) summarizes all paths ending in state \(i\) at time \(n\); by the Markov property, the next step only needs these \(S\) numbers, each updated with a sum over \(S\) predecessors. Dynamic programming replaces the \(S^N\) paths by \(N\) steps of \(S^2\) operations.

14 · Why can the Viterbi path differ from the sequence of individually most likely states?

Viterbi maximizes the probability of the whole sequence; the other maximizes each marginal separately, ignoring how the choices fit together. The day-by-day sequence can have a lower joint probability, and if some transitions have zero probability it can even be impossible.

15 · What does the transition matrix do in an HMM segmentation? What happens with p_ii = 1/S?

It is the temporal prior: with \(p_{i,i}\) close to 1, changing state is expensive, so evidence is accumulated over many samples and segments are long (mean duration \(1/(1-p_{i,i})\)). With \(p_{i,i} = 1/S\) the states are independent across time: each sample is classified on its own and the labels flicker.

16 · What is the innovation in a Kalman filter, and how is it used for anomalies? What happens in a gap?

\(x[n] - \bc^\top\hat\bs[n \mid n-1]\), the part of the observation that the model did not predict. When the model holds it is white noise with a known variance, so a value beyond a few standard deviations signals an event (Lecture 5). In a gap there is no observation: skip the update, keep predicting, and the covariance grows by \(\bQ\) at each step.

17 · Why is sparse coding needed for a redundant dictionary but not for an orthonormal basis?

In an orthonormal basis the coefficients are unique (\(z_k = \langle \bx, \bd_k\rangle\)) and keeping the largest is the exact best \(K_0\)-term approximation (Parseval). With \(K \gt N\) atoms, infinitely many \(\bz\) give \(\bx = \bD\bz\); finding the sparsest is combinatorial, hence greedy (MP, OMP), thresholding (IHT) or convex (\(\ell_1\), ISTA) methods.

18 · Two differences between MP and OMP. Why can MP select an atom twice?

OMP refits all selected coefficients by least squares at every step, MP only updates the new one; OMP never re-selects, MP can. MP's residual is orthogonal to the last atom only, so an earlier atom can again become the best match (its coefficient must then be accumulated).

19 · Which objective does the ISTA update of slide 81 minimize exactly? Why is its solution biased?

\(\tfrac12\|\bx - \bD\bz\|_2^2 + \lambda\|\bz\|_1\): the gradient step uses \(\bD^\top(\bD\bz - \bx)\), the gradient of the half squared norm, with threshold \(\lambda\mu\). Soft thresholding shrinks every kept coefficient by \(\lambda\mu\), so amplitudes are pulled towards 0; refitting by least squares on the support removes the bias.

20 · Why constrain the atoms' norms? And what goes wrong with frames when a pattern can appear anywhere?

Without the constraint, \((\alpha\bd_k, \bz_k/\alpha)\) gives the same reconstruction for any \(\alpha\), so with an \(\ell_1\) penalty the atoms would grow and the codes shrink without limit. With frames, a pattern at a different position in the window is a different vector, so the dictionary learns one atom per shift; convolutional dictionary learning (Lecture 1) removes this by sliding atoms.

21 · What does dictionary learning with K₀ = 1 amount to?

A clustering of the frames by shape (k-means with a free gain per frame): each frame is assigned to the one atom that explains it best, and each atom is updated towards its frames. On the unemployment example, one cluster collects the recession years.
