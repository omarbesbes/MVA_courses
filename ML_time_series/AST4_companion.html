Data enhancement and preprocessing

Lectures 1 to 3 assumed clean data. This lecture cleans it, with the tools of those three lectures: spectra, sparse codes, dictionaries, regressions and AR models, now used to separate what is signal from what is not.

A question that comes back

How much should be removed? Every method here has a knob: a cutoff frequency, \(K_0\) or \(\lambda\), a number of singular values, a polynomial degree, a detection threshold. Too little and the perturbation stays; too much and signal goes with it. Slide 25 draws both errors: the distance to the noisy signal, which only decreases, and the distance to the clean signal, which has a minimum. But the clean signal is exactly what you do not have. This is Lecture 3's warning, "the fit error alone can never choose", in a harsher form, because preprocessing "is almost never evaluated" (slide 91). The experiments below show four ways out: calibrate on pure noise (slide 29), estimate the error without the clean signal (SURE, slide 26), corrupt a clean signal on purpose (slide 90), and judge the whole pipeline on a validation set (slide 90).

Problem statement

AST4 · slides 3–11

The typical use case is a noisy time series with outliers and missing values (slide 4). To apply ML algorithms, the data scientist first needs to clean and consolidate the data: a time-consuming and tedious task, for which ML also provides tools. "Careful! All these preprocessings have a strong impact on the expected results and on the future learned rules!"

Slides 5 to 11 take one recording, an ECG during general anesthesia, and point out its problems one at a time. The experiment below rebuilds it with a synthetic ECG and lets you switch each problem on and off.

The four problems of this lecture differ in what is known about the perturbation. Noise touches every sample and has a known statistical model. A trend also touches every sample but has no model, only a scale. Missing samples come with their positions. Outliers come with nothing, which is why they are the hardest: they need a detection step, and then they become missing samples (slide 73).

Key idea

A perturbation can only be removed if the clean signal is assumed to be different from it. Noise is white, so a signal that is band-limited, sparse, low rank, smooth or predictable can be separated from it. When the assumption is wrong, the method removes signal as confidently as it removes noise, and nothing in the output warns you.

Watch out

Three sentences of the slides apply to every section below. "All these preprocessings have a strong impact on the expected results and on the future learned rules" (slide 4). "What is removed must be examined, not only what is kept" (slide 45). "Reconstructed samples are not data: they should be flagged, and should not be counted in the sample size of a statistical test" (slide 56).

§2

Denoising

AST4 · slides 12–42

The most common model for a noisy signal is (slide 13)

\[ y[n] = x[n] + b[n] \]
  • \(x[n]\) is the clean, unknown signal; \(y[n]\) is the measured signal;
  • \(b[n]\) is the measurement noise, assumed additive, white and Gaussian (AWGN), and uncorrelated with \(x[n]\).

Each word of AWGN is an assumption (slide 14). Additive: the noise corrupts all the samples. White: a zero-mean stationary process whose samples are pairwise uncorrelated, \(\gamma_b[m] = \sigma^2\) if \(m = 0\) and 0 otherwise. Gaussian: the samples are i.i.d., \(b[n] \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)\). Denoising: given a noisy signal \(y[n]\) corrupted by AWGN, retrieve the clean signal \(x[n]\).

The running example (slide 15) is one day of electricity consumption, one sample per minute (\(N = 1440\)): the axis of slide 17 stops at \(\pm 8.3\) mHz, the Nyquist frequency of \(F_s = 1/60\) Hz. The experiments below use a synthetic day of the same shape, with the slide's noise level \(\sigma^2 = 0.01\).

2.1 · Filtering

slides 16–20

The first solution uses results from signal processing and statistics (slide 16). Since \(x\) and \(b\) are uncorrelated and \(b\) has zero mean, the cross terms of \(\gamma_y[m] = \Esp[y[n]y[n+m]]\) vanish:

\[ \gamma_y[m] = \gamma_x[m] + \gamma_b[m] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Esp\big[|Y[k]|^2\big] = \Esp\big[|X[k]|^2\big] + N\sigma^2. \]

Adding AWGN is adding a constant to the power spectral density, in linear scale. With the periodogram of Lecture 2, in the band where only noise is present (slide 17):

\[ 10\log_{10}\frac{|Y[k]|^2}{N} = 10\log_{10}\Big(\frac{|X[k]|^2}{N} + \sigma^2\Big) \approx 10\log_{10}(0.01) = -20 \text{ dB}. \]

Plot the log-spectrum of the noisy signal, draw the line \(10\log_{10}\sigma^2\), and the frequencies where the spectrum lies on that line only contain noise: on slide 18, everything above about 0.001 Hz. From there on it is filter design, "out of scope for this course" (slide 19). Two popular solutions:

  • the moving average of length \(L\), \(\hat x[n] = \frac1L\sum_{k=0}^{L-1} y[n-k]\), a low-pass filter with cutoff \(f_c \approx 0.442947\, F_s / \sqrt{L^2 - 1}\);
  • Butterworth filters, low-pass, band-pass, etc. Slide 20 uses a low-pass of order 4 with \(f_c = 0.001\) Hz.

Key idea

Because noise adds a constant to the spectrum, the spectrum shows where the noise is alone. Above the frequency where the noisy spectrum meets the floor \(\sigma^2\), keeping a frequency keeps nothing but noise; below it, the signal dominates. The cutoff is read, not guessed, but only because the noise is white and the signal is band-limited.

Beyond the slides

Zero phase. Any causal filter delays the signal. The lab runs the Butterworth filter forward then backward (scipy.signal.filtfilt), which cancels the delay and squares the gain, so the effective order is 8.

The Wiener filter. With \(P_y = P_x + \sigma^2\), the linear filter with the smallest mean squared error multiplies each frequency by \(P_x/(P_x + \sigma^2) = 1 - \sigma^2/P_y\). It is a soft version of the cutoff: frequencies far above the floor pass, frequencies at the floor are removed, those in between are attenuated. The same idea applied to coefficients instead of frequencies is the soft thresholding of §2.2.

2.2 · Sparse approximations

slides 21–29

Filtering picks the frequencies to keep, the same ones for every signal (slide 21). Instead of designing a filter, we can look for a sparse frequency representation of the signal, which amounts to removing the small values of the spectrum. The assumption:

\[ \text{large Fourier coefficients} \to \text{signal}, \qquad \text{small Fourier coefficients} \to \text{noise}. \]

It is the principle of data compression: threshold the small values in an appropriate representation space, \(\hat\bx = \sum_{k \in \cK} z_k \bd_k\) with \(|\cK| \lt N\). Several dictionaries can be used (slide 22; Rubinstein et al., 2010):

  • the Fourier dictionary, in practice the discrete cosine transform for real signals: \(d_0[n] = 1/\sqrt N\) and \(d_k[n] = \sqrt{2/N}\cos\big(\frac\pi N (n + \frac12) k\big)\) for \(1 \le k \le N-1\);
  • wavelets, with a wavelet function \(\psi\) and a scaling function \(\phi\): \(\psi_{j,l}[n] = 2^{-j/2}\psi(2^{-j}n - l)\), \(\phi_{j,l}[n] = 2^{-j/2}\phi(2^{-j}n - l)\). Up to level \(j_{\max}\), the dictionary holds \(N 2^{-j}\) wavelets at each level \(1 \le j \le j_{\max}\) (the details) and \(N 2^{-j_{\max}}\) scaling functions (the approximation);
  • Gabor atoms, the modified discrete cosine transform (MDCT)…

With the dictionary \(\bD\) fixed, denoising is a sparse coding task, and all the algorithms of Lecture 3 apply (slide 23). The \(\ell_0\) version keeps only the \(K_0\) largest coefficients (hard thresholding), \(\bz^* = \argmin_{\|\bz\|_0 = K_0}\|\by - \bD\bz\|_2^2\). The \(\ell_1\) version, \(\bz^* = \argmin_{\bz}\|\by - \bD\bz\|_2^2 + \lambda\|\bz\|_1\), sets to zero the coefficients below a threshold and shrinks the others (soft thresholding), \(S_\lambda(z) = \sgn(z)\max(|z| - \lambda, 0)\). Slide 24 denoises the day with matching pursuit, a Fourier dictionary and \(K_0 = 40\); slide 25 plots the two distances as \(K_0\) grows.

How to set \(K_0\) or \(\lambda\) (slide 26)? It depends on the dictionary (in particular on the orthogonality of its atoms) and on the algorithm. Heuristics: use a training set; take \(\lambda\) as a percentage of \(\lambda_{\max} = \|\bD^\top\by\|_\infty\); find an elbow in the distribution of the activations; or, with an over-redundant dictionary, split it into small dictionaries and average their decompositions. Statistics, which often require a probabilistic model of the data or the noise: estimators such as Stein's Unbiased Risk Estimate (SURE) or minimax criteria give the range of relevant parameters, and model selection can be used (Lecture 5, Tutorial session 3).

When to stop matching pursuit (slides 27–29)? Stop once the atoms have captured all the relevant information, when the residual is pure noise. The tool is the normalized coherence between a signal and the dictionary, \(\rho_\bD(\by) = \max_{\bd \in \bD} |\langle \by, \bd\rangle| / \|\by\|_2\). With \(\br^{(\ell)} = \br^{(\ell-1)} - \langle \br^{(\ell-1)}, \bd^*\rangle \bd^*\),

\[ \frac{\|\br^{(\ell)}\|_2^2}{\|\br^{(\ell-1)}\|_2^2} = 1 - \rho_\bD^2\big(\br^{(\ell-1)}\big), \]

so the residual shrinks fast while the coherence is large, and it is worth continuing; when the coherence becomes too small, the algorithm can stop. "Too small" means: of the order of \(\rho_{p(\br)}(\bD) = \Esp_{p(\br)}[\rho_\bD(\br)]\), the coherence of pure noise with the dictionary, computed on a training set of noise signals or from the distribution of the noise.

Watch out

A factor of two. In an orthonormal basis, the minimizer of slide 23's \(\|\by - \bD\bz\|_2^2 + \lambda\|\bz\|_1\) is \(S_{\lambda/2}(\bD^\top\by)\): the threshold is \(\lambda/2\), not \(\lambda\). The threshold equals \(\lambda\) with the convention \(\frac12\|\by - \bD\bz\|_2^2\), which is also the one where \(\lambda_{\max} = \|\bD^\top\by\|_\infty\) (slide 26) and the one of Lecture 3's ISTA.

Soft is not better than hard here. On this day, soft thresholding never beats hard thresholding: its best error is 1.73, against 1.47 for hard thresholding at \(K_0 = 38\). Soft thresholding also shrinks the few large coefficients that carry the daily shape, and to compensate the best threshold keeps 262 coefficients, most of them noise.

Key idea

White noise stays white in every orthonormal basis: \(\bD^\top\bb\) is again \(\mathcal N(0, \sigma^2\bI)\), spread evenly over the \(N\) coefficients. The signal, in a well-chosen basis, piles up in a few. Thresholding works on this contrast, and the coherence rule measures it: while the residual is more coherent with the dictionary than noise would be, there is signal left to take.

Beyond the slides

SURE. For soft thresholding at \(t\) in an orthonormal basis with known \(\sigma\), \(\SURE(t) = -N\sigma^2 + \sum_k \min(z_k^2, t^2) + 2\sigma^2\,\#\{k : |z_k| \gt t\}\) is an unbiased estimate of \(\|\hat\bx - \bx\|_2^2\), computed from the noisy coefficients alone (Stein, 1981; Donoho and Johnstone's SureShrink, 1995). The green curve of the lab tracks the true error it cannot see.

The universal threshold \(t = \sigma\sqrt{2\ln N}\) (Donoho and Johnstone, 1994, the reference paper of slide 92) is the level that the largest of \(N\) Gaussian noise coefficients almost never exceeds. It is the coherence rule in another form: \(\sqrt{2\ln N / N} = 0.10\) for \(N = 1440\), close to the measured noise coherence of 0.092. With hard thresholding it keeps 30 DCT coefficients, as the coherence rule does; with soft thresholding it shrinks too much (error 2.62). When \(\sigma\) is unknown, the usual estimate is the median of the absolute finest-scale wavelet coefficients divided by 0.6745.

2.2 (continued) · Learned dictionaries

slides 30–34

Instead of off-the-shelf dictionaries, the representation can be learned from the signal itself: dictionary learning, as in Lecture 3 (slide 30). The input is the trajectory matrix \(\bX\), the frames of the signal side by side (slide 31): with a window of \(N_w\) samples and an overlap of \(N_o\), it has \(N_w\) rows and \(N_f = \lfloor \frac{N - N_w}{N_w - N_o}\rfloor + 1\) columns. The algorithms of Lecture 3 approximate it (slide 32):

\[ \hat\bX = \bD\bZ \approx \bX, \qquad \bD \in \R^{N_w \times K}, \quad \bZ \in \R^{K \times N_f} \text{ sparse (level } K_0 \text{ or } \lambda). \]

Each frame is a sparse linear combination of the learned atoms. The denoised signal is read back by unfolding the matrix and averaging along the overlapping frames (slide 33). The argument: "noise is random: when sparsity is enforced, the approximation tends to only model signal". Slide 34 uses \(K = 5\) atoms, \(K_0 = 2\) per frame, \(N_w = 32\), \(N_o = 28\).

2.3 · Low-rank approximations: singular spectrum analysis

slides 35–41

With the largest overlap, \(N_o = N_w - 1\), the trajectory matrix has \(N_f = N - N_w + 1\) columns and a particular form (slide 35): it contains all the \(N_f\) sub-sequences of length \(N_w\), and is constant along its anti-diagonals (a Hankel matrix).

\[ \bX = \begin{pmatrix} x[0] & \cdots & x[N-N_w] \\ x[1] & \cdots & x[N-N_w+1] \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ x[N_w-1] & \cdots & x[N-1] \end{pmatrix} \]

Low-rank approximations rebuild \(\bX\) as a sum of \(K \lt \min(N_w, N_f)\) rank-one matrices. With \(N_w \lt N_f\), the singular value decomposition writes \(\bX = \bU\bSigma\bV^\top\), with \(\bU\) (\(N_w \times N_w\)) and \(\bV\) (\(N_f \times N_f\)) orthogonal and \(\bSigma\) holding at most \(N_w\) singular values \(\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_{N_w}\) on its diagonal (slide 36):

\[ \bX = \sum_{k=1}^{N_w} \lambda_k \bu_k \bv_k^\top. \]

What a singular value means (slide 37). For a zero-mean stationary signal, \(\bC_\bX = \frac{1}{N_f}\bX\bX^\top\) estimates the lag-covariance matrix for lag \(N_w\). It is positive semi-definite, with eigendecomposition \(\bC_\bX = \tilde\bU\tilde\bSigma\tilde\bU^\top\), and \(\tilde\lambda_k\) is the contribution of the \(k\)-th eigenvector to the global variance: this is PCA of the windows (Lecture 2). Since \(\bX\bX^\top = \bU\bSigma^2\bU^\top\), \(\lambda_k = \sqrt{N_f\tilde\lambda_k} \propto \sqrt{\tilde\lambda_k}\).

Singular spectrum analysis (SSA; Vautard et al., 1992; slide 38): (1) compute the SVD of the trajectory matrix; (2) by analyzing the singular values, form groups \(\cK_1, \dots, \cK_M\) that correspond to similar phenomena, \(\bX \approx \sum_{k \in \cK_1}\lambda_k\bu_k\bv_k^\top + \dots + \sum_{k \in \cK_M}\lambda_k\bu_k\bv_k^\top\). Each group, averaged along the anti-diagonals, gives a component of the series. For denoising (slide 39): at a reasonable signal-to-noise ratio the signal dominates, so it holds the largest singular values; remove the components with small ones. The only parameter is \(N_w\): the longest periodicity SSA can capture. Slide 40 (\(N_w = 32\)): "the two first singular values are likely to be signal"; slide 41 keeps them.

Key idea

Over a short window, a smooth signal is almost a combination of a few fixed shapes, so its windows span a few directions: the trajectory matrix is nearly low rank. White noise spreads evenly over every direction, with singular values near \(\sigma\sqrt{N_f}\). Keeping the directions that stand above this floor keeps the signal. It is thresholding again, applied to the singular values of a matrix built from the signal instead of to coefficients in a fixed basis.

Link

SSA and the dictionary learning of the previous experiment approximate the same matrix of frames. SSA finds \(K\) orthogonal directions with one SVD, and every frame uses all of them. Dictionary learning finds \(K\) atoms by alternating, and each frame uses only \(K_0\) of them. Lecture 3 read the same matrix differently: the AR model regresses each sample on the rows of this Hankel matrix (L3 §3.1).

Beyond the slides

Why "spectrum". For a stationary signal and a long window, the eigenvectors of the lag-covariance matrix tend to sinusoids and its eigenvalues to samples of the power spectral density: the singular spectrum is a data-adapted cousin of the Fourier spectrum. A sinusoid needs a sine and a cosine, hence the pair of nearly equal singular values in the lab.

Where to cut. Mini-project 4.3 (Gavish and Donoho, 2014) replaces the elbow by an optimal hard threshold on singular values, \(4/\sqrt3\,\sqrt{N}\sigma\) for a square \(N \times N\) matrix with known noise level. Their theory assumes independent noise entries. In a trajectory matrix the same noise sample appears along a whole anti-diagonal, so there the constant is only a guide.

§3

Detrending

AST4 · slides 43–51

The trend + seasonality model of Lecture 3 (slide 44) splits the known functions into two groups:

\[ x[n] = \underbrace{\alpha_1\beta_1(nT_s) + \dots + \alpha_j\beta_j(nT_s)}_{x^{\text{trend}}[n]} + \underbrace{\alpha_{j+1}\beta_{j+1}(nT_s) + \dots + \alpha_d\beta_d(nT_s)}_{x^{\text{seasonality}}[n]} + b[n] \]

The seasonality is a pseudo-periodic component. The trend is made of smooth variations, a systematic increase or decrease in the data. Detrending: given a signal \(x[n]\), estimate and remove the trend component \(x^{\text{trend}}[n]\).

An ill-posed decomposition (slide 45)

The decomposition \(x = x^{\text{trend}} + x^{\text{seasonality}} + b\) is not identifiable: any slowly varying part of the residual can be moved into the trend, and conversely. What separates them is a convention on what counts as slow, and "this convention is a modelling choice, not a property of the data". Unlike noise, which is defined by a statistical property, a trend is only defined by a scale. Two consequences: there is no universally correct detrending, only one adapted to the question asked; and what is removed must be examined, not only what is kept.

3.1 · Least-squares regression

slides 46–50

The most common trend models are a constant, \(x^{\text{trend}}[n] = \alpha_0\); a linear trend, \(\alpha_1 (nT_s) + \alpha_0\); and a polynomial trend, \(\sum_{k=0}^{K}\alpha_k (nT_s)^k\) (slide 46). The least-squares estimator minimizes \(\|\bx - \bbeta\balpha\|_2^2\), with one column of \(\bbeta\) per function sampled at \(0, T_s, \dots, (N-1)T_s\) (slide 47):

\[ \hat\balpha = (\bbeta^\top\bbeta)^{-1}\bbeta^\top\bx, \qquad \bx^{\text{trend}} = \bbeta\hat\balpha. \]

Slides 48–50 detrend a 100 s oscillating signal with a regression on polynomials of order 3: the estimated trend is a concave curve from about 0.08 to −0.8, and the detrended signal lies on the signal without trend. The experiment below rebuilds that example.

3.2 · Other approaches

slide 51
  • Differencing: a trend disappears when taking successive differences \(x[n] - x[n-1]\). One difference removes a linear trend, two a quadratic one (Lecture 3's ARIMA). There is no model and no parameter to estimate, "but the operation amplifies the high frequencies, hence the noise".
  • Filtering: trends often correspond to low frequencies or smooth components, so low-pass or band-pass filters, Fourier or wavelet thresholding can estimate them. Least-squares regression is itself a filter: \(\bx^{\text{trend}} = \bbeta(\bbeta^\top\bbeta)^{-1}\bbeta^\top\bx\) is a projection, hence a linear operation on \(\bx\).
  • Decomposition techniques, since the trend may be considered independent of the seasonality or the noise: EMD, SSA, ICA… (§2.3–2.4).

Interpolation of missing samples

AST4 · slides 52–70

Given a signal \(\bx\) and a set of missing samples \(\cT\), estimate the missing samples \(\hat\bx_\cT\) (slide 53). Missing data are very frequent: sensor malfunctions, clipping, corrupted samples (slide 54). They take two forms. Isolated samples are easy to handle. Contiguous samples, up to about 100, need a full reconstruction. Interpolation includes prediction and inpainting (Lepot et al., 2017).

Is it always useful to interpolate? (slide 56)

It is a complex question. It depends on the percentage of missing samples, on their positions with respect to each other, and on the mechanism that generated them. Reconstructed samples are not data: they should be flagged, and not counted in the sample size of a statistical test. To use interpolated samples in the best possible way, one idea is to model the missingness mechanism itself inside the global time series model, but it is very complex (mini-project 4.8 does it inside a recurrent network).

Key idea

Interpolation methods fix values, not biases. If the probability of being missing depends on the value itself, the information is not in the data, and no method in this section can bring it back. Under MAR it is in the data, but only a method that conditions on the right observed variable uses it.

4.1 · Polynomial interpolation

slides 57–59

To interpolate on the integer set \(\cT = [\![ n_{\text{start}}, n_{\text{end}} ]\!]\), the easiest strategy is a polynomial model (slide 57):

  • constant value: \(\hat x[n] = \frac12\big(x[n_{\text{start}} - 1] + x[n_{\text{end}} + 1]\big)\);
  • linear interpolation: \(\hat x[n] = \beta_1 n + \beta_0\), through \(x[n_{\text{start}} - 1]\) and \(x[n_{\text{end}} + 1]\);
  • cubic (spline) interpolation (McKinley et al., 1998): \(\hat x[n] = \beta_3 n^3 + \beta_2 n^2 + \beta_1 n + \beta_0\), with the \(\beta_k\) obtained from \(x[n_{\text{start}} - 2]\), \(x[n_{\text{start}} - 1]\), \(x[n_{\text{end}} + 1]\) and \(x[n_{\text{end}} + 2]\).

Pros and cons (slide 59): easy to implement, and good results for small segments; when only a few samples are missing, constant or linear values are often the best. When the degree increases, instabilities may occur, because the polynomial depends strongly on the neighbouring samples. Used extensively, polynomials smooth the signal, which changes its spectrum by boosting the low frequencies.

4.2 · Low-rank interpolation

slides 60–64

The low-rank assumption on the trajectory matrix of §2.3 can also reconstruct missing samples (slide 60), with an SVD adapted to data with missing values (Srebro and Jaakkola, 2003). It is efficient for medium-size missing patches, as the low-rank assumption usually only holds for relatively small windows. The idea (slide 61): compute a low-rank approximation \(\hat\bX = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_k\bu_k\bv_k^\top\), \(K \lt \min(N_w, N_f)\), and use it only to update the missing entries, marked by the mask \(W_{i,j} = 0\) if \(X_{i,j}\) is missing and 1 otherwise.

Algorithm 1 · Low-rank interpolation (slide 62)
Input   trajectory matrix X with missing values, mask W, expected rank K

Pros and cons (slide 64): good results for medium-size segments. Always set \(N_w\) greater than the largest missing patch, but when \(N_w\) becomes too large the low-rank approximation becomes less valid. A variant with an adaptive rank, \(K_{n_{\text{iter}}} = \max(\min(N_w, N_f) - n_{\text{iter}}, K)\), can give better results. The rank \(K\) can be estimated from the SVD of the initialized trajectory matrix, with a polynomial reconstruction for instance. Slide 63 uses \(N_w = 300\), \(K = 10\).

4.3 · Model-based interpolation

slides 65–70

For long segments, interpolation becomes a full reconstruction task, and a model is necessary (slide 65): choose an adequate model, infer its parameters from the known samples, and replace the missing samples by values consistent with the learned model. How can parameters be estimated from a series with holes? Iteratively (slide 66): initialize the missing samples with rough estimates (zero, constant, linear), infer the parameters from all the samples, reconstruct the missing samples from the model, and repeat the last two steps until convergence.

AR-based interpolation (slides 67–68; Janssen et al., 1986). With AR(p) estimates \(\hat\ba\), the signal should satisfy \(x[n] \approx -\sum_{i=1}^p \hat a_i x[n-i]\), so choose the missing samples that make the prediction error over the whole series as small as possible:

\[ E(\bx) = \sum_{n=p}^{N-1}\Big| x[n] + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\hat a_i x[n-i] \Big|^2, \qquad \bx^* = \argmin_{\tilde\bx \,:\, \tilde x[n] = x[n] \ \forall n \notin \cT} E(\tilde\bx). \]

Each prediction error is linear in the missing samples, so \(E\) is quadratic in \(\bx_\cT\): \(E(\bx) = \bx_\cT^\top\bB\bx_\cT + 2\bx_\cT^\top\bd + C\), where, with \(\hat a_0 = 1\),

\[ b_{t,t'} = \sum_{l=0}^{p-|t-t'|}\hat a_l\,\hat a_{l+|t-t'|} \ \text{ if } |t-t'| \le p, \ 0 \text{ otherwise}; \qquad d_t = \sum_{\substack{-p \le k \le p \\ t-k \notin \cT}} b_{|k|}\, x[t-k], \]

and \(C\) only depends on the known samples. The minimum solves a linear system, \(\bB\bx_\cT = -\bd\).

Beyond the slides

Where \(\bB\) and \(\bd\) come from. Stack the prediction errors \(e[p], \dots, e[N-1]\) in a vector: \(\be = \mathbf A_\cT\bx_\cT + \mathbf A_{\bar\cT}\bx_{\bar\cT}\), where the columns of \(\mathbf A\) are shifted copies of \((\hat a_0, \dots, \hat a_p)\). Then \(E = \|\be\|^2\) and the normal equations give \(\bB = \mathbf A_\cT^\top\mathbf A_\cT\), \(\bd = \mathbf A_\cT^\top\mathbf A_{\bar\cT}\bx_{\bar\cT}\). \(\bB\) is banded (\(b_{t,t'} = 0\) when \(|t - t'| \gt p\)), so gaps more than \(p\) samples apart are solved independently, and \(\bB\) is positive definite because \(\hat a_0 = 1\).

§5

Outlier removal

AST4 · slides 71–87

Outliers, also called impulsive noise as opposed to AWGN, are spurious samples, isolated or contiguous, that take unlikely values (slide 72). Outlier removal consists in detecting the locations \(\cT\) of the outliers (detection phase), then replacing these values with more adequate ones (interpolation phase) (slide 73). The interpolation phase is §4, so this section is about detection. Two settings, isolated samples or contiguous groups, and one principle: outliers are characterized not only by their values but also by their positions in the series. "Context is fundamental".

5.1 · Isolated samples

slides 74–77

When the impulsive noise only corrupts isolated samples (slide 74), two tools are enough. If its values are particularly large with respect to the signal, they stand out in the histogram of the values taken by the samples, as in outlier detection for statistical data (slide 75). Otherwise, a sliding median filter both detects and removes them, replacing each value by the median of a window of \(2w+1\) samples (slide 76):

\[ \hat x[n] = \med_{-w \le i \le w}\{x[n-i]\}. \]

Median filtering smooths the series while preserving discontinuities. Slide 76's example: the original signal \([0.3 \ \ 0.4 \ \ 0.45]\) is corrupted into \([0.3 \ \ 9 \ \ 0.45]\). The moving average replaces the 9 by 3.25, the median by 0.45 (the slide prints "0.9 →" where the corrupted value is 9). Slide 77: "perfect reconstruction with median filtering (2w + 1 = 3 samples)".

Beyond the slides

The Hampel filter (mini-project 4.11): in a window of \(2w+1\) samples, flag \(x[n]\) when \(|x[n] - m_n| \gt k \cdot 1.4826\,\MAD_n\), where \(m_n\) is the local median and \(\MAD_n\) the median absolute deviation from it (1.4826 makes it a consistent estimate of \(\sigma\) for Gaussian data); replace only the flagged samples by \(m_n\). It keeps the robustness of the median without its side effect on the extrema. Lecture 5 cites the same rule, "do not use standard deviation around the mean, use absolute deviation around the median" (Leys et al., 2013), for its sliding-window detectors. The lab uses \(w = 3\), \(k = 3\).

5.2 · Contiguous samples

slides 78–87

When the impulsive noise corrupts groups of contiguous samples (slide 78), values are not sufficient: a median over a window shorter than the burst returns a corrupted value. A model becomes necessary (slide 79). Outliers are samples that are far from their predicted values according to a model. The principle is the one of model-based interpolation: estimate the parameters, detect, interpolate, and iterate. The task is close to anomaly detection (Lecture 5).

AR-based detection (slide 80). Under \(x[n] = -\sum_{i=1}^p a_i x[n-i] + b[n]\), with estimates \(\hat\ba\) the prediction error is

\[ e[n] = x[n] + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\hat a_i\, x[n-i]. \]

If the model is adapted, the parameters well estimated and the noise variance low, this quantity is small for samples that are not outliers (Janssen et al., 1986). Detect with a threshold \(\lambda\): \(\cT = \{n \text{ s.t. } |e[n]| \gt \lambda\}\) (slide 81 uses AR(10)). To detect and remove, alternate (slide 82): (1) estimation, learn the AR parameters from the current series; (2) detection of the outliers; (3) interpolation of these outliers by appropriate values; (4) reiterate. Slides 84–87 show two rounds.

6.1 · How to apply preprocessings: in which order?

slide 89

The lecture discussed each preprocessing separately, but a single series is likely to suffer from several perturbations at once, and they interact (slide 89):

  • the presence of outliers can disrupt all the preprocessing methods based on model estimates;
  • the presence of a trend disrupts the methods based on spectral estimation;
  • with missing samples or irregular sampling rates, Fourier-based techniques cannot be used.

So the steps must be applied in a logical order. "In most cases it is advised to do outliers, then missing samples, then trend, then noise." Above all, check and keep track of every step.

Removing outliers before the AR interpolation (0.055 against 0.074) and before the trend fit (0.053 against 0.076) helps too: spikes bias both estimates. Only the order of trend and missing samples makes no difference here (0.066 against 0.064), because least squares simply skips the missing samples. Slide 89's order averages 0.026, third of the 24; the best on average, O T M N (0.025), only swaps those two steps.

Key idea

Each method is exact only under its own assumption, and the other perturbations break it: a spike is not band-limited, a gap is not a sample, a trend is not stationary. Remove first the perturbation that breaks the most assumptions. That is outliers, which bias every estimate. Keep the filter for last: it needs a complete grid, and it hides what the other detectors look for.

6.2 · How to evaluate a preprocessing

slides 90–91

Preprocessing is often a small part of a larger pipeline, and it is rarely evaluated properly (slide 90). Four ways to do it:

  • synthetic ground truth: corrupt a clean signal in a controlled way and measure the reconstruction error, as every experiment on this page does;
  • silent parts: for some series, isolate stretches where only the perturbations occur, with no signal (the noise floor of §2.1 is one);
  • the whole pipeline: most often, preprocessings are assessed by running the entire pipeline and measuring the final performance;
  • in every case, use a validation set to learn the preprocessing parameters: "estimating them by using the final scores is wrong".

The lecture's conclusion (slide 91)

  • Preprocessing is crucial: a tedious task that plays a key role in improving, or worsening, performance. It is missing from most published work, which raises serious reproducibility issues.
  • Preprocessing is almost never evaluated: it happens before any model is fitted, so it is not part of the model's validation, and there is no benchmark because real recordings have no ground truth.
  • A neglected field seen as "has been": denoising and detrending have almost disappeared from the recent literature, their tools considered settled. Imputation is the exception: it has become a task in its own right, with deep architectures and benchmarks, rather than a preprocessing step.
Watch out

Tuning the cutoff, \(K_0\) or the detection threshold on the test scores leaks the test set into the pipeline. It is the same mistake as selecting features on the whole data set in Lecture 2 (L2 §3): the preprocessing is part of the model, and its parameters are learned on training and validation data only.

Noise floor, Butterworth and moving average 
import numpy as np
from scipy import signal

def noise_floor(y, f_min):
    """sigma^2 read off the flat part of the periodogram |Y[k]|^2 / N (f in cycles per sample)."""
    P = np.abs(np.fft.fft(y)) ** 2 / len(y)
    f = np.arange(len(y)) / len(y)
    return P[(f > f_min) & (f < 1 - f_min)].mean()

def moving_average(y, L):
    """Centered moving average of odd length L; near the edges, the mean of the available samples."""
    c = np.concatenate([[0.0], np.cumsum(y)])
    n = np.arange(len(y))
    lo, hi = np.maximum(0, n - L // 2), np.minimum(len(y), n + L // 2 + 1)
    return (c[hi] - c[lo]) / (hi - lo)

Hard and soft thresholding, the coherence rule and SURE 
from scipy.fft import dct, idct

def hard_threshold(y, K0):
    """Keep the K0 largest DCT coefficients: matching pursuit in an orthonormal basis."""
    z = dct(y, norm="ortho")
    zk = np.zeros_like(z); keep = np.argsort(-np.abs(z))[:K0]; zk[keep] = z[keep]
    return idct(zk, norm="ortho")

def soft_threshold(y, t):
    z = dct(y, norm="ortho")
    return idct(np.sign(z) * np.maximum(np.abs(z) - t, 0), norm="ortho")

def mp_stop(y, n_noise=200, seed=0):
    """Slides 27-29: first iteration where the residual is no more coherent with D than pure noise."""
    s = np.sort(np.abs(dct(y, norm="ortho")))[::-1]
    rho = s / np.sqrt(np.cumsum((s ** 2)[::-1])[::-1])    # coherence of the residual after l atoms
    Zb = dct(np.random.default_rng(seed).standard_normal((n_noise, len(y))), norm="ortho", axis=1)
    rho_noise = np.mean(np.abs(Zb).max(axis=1) / np.linalg.norm(Zb, axis=1))
    return int(np.argmax(rho < rho_noise)), rho_noise

def sure_soft(z, t, sigma):
    """Stein's unbiased estimate of ||x_hat - x||^2 for soft thresholding of z at t."""
    return -len(z) * sigma**2 + np.minimum(z**2, t**2).sum() + 2 * sigma**2 * (np.abs(z) > t).sum()

Singular spectrum analysis 
def ssa(x, Nw, keep):
    """Hankel trajectory matrix, SVD, anti-diagonal average of the kept components (0-based)."""
    N = len(x); Nf = N - Nw + 1
    X = np.lib.stride_tricks.sliding_window_view(x, Nw).T      # Nw x Nf, column c = x[c : c + Nw]
    U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X, full_matrices=False)
    Xk = (U[:, keep] * s[keep]) @ Vt[keep]                     # sum of the kept rank-one terms
    out, cnt = np.zeros(N), np.zeros(N)
    for i in range(Nw):                                         # entry (i, c) belongs to sample i + c
        out[i:i + Nf] += Xk[i]; cnt[i:i + Nf] += 1
    return out / cnt, s

Low-rank interpolation (Algorithm 1) 
def lowrank_interp(x, missing, Nw, K, n_iter=20):
    """Algorithm 1 (slide 62), started from linear interpolation. missing: boolean mask."""
    N = len(x); Nf = N - Nw + 1; n = np.arange(N)
    x0 = x.copy(); x0[missing] = np.interp(n[missing], n[~missing], x[~missing])
    X = np.lib.stride_tricks.sliding_window_view(x0, Nw).T.copy()
    W = ~np.lib.stride_tricks.sliding_window_view(missing, Nw).T   # True = known entry
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X, full_matrices=False)
        X = np.where(W, X, (U[:, :K] * s[:K]) @ Vt[:K])            # X o W + X_hat o (1 - W)
    acc, cnt = np.zeros(N), np.zeros(N)
    for i in range(Nw):
        acc[i:i + Nf] += np.where(W[i], 0, X[i]); cnt[i:i + Nf] += ~W[i]
    y = x0.copy(); y[missing] = acc[missing] / cnt[missing]      # average along anti-diagonals
    return y

AR interpolation (Algorithm 2) 
def levinson(g, p):
    """Slides' convention (Lecture 3): returns a_1..a_p of x[n] = -sum_i a_i x[n-i] + b[n]."""
    a, E = np.zeros(0), g[0]
    for k in range(p):
        kappa = -(g[k + 1] + a @ g[k:0:-1]) / E
        a = np.concatenate([a + kappa * a[::-1], [kappa]]); E *= 1 - kappa ** 2
    return a

def acf(y, p):
    yc = y - y.mean()
    return np.array([yc[:len(y) - m] @ yc[m:] for m in range(p + 1)]) / len(y)

def ar_fill(y, T, a):
    """Missing samples T minimizing sum_{n>=p} e[n]^2: normal equations B x_T = -d (slide 68)."""
    N, p = len(y), len(a); c = np.concatenate([[1.0], a])
    A = np.zeros((N - p, N))                                   # e = A y, rows n = p..N-1
    for l in range(p + 1):
        A[np.arange(N - p), np.arange(p, N) - l] = c[l]
    known = np.setdiff1d(np.arange(N), T)
    AT = A[:, T]
    y = y.copy(); y[T] = np.linalg.solve(AT.T @ AT, -AT.T @ (A[:, known] @ y[known]))
    return y

def ar_interp(x, missing, p, n_iter=10):
    """Algorithm 2 (slide 69): missing samples start at 0 (the mean), then alternate."""
    mu = x[~missing].mean(); y = np.where(missing, 0.0, x - mu); T = np.flatnonzero(missing)
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        y = ar_fill(y, T, levinson(acf(y, p), p))
    return y + mu

Hampel filter and AR-based outlier removal (Algorithm 3) 
def hampel(x, w=3, k=3.0):
    """Flag |x[n] - local median| > k * 1.4826 * local MAD (windows of 2w+1); replace by the median."""
    y, flag = x.copy(), np.zeros(len(x), bool)
    for n in range(len(x)):
        win = x[max(0, n - w): n + w + 1]; m = np.median(win)
        if abs(x[n] - m) > k * 1.4826 * np.median(np.abs(win - m)):
            y[n], flag[n] = m, True
    return y, flag

def ar_outliers(x, p=10, lam=4.0, n_iter=4):
    """Algorithm 3 (slide 83); lam is relative to the robust scale of e, recomputed each time."""
    mu = np.median(x); y = x - mu
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        a = levinson(acf(y, p), p)
        e = np.convolve(y, np.concatenate([[1.0], a]))[p:len(y)]   # e[n] = y[n] + sum a_i y[n-i], n >= p
        scale = 1.4826 * np.median(np.abs(e - np.median(e)))
        T = p + np.flatnonzero(np.abs(e) > lam * scale)
        if len(T): y = ar_fill(y, T, a)
    return y + mu

Check yourself

try first, then open
1 · Why does white noise add a constant to the PSD, and what does that give you on a log-spectrum?

\(\gamma_y = \gamma_x + \gamma_b\) because the cross terms vanish (\(x\) and \(b\) uncorrelated, \(b\) zero-mean), and \(\gamma_b[m] = \sigma^2\delta[m]\) has a flat transform \(\sigma^2\). On a log-spectrum the noisy curve lies on the line \(10\log_{10}\sigma^2\) wherever the signal is weaker than the noise, which shows the band where only noise is present.

2 · The noisy spectrum meets the floor at 0.5 mHz. What goes wrong with a cutoff at 0.1 mHz, and at 3 mHz?

At 0.1 mHz the filter removes frequencies where the signal dominates, so it distorts the daily shape (error 0.087 in the lab). At 3 mHz it keeps a band that holds only noise (0.057). The error is smallest near the frequency where signal and floor meet.

3 · Why does the causal moving average of slide 19 do worse than the centered one with the same L?

Same magnitude response, but the causal version averages the last \(L\) samples, which delays the output by \((L-1)/2\) samples. On a changing signal the delay is an error: 0.053 instead of 0.041 for \(L = 7\) in the lab.

4 · What does a filter keep, and what does thresholding keep?

A low-pass filter keeps a fixed band of frequencies, the same for every signal. Thresholding keeps the largest coefficients in a representation, wherever they are: it adapts to the signal. Both assume the signal concentrates somewhere the noise does not.

5 · Why is matching pursuit in an orthonormal basis the same as keeping the K₀ largest coefficients?

The atom most correlated with \(\by\) is the one with the largest \(|z_k| = |\langle\by, \bd_k\rangle|\). Subtracting \(z_k\bd_k\) leaves the other coefficients unchanged and sets this one to zero, so the next pick is the next largest, and no atom is picked twice.

6 · On slide 25, why does the distance to the noisy signal always decrease, and why can it not choose K₀?

With more coefficients the approximation gets closer to \(\by\), and with all \(N\) it is \(\by\) itself: the distance goes to 0. Following it leads to keeping the noise. Only the distance to the clean signal has a minimum, and it needs \(\bx\): hence heuristics, the coherence rule, SURE or a validation set.

7 · What does the coherence stopping rule compare, and why is √(2 ln N / N) a sensible noise level?

The coherence of the current residual with the dictionary against the coherence of pure noise. For white noise in an orthonormal basis of size \(N\), the coefficients are \(N\) independent \(\mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)\): their maximum is about \(\sigma\sqrt{2\ln N}\) and the norm about \(\sigma\sqrt N\). For \(N = 1440\), 0.10, against 0.092 measured on 200 noise signals.

8 · Why can soft thresholding be worse than hard thresholding on a smooth signal?

Soft thresholding shrinks every kept coefficient by \(t\), including the few large ones that carry the shape. To limit this bias the best threshold is lower, so more noisy coefficients survive (262 for soft against 38 for hard on the day, with errors 1.73 and 1.47).

9 · What is the trajectory matrix, how many columns does it have, and what does unfolding do?

The frames of the signal (windows of \(N_w\) samples, starting every \(N_w - N_o\) samples) placed side by side: \(N_f = \lfloor (N-N_w)/(N_w-N_o)\rfloor + 1\) columns. Unfolding puts each approximated column back at its position and averages the values where several frames cover the same sample.

10 · Why are the singular values of white noise's trajectory matrix all close to σ√N_f?

\(\bX\bX^\top/N_f\) estimates the lag-covariance matrix, which is \(\sigma^2\bI\) for white noise: every direction has variance \(\sigma^2\), so every singular value is about \(\sqrt{N_f\sigma^2}\). The estimation error spreads them a little (3.2 to 4.4 around 3.75 in the lab).

11 · What does a pair of nearly equal singular values indicate, and why must N_w exceed the period?

An oscillation: a sinusoid is a combination of a sine and a cosine, two directions of equal energy. A window shorter than the period sees only part of a cycle, which looks like a slope or a bend and mixes with the trend components (the 25-minute cycle only separates at \(N_w = 64\)).

12 · Why is detrending ill-posed? Give a case where two answers are both defensible.

Trend and signal are separated only by a convention on scale, not by a property of the data (slide 45). A slow 60 s cycle in a 100 s record is "trend" for a degree-6 polynomial and "signal" for a degree-2 one; the ECG baseline of slides 7 and 10 is a smooth trend or two levels with a jump. The right choice depends on the question.

13 · Why does differencing destroy a smooth, oversampled signal?

\(x[n] - x[n-1]\) is a filter with gain \(4\sin^2(\pi f/F_s)\): about \(10^{-3}\) at 0.1 Hz with 20 samples per second, but 2 on average over white noise. The signal-to-noise ratio falls from 17.8 to 0.8 in the lab.

14 · MCAR, MAR, MNAR: which estimates stay unbiased, and what repairs MAR?

Under MCAR all of them. Under MAR the plain mean is biased, but conditioning on the observed cause (averaging hour by hour) removes the bias. Under MNAR nothing observed carries the lost information: every estimate is biased, and interpolation cannot recreate the values above the saturation.

15 · Why is cubic interpolation unstable on a long gap?

It is fitted on two samples on each side, so it extrapolates the local slopes and curvatures across the whole gap. Over 120 samples a small curvature becomes a large swing (error 0.367 against 0.091 for linear on the day).

16 · In AR interpolation, what is B, why is it positive definite, and why is it banded?

\(\bB = \mathbf A_\cT^\top\mathbf A_\cT\), where \(\mathbf A_\cT\) holds the columns of the prediction-error operator for the missing samples. Because \(\hat a_0 = 1\), these columns are independent, so \(\bB\) is positive definite. Two samples more than \(p\) apart never appear in the same prediction error, so \(b_{t,t'} = 0\) when \(|t-t'| \gt p\).

17 · Why does low-rank interpolation barely improve on linear interpolation on the day, and when does it help?

It fills a gap with shapes that the other windows share. The day never repeats itself, and a straight segment is itself low rank, so the linear start is already almost a fixed point. When a pattern repeats elsewhere, as the 25-minute cycle does, the other windows show what belongs in the gap (0.090 against 0.142 for linear).

18 · Why can a histogram miss outliers that a median filter catches?

The histogram only sees values. An outlier that stays within the signal's range has an ordinary value at the wrong place. The median compares each sample with its neighbours, so it sees the context (0 of 25 against 22 of 25 at size 0.2 in the lab).

19 · Why does AR-based detection flag samples just after a burst, and why is that harmless?

The corrupted samples enter the prediction of the next \(p\) samples, so their prediction errors are large too. These samples are then re-interpolated from the model; if the model is good, the interpolated values are close to the true ones.

20 · Why iterate Algorithm 3 instead of detecting once?

The first AR estimate is computed with the outliers in, so it is biased and misses some of them. Each round cleans the series, the next estimate is better and detects more: in the lab, 88–96% of the corrupted samples are found at the first iteration, all of them by the second.

21 · What is the worst mistake when ordering the four steps, and why?

Filtering before removing the outliers: the filter spreads each spike into a smooth bump that local detectors no longer see as an outlier, and the bump stays (0.090 against 0.040 on average in the lab). Filtering before filling the gaps is next: a filter needs every sample.

22 · How can you evaluate a preprocessing when there is no clean signal?

Corrupt a clean signal on purpose (synthetic ground truth), use stretches where only noise is present, calibrate thresholds on pure noise, estimate the error with SURE, and judge the whole pipeline on a validation set. Never tune the parameters on the final test scores.
