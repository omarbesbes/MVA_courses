§1

Two problems

AST2 · slides 4–6

Slide 4 shows a brain-computer interface: EEG recorded while a person imagines moving a cursor, then a chain of processing steps, then a classifier that recognizes the mental task. Feature extraction and selection sit in the middle of that chain, and the slide calls them the fundamental step for using ML on time series.

Problem 1 · Feature extraction

Given a time series of length N, extract a set of D features that characterizes it.

  • useful for indexing, classification, clustering…
  • often based on expertise: feature engineering, ad hoc features built from domain knowledge;
  • the result is a bag of features representation of the series.

Problem 2 · Feature selection

Given a dataset of M time series, each represented with D features, select the K < D features that are relevant for a given task.

  • supervised (labels available) or unsupervised;
  • it only makes sense relative to a task: a feature can be decisive for one and useless for another.
Key idea

A feature vector is a deliberate loss of information. You keep D numbers out of N samples, and you choose which ones. Every family of features in this lecture keeps something and throws something away. The useful question for each is: what does it assume about the series, and what is it blind to?

Feature extraction

AST2 · slides 8–90

Good feature engineering is based on domain knowledge, adapted to the data and to the model behind it (Lecture 3). When you have none, the fallback is classical features that capture statistical or physical properties of the series (slide 8). The two visions of the intro map directly onto them:

2.1 · Stationarity and ergodicity: the assumptions behind every feature

slides 9–13

Many data scientists extract bags of features without asking whether they are correct. Even the empirical mean can be wrong. Slide 9 shows the air-carrier passenger series, which grows for twenty years: its mean describes no particular moment of it. Before extracting anything, ask: is it meaningful to describe the whole series with one number?

To make the question precise, treat \(x[0:N-1]\) as a realization of a stochastic process \(X[0:N-1]\), where each \(X[n]\) is a random variable. What you can extract depends on the properties of that process.

Stationarity: the statistics do not change over time

\[ \text{order 1:} \quad \forall n,\ \ \mathbb E[X[n]] = \mu \]
\[ \text{order 2:} \quad \forall n_1, n_2,\ \ \mathbb E\big[X[n_1]X[n_2]\big] = \gamma_X\big[|n_2 - n_1|\big] \]

Both together: wide-sense stationarity (WSS), the most common assumption. The second-order statistics depend only on the lag, not on the date.

Ergodicity: one realization is enough

\[ \mu = \lim_{N\to\infty} \frac1N \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n], \qquad \gamma_X[k] = \lim_{N\to\infty} \frac1N \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\,x[n+k] \]

For a WSS process, ergodicity says that time averages along one realization converge to the ensemble averages over all possible realizations.

Key idea

A feature computed on one series is a time average. It estimates a property of the process only if the property does not change over time (stationarity) and if one long realization reveals it (ergodicity). The experiment separates the two ideas.

Beyond the slides

The second process is the textbook case of stationary but not ergodic: \(X[n] = A + W[n]\), where the level \(A\) is drawn once per realization. Its mean is 0 at every instant and its covariance depends only on the lag, so it is WSS. But one realization never visits the other levels: its time average converges to its own \(A\), not to 0. The "spread of time averages" readout is the signature: it shrinks with \(N\) for an ergodic process and stays at \(\mathrm{std}(A)\) here.

None of the three non-stationary signals of slide 13 (a jump in the mean, a change in variance) admits correct global statistics: any feature computed on the whole series is wrong. Two solutions:

  • cut the signal into small frames where it can be assumed stationary and ergodic, and compute features per frame (tick the box above);
  • detect the changes with a change-point detection algorithm and work separately on each segment (Lecture 5).
Watch out

Frames trade one problem for another. Short frames are more likely to be stationary, but each estimate then uses fewer samples and is noisier. This bias-variance trade-off comes back in the spectrogram (§2.3), where it becomes the time-frequency trade-off.

2.2 · Statistical features

slides 14–20

Assuming \(X\) is ergodic and wide-sense stationary, estimate its properties from \(x[0:N-1]\), with \(x[n] = 0\) outside \([0, N-1]\):

\[ \hat\mu = \frac1N \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n], \qquad \hat\gamma_x^{\,biased}[m] = \frac1N \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\,x[n+m], \qquad \hat\gamma_x^{\,unbiased}[m] = \frac1{N-|m|} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\,x[n+m] \]

The autocorrelation function (ACF) measures how much the series resembles a shifted copy of itself. Its most useful property is to reveal periodicities buried in noise: noise is uncorrelated with its shifted copies, a periodic component is not. Slides 16–18: a signal that looks like pure noise has an ACF with peaks at every multiple of 50 lags, and a sinusoid of period \(50 \times \tfrac1{100} = 0.5\) s was hiding in it.

Features from the ACF (slide 19). For a centered process, the renormalized coefficient \(\gamma'_X[m] = \gamma_X[m]/\gamma_X[0] \in [-1, 1]\) is the correlation between samples \(m\) apart. When the signal has a seasonality or periodic component (Lecture 3), store \(\gamma'_X[m_0]\), where \(m_0 \gt 0\) is the lag of the first peak.

Other statistics (slide 20): minimum, maximum, and robust statistics such as the median or percentiles (5%, 25%…). Most of these purely statistical features remove the time information. When needed, compute them on sliding windows (50% overlap in the slide): more features, and the time information is partly preserved.

Beyond the slides

The biased estimator is the one used in practice. Its variance stays small at large lags, and it is positive semi-definite: its Fourier transform is exactly the periodogram \(|X[k]|^2/N\) of §2.3, which is never negative. This is the empirical version of the Wiener-Khinchin theorem, and it ties the statistical and the spectral views together: the autocorrelation and the power spectrum carry the same information.

2.3 · Spectral features

slides 21–29

The physical view: \(x[n] = x(nT_s)\) is a uniform sampling of a continuous signal, with sampling period \(T_s\) and frequency \(F_s = 1/T_s\). The Discrete Fourier Transform decomposes the \(N\) samples on \(N\) frequencies:

\[ X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\, e^{-j2\pi \frac{kn}{N}}, \quad 0 \le k \le N-1, \qquad \Delta f = \frac{F_s}{N}, \qquad f[k] = k\,\frac{F_s}{N} \ \text{ for } 0 \le k \le \frac N2 \]
  • \(\Delta f\) is the frequency resolution, the spacing between two observable frequencies.
  • By periodicity of the DFT, the indices \(k \gt N/2\) are the negative frequencies \(f[k] = (k - N) F_s/N\).
  • No frequency of modulus above \(F_s/2\) can be observed (Nyquist).
  • \(X[k]\) is complex; we use \(|X[k]|^2\). Plotted against \(f[k]\), it is the spectrum. Low frequencies correspond to smooth variations, high frequencies to fast ones.

The two classes of slides 24–25 are the example of the intro (Intro I.6): indistinguishable in time, separated by their DFT coefficients.

Relative band energy (slide 26)

Merge the \(|X[k]|^2\) over a band of interest \([f_1, f_2]\), normalized by the total energy:

\[ E_{[f_1, f_2]} = \frac{\sum_{k:\, f[k] \in [f_1, f_2]} |X[k]|^2}{\sum_{k:\, f[k] \in [0, F_s/2]} |X[k]|^2} \]

Shape of the spectrum (slide 27)

With \(P[k] = |X[k]|^2\) and \(p[k] = P[k]/\sum_{k'} P[k']\) seen as a distribution over frequencies:

  • centroid \(c = \sum_k f[k]\,p[k]\): where the energy is;
  • spread \(\sigma_s^2 = \sum_k (f[k] - c)^2 p[k]\): how concentrated;
  • entropy \(H_s = -\sum_k p[k] \log_2 p[k]\);
  • flatness \(\mathrm{SFM} = \big(\prod_k P[k]\big)^{1/K} \big/ \tfrac1K\sum_k P[k]\), geometric over arithmetic mean;
  • roll-off: the frequency below which a fraction \(\beta\) of the energy lies.

Practical example: EEG (slides 28–29). Electroencephalography measures the brain's electrical activity with a network of sensors, to study epilepsy, stroke and sleep. Each sleep phase corresponds to the emergence of typical frequencies, so spectral features tell which phase a subject is in:

2.3 (continued) · The spectrogram

slides 30–41

The DFT of the whole series averages the frequency content over time. When properties change with time, compute the DFT on sliding windows instead: the resulting time-frequency matrix (frame number by frequency bin) is the spectrogram. Slide 36's advice: only use the global DFT when you are sure there are no abrupt changes, and always look at the spectrogram first. Slides 37–39 show why: a signal whose DFT suggests a sinusoid near 100 Hz is in fact a chirp sweeping from 100 to 500 Hz.

\(N_w\), window length: often a power of 2 (for the FFT), linked to the frequency resolution you need.

\(N_o\), overlap between successive frames: often 50% or 75% of \(N_w\); it sets the time step (finest when \(N_o = N_w - 1\)).

\(w\), analysis window (Hann, Hamming, Blackman…): the frame is multiplied by it to limit side effects (spectral leakage).

Key result

With a window of \(N_w\) samples, \(\Delta t \approx N_w T_s\) and \(\Delta f \approx F_s / N_w\), hence \(\Delta t \cdot \Delta f \approx 1\): time and frequency resolution cannot both be improved. A long window separates two close frequencies but spreads a brief event over the whole window. A short window locates a brief event precisely but merges close frequencies into a single peak (slide 41).

2.3 (end) · The periodogram is not a consistent estimator

slides 42–43

Spectral estimation is harder than it looks, and linked to the statistical assumptions. When we compute the spectrum of a random signal, we assume it is WSS and estimate its power spectral density (PSD) \(S_x[k]\). The natural estimator is the periodogram:

\[ \hat S_x[k] = \frac{|X[k]|^2}{N}, \qquad \mathbb E\big[\hat S_x[k]\big] \to S_x[k] \ \ \text{(asymptotically unbiased)}, \qquad \mathrm{var}\big(\hat S_x[k]\big) \to S_x[k]^2 \ \ \text{(not consistent)} \]

The variance does not decrease with \(N\): increasing \(N\) only adds more frequency bins, each just as noisy. The fix is to average (slide 43): Bartlett averages the periodograms of \(K\) non-overlapping segments (variance divided by \(K\), resolution \(K\) times coarser); Welch does the same with overlapping windowed segments, typically 50%; multitaper averages periodograms computed with several orthogonal windows. Averaging only makes sense for WSS signals.

For the periodogram the relative standard deviation stays near 1 at every \(N\): each bin is as uncertain as the value it estimates. Averaging \(K\) segments divides it by about \(\sqrt K\), at the price of a resolution \(K\) times coarser. Welch reaches a lower variance than Bartlett for the same segment length, because 50% overlap nearly doubles the number of segments.

Beyond the slides

For white noise, each periodogram bin follows (approximately) an exponential distribution. For an exponential variable the geometric mean is \(e^{-\gamma} \approx 0.56\) times the arithmetic mean (\(\gamma\) is Euler's constant). That is why the flatness of a raw periodogram of white noise is about 0.56 instead of 1 in the EEG experiment. Spectral features inherit the variance of the spectral estimator: estimate the spectrum with Welch before computing them.

2.4 · Local symbolic features

slides 44–55

How would you describe the signal of slide 44 to someone on the phone? "Small up, small down, big up, big down, up, down…" That is the idea: describe the whole series as a succession of local behaviors, each encoded by a symbol. This is symbolization: it takes a real-valued signal of length \(N\) and outputs a discrete-valued sequence of smaller length. Statistical features are then extracted from the symbols (number of occurrences of each symbol, number of transitions…). These local symbolic features are the basis of state-of-the-art classifiers such as BOSS and its variants (Schäfer, 2015).

The example of slides 47–51: a signal sampled at 10 kHz for 1 s, cut into non-overlapping frames of 500 samples, each frame replaced by its average, and the averages quantized on 10 levels A to J. The whole second becomes the word CCCFJHCBEEBBBACCBEHF: 640 kbits (10,000 samples of 64 bits) against less than 0.1 kbit (20 symbols).

SAX (Lin et al., 2007)

Symbolic Aggregate approXimation, with two parameters: the output size \(w\) and the alphabet size \(A\). Each series is encoded on \(w \log_2 A\) bits.

  1. Normalize the series (zero mean, unit variance).
  2. Divide it into \(w\) segments and compute each mean (this is PAA, piecewise aggregate approximation).
  3. Assign one symbol per segment, with the \(A\) intervals chosen so that all symbols have the same probability under \(\mathcal N(0, 1)\).

A lower bound, again

The authors also define a distance on words, MINDIST, such that for two series

\[ \mathrm{MINDIST}\big(\mathrm{SAX}(\bx), \mathrm{SAX}(\by)\big) \le d_{EUC}(\bx, \by), \]

which preserves some classification properties in the symbolic space. It is the idea of L1 §3.2 in another guise: a cheap bound that can reject candidates without ever computing the true distance.

Beyond the slides

The MINDIST formula: with \(n\) samples, \(w\) segments and breakpoints \(\beta_1 \lt \dots \lt \beta_{A-1}\), \(\mathrm{MINDIST} = \sqrt{n/w}\,\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{w} \mathrm{dist}(a_i, b_i)^2}\), where \(\mathrm{dist}(r, c) = 0\) if the symbols are equal or adjacent, and otherwise the gap between their intervals, \(\beta_{\max(r,c)-1} - \beta_{\min(r,c)}\). Two segments whose means fall in adjacent cells could be arbitrarily close, so they contribute nothing: that is what makes it a lower bound.

How to build features (slide 54): histograms, transition counts, histograms of n-grams; symbolization on sliding windows to form super-symbols; multiscale approaches merging histograms computed with several window sizes; and dozens of other symbolizations (slopes, local Fourier coefficients, min/max values…). Other uses (slide 55): reducing time and storage, enumerating all patterns or anomalies exhaustively, and applying tools built for discrete sequences in NLP and bioinformatics.

2.5 · Information theory features

slides 56–64

Shannon's theory (late 1940s) made digital communication possible: data can be represented in binary, compressed, encrypted, transmitted with low error. Its central idea is that a message carries a quantity of information linked to its probability of occurrence. For a random variable \(X\) on a finite alphabet \(\mathcal X\):

\[ H(X) = -\sum_{x \in \mathcal X} p_X(x) \log_2 p_X(x), \qquad 0 \le H(X) \le \log_2 |\mathcal X| \ \ \text{(maximum for the uniform distribution)} \]
\[ H(X, Y) = -\sum_{x, y} p_{XY}(x,y) \log_2 p_{XY}(x,y), \quad H(X|Y) = -\sum_{x,y} p_{XY}(x,y) \log_2 p_{X|Y}(x|y), \quad I(X;Y) = \sum_{x,y} p_{XY}(x,y) \log_2 \frac{p_{XY}(x,y)}{p_X(x)\,p_Y(y)} \]

The entropy is the average information in \(X\); the joint entropy, that of the pair; the conditional entropy, the uncertainty left on \(X\) once \(Y\) is known; the mutual information, what the two share. Applying this to time series raises two problems (slide 59):

  • samples are real-valued: quantize or symbolize first (§2.4), or redefine \(\mathbb P(X = x)\) as \(\mathbb P(d(X, x) \le \epsilon)\) with a distance and a tolerance;
  • entropy ignores time: \(\bx = (1.6, 0.2, 1.6, 0.2, 1.6, 0.2, 1.6, 0.2)\) and \(\by = (1.6, 1.6, 1.6, 1.6, 0.2, 0.2, 0.2, 0.2)\) have the same entropy.

The fix is the extension of degree \(m\) of the source, \(X^{[m]}\): the source that emits groups of \(m\) consecutive symbols (for \(\{A, B\}\), \(X^{[2]}\) emits AA, AB, BA, BB). Its alphabet has \(|\mathcal X|^m\) words, and its distribution is estimated by counting the subsequences of length \(m\). On the two sequences of slide 59:

Approximate entropy (Pincus, 1991)

For real-valued series, the event "the next \(m\) values equal \((x[n], \dots, x[n+m-1])\)" is replaced by "they stay within \(\epsilon\) of them": \(\max_{0 \le i \le m-1} |X[n+i] - x[n+i]| \le \epsilon\). Then estimate the distributions of \(X^{[m]}\) and \(X^{[m+1]}\) and compute

\[ \mathrm{ApEn} = H\big(X^{[m+1]}\big) - H\big(X^{[m]}\big). \]
Key idea

By the chain rule, \(H(X^{[m+1]}) - H(X^{[m]})\) is the entropy of the next sample given the \(m\) previous ones (beyond the slides). So ApEn measures how unpredictable the next value remains once you know the recent past. The two limit cases of slide 62 follow: for i.i.d. samples \(H(X^{[m]}) = mH(X)\) and ApEn \(= H(X)\), maximum chaos; for a constant series ApEn \(= 0\), full determinism.

2.6 · Convolutional features

slides 65–78

Everything so far describes global properties, computed either directly from the values (statistical, spectral) or through a fixed vocabulary of local behaviors (symbolic, information theory). Another approach describes a series by the local shapes it contains: which local shapes are present, how strongly, how often, and where? The answer depends on the scale of the shapes, on the features extracted from their detection, and on how the shapes are obtained.

Let \(\mathbf w \in \R^\ell\) be a filter. Its activation map \(\mathbf z = (z_1, \dots, z_m)\) measures the resemblance between \(\mathbf w\) and the subsequence starting at each \(i\):

\[ z_i = \sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1} x[i+j]\, w_j + b, \qquad d_{EUC}^2\big(\mathbf w, x[i : i+\ell-1]\big) = \underbrace{\|\mathbf w\|^2 + 2b}_{\text{constant}} + \big\|x[i:i+\ell-1]\big\|^2 - 2 z_i \]
Link

This is the sliding dot product of L1 §3.1: \(\mathbf z\) is the convolution of \(\bx\) with the time-reversed filter, computed in \(O(N \log N)\) by FFT whatever \(\ell\). The identity above says that at constant local energy, maximizing \(z_i\) is minimizing the distance: an activation map is an upside-down distance profile.

Scale (slides 68–69). Real patterns occur at unknown scales, and a longer filter means more parameters and more computation. Dilation inserts \(d - 1\) gaps between consecutive taps: \(z_i = \sum_j x[i + d\cdot j]\,w_j + b\). The filter now spans \(d(\ell - 1) + 1\) samples with still only \(\ell\) multiplications: it detects the same shape, subsampled, at a coarser time scale.

Why random filters work

It is an instance of the random features paradigm (slide 77): a feature map \(\Phi(\bx) \in \R^D\) with \(D\) large and fixed at random, followed by a linear model, approximates a rich class of nonlinear functions. With \(D \sim 10^4\) features, some are bound to be informative, and the linear model does the selection by giving them more weight, a supervised feature selection (§3).

Two practical consequences: \(D \gg M\), so ridge regression or another regularized model is required; and standardization is mandatory, since max, PPV and MIPV live on different scales.

Deep-learning implementation (slide 78). Standard DL libraries compute such convolutions efficiently. But note where the performance comes from: the activation maps and features are computed without labels and are mostly redundant or irrelevant; the strong results come from combining them with a supervised linear classifier. These features are therefore reserved for supervised tasks and are hard to use for clustering.

Beyond the slides

With \(D \gg M\), solve ridge regression in its dual form: \(\mathbf w = \mathbf X^\top(\mathbf X\mathbf X^\top + \alpha I)^{-1}\by\), where \(\mathbf X\mathbf X^\top\) is only \(M \times M\). The leave-one-out error then has a closed form, which is how the regularization \(\alpha\) is chosen in the next experiment.

Synthesis · Which features can tell a shape from its mirror in time?

an experiment across §2.2–2.6

Here is a test that sums up the lecture. Class A contains a bump with a fast rise and a slow decay, at a random position; class B contains the same bump reversed in time. Reversing a signal leaves its histogram unchanged, its autocorrelation unchanged, and the modulus of its DFT unchanged. So every feature family that looks only at values, lags or frequencies should be blind to the difference.

Statistics, autocorrelation, spectral shape and symbol histograms stay at chance: they are provably blind to time reversal. Symbol transitions keep the local order and do better. Random convolutional kernels (length 7 to 11, random weights and dilations, features max and PPV) reach the highest accuracy: a kernel and its reversal are different kernels, so the direction of time survives.

Key idea

This is the intro's "time is information" (Intro I.3) made quantitative. Features that only look at values, lags or frequency magnitudes cannot see the direction of time. When the order of events matters, you need order-sensitive features: n-grams of symbols, convolutional features, or models (Lecture 3).

Slide 79 warns that this is not a proper deep-learning class: only the basic ideas. Everything so far works without deep learning. Where are the features? (slides 82–83). A network is a stack of transformations and the representation is the output of an intermediate layer, in practice the layer just before the classifier. That layer is almost always a global average pooling, \(\mathrm{GAP}(\mathbf z)_c = \frac1m \sum_i z_c[i]\) for each channel \(c\), which gives a fixed-size output whatever the input length, and invariance to translation.

Three regimes of supervision (slide 84): the loss and the task decide what the features encode.

  • Supervised: the network predicts a label and the representation is a by-product. Optimal for that task, may transfer poorly, and needs annotations, the scarce resource of time series.
  • Reconstruction-based: no labels; the representation must suffice to rebuild the input, so only what reconstruction needs is kept.
  • Self-supervised: no labels; a pretext task, of no interest in itself, forces the encoder to represent a given type of information.

The autoencoder (slide 85) learns two maps at once, an encoder \(\phi : \mathcal X \to \R^D\) and a decoder \(\psi : \R^D \to \mathcal X\), with \(\phi^\star, \psi^\star = \argmin_{\phi, \psi} \|\mathbf X - (\psi \circ \phi)(\mathbf X)\|^2\), the series being cut into frames of length \(N_w\) stored in \(\mathbf X\). Two parameters only: \(N_w\) and \(D\). With \(D \ll N_w\) the input cannot be copied, and only what is needed to rebuild it is retained.

The loss matters (slides 86–87). A perfect reconstruction encodes everything, including what we would like to discard (noise, exact amplitudes, exact positions). And the Euclidean loss penalizes a perfectly shaped reconstruction shifted by one sample as heavily as a completely wrong shape.

From a shift of a few samples on, the Euclidean loss rates the flat line as the better prediction. A network trained with it learns to hedge towards smooth, flat outputs. DTW (L1 §2.3) rates the shifted shape as almost perfect: choosing a loss is choosing which deformations are irrelevant, the same decision as choosing a distance.

Self-supervised techniques (slide 88). The most popular is contrastive learning: feed pairs of series; similar series must get similar representations and dissimilar series dissimilar ones. The similar pairs are usually made by data augmentation, deliberately deformed versions of the same series, and a contrastive loss enforces the behavior. An alternative pretext task is masked modelling: hide part of the input and reconstruct only the hidden part, which forces the encoder to use the context instead of copying.

Watch out

"An augmentation that destroys task-relevant information degrades the features, without any visible symptom" (slide 89). Permutation declares the order of segments irrelevant: an encoder trained with it would be blind to exactly what separates the two classes of the mirror experiment above. Scaling is harmless for gesture shape but destroys the information in a power-level signal.

§3

Feature selection

AST2 · slides 92–111

Given \(M\) series, each represented by \(D\) features, select the \(K \lt D\) features relevant for a task. After §2 this is not optional: random convolutions alone produce thousands of features, most of them useless. What is a good feature? (slide 93) One that is robust, well computed and relevant; that reveals differences actually present in the database; and that generalizes.

Notation: \(M\) observations \(\bx_1, \dots, \bx_M\), \(D\) features, data matrix \(\mathbf X \in \R^{D \times M}\) (one row per feature), and labels \(\by \in \{-1, +1\}^M\) when available.

3.1 · Unsupervised setting: reliability and utility

slides 94–99

Reliability: are the values trustworthy?

  • flag outliers: \(|X_{d,m} - \mu_{X_{d,:}}| \gt 3 \times \mathrm{std}(X_{d,:})\);
  • compare the 5% and 95% percentiles with the minimum and maximum;
  • a variance test to find features that are almost constant on the database;
  • look at boxplots (quartiles, whiskers, isolated points).

The main question is whether extreme values come from the observations themselves or from processing flaws: impulsive noise in the series, a bad feature computation, non-stationarities. Fixing the latter is Lecture 4.

Utility: do the features show differences?

Principal component analysis finds the linear combinations of features \(\tilde f_j = \sum_d U_{d,j} f_d\) that carry the most variance. The principal components are uncorrelated and ordered by the variance they explain; the data are usually plotted on the first two. For feature selection (slide 97, after Wold et al., 1987 and Song et al., 2010):

  1. normalize each row (feature) of \(\mathbf X\) to zero mean and unit variance;
  2. take the SVD \(\tilde{\mathbf X} = \mathbf U \boldsymbol\Sigma \mathbf V^\top\);
  3. plot each feature's contribution \(U_{d,j}\) to the first two components: the correlation circle;
  4. plot the observations, \(S_{j,m}\), their projections on the components.
Reading the circle

A good feature is close to the unit circle: it is well represented by the first two components. Arrows pointing the same way are redundant features; orthogonal arrows carry independent information (slide 98). If several classes are expected, run k-means on the projections to visualize the groups; distances to the centroids assess the representation (slide 99).

Beyond the slides: the arrow of feature \(d\) is its correlation with each component, \(U_{d,j}\sigma_j/\sqrt M\) for standardized data, which is why it lies inside the unit circle. PCA looks for variance, not for class separation: a feature can be decisive for the task and still sit near the center.

3.2 · Supervised setting: filter, wrapper, embedded

slides 100–111 · Li et al., 2017

Filter methods (slides 101–106)

Test the link between each feature and the labels, usually one feature at a time.

\[ \rho(d) = \frac{\mathrm{cov}(X_{d,:}, \by)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{var}(X_{d,:})\,\mathrm{var}(\by)}}, \qquad \text{Fisher}(d) = \frac{\sum_{c=1}^{C} n_c\,(\mu_{c,d} - \mu_d)^2}{\sum_{c=1}^{C} n_c\,\sigma_{c,d}^2} \]
\[ \text{information gain}(d) = I(X_{d,:}; \by), \qquad \mathrm{MRMR}(d) = I(X_{d,:}; \by) - \frac1{|\mathcal S|}\sum_{j \in \mathcal S} I(X_{d,:}; X_{j,:}) \]
  • Correlation: \(\rho(d) \in [-1, 1]\) measures a linear link; keep the features with \(|\rho(d)| \gt \lambda\). Careful: selected one by one, highly correlated features can be redundant.
  • Fisher score (Gu et al., 2011), for \(C\) classes with \(n_c\) points, class means \(\mu_{c,d}\), class standard deviations \(\sigma_{c,d}\) and global mean \(\mu_d\): between-class spread of the means over within-class spread.
  • Information gain: the mutual information between the (discretized) feature and the labels, a measure of any dependency, not only linear.
  • MRMR, minimum redundancy maximum relevance: selecting greedily, a candidate's relevance is reduced by its average information shared with the already selected set \(\mathcal S\). The more non-redundant features are selected, the harder it is for a new one to be redundant with them. Other criteria use conditional entropies (mini-projects).

Wrapper methods (slides 107–108)

Test off-the-shelf classifiers (k-NN, SVM, CNN…) on different sets of features, compare their cross-validated performance, keep the best set. With \(D\) features there are \(2^D - 1\) subsets, so the search is heuristic: iterative addition (best single feature, then best pair containing it…), iterative removal, or random subsets.

Embedded methods (slides 109–111)

Models that select while they learn. The LASSO (Tibshirani, 1996) regresses the labels on the features with an \(\ell_1\) penalty,

\[ \bz^* = \argmin_{\bz} \big\|\by - \mathbf X^\top \bz\big\|_2^2 + \lambda\|\bz\|_1, \]

so many coefficients \(z_d\) are exactly 0 and the others measure the importance of feature \(d\). It is solved by proximal gradient descent (Lecture 3), with the same soft thresholding as the sparse coding step of L1 §4.2. Decision trees select by construction: only the features used in splits matter.

Beyond the slides, but it bites in every project: selection is part of training. Autocorrelation, biased and unbiased 
import numpy as np

def acf(x, max_lag, biased=True):
    """Autocorrelation of the centered series for lags 0..max_lag (biased: 1/N, unbiased: 1/(N-m))."""
    x = np.asarray(x, float) - np.mean(x)
    N = len(x)
    g = np.array([x[:N - m] @ x[m:] for m in range(max_lag + 1)])
    return g / N if biased else g / (N - np.arange(max_lag + 1))
Spectral features from the periodogram 
def spectral_features(x, fs, band=(8, 12), beta=0.85):
    """Relative band energy and shape descriptors of the periodogram P[k] = |X[k]|^2 / N."""
    P = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(x)) ** 2 / len(x)
    f = np.fft.rfftfreq(len(x), 1 / fs)
    p = P / P.sum()                                  # the spectrum seen as a distribution
    centroid = np.sum(f * p)
    spread = np.sqrt(np.sum((f - centroid) ** 2 * p))
    entropy = -np.sum(p[p > 0] * np.log2(p[p > 0]))
    flatness = np.exp(np.mean(np.log(P + 1e-12))) / np.mean(P)
    rolloff = f[np.searchsorted(np.cumsum(p), beta)]
    rel_energy = P[(f >= band[0]) & (f < band[1])].sum() / P.sum()
    return dict(rel_energy=rel_energy, centroid=centroid, spread=spread,
                entropy=entropy, flatness=flatness, rolloff=rolloff)
SAX word 
from statistics import NormalDist

def sax(x, w, A):
    """SAX word: z-normalize, average on w segments (PAA), quantize with equiprobable N(0,1) cuts."""
    x = (x - np.mean(x)) / np.std(x)
    paa = np.array([seg.mean() for seg in np.array_split(x, w)])
    cuts = [NormalDist().inv_cdf(i / A) for i in range(1, A)]
    return "".join(chr(97 + k) for k in np.searchsorted(cuts, paa))
Approximate entropy 
def approximate_entropy(x, m=2, r_factor=0.2):
    """ApEn (Pincus, 1991) with tolerance r = r_factor * std(x); natural log."""
    x = np.asarray(x, float)
    r = r_factor * np.std(x)
    def phi(mm):
        T = np.lib.stride_tricks.sliding_window_view(x, mm)          # all templates of length mm
        dist = np.max(np.abs(T[:, None, :] - T[None, :, :]), axis=2)  # Chebyshev distance
        C = np.mean(dist <= r, axis=1)                               # self-match included
        return np.mean(np.log(C))
    return phi(m) - phi(m + 1)

Memory grows as \(N^2\): fine for a few thousand samples.

Activation map with dilation, and its five features 
def activation_features(x, w, b=0.0, d=1):
    """Activation map z_i = sum_j x[i + d j] w_j + b and its five summary features."""
    L = len(w)
    idx = np.arange(len(x) - d * (L - 1))[:, None] + d * np.arange(L)[None, :]
    z = x[idx] @ w + b
    pos = z > 0
    runs = np.diff(np.flatnonzero(np.diff(np.r_[0, pos.astype(int), 0])))[::2]   # lengths of positive runs
    return dict(max=z.max(), ppv=pos.mean(),
                mpv=z[pos].mean() if pos.any() else 0.0,
                mipv=np.flatnonzero(pos).mean() if pos.any() else np.nan,
                lspv=(runs.max() - 1) if pos.any() else 0)
Fisher score 
def fisher_score(X, y):
    """Fisher score of each feature (columns of X, one row per observation) for labels y."""
    mu = X.mean(axis=0)
    num = sum((y == c).sum() * (X[y == c].mean(axis=0) - mu) ** 2 for c in np.unique(y))
    den = sum((y == c).sum() * X[y == c].var(axis=0) for c in np.unique(y))
    return num / den

Here observations are rows, the scikit-learn convention; the slides store features as rows of \(\mathbf X \in \R^{D \times M}\).

R.2

Check yourself

Answer before opening. Several questions ask for a small computation.

1 · Why is the mean of the passenger series of slide 9 a poor feature?

The series has a strong trend, so it is not stationary: the mean depends on which years you average over and describes no particular moment. Features assume a WSS, ergodic process. Detrend first (Lecture 4), use frames, or segment with change points (Lecture 5).

2 · Give a process that is stationary but not ergodic.

\(X[n] = A + W[n]\) with \(A\) drawn once per realization and \(W\) white noise. The mean is 0 and the covariance depends only on the lag, but the time average of one realization converges to its own \(A\), not to 0.

3 · Why does the ACF reveal a periodicity hidden in noise? Which feature do you keep?

White noise is uncorrelated with its shifted copies, a periodic component correlates with itself at every multiple of its period. The ACF of the sum shows peaks at those lags. Keep \(\gamma'[m_0]\) at the first peak, and the period \(m_0/F_s\).

4 · Biased or unbiased autocorrelation estimator?

Usually biased (1/N). The unbiased one divides the last lags by a small \(N - m\) and becomes very noisy; the biased one has lower variance and is positive semi-definite (its Fourier transform is the periodogram).

5 · Fs = 1000 Hz, N = 2000 samples. Frequency resolution? Can you see a 700 Hz component?

\(\Delta f = 1000/2000 = 0.5\) Hz. No: the highest observable frequency is \(F_s/2 = 500\) Hz. A 700 Hz component, if not filtered before sampling, aliases to \(|700 - 1000| = 300\) Hz.

6 · Why normalize a band energy by the total energy?

The relative energy does not depend on the gain of the sensor or on the overall amplitude, so values are comparable across recordings and subjects. It is the spectral counterpart of amplitude normalization.

7 · Separate two tones 5 Hz apart at Fs = 1000 Hz: which window, and what do you lose?

\(\Delta f \approx F_s/N_w \le 5\) Hz, so \(N_w \ge 200\), in practice 256 (a power of 2), which gives \(\Delta t \approx 0.26\) s. Events shorter than about a quarter of a second will be smeared.

8 · Why doesn't the periodogram improve with more samples? How do you fix it?

Its variance tends to \(S_x[k]^2\) whatever \(N\): more samples give more bins, each as noisy. Average the periodograms of \(K\) segments (Bartlett, Welch, multitaper): the variance is divided by about \(K\), the resolution is \(K\) times coarser. Valid only for WSS signals.

9 · SAX with w = 20 and A = 8: how many bits? Why Gaussian breakpoints?

\(20 \times \log_2 8 = 60\) bits. After z-normalization the values are roughly \(\mathcal N(0,1)\); equiprobable intervals make all symbols equally frequent, which maximizes the information carried by each symbol.

10 · What is MINDIST for?

It lower-bounds the Euclidean distance between the original (normalized) series. Candidates can be ranked or rejected in the cheap symbolic space without false dismissals, as with the DTW lower bounds of Lecture 1.

11 · ApEn of a constant series? Of i.i.d. samples? What does ApEn measure?

0 and \(H(X)\). By the chain rule, \(H(X^{[m+1]}) - H(X^{[m]})\) is the uncertainty on the next value once the previous \(m\) are known: regularity, or predictability, of the series.

12 · Why can't statistical, ACF or spectral features tell a signal from its time reversal?

Reversal permutes the values (same histogram), leaves every lagged product sum unchanged (same ACF), and only conjugates the DFT up to a phase (same \(|X[k]|\)). Any function of these is identical for both.

13 · A zero-sum filter makes the activation map invariant to what? Its Lecture 1 analogue?

To an additive offset: \(\sum_j (x + \beta)w_j = \sum_j x\,w_j + \beta\sum_j w_j\) and the last sum is 0. It is the counterpart of subtracting the mean in z-normalization.

14 · 10,000 random-kernel features, 200 training series: what must you do?

Standardize the features (max, PPV and MIPV have different scales) and use a regularized linear model such as ridge, since \(D \gg M\). The dual form only needs a \(200 \times 200\) system.

15 · A correlation filter kept v3 and v4, correlated at 0.95. Problem, and remedies?

They are redundant: the second one adds almost nothing. MRMR penalizes redundancy, a wrapper sees that the pair is no better than one of them, and the LASSO tends to keep only one of two correlated features.

16 · A feature has ρ = 0 with the labels. Is it useless?

Not necessarily: correlation only sees linear links. A feature with the same mean in both classes but a different spread has ρ ≈ 0 and Fisher ≈ 0, yet mutual information, trees and k-NN can use it (v6 in the lab).

17 · Why does a Euclidean loss prefer a flat prediction to a slightly shifted correct shape?

It compares sample to sample (lock-step, Lecture 1): a shifted peak pays twice, once where it is and once where it should be, which can exceed the cost of predicting nothing. Use a loss with the right invariance, such as soft-DTW or DILATE (mini-projects 1.1–1.2).
