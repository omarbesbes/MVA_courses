Method

How to use the guide

Each lecture page is a sequence of cards, one per definition, theorem, remark or exercise of its deck, in the order of the slides. A card is built to be studied in three passes, and ticked only after the third.

  1. Read the statement, then the "in one sentence". Say why it should be true before you open "Why it is true"; check the hypotheses against "What each hypothesis buys".
  2. Prove it. Try with the hint first, then compare with the proof; note the trick chips (T01–T22) on the proof line, they come back on every page.
  3. Play, then tick "known". Move the widget's sliders and predict before you look; tick "known" when you can restate the result and its mechanism from memory.

Overview

What the course is about, lecture by lecture

Large problems in inverse problems, learning, vision and networks call for algorithms that use multicore and distributed machines. The course gives the convex-analysis theory behind them: proximal tools, splitting techniques and majorization–minimization. Each page answers one question; here are its central results and the tricks that prove them.

The thread

One principle behind everything: Fermat → fixed point → splitting

Every algorithm of pages 4–6 is a fixed-point iteration of an averaged (or nonexpansive) operator whose fixed points are the solutions. Page 7 adds a second principle for what the first cannot reach: majorize, then minimize.

Interactive · one chain of five steps, six algorithms

Pick an algorithm: the boxes and arrows it uses light up, and the panel below spells out each step of the chain for that algorithm (inclusion, operator, why it is averaged, what the iteration needs). Hover a box to see its arrows; click it to open the card.

Why every algorithm is a fixed-point iteration of an averaged operator

Take the problem of minimizing $f+g$. Fermat's rule says that $\hat x$ is a solution exactly when $0\in\partial(f+g)(\hat x)$. The sum rule splits the right-hand side into $\partial f(\hat x)+\partial g(\hat x)$ when a qualification condition holds (always, if one of the two functions is finite-valued). We now look for a zero of a sum of two operators, both monotone. A monotone operator has a resolvent, and for a subdifferential the resolvent is the prox: $\prox_{\gamma f}=(\Id+\gamma\partial f)^{-1}$ (characterization). Rewriting the inclusion with resolvents turns it into a fixed-point equation $\hat x=T\hat x$ (the dictionary T15 zeros = fixed points). Three facts then do all the work.

  1. The fixed points are the solutions: $\Fix T=\Argmin(f+g)$ for Forward–Backward; for Douglas–Rachford the solutions are the images of the fixed points by $\prox_{\gamma g}$ (DR fixed points).
  2. $T$ is averaged or nonexpansive, because it is assembled from pieces that are: $\prox_{\gamma f}$ is $\tfrac12$-averaged (firmly nonexpansive), its reflection $2\prox_{\gamma f}-\Id$ is nonexpansive (rprox), a gradient step $\Id-\gamma\nabla g$ is $\tfrac{\gamma\nu}2$-averaged for $\gamma\lt2/\nu$ (Baillon–Haddad), and averagedness survives composition with an explicit constant (composition formula, T22).
  3. Relaxed iterations of such a $T$ converge weakly to a fixed point (Krasnosel'skii–Mann, averaged iteration): Fejér monotonicity gives boundedness and converging distances, the λ-identity makes the residuals summable, demiclosedness puts every cluster point in $\Fix T$ (T12 T13 T14).

So the step sizes and relaxation ranges are not separate facts to memorize: they are what the averagedness count allows. For Forward–Backward, composing a $\tfrac12$-averaged prox with a $\tfrac{\gamma\nu}2$-averaged gradient step gives a $\tfrac1\delta$-averaged operator with $\delta=2-\tfrac{\gamma\nu}2$, hence $\lambda_n\in[0,\delta]$. For Douglas–Rachford, the update $x_{n+1}=x_n+\lambda_n(z_n-y_n)$ is $x_{n+1}=x_n+\tfrac{\lambda_n}2(Tx_n-x_n)$, a Krasnosel'skii–Mann step with $\mu_n=\lambda_n/2\in[0,1]$, hence $\lambda_n\in[0,2]$.

Where duality enters

The weak point of the first principle is a composite term $g\circ L$: its prox has no closed form in general (page 3 gives one only when $LL^*=\mu\Id$, composition with a semi-orthogonal $L$). Conjugation takes $L$ out of $g$ (T17 dualize): $g(Lx)=\sup_v\ps{v}{Lx}-g^*(v)$. The problem becomes a saddle-point problem in $(x,v)$, and its dual problem $\min_v f^*(-L^*v)+g^*(v)$ involves $g^*$ alone. The prox of $g^*$ costs one prox of $g$, by the Moreau decomposition: $\prox_{\gamma g^*}u=u-\gamma\prox_{\gamma^{-1}g}(\gamma^{-1}u)$. Two strategies follow. ADMM runs Douglas–Rachford on the dual: its $x$-step minimizes $f$ plus a quadratic in $Lx$, and its convergence needs $L^*L$ invertible (ADMM converges). The primal-dual algorithm runs Forward–Backward on the pair $(x,v)$ in a metric that absorbs $L$: it only applies $L$ and $L^*$, and pays with the step condition $\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm{L}^2\gt\beta/2$. In both cases the limit is a Kuhn–Tucker point, which certifies the primal and the dual solution at once.

The second principle: no prox? majorize

Some criteria have no computable prox at all, or are not convex (edge-preserving penalties, the tomography criterion of Lab 4). Page 7 replaces "compute a resolvent" by "minimize a surrogate": at $x_n$, build $h(\cdot,x_n)\ge f$ with $h(x_n,x_n)=f(x_n)$, and take $x_{n+1}\in\Argmin h(\cdot,x_n)$ (MM algorithm). The sandwich $f(x_{n+1})\le h(x_{n+1},x_n)\le h(x_n,x_n)=f(x_n)$ makes $f$ decrease for free (T19). Quadratic majorants (the descent lemma, a Hessian bound, the half-quadratic construction, a diagonal bound from Jensen) make each step a preconditioned gradient step $x_{n+1}=x_n-\theta_nA(x_n)^{-1}\nabla f(x_n)$. Convergence is proved differently from pages 4–6: not by Fejér monotonicity, but by sufficient descent, a telescoping sum and coercivity (MM quadratic convergence, T13). The two principles meet in VMFB: majorize the smooth part only, keep the nonsmooth part exact, and the MM step is a Forward–Backward step in the metric $A(x_n)$; with $A(x_n)\equiv\beta\Id$ it is Forward–Backward with step $1/\beta$.

Say it out loud

Write the optimality condition as an inclusion; replace each subdifferential by its resolvent to get a fixed-point equation; check that the operator is averaged and count the constant; relax and iterate. If a resolvent is not computable, dualize (T17) or majorize (T19).

Two habits of the slides are worth adopting at once. Functions take values in $]-\infty,+\infty]$, so that constraints are written $+\ind_C$ and every problem is unconstrained. And every convergence statement is weak in $\H$; in $\R^N$, where you compute, weak and strong convergence are the same thing.

Preparing the exam

How the exam works

According to the course website, the exam of 2026/2027 is based on a research paper to study, still to be announced ("TBA"); the website also offers a past exam (exam19.pdf). Neither is in the course folder: download them from the course website. A paper-based exam rewards one skill above all: reading someone else's algorithm in the language of the course. For each algorithm of the paper, be ready to write the problem as $f+g\circ L\,(+h)$, say which terms are proximable, smooth or constraints, compute the proxes and conjugates that appear, recognize the algorithm (FB, DR, PPXA+, ADMM, primal-dual, MM), state its fixed-point equation and its step conditions, and adapt the convergence proof.

Fifteen proofs most likely to be asked

Short, classical, and reused by everything after them. For each one, the route in one line and the tricks it uses.

  1. Existence in finite dimension (coercive): a minimizing sequence stays in a ball (coercivity), has a convergent subsequence (finite dimension), and lsc passes to the limit. T02 T03
  2. Convex minimizers: local = global, uniqueness: a chord from the candidate to a better point would go below the candidate's value. T04
  3. Convexity ⇔ monotone gradient: write the gradient inequality at $x$ and at $y$ and add; conversely, integrate $\varphi'$ along the segment. T05 T06
  4. Fenchel–Young and $\partial f^*=(\partial f)^{-1}$: the inequality is the definition of $f^*$; equality is the subgradient inequality; the inversion uses $f^{**}=f$. T10
  5. The prox characterization: Fermat's rule for $f+\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-x}^2$, the sum rule with a smooth term, then rewrite. T08
  6. Moreau decomposition: write the characterization of $\prox_{\gamma f^*}$ and invert it with $\partial f^*=(\partial f)^{-1}$. T08 T17
  7. The prox of a quadratic (TD): solve $x-p=\nabla f(p)=\gamma L^*(Lp-z)$, a linear system. T08
  8. prox is firmly nonexpansive: the characterization at two points and the monotonicity of $\partial f$. T06 T08
  9. Fejér convergence (with Opial's lemma): the distances converge; two weak cluster points would give two limits of $\ps{x_n}{\hat x'-\hat x}$. T14
  10. Krasnosel'skii–Mann: λ-identity, telescoping sum, residual $\to0$, demiclosedness, Fejér convergence. T12 T13 T14
  11. Forward–Backward: fixed points by Fermat + sum rule; the averagedness count $\tfrac1\delta$; the averaged iteration theorem. T15 T22
  12. Douglas–Rachford fixed points: set $x=\prox_{\gamma g}y$ and show that $Ty=y$ exactly when $0\in\partial f(x)+\partial g(x)$. T08 T11 T15
  13. Weak duality: Fenchel–Young for $f$ at $(x,-L^*v)$ and for $g$ at $(Lx,v)$, then add. T06 T10
  14. Kuhn–Tucker points solve both problems: the sum inclusion and Fermat for the primal; the two Fenchel–Young equalities give a zero gap. T08 T10
  15. Sufficient descent of MM quadratic: majorize at $x_{n+1}$, insert the update, use the bounds on $A(x_n)$ and $\theta_n$, telescope. T13 T19

Formulas to know cold

All of them, with their hypotheses, are on the constants cheat-sheet and in the prox cookbook of the toolbox. The short list:

  • KM: $\lambda_n\in[0,1]$, $\sum\lambda_n(1-\lambda_n)=+\infty$. Averaged iteration: $\lambda_n\in[0,1/\alpha]$, $\sum\lambda_n(1-\alpha\lambda_n)=+\infty$.
  • Composition of an $\alpha_1$- and an $\alpha_2$-averaged operator: $\alpha=\frac{\alpha_1+\alpha_2-2\alpha_1\alpha_2}{1-\alpha_1\alpha_2}$; convex combination: $\lambda\alpha_1+(1-\lambda)\alpha_2$.
  • FB: $\gamma\in\,]0,2/\nu[$, $\delta=2-\gamma\nu/2$, $\lambda_n\in[0,\delta]$, $\sum\lambda_n(\delta-\lambda_n)=+\infty$. DR: any $\gamma\gt0$, $\lambda_n\in[0,2]$, $\sum\lambda_n(2-\lambda_n)=+\infty$.
  • Primal-dual: $\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm{L}^2\gt\beta/2$; relaxed: $\delta=2-\tfrac\beta2\big(\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm{L}^2\big)^{-1}$. MM quadratic: $\theta_n\in\,]0,2[$.
  • Proxes: $\prox_{\ind_C}=P_C$; $\prox_{\chi\abs{\cdot}}$ is soft-thresholding at level $\chi$; $\prox_{\frac\gamma2\norm{L\cdot-z}^2}=(\Id+\gamma L^*L)^{-1}(\cdot+\gamma L^*z)$; $\prox_{\gamma f^*}x=x-\gamma\prox_{\gamma^{-1}f}(\gamma^{-1}x)$; $\prox_{\sigma_C}=\Id-P_C$.
  • Conjugates: $\big(\tfrac12\norm{\cdot}^2\big)^*=\tfrac12\norm{\cdot}^2$; $\ind_C^*=\sigma_C$; the conjugate of a norm is the indicator of the dual unit ball; $\ps{c}{\cdot}^*=\ind_{\{c\}}$. Dual of $\min_x f(x)+g(Lx)$: $\min_v f^*(-L^*v)+g^*(v)$.

Six questions in the style of the exam

Written for this guide; the memorize page has more exam-style problems. Try each one on paper before opening the answer.

The $\ell^1$ norm and its conjugate. Let $\lambda\gt0$ and $f=\lambda\norm{\cdot}_1$ on $\R^N$. (a) Compute $\prox_{\gamma f}$. (b) Identify $f^*$, deduce $\prox_{\gamma f^*}$ and check it with the Moreau decomposition.

(a) $f$ is separable, so its prox acts coordinate by coordinate, and the characterization $x_i-p_i\in\gamma\lambda\,\partial\abs{\cdot}(p_i)$ gives soft-thresholding at level $\gamma\lambda$: $p_i=\sgn(x_i)\max\{\abs{x_i}-\gamma\lambda,0\}$.

(b) $\lambda\norm{\cdot}_1=\sigma_C$ with $C=[-\lambda,\lambda]^N$ (a norm is the support function of the dual ball), so $f^*=\ind_C$ and $\prox_{\gamma f^*}=P_C$, the clipping to $[-\lambda,\lambda]$, for every $\gamma$. Check: $x-\gamma\prox_{\gamma^{-1}f}(x/\gamma)=x-\operatorname{soft}_\lambda(x)$, which is exactly the clipping.

Where does $\delta=2-\gamma\nu/2$ come from? Let $f\in\Gz{\H}$ and let $g$ be convex with a $\nu$-Lipschitz gradient. Show that $T=\prox_{\gamma f}\circ(\Id-\gamma\nabla g)$ is $\tfrac1\delta$-averaged for $\gamma\in\,]0,2/\nu[$, and deduce the relaxation range of Forward–Backward.

$\prox_{\gamma f}$ is $\tfrac12$-averaged and $\Id-\gamma\nabla g$ is $\tfrac{\gamma\nu}2$-averaged (Baillon–Haddad). The composition formula with $\alpha_1=\tfrac12$ and $\alpha_2=\tfrac{\gamma\nu}2$ gives $\alpha=\frac{1/2+\gamma\nu/2-\gamma\nu/2}{1-\gamma\nu/4}=\frac1{2-\gamma\nu/2}=\frac1\delta$. The averaged iteration theorem allows $\lambda_n\in[0,1/\alpha]=[0,\delta]$ with $\sum\lambda_n(1-\alpha\lambda_n)=+\infty$, that is $\sum\lambda_n(\delta-\lambda_n)=+\infty$.

A dual problem. Write the Fenchel–Rockafellar dual of $\min_x\tfrac12\norm{x-z}^2+\lambda\norm{Dx}_1$, where $D$ is a finite-difference operator, and express the primal solution through a dual solution.

Take $f=\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-z}^2$, $g=\lambda\norm{\cdot}_1$, $L=D$. Then $f^*(u)=\tfrac12\norm{u}^2+\ps{u}{z}$ and $g^*=\ind_{[-\lambda,\lambda]^M}$, so the dual problem is $\min_v\tfrac12\norm{D^*v}^2-\ps{D^*v}{z}$ subject to $\norm{v}_\infty\le\lambda$, that is, up to a constant, $\min_{\norm{v}_\infty\le\lambda}\tfrac12\norm{z-D^*v}^2$. The Kuhn–Tucker inclusion $-D^*\hat v\in\partial f(\hat x)=\{\hat x-z\}$ gives $\hat x=z-D^*\hat v$. The dual is a box-constrained least-squares problem: projected gradient, i.e. Forward–Backward on the dual, solves it.

Recognize the algorithm. A paper iterates $x_{n+1}=\prox_{\tau f}(x_n-\tau L^*v_n)$ and $v_{n+1}=\prox_{\sigma g^*}\big(v_n+\sigma L(2x_{n+1}-x_n)\big)$. Which problem does it solve, when does it converge, and how do you compute $\prox_{\sigma g^*}$?

It is the primal-dual algorithm of page 6 with $h=0$, for $\min_x f(x)+g(Lx)$; its fixed points are the Kuhn–Tucker pairs $(\hat x,\hat v)$. A zero gradient is $\beta$-Lipschitz for every $\beta\gt0$, so the condition $\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm{L}^2\gt\beta/2$ can be met as soon as $\tau\sigma\norm{L}^2\lt1$: the iteration converges under $\tau\sigma\norm{L}^2\lt1$ when a Kuhn–Tucker point exists. By the Moreau decomposition, $\prox_{\sigma g^*}u=u-\sigma\prox_{\sigma^{-1}g}(u/\sigma)$: one prox of $g$.

A half-quadratic step. Let $\psi(u)=\sqrt{1+u^2/\delta^2}$. Show that $\psi(\abs{\cdot})$ has at $y$ a quadratic majorant of curvature $\omega(\abs{y})=1/\big(\delta^2\psi(y)\big)$, and write one MM quadratic step for $f(x)=\tfrac12\norm{Hx-b}^2+\lambda\sum_n\psi([Gx]_n)$.

$t\mapsto\psi(\sqrt t)=\sqrt{1+t/\delta^2}$ is concave on $[0,+\infty[$, $\dot\psi(u)=u/\big(\delta^2\psi(u)\big)\ge0$ for $u\ge0$, and $\omega(u)=\dot\psi(u)/u=1/\big(\delta^2\psi(u)\big)$ has the finite limit $1/\delta^2$ at $0$. The half-quadratic theorem gives $\psi(\abs{x})\le\psi(\abs{y})+\dot\psi(y)(x-y)+\tfrac12\omega(\abs{y})(x-y)^2$. Summing majorants, $f\le f(x_k)+\ps{\nabla f(x_k)}{\cdot-x_k}+\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-x_k}^2_{A(x_k)}$ with $A(x_k)=H^\top H+\lambda G^\top\diag\big(\omega(Gx_k)\big)G$, and the step is $x_{k+1}=x_k-\theta_kA(x_k)^{-1}\nabla f(x_k)$ with $\theta_k\in\,]0,2[$.

Why the shadow? In Douglas–Rachford, why do we return $y_n=\prox_{\gamma g}x_n$ rather than $x_n$? Give an example where $x_n$ converges to a point that is not a solution.

The solutions are $\prox_{\gamma g}(\Fix T)$ with $T=\rprox_{\gamma f}\rprox_{\gamma g}$: a fixed point is $\bar x=\hat x+\gamma u$ with $u\in\partial g(\hat x)$ and $-u\in\partial f(\hat x)$, not $\hat x$ itself. Example on $\R$: $f=\abs{\cdot-1}$, $g=\tfrac12(\cdot-3)^2$, $\gamma=1$. The minimizer of $f+g$ is $\hat x=2$, but $\bar x=\hat x+\nabla g(\hat x)=1$: indeed $\prox_g(1)=2$, $\rprox_g(1)=3$, $\prox_f(3)=2$, $\rprox_f(3)=1$, so $T(1)=1$. The governing sequence converges to $1$, its shadow to $2$.
