Subdifferential & conjugate

A derivative for functions that have none: the subdifferential $\partial f$, Fermat's rule $0\in\partial f(\hat x)$, the calculus that computes $\partial$ of sums and compositions, and the Legendre–Fenchel conjugate $f^*$, whose dictionary $\partial f^*=(\partial f)^{-1}$ runs the rest of the course.

Overview

Where we are

Page 1 settled existence and characterized the minimizers of a differentiable convex function by the variational inequality $\ps{\nabla f(\hat x)}{y-\hat x}\ge0$. The functions we actually want to minimize ($\norm{\cdot}_1$, indicators of constraint sets, total variation) have no gradient. This page builds the object that replaces it.

Three ideas carry the whole page, and you should be able to say them in one breath.

  1. Slopes instead of derivatives. At a point $x$, a convex function may have many affine functions that touch its graph at $x$ and stay below it everywhere. The set of their slopes is the subdifferential $\partial f(x)$. For a differentiable convex function it is $\{\nabla f(x)\}$; for $\abs{\cdot}$ at $0$ it is $[-1,1]$; for an indicator function it is the normal cone.
  2. Fermat's rule. $\hat x$ minimizes $f$ if and only if $0\in\partial f(\hat x)$ — the horizontal line touches the graph from below. This is the complete answer to the question "characterization" of page 1, and it holds with no convexity at all. All the difficulty has moved into computing $\partial f$: hence a calculus (scaling, sums, compositions with $L$, separable sums) with one deep theorem, Moreau–Rockafellar, that tells you when $\partial(f+g\circ L)=\partial f+L^*\partial g(L\cdot)$.
  3. Change of viewpoint: the conjugate. Instead of asking "which slopes support $f$ at $x$", ask "for a given slope $u$, where is the best affine minorant of slope $u$": its intercept is $-f^*(u)$. This defines the Legendre–Fenchel conjugate $f^*$, a function of slopes. It is always lsc convex, it satisfies $f^{**}=f$ exactly for $f\in\Gz{\H}$ (Moreau–Fenchel), and it inverts the subdifferential: $u\in\partial f(x)\iff x\in\partial f^*(u)$ (Fenchel–Young). This dictionary is what pages 3 (Moreau decomposition) and 6 (duality) live on.
The one principle

The course is a long commentary on one line: $0\in\partial f(\hat x)$. Page 3 rewrites it as a fixed-point equation $\hat x=\prox_{\gamma f}\hat x$, pages 4–5 iterate such equations, page 6 dualizes them with $f^*$. Learn this page well and the others become bookkeeping.

The subdifferential

Jean-Jacques Moreau (1923–2014) replaced "the tangent line" by "all the lines that touch from below". That single move gives a derivative to every convex function, a one-line Fermat rule, and a derivative for constraints: the normal cone.

Definition

The (Moreau) subdifferential

Part III · slide 4SlidesKey#
Definition

Let $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be a proper function. The (Moreau) subdifferential of $f$, denoted by $\partial f$, is the set-valued map $$\partial f:\H\to2^{\H},\qquad x\mapsto\big\{u\in\H\ \big|\ (\forall y\in\H)\ \ \ps{y-x}{u}+f(x)\le f(y)\big\}.$$ An element $u\in\partial f(x)$ is called a subgradient of $f$ at $x$.

In one sentence

$u\in\partial f(x)$ means: the affine function $y\mapsto f(x)+\ps{y-x}{u}$, of slope $u$ and passing through $(x,f(x))$, lies below the whole graph of $f$. The subdifferential is the set of slopes of the affine minorants that touch the graph at $x$.

Why the definition makes sense

For a differentiable convex function the tangent at $x$ lies below the graph (page 1, Gâteaux derivative + convexity), and it is the only affine function through $(x,f(x))$ with that property. At a kink there is no tangent, but there is still a whole fan of lines through the kink that stay below the graph; at $0$ for $\abs{\cdot}$, every slope in $[-1,1]$ works. Moreau's idea: forget the tangent, keep the fan. The definition asks for a global inequality (for every $y\in\H$), so it is really a definition tailored to convex functions: for a nonconvex $f$ the fan is usually empty.

Three things to notice. (1) No convexity is assumed in the definition: $\partial f$ is defined for every proper $f$, it is just more interesting for convex ones. (2) The inequality is trivially true when $f(y)=+\infty$, so only $y\in\dom f$ matters. (3) $\partial f(x)$ is a set: empty, a singleton, an interval, a cone… the map $\partial f$ takes values in $2^{\H}$ (the set of subsets of $\H$).

Worked examples (read $\partial f(x)$ on the right panel of the widget)
  • $f=\abs{\cdot}$ on $\R$: $\partial f(x)=\{\sgn(x)\}$ for $x\ne0$ and $\partial f(0)=[-1,1]$. Indeed $u\in\partial f(0)\iff(\forall y)\ uy\le\abs{y}\iff\abs{u}\le1$.
  • $f$ convex and Gâteaux differentiable at $x$: $\partial f(x)=\{\nabla f(x)\}$ (theorem below).
  • $x\notin\dom f$: $\partial f(x)=\emptyset$, because $f(x)=+\infty$ cannot be $\le f(y)$ for $y\in\dom f$ (properties).
  • $f=-\abs{\cdot}$ (concave): $\partial f(x)=\emptyset$ for every $x$. No line through a point of a "roof" stays below the roof.
  • $f=\ind_C$: $\partial\ind_C(x)$ is the normal cone $N_C(x)$, a cone of outward directions.

Pitfall

The inequality must hold for every $y\in\H$, not only near $x$. A line that is locally below the graph but crosses it far away is not a subgradient. This is why $\partial f$ is a global object, and why Fermat's rule below detects global minimizers only.

Subgradient = slope of a supporting line. $u\in\partial f(x)\iff f\ge f(x)+\ps{\cdot-x}{u}$ on all of $\H$. Picture: the fan of lines through the kink.

Theorem

Fermat's rule: $0\in\partial f(x)\iff x\in\Argmin f$

Part III · slide 5SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Let $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be proper. For every $x\in\H$, $$0\in\partial f(x)\iff x\in\Argmin f.$$

In one sentence

The horizontal line through $(x,f(x))$ lies below the whole graph exactly when $f(x)$ is the lowest value.

Why it is true

Put $u=0$ in the definition of $\partial f(x)$: the inequality $\ps{y-x}{0}+f(x)\le f(y)$ is just $f(x)\le f(y)$. "For every $y$" then says that $x$ is a global minimizer. There is nothing more: Fermat's rule is the definition of the subdifferential read at the slope $0$. The depth is elsewhere — in the rules that let you compute $\partial f$ for a sum, a composition, an indicator. See it in the subdifferential widget above: put $x$ at a minimizer of $\abs{x}$, Huber, $\max(0,x)$ or $x^2/2$ and the horizontal line (green) is one of the supporting lines; move $x$ away and no supporting line is horizontal any more. Say it out loud: minimizing $f$ is solving the inclusion $0\in\partial f(x)$.

What each hypothesis buys
  • No convexity, no differentiability is needed: the equivalence is a tautology for every proper $f$. But for a nonconvex $f$ the rule only sees global minimizers. At a local minimizer $x$ that is not global, $0\notin\partial f(x)$: the horizontal line through $(x,f(x))$ crosses the graph elsewhere. (Other slopes may still support $f$ there: for $f=\abs{\cdot}$ on $[-1,+\infty[$ and $f=\max(-\tfrac12,3x+4)$ on $]-\infty,-1[$, the point $0$ is a local, non-global minimizer and $1\in\partial f(0)$.) And $\partial f(x)=\emptyset$ wherever $f$ lies strictly above its closed convex envelope (the largest lsc convex function below $f$): on $]-1,1[$ for $(\abs{x}-1)^2$, everywhere for $-x^2$. So the rule cannot locate local minimizers, and the calculus that makes it computable (the sum rule with equality) needs $\Gz{}$ functions.
  • Proper only avoids the degenerate $f\equiv+\infty$; it is the standing assumption of the definition.
Hint — try it first

Write the definition of $0\in\partial f(x)$. What is $\ps{y-x}{0}$?

Proof (two lines)

1. Unfold. By definition, $0\in\partial f(x)\iff(\forall y\in\H)\ \ps{y-x}{0}+f(x)\le f(y)\iff(\forall y\in\H)\ f(x)\le f(y)$.

2. Read. The last statement, together with $f(x)\lt+\infty$ (forced by taking $y\in\dom f$, nonempty since $f$ is proper), is exactly $x\in\Argmin f$ as defined on page 1: $f(x)=\inf f\lt+\infty$.

□
Trick

Every algorithm of this course is obtained by rewriting $0\in\partial f(\hat x)$: split $f=g+h$ and use the sum rule, write $0\in\nabla g(\hat x)+\partial h(\hat x)$ as $\hat x-\gamma\nabla g(\hat x)\in(\Id+\gamma\partial h)(\hat x)$, then invert (T08 Fermat + sum rule, then rewrite). Fermat's rule is the first line of every derivation.

Pitfall

$\Argmin f$ may be empty even when $f$ is bounded below ($f=e^x$): then $0\notin\partial f(x)$ for every $x$, and Fermat's rule says so — it characterizes minimizers, it does not create them. Existence is page 1's job (coercivity).

To remember

$0\in\partial f(\hat x)$: the flat line supports the graph. True for any proper $f$; global minimizers only.

Proposition

Subgradients; $\partial f(x)=\emptyset$ outside the domain; $\partial f(x)$ is closed and convex

Part III · slide 6Slides#
Statement

Let $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be proper.

  • $u\in\partial f(x)$ is called a subgradient of $f$ at $x$.
  • If $x\notin\dom f$, then $\partial f(x)=\emptyset$.
  • For every $x\in\dom f$, $\partial f(x)$ is a closed and convex set.
In one sentence

$\partial f(x)$ is an intersection of closed half-spaces (one per test point $y$), hence closed and convex; and no line can touch the graph at a point where $f=+\infty$.

Why it is true

Fix $x\in\dom f$. For each $y\in\dom f$, the condition "$\ps{y-x}{u}\le f(y)-f(x)$" is a linear inequality in $u$ with a finite right-hand side: it describes a closed half-space of $\H$ (or all of $\H$ when $y=x$). Points $y\notin\dom f$ impose nothing. So $\partial f(x)$ is the intersection of a family of closed half-spaces, and intersections preserve both closedness and convexity. The emptiness outside the domain is the same inequality read with $f(x)=+\infty$ on the left: impossible as soon as some $y$ has $f(y)\lt+\infty$.

What each hypothesis buys
  • Proper: gives a $y\in\dom f$ to test against, which is what empties $\partial f(x)$ outside $\dom f$.
  • $x\in\dom f$: makes $f(y)-f(x)$ a well-defined real number for $y\in\dom f$, so that each constraint is an honest half-space. The set may still be empty, bounded or unbounded — closed convex says nothing about size.
Hint — try it first

Write $\partial f(x)=\bigcap_{y\in\dom f}\{u:\ \ps{y-x}{u}\le f(y)-f(x)\}$ and look at one set of the intersection.

Proof T01 $+\infty$ values

1. Outside the domain. Let $x\notin\dom f$, i.e. $f(x)=+\infty$, and let $u\in\H$. Since $f$ is proper there is $y_0\in\dom f$; the inequality $\ps{y_0-x}{u}+f(x)\le f(y_0)$ reads $+\infty\le f(y_0)\lt+\infty$, which is false. Hence no $u$ belongs to $\partial f(x)$: $\partial f(x)=\emptyset$.

2. A half-space per test point. Let $x\in\dom f$. For $y\notin\dom f$ the inequality $\ps{y-x}{u}+f(x)\le+\infty$ holds for every $u$. Therefore $$\partial f(x)=\bigcap_{y\in\dom f}H_y,\qquad H_y=\{u\in\H\ |\ \ps{y-x}{u}\le f(y)-f(x)\}.$$ For $y\ne x$, $H_y$ is a closed half-space: $u\mapsto\ps{y-x}{u}$ is continuous and linear, and $f(y)-f(x)\in\R$. For $y=x$, $H_y=\H$.

3. Intersect. Each $H_y$ is closed (preimage of $]-\infty,f(y)-f(x)]$ under a continuous map) and convex (a linear inequality is preserved by convex combinations). An arbitrary intersection of closed sets is closed and an arbitrary intersection of convex sets is convex. So $\partial f(x)$ is closed and convex.

□
Pitfall

Closed and convex, but possibly empty inside the domain: $f(x)=-\sqrt{x}$ on $[0,+\infty[$ ($+\infty$ elsewhere) belongs to $\Gz{\R}$ and has $\partial f(0)=\emptyset$, because the slope at $0$ is $-\infty$: no line of finite slope through $(0,0)$ stays below the graph. For $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $\partial f(x)\ne\emptyset$ at every $x\in\inte\dom f$ ($f$ is continuous there; in finite dimension convexity alone suffices): inside $\dom f$, emptiness can only happen at points of $\dom f\setminus\inte\dom f$ (Extra, not on the slides).

To remember

$\partial f(x)=\bigcap_{y}\{u:\ \ps{y-x}{u}\le f(y)-f(x)\}$: closed convex, empty off the domain.

Proposition

The subdifferential is a monotone operator

Part III · slide 7SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be proper. Its subdifferential is a monotone operator, i.e. $$\big(\forall(x_1,x_2)\in\H^2\big)\big(\forall u_1\in\partial f(x_1)\big)\big(\forall u_2\in\partial f(x_2)\big)\qquad\ps{u_1-u_2}{x_1-x_2}\ge0.$$

In one sentence

Moving from $x_2$ to $x_1$, the subgradient never turns against the displacement: $\ps{u_1-u_2}{x_1-x_2}\ge0$. In 1-D: the graph of $\partial f$ is a nondecreasing "curve", vertical segments allowed.

Why it is true

Each subgradient inequality bounds $f$ at the other point: $u_1$ says $f(x_2)\ge f(x_1)+\ps{x_2-x_1}{u_1}$, $u_2$ says $f(x_1)\ge f(x_2)+\ps{x_1-x_2}{u_2}$. Add the two: the values $f(x_1)+f(x_2)$ appear on both sides and cancel (they are finite), leaving $0\ge\ps{x_2-x_1}{u_1}+\ps{x_1-x_2}{u_2}=-\ps{x_1-x_2}{u_1-u_2}$. The mechanism is "the two supporting lines, each evaluated at the other's contact point": the sum of the two gaps is nonnegative, and that sum is exactly $\ps{u_1-u_2}{x_1-x_2}$.

What each hypothesis buys
  • Proper only. No convexity: the statement says nothing at points where $\partial f$ is empty (frequent for nonconvex $f$), and it is the whole story when $f$ is convex.
  • Finiteness of $f(x_1),f(x_2)$ (automatic: $\partial f(x_i)\ne\emptyset\Rightarrow x_i\in\dom f$) is what allows the cancellation.
Hint — try it first

Write the definition of $u_1\in\partial f(x_1)$ with $y=x_2$, and the definition of $u_2\in\partial f(x_2)$ with $y=x_1$. Add.

Proof T06 write the inequality twice and add

1. Twice. By definition of $u_1\in\partial f(x_1)$ tested at $y=x_2$, and of $u_2\in\partial f(x_2)$ tested at $y=x_1$: $$\ps{x_2-x_1}{u_1}+f(x_1)\le f(x_2),\qquad\ps{x_1-x_2}{u_2}+f(x_2)\le f(x_1).$$

2. Add. Both $f(x_1)$ and $f(x_2)$ are finite (a point with a nonempty subdifferential is in $\dom f$ by the previous proposition), so adding and cancelling them is legitimate: $$\ps{x_2-x_1}{u_1}+\ps{x_1-x_2}{u_2}\le0\iff\ps{x_2-x_1}{u_1-u_2}\le0\iff\ps{x_1-x_2}{u_1-u_2}\ge0.$$

□
Trick

Whenever you hold two instances of a defining inequality, arrange them so that the unknown values cancel, and add (T06). Here each one is evaluated at the other's point, so $f(x_1)+f(x_2)$ cancels and only the inner products survive — the same swap proves that prox is firmly nonexpansive. Cousins of the move: adding two subgradient inequalities written at the same test point proves the sum inclusion, and adding two Fenchel–Young inequalities proves weak duality.

Theorem

Convex and Gâteaux differentiable at $x$ $\Rightarrow$ $\partial f(x)=\{\nabla f(x)\}$

Part III · slide 8SlidesKey#
Statement

If $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ is convex and Gâteaux differentiable at $x$, then $$\partial f(x)=\{\nabla f(x)\},\qquad\text{where}\quad(\forall y\in\H)\quad\ps{\nabla f(x)}{y}=\lim_{\alpha\to0,\ \alpha\ne0}\frac{f(x+\alpha y)-f(x)}{\alpha}.$$

In one sentence

The tangent is a supporting line (convexity), and a supporting line through a smooth point can only be the tangent (differentiability).

Why it is true

Two separate mechanisms, one per inclusion. $\nabla f(x)$ is a subgradient because of convexity: the chord from $(x,f(x))$ to $(y,f(y))$ lies above the graph, so the slope of the graph at $x$ in the direction $y-x$ — the directional derivative $\ps{\nabla f(x)}{y-x}$ — is at most the slope of the chord, $f(y)-f(x)$. That is the subgradient inequality. No other subgradient exists because a subgradient $u$ gives $f(x+\alpha y)\ge f(x)+\alpha\ps{u}{y}$ for all directions $y$: the directional derivative dominates $\ps{u}{y}$ in every direction, i.e. $\ps{\nabla f(x)-u}{y}\ge0$ for all $y$. A linear form that is nonnegative everywhere is zero (test it on $y=u-\nabla f(x)$). Note that this second half uses no convexity at all.

What each hypothesis buys
  • Convex: used only for $\nabla f(x)\in\partial f(x)$ (the tangent lies below). Without it, $\partial f(x)\subset\{\nabla f(x)\}$ still holds, so for a differentiable nonconvex $f$ the subdifferential is either $\{\nabla f(x)\}$ or $\emptyset$ ($f=-x^2$: empty everywhere).
  • Gâteaux differentiable at $x$: the two-sided directional derivative exists in every direction $y$ and equals $\ps{\nabla f(x)}{y}$ for one vector $\nabla f(x)$ — a continuous linear function of the direction. Step 1 uses it in the direction $y-x$; step 3 uses exactly this linearity (test $y=u-\nabla f(x)$). No Fréchet differentiability is needed.
Hint — try it first

$\subset$-direction: from $u\in\partial f(x)$ get $\ps{\nabla f(x)}{y}\ge\ps{u}{y}$ for all $y$, then choose $y$ well. $\supset$-direction: convexity on the segment $[x,y]$, divide by $\alpha$, let $\alpha\to0^+$.

Proof T04 chord argument T07 test vector $y=u-\nabla f(x)$

1. $\nabla f(x)\in\partial f(x)$. Let $y\in\H$; if $f(y)=+\infty$ there is nothing to prove, so assume $y\in\dom f$. For every $\alpha\in\,]0,1]$, convexity gives $$f\big(x+\alpha(y-x)\big)=f\big((1-\alpha)x+\alpha y\big)\le(1-\alpha)f(x)+\alpha f(y),$$ hence $\dfrac{f(x+\alpha(y-x))-f(x)}{\alpha}\le f(y)-f(x)$. Letting $\alpha\to0^+$ and using the Gâteaux derivative in the direction $y-x$: $$\ps{\nabla f(x)}{y-x}=\lim_{\alpha\to0^+}\frac{f(x+\alpha(y-x))-f(x)}{\alpha}\le f(y)-f(x).$$ This is the subgradient inequality, so $\nabla f(x)\in\partial f(x)$.

2. Any subgradient dominates in every direction. Conversely, let $u\in\partial f(x)$. For every $\alpha\in\,]0,+\infty[$ and $y\in\H$, the subgradient inequality at the point $x+\alpha y$ reads $f(x+\alpha y)\ge f(x)+\ps{u}{x+\alpha y-x}=f(x)+\alpha\ps{u}{y}$. Dividing by $\alpha\gt0$ and letting $\alpha\to0^+$: $$\ps{\nabla f(x)}{y}=\lim_{\alpha\to0^+}\frac{f(x+\alpha y)-f(x)}{\alpha}\ge\ps{u}{y}.$$

3. Choose the test vector. Step 2 says $\ps{\nabla f(x)-u}{y}\ge0$ for every $y\in\H$. Select $y=u-\nabla f(x)$: it gives $-\norm{u-\nabla f(x)}^2\ge0$, hence $u=\nabla f(x)$. So $\partial f(x)\subset\{\nabla f(x)\}$, and with step 1, $\partial f(x)=\{\nabla f(x)\}$.

□
Trick

"$\ps{a}{y}\ge0$ for every $y$" forces $a=0$: test $y=-a$ (T07). And "divide the convexity inequality by $\alpha$ and let $\alpha\to0^+$" converts a global chord inequality into a local derivative statement (T04): you used it on page 1 for the variational inequality.

To remember

Smooth + convex: $\partial f=\{\nabla f\}$. Convexity puts the tangent below; differentiability leaves room for nothing else.

Theorem

Convexity $\iff$ gradient inequality

Part III · slide 9SlidesExam#
Statement

Let $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be Gâteaux differentiable on $\dom f$, which is an open convex set. Then $f$ is convex if and only if $$\big(\forall(x,y)\in(\dom f)^2\big)\qquad f(y)\ge f(x)+\ps{\nabla f(x)}{y-x}.$$

In one sentence

A differentiable function is convex exactly when its graph lies above all of its tangent hyperplanes.

Why it is true

"Convex $\Rightarrow$ above the tangents" is the previous theorem. The converse is the nice part: take two points $x,y$ and the intermediate point $z=\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y$. The tangent at $z$ is below the graph, so it is below $f(x)$ and below $f(y)$. But the tangent at $z$ is an affine function, and $z$ is the convex combination of $x$ and $y$ with weights $\alpha,1-\alpha$: the value of the tangent at $z$, which is $f(z)$, equals the same convex combination of its values at $x$ and $y$, each of which is $\le f(x)$, resp. $\le f(y)$. Hence $f(z)\le\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y)$. One tangent plane, pinned at the intermediate point, lifts both endpoints.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $\dom f$ convex: the intermediate point $z$ is in $\dom f$, so $\nabla f(z)$ exists and the gradient inequality may be applied at $z$. Without it the convexity inequality would involve a $z$ where $f=+\infty$ and the converse would fail.
  • $\dom f$ open: Gâteaux differentiability "on $\dom f$" needs $f$ finite on a neighbourhood of each point along every direction; openness guarantees it.
  • Outside $\dom f$ the convexity inequality is automatic ($+\infty$ on the right), so convexity of $f$ on $\H$ reduces to convexity on $\dom f$.
Hint — try it first

Write the gradient inequality twice, both times at the base point $z=\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y$: once towards $x$, once towards $y$. Note $x-z=(1-\alpha)(x-y)$ and $y-z=\alpha(y-x)$. Combine with weights $\alpha$ and $1-\alpha$.

Proof T06 two inequalities, combined

1. ($\Rightarrow$) We have already seen that the gradient inequality holds when $f$ is convex and Gâteaux differentiable at $x$: this is $\nabla f(x)\in\partial f(x)$ in the previous theorem.

2. ($\Leftarrow$) Set up. Assume the gradient inequality. Let $(x,y)\in(\dom f)^2$ and $\alpha\in[0,1]$. Since $\dom f$ is convex, $z=\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y\in\dom f$, so $\nabla f(z)$ exists. Observe that $x-z=(1-\alpha)(x-y)$ and $y-z=\alpha(y-x)$.

3. The gradient inequality at $z$, twice. Towards $x$ and towards $y$: $$f(x)\ge f(z)+\ps{\nabla f(z)}{x-z}=f(z)+(1-\alpha)\ps{\nabla f(z)}{x-y},$$ $$f(y)\ge f(z)+\ps{\nabla f(z)}{y-z}=f(z)+\alpha\ps{\nabla f(z)}{y-x}.$$

4. Combine. Multiply the first inequality by $\alpha\ge0$, the second by $1-\alpha\ge0$, and add. The gradient terms are $\alpha(1-\alpha)\ps{\nabla f(z)}{x-y}+(1-\alpha)\alpha\ps{\nabla f(z)}{y-x}=0$, so $$\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y)\ge f(z)=f\big(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y\big).$$ This is the convexity inequality on $\dom f$; when $x$ or $y$ is outside $\dom f$ the right-hand side $\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y)$ is $+\infty$ (for $\alpha\in\,]0,1[$) and the inequality is trivial. Hence $f$ is convex.

Theorem

Convexity $\iff$ monotone gradient

Part III · slide 10SlidesExam#
Statement

Let $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be Gâteaux differentiable on $\dom f$, which is an open convex set. Then $f$ is convex if and only if $\nabla f$ is monotone on $\dom f$, i.e. $$\big(\forall(x,y)\in(\dom f)^2\big)\qquad\ps{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}{y-x}\ge0.$$

In one sentence

In one dimension: $f$ is convex iff $f'$ is nondecreasing; in general, iff the gradient never turns back against the displacement.

Why it is true

($\Rightarrow$) is monotonicity of $\partial f$ plus $\partial f=\{\nabla f\}$. ($\Leftarrow$) is the 1-D fact "nondecreasing derivative $\Rightarrow$ convex", transported along a segment: restrict $f$ to the line through $x$ and $y$, $\varphi(\alpha)=f(x+\alpha(y-x))$. Monotonicity of $\nabla f$ between $x$ and $x+\alpha(y-x)$ says precisely $\varphi'(\alpha)\ge\varphi'(0)$. Integrating from $0$ to $1$, the total increase $\varphi(1)-\varphi(0)=f(y)-f(x)$ is at least $\varphi'(0)=\ps{\nabla f(x)}{y-x}$: that is the gradient inequality, hence convexity by the previous theorem. The mechanism: monotone gradient $\Rightarrow$ the directional derivative along a segment only grows $\Rightarrow$ the function lies above its tangent.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $\dom f$ open convex: the whole segment $[x,y]$ lies in $\dom f$, so $\varphi$ is differentiable on $[0,1]$ with $\varphi'(\alpha)=\ps{\nabla f(x+\alpha(y-x))}{y-x}$ (Gâteaux derivative in the direction $y-x$).
  • The slides integrate $\varphi'$; this tacitly assumes $\varphi'$ is integrable (true e.g. when $f$ is $C^1$). The mean value theorem gives the same conclusion with differentiability only: $\varphi(1)-\varphi(0)=\varphi'(\xi)\ge\varphi'(0)$ for some $\xi\in\,]0,1[$.
Hint — try it first

Proof T05 integrate along the segment

1. ($\Rightarrow$) When $f$ is convex, its subdifferential is monotone and, for every $x\in\dom f$, $\partial f(x)=\{\nabla f(x)\}$ by the theorem above. Monotonicity of $\partial f$ with $u_1=\nabla f(y)$, $u_2=\nabla f(x)$ is the claim.

2. ($\Leftarrow$) Restrict to a segment. Assume $\nabla f$ monotone on $\dom f$. For $(x,y)\in(\dom f)^2$ let $\varphi:[0,1]\to\R$, $\alpha\mapsto f(x+\alpha(y-x))$. Since $\dom f$ is convex, $x+\alpha(y-x)\in\dom f$ for all $\alpha\in[0,1]$, and by Gâteaux differentiability $\varphi$ is differentiable on $[0,1]$ with $$(\forall\alpha\in[0,1])\qquad\varphi'(\alpha)=\ps{\nabla f(x+\alpha(y-x))}{y-x}.$$

3. Monotonicity $\Rightarrow$ $\varphi'$ dominated by $\varphi'(0)$. For $\alpha\in\,]0,1]$, apply monotonicity to the pair $x$, $x+\alpha(y-x)$ (difference $\alpha(y-x)$): $$\ps{\nabla f(x+\alpha(y-x))-\nabla f(x)}{\alpha(y-x)}\ge0\iff\varphi'(\alpha)\ge\ps{\nabla f(x)}{y-x}=\varphi'(0)$$ after division by $\alpha\gt0$.

4. Integrate. $\varphi(1)-\varphi(0)=\int_0^1\varphi'(\alpha)\,d\alpha\ge\int_0^1\varphi'(0)\,d\alpha=\ps{\nabla f(x)}{y-x}$, i.e. $f(y)-f(x)\ge\ps{\nabla f(x)}{y-x}$. (Or: by the mean value theorem $\varphi(1)-\varphi(0)=\varphi'(\xi)\ge\varphi'(0)$ for some $\xi\in\,]0,1[$.)

5. Conclude. This is the gradient inequality for every $(x,y)\in(\dom f)^2$, so $f$ is convex by the previous theorem.

Proposition

$\partial\ind_C$ is the normal cone; interior, subspace and ball examples

Part III · slide 11SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $C$ be a nonempty subset of $\H$. For every $x\in\H$, $\partial\ind_C(x)$ is the normal cone to $C$ at $x$, defined by $$N_C(x)=\begin{cases}\big\{u\in\H\ \big|\ (\forall y\in C)\ \ps{u}{y-x}\le0\big\}&\text{if }x\in C,\\ \emptyset&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

  • If $x\in\inte C$, then $N_C(x)=\{0\}$.
  • If $C$ is a vector space, then for every $x\in C$, $N_C(x)=C^\perp$.
  • Let $c\in\H$, $\rho\in\,]0,+\infty[$ and $C=\overline{B}(c,\rho)=\{y\in\H\ |\ \norm{y-c}\le\rho\}$. For every $x\in C$, $$N_C(x)=\begin{cases}\{\alpha(x-c)\ |\ \alpha\in[0,+\infty[\}&\text{if }\norm{x-c}=\rho,\\ \{0\}&\text{if }\norm{x-c}\lt\rho.\end{cases}$$
In one sentence

A subgradient of the indicator at $x\in C$ is a direction $u$ that makes an obtuse angle with every direction $y-x$ pointing into $C$: an outward normal. Inside, only $0$; on a flat part of the boundary, the half-line $\R_+n$ of outward normals; at a corner, a whole cone.

Why it is true

Write the subgradient inequality for $f=\ind_C$ at $x\in C$: $\ind_C(y)\ge0+\ps{y-x}{u}$. For $y\notin C$ the left side is $+\infty$ and nothing is required; for $y\in C$ the left side is $0$, so the requirement is $\ps{u}{y-x}\le0$: the affine function $\ps{\cdot-x}{u}$ must be $\le0$ on $C$, i.e. $C$ lies in the half-space "behind" the hyperplane through $x$ orthogonal to $u$. That is what "normal" means. The three examples are then geometry: at an interior point every direction points into $C$, so only $u=0$ survives; for a subspace, $y-x$ ranges over $C$ itself and both $z$ and $-z$ are allowed, forcing $\ps{u}{z}=0$; for a ball, the only points of $C$ where a linear form $\ps{u}{\cdot}$ is maximal are $c+\rho u/\norm{u}$ (Cauchy–Schwarz), so $u$ must be a nonnegative multiple of $x-c$.

The reformulation worth memorizing: $u\in N_C(x)\iff x\in C$ and $\ps{u}{x}=\max_{y\in C}\ps{u}{y}$ — $x$ maximizes the linear form $\ps{u}{\cdot}$ over $C$. You will meet it again as $\ps{u}{x}=\sigma_C(u)$ in the support function section.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $C$ nonempty: $\ind_C$ is proper, so $\partial\ind_C$ is defined. No convexity or closedness is needed for the formula $\partial\ind_C=N_C$; they are needed for $\ind_C\in\Gz{\H}$, hence for the sum rule and for projections (page 3).
  • $x\in C$: otherwise $\ind_C(x)=+\infty$ and the subdifferential is empty (not $\{0\}$!).
Hint — try it first

Proof T01 indicator trick T07 test vector

1. $\partial\ind_C=N_C$. If $x\notin C$, $\ind_C(x)=+\infty$ and $\partial\ind_C(x)=\emptyset=N_C(x)$ (off the domain). If $x\in C$: $u\in\partial\ind_C(x)\iff(\forall y\in\H)\ \ps{y-x}{u}+0\le\ind_C(y)$. For $y\notin C$ the right side is $+\infty$: no constraint. For $y\in C$ it is $0$: the constraint is $\ps{u}{y-x}\le0$. Hence $\partial\ind_C(x)=\{u\ |\ (\forall y\in C)\ \ps{u}{y-x}\le0\}=N_C(x)$.

2. Interior point. Let $x\in\inte C$: there is $\varepsilon\gt0$ with $\overline B(x,\varepsilon)\subset C$. First, $0\in N_C(x)$ since $\ps{0}{y-x}=0\le0$. Let $u\in N_C(x)$ with $u\ne0$ and test $y=x+\varepsilon\,u/\norm{u}\in C$: $\ps{u}{y-x}=\varepsilon\norm{u}\le0$ is impossible. So $N_C(x)=\{0\}$.

3. Vector space. Let $x\in C$. Since $C$ is a vector space, $\{y-x\ |\ y\in C\}=C$. So $u\in N_C(x)\iff(\forall z\in C)\ \ps{u}{z}\le0$. Replacing $z$ by $-z\in C$ gives $\ps{u}{z}\ge0$ as well, hence $\ps{u}{z}=0$ for all $z\in C$: $u\in C^\perp$. Conversely $u\in C^\perp$ gives $\ps{u}{z}=0\le0$. So $N_C(x)=C^\perp$.

4. Ball, interior case. If $\norm{x-c}\lt\rho$, $x$ is in the open ball, which is contained in $C$: $x\in\inte C$ and step 2 gives $N_C(x)=\{0\}$.

5. Ball, boundary case, $\supset$. Let $\norm{x-c}=\rho$, $\alpha\ge0$, $u=\alpha(x-c)$ and $y\in C$. Then $$\ps{x-c}{y-x}=\ps{x-c}{(y-c)-(x-c)}=\ps{x-c}{y-c}-\rho^2\le\norm{x-c}\,\norm{y-c}-\rho^2\le\rho\cdot\rho-\rho^2=0$$ by Cauchy–Schwarz and $\norm{y-c}\le\rho$. Multiplying by $\alpha\ge0$: $\ps{u}{y-x}\le0$, so $u\in N_C(x)$.

6. Ball, boundary case, $\subset$. Let $u\in N_C(x)$, $u\ne0$ (the case $u=0$ is $\alpha=0$). The definition says $\ps{u}{y}\le\ps{u}{x}$ for all $y\in C$: $x$ maximizes $\ps{u}{\cdot}$ over the ball. But for $y\in C$, $\ps{u}{y}=\ps{u}{c}+\ps{u}{y-c}\le\ps{u}{c}+\rho\norm{u}$, with equality in Cauchy–Schwarz if and only if $y-c=\rho\,u/\norm{u}$. The maximum is therefore attained at the single point $c+\rho u/\norm{u}$, so $x=c+\rho u/\norm{u}$, i.e. $u=\frac{\norm{u}}{\rho}(x-c)$ with $\alpha=\norm{u}/\rho\ge0$.

Proposition

$\partial(\lambda f)=\lambda\,\partial f$ for $\lambda\gt0$

Part III · slide 12Slides#
Statement

Let $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be proper. Then for every $\lambda\in\,]0,+\infty[$, $\partial(\lambda f)=\lambda\,\partial f$, i.e. $\partial(\lambda f)(x)=\{\lambda u\ |\ u\in\partial f(x)\}$ for every $x$.

In one sentence

Scaling the function by $\lambda\gt0$ scales every supporting line's slope by $\lambda$ and nothing else.

Why it is true

Multiply the whole picture (graph and supporting lines) vertically by $\lambda\gt0$: a line that was below the graph stays below it, touching at the same point, and its slope is multiplied by $\lambda$. Algebraically, dividing the subgradient inequality of $\lambda f$ by $\lambda\gt0$ gives the subgradient inequality of $f$ with slope $u/\lambda$; the direction of the inequality is preserved because $\lambda\gt0$.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $\lambda\gt0$: dividing by $\lambda$ keeps the inequality's direction and $\lambda f$ stays proper. For $\lambda\lt0$ the statement is false ($f=\abs{\cdot}$: $\partial(-f)(0)=\emptyset\ne-[-1,1]$). For $\lambda=0$ it fails whatever the convention for $0\cdot(+\infty)$: with $0\cdot(+\infty)=0$, $0\cdot f\equiv0$ and $\partial(0\cdot f)(x)=\{0\}$ everywhere; with $0\cdot(+\infty)=+\infty$, $0\cdot f=\ind_{\dom f}$ and $\partial(0\cdot f)(x)=N_{\dom f}(x)\ni0$ on $\dom f$. Either way this differs from $0\cdot\partial f(x)=\emptyset$ at a point $x\in\dom f$ where $\partial f(x)=\emptyset$ ($f=-\sqrt{\cdot}$ at $0$).
Proof (divide by $\lambda$)

1. For $x,u\in\H$: $u\in\partial(\lambda f)(x)\iff(\forall y)\ \ps{y-x}{u}+\lambda f(x)\le\lambda f(y)$.

2. Dividing by $\lambda\gt0$ (which preserves $\le$, also when a side is $+\infty$): $\iff(\forall y)\ \ps{y-x}{u/\lambda}+f(x)\le f(y)\iff u/\lambda\in\partial f(x)\iff u\in\lambda\,\partial f(x)$.

Proposition

$\partial f(x)+L^*\partial g(Lx)\subset\partial(f+g\circ L)(x)$

Part III · slide 12SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $\H$ and $\G$ be two real Hilbert spaces, $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$, $g:\G\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ and $L\in\B(\H,\G)$. If $\dom g\cap L(\dom f)\ne\emptyset$, then $$(\forall x\in\H)\qquad\partial f(x)+L^*\partial g(Lx)\subset\partial(f+g\circ L)(x).$$

In one sentence

Add a supporting line of $f$ at $x$ and a supporting line of $g$ at $Lx$ (pulled back by $L^*$): you get a supporting line of $f+g\circ L$ at $x$.

Why it is true

Two inequalities, one for $f$ and one for $g$ at the image point $Lx$, are added. The only computation is moving $L$ to the other side of the inner product: $\ps{L(y-x)}{v}=\ps{y-x}{L^*v}$, which is the definition of the adjoint. The sum of two affine minorants is an affine minorant of the sum, with slope $u+L^*v$. The hypothesis $\dom g\cap L(\dom f)\ne\emptyset$ just makes $f+g\circ L$ proper so that its subdifferential is defined as above.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $\dom g\cap L(\dom f)\ne\emptyset$: there is $x_0$ with $f(x_0)\lt+\infty$ and $g(Lx_0)\lt+\infty$, so $f+g\circ L$ is proper. Nothing else: no convexity, no lsc.
  • $L$ bounded linear: gives the adjoint $L^*$ (page 1).
  • The inclusion is trivially true when $\partial f(x)$ or $\partial g(Lx)$ is empty (the left side is then empty).
Hint — try it first

Take $u\in\partial f(x)$, $v\in\partial g(Lx)$, write both subgradient inequalities at $y$ and $Ly$, add, use $\ps{L(y-x)}{v}=\ps{y-x}{L^*v}$.

Proof T06 write twice and add

1. Two inequalities. Let $x\in\H$, $u\in\partial f(x)$ and $v\in\partial g(Lx)$; the candidate is $u+L^*v\in\partial f(x)+L^*\partial g(Lx)$. For every $y\in\H$: $$f(y)\ge f(x)+\ps{y-x}{u},\qquad g(Ly)\ge g(Lx)+\ps{L(y-x)}{v}.$$

2. Add and use the adjoint. Summing (all quantities on the right are finite since $x\in\dom f$, $Lx\in\dom g$) and using $\ps{L(y-x)}{v}=\ps{y-x}{L^*v}$: $$f(y)+g(Ly)\ge f(x)+g(Lx)+\ps{y-x}{u+L^*v}.$$

3. Read. This is the subgradient inequality of $f+g\circ L$ at $x$ with slope $u+L^*v$: $u+L^*v\in\partial(f+g\circ L)(x)$.

Theorem

Sum rule with equality under a qualification condition (Moreau–Rockafellar)

Part III · slide 13SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Let $\H$ and $\G$ be two real Hilbert spaces, $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $g\in\Gz{\G}$ and $L\in\B(\H,\G)$. If $$\inte(\dom g)\cap L(\dom f)\ne\emptyset\qquad\text{or}\qquad\dom g\cap\inte\big(L(\dom f)\big)\ne\emptyset,$$ then $$\partial f+L^*\partial g\,L=\partial(f+g\circ L).$$ Particular cases:

  • If $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $g\in\Gz{\H}$ and $g$ is finite valued, then $\partial f+\partial g=\partial(f+g)$.
  • If $g\in\Gz{\G}$, $L\in\B(\H,\G)$ and $\inte(\dom g)\cap\ran L\ne\emptyset$, then $L^*\partial g\,L=\partial(g\circ L)$.
In one sentence

When the two domains overlap "with some room" (one of them meets the interior of the other), every supporting line of the sum splits as a supporting line of $f$ plus one of $g\circ L$.

Why it is true

The inclusion $\supset$ is the previous proposition. The hard inclusion is a decomposition problem: given one slope $w$ supporting $f+g\circ L$ at $x$, produce $u\in\partial f(x)$ and $v\in\partial g(Lx)$ with $w=u+L^*v$. Nothing in the definitions produces $u$ and $v$; they come from the Hahn–Banach separation theorem, applied to two convex sets built from $f$ and $g$ (the epigraph of one, the hypograph of the other, after subtracting the affine function of slope $w$). Two disjoint convex sets can always be separated by a hyperplane (in infinite dimension provided one of them is open: a convex lsc function is continuous on the interior of its domain, which makes the region below the graph of $-g$ open there). The real danger is that the separating hyperplane is vertical: a vertical hyperplane carries no slope, so it produces no $u$ and no $v$. A vertical hyperplane would leave the domain of $f$ on one side and the domain of $g$ on the other, which is impossible when a point of $L(\dom f)$ lies in the interior of $\dom g$: that is the job of the qualification condition. Without it the subdifferential of the sum can be strictly larger — in the one-dimensional example below, the two sets are separated only by the vertical line $y=0$. Take the slogan: sum rule = easy inclusion + separation theorem, paid for by an interior condition.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $f,g\in\Gz{}$: convexity to separate; lsc so that $g$ is continuous on the interior of its domain (automatic in finite dimension), which makes the region below the graph of $-g$ open, as the separation theorem requires in a Hilbert space.
  • The qualification condition ($\inte(\dom g)\cap L(\dom f)\ne\emptyset$ or $\dom g\cap\inte L(\dom f)\ne\emptyset$): the domains overlap through an interior point. It is a condition on the domains, not on the point $x$ where you compute. When it fails the equality can fail (see the pitfall).
  • $g$ finite valued (first particular case): then $\inte(\dom g)=\H$ meets $\dom f$ as soon as $f$ is proper — the condition is automatic. This is the case you use most: a smooth data term plus any $f\in\Gz{\H}$.
  • $\inte(\dom g)\cap\ran L\ne\emptyset$ (second particular case): $f=0$, so $L(\dom f)=\ran L$.
Note

The slides state this theorem without proof. Below is the standard proof of the first particular case ($L=\Id$, $g$ finite valued; the argument works in any Hilbert space), which shows exactly where the separation theorem and the "interior" condition enter; the general statement needs a more delicate argument, through the conjugate of a sum (Bauschke–Combettes, chapter 16). You are not expected to reproduce it at the exam, but you must know the statement, its particular cases, and that the inclusion can be strict.

Proof of the particular case $\partial(f+g)=\partial f+\partial g$ (not on the slides; uses the separation theorem)

0. Setting. $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $g:\H\to\R$ convex (hence continuous in finite dimension; in a Hilbert space a finite-valued lsc convex function is continuous as well). The inclusion $\partial f(x)+\partial g(x)\subset\partial(f+g)(x)$ is the previous proposition with $L=\Id$. Let $x\in\H$ and $w\in\partial(f+g)(x)$; in particular $x\in\dom f$. We must find $a\in\partial g(x)$ with $w-a\in\partial f(x)$.

1. Normalize. Define $\varphi(y)=f(y)-f(x)-\ps{y-x}{w}$ and $\psi(y)=g(y)-g(x)$. Both are convex, $\varphi(x)=\psi(x)=0$, $\psi$ is finite and continuous, and the hypothesis $w\in\partial(f+g)(x)$ reads $(\forall y)\ \varphi(y)+\psi(y)\ge0$, i.e. $\varphi\ge-\psi$.

2. Two disjoint convex sets in $\H\times\R$. Let $A=\epi\varphi=\{(y,t)\ |\ t\ge\varphi(y)\}$ (convex, nonempty: $(x,0)\in A$) and $B=\{(y,t)\ |\ t\lt-\psi(y)\}$ (convex because $-\psi$ is concave; open and nonempty because $\psi$ is continuous and finite). They are disjoint: $(y,t)\in A\cap B$ would give $\varphi(y)\le t\lt-\psi(y)$, contradicting $\varphi\ge-\psi$.

3. Separate. By the Hahn–Banach separation theorem (a nonempty open convex set and a disjoint nonempty convex set can be separated by a closed hyperplane), there exist $(a,\beta)\in\H\times\R$, $(a,\beta)\ne(0,0)$, and $\gamma\in\R$ such that $$(\forall(y,t)\in A)\ \ \ps{a}{y}+\beta t\ge\gamma,\qquad(\forall(y,t)\in B)\ \ \ps{a}{y}+\beta t\le\gamma.$$

4. The hyperplane is not vertical. $A$ contains $(x,t)$ for all $t\ge0$, so $\beta t\ge\gamma-\ps{a}{x}$ for all $t\ge0$ forces $\beta\ge0$. If $\beta=0$, the $B$-inequality says $\ps{a}{y}\le\gamma$ for every $y\in\H$ (every $y$ appears in $B$, with $t\lt-\psi(y)$), which forces $a=0$: contradiction with $(a,\beta)\ne0$. Hence $\beta\gt0$ and we may normalize $\beta=1$.

5. Identify $\gamma$. $(x,0)\in A$ gives $\ps{a}{x}\ge\gamma$; $(x,-\varepsilon)\in B$ for every $\varepsilon\gt0$ gives $\ps{a}{x}-\varepsilon\le\gamma$. So $\gamma=\ps{a}{x}$.

6. Read the two inequalities. On $A$ with $t=\varphi(y)$ (for $y\in\dom\varphi=\dom f$): $\varphi(y)\ge\ps{a}{x-y}$, i.e. $f(y)\ge f(x)+\ps{y-x}{w-a}$; this also holds trivially for $y\notin\dom f$. So $w-a\in\partial f(x)$. On $B$, letting $t\uparrow-\psi(y)$: $-\psi(y)+\ps{a}{y}\le\ps{a}{x}$, i.e. $g(y)\ge g(x)+\ps{y-x}{a}$. So $a\in\partial g(x)$.

7. Conclude. $w=(w-a)+a\in\partial f(x)+\partial g(x)$. Two properties of $g$ were used: continuity, to make $B$ open so that separation applies (step 3), and $\dom g=\H$, to forbid a vertical hyperplane (step 4). In the general theorem the qualification condition $\inte(\dom g)\cap L(\dom f)\ne\emptyset$ plays both roles.

Pitfall · the inclusion can be strict

In one dimension: $f(x)=-\sqrt{x}$ on $[0,+\infty[$, $+\infty$ elsewhere, and $g=\ind_{]-\infty,0]}$. Both are in $\Gz{\R}$, $\dom f\cap\dom g=\{0\}$ so $f+g=\ind_{\{0\}}$ and $\partial(f+g)(0)=\R$. But $\partial f(0)=\emptyset$ (no finite slope fits under $-\sqrt{\cdot}$ at $0$), so $\partial f(0)+\partial g(0)=\emptyset\subsetneq\R$. Check the condition: $\inte(\dom g)\cap\dom f=\,]-\infty,0[\,\cap[0,+\infty[=\emptyset$ and $\dom g\cap\inte(\dom f)=\,]-\infty,0]\cap\,]0,+\infty[=\emptyset$. Beware of the tempting "example" $\ind_{]-\infty,0]}+\ind_{[0,+\infty[}$: there $N(0)+N(0)=[0,+\infty[+\,]-\infty,0]=\R$, the sum rule happens to hold although the condition fails (the condition is sufficient, not necessary).

Why every splitting algorithm needs the equality

Splitting algorithms never handle $f+g\circ L$ as a whole: they use $\nabla f$ or $\prox_f$, $\prox_g$, $L$ and $L^*$, so what they compute is a zero of $\partial f+L^*\partial g\,L$. The free inclusion gives $\zer(\partial f+L^*\partial g\,L)\subset\Argmin(f+g\circ L)$: whatever the algorithm converges to is a minimizer (sufficiency). The equality gives the converse, $\zer(\partial f+L^*\partial g\,L)=\Argmin(f+g\circ L)$: every minimizer is such a zero (necessity), so that "a minimizer exists" yields the hypothesis "a zero exists" of the convergence theorems. For Forward–Backward it is automatic (the smooth term is finite valued). Douglas–Rachford and PPXA+ assume directly that $\zer(\partial f+\partial g)$, resp. $\zer(\sum_iL_i^*\partial g_iL_i)$, is nonempty; a minimizer plus the qualification condition is the usual way to check it. ADMM and strong duality put the qualification condition in their hypotheses: Fermat + equality split $0\in\partial(f+g\circ L)(\hat x)$ into a primal piece $-L^*\hat v\in\partial f(\hat x)$ and a dual piece $\hat v\in\partial g(L\hat x)$.

Trick

In practice you check the particular cases: one function finite valued (a quadratic data term, a Huber penalty, any $g\in\Gz{\H}$ with $\dom g=\H$) and you write $\partial(f+g)=\partial f+\partial g$ — $=\partial f+\nabla g$ when $g$ is also differentiable — without further ado. For $g\circ L$, check $\inte(\dom g)\cap\ran L\ne\emptyset$ (automatic when $\dom g=\G$, e.g. a norm) or, from the general condition with $f=0$, $\dom g\cap\inte(\ran L)\ne\emptyset$ (automatic when $L$ is onto, for every proper $g$, indicators included).

To remember

For $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $g\in\Gz{\G}$: $\partial(f+g\circ L)=\partial f+L^*\partial g\,L$ if one domain meets the interior of the other ($\inte(\dom g)\cap L(\dom f)\ne\emptyset$ or $\dom g\cap\inte L(\dom f)\ne\emptyset$). Free when $g$ is finite valued. Inclusion = sufficiency, equality = necessity.

Proposition

Subdifferential of a separable sum: $\partial f(x)=\times_i\,\partial f_i(x_i)$

Part III · slide 14SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $(\H_i)_{i\in I}$, $I\subset\N$, be Hilbert spaces and $\H=\bigoplus_{i\in I}\H_i$. For every $i\in I$ let $f_i:\H_i\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be proper, and set $$f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]:\ x=(x_i)_{i\in I}\mapsto\sum_{i\in I}f_i(x_i).$$ Then $$\big(\forall x=(x_i)_{i\in I}\in\H\big)\qquad\partial f(x)=\underset{i\in I}{\times}\,\partial f_i(x_i).$$

In one sentence

A separable function is supported coordinate by coordinate: a subgradient is a vector of subgradients.

Why it is true

$\supset$: add the coordinatewise subgradient inequalities — the inner product on $\H$ is the sum of the coordinate inner products, so the sum of affine minorants of the $f_i$ is an affine minorant of $f$. $\subset$: a global subgradient inequality holds for every $y$; freezing all coordinates but $y_j$ at their current values $x_i$ makes every term $i\ne j$ cancel (they are finite), leaving the subgradient inequality of $f_j$ alone. The mechanism is test with vectors that differ from $x$ in one coordinate only.

What each hypothesis buys
  • Each $f_i$ proper: so that each $\partial f_i$ is defined; the cancellation in $\subset$ needs $x\in\dom f$, i.e. all $f_i(x_i)$ finite (if $x\notin\dom f$ both sides are empty).
  • For an infinite index set the sums are understood as on the slides (the slides do not discuss summability; in the labs $I$ is finite — $\R^N=\bigoplus_{i=1}^N\R$).

For $\subset$: fix $j$ and choose $y$ with $y_i=x_i$ for $i\ne j$.

Proof T07 freeze all coordinates but one

1. ($\supset$) Let $x\in\H$ and $t=(t_i)_{i\in I}\in\times_{i\in I}\partial f_i(x_i)$, i.e. $(\forall i\in I)(\forall y_i\in\H_i)\ f_i(y_i)\ge f_i(x_i)+\ps{t_i}{y_i-x_i}$. Summing over $i$, for every $y=(y_i)_{i\in I}\in\H$: $$\sum_{i\in I}f_i(y_i)\ge\sum_{i\in I}f_i(x_i)+\sum_{i\in I}\ps{t_i}{y_i-x_i}\iff f(y)\ge f(x)+\ps{t}{y-x},$$ since the inner product of $\H=\bigoplus_i\H_i$ is $\ps{t}{z}=\sum_i\ps{t_i}{z_i}$. Hence $t\in\partial f(x)$.

2. ($\subset$) Conversely let $t\in\partial f(x)$: $(\forall y\in\H)\ \sum_i f_i(y_i)\ge\sum_i f_i(x_i)+\sum_i\ps{t_i}{y_i-x_i}$, with $x\in\dom f$ so that every $f_i(x_i)$ is finite. Fix $j\in I$ and test with $y$ such that $y_i=x_i\in\dom f_i$ for all $i\ne j$: the terms $i\ne j$ are equal on both sides and cancel, leaving $$(\forall y_j\in\H_j)\qquad f_j(y_j)\ge f_j(x_j)+\ps{t_j}{y_j-x_j},$$ i.e. $t_j\in\partial f_j(x_j)$. As $j$ is arbitrary, $t\in\times_{i\in I}\partial f_i(x_i)$.

Exercise 1

The Huber function

Part III · slide 15★★☆Exam#

Let $\rho\gt0$ and set $$f:\R\to\R:\ x\mapsto\begin{cases}\dfrac{x^2}{2}&\text{if }\abs{x}\le\rho,\\[4pt]\rho\abs{x}-\dfrac{\rho^2}{2}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

  1. What is the domain of $f$?
  2. Is $f$ differentiable? twice-differentiable?
  3. Prove that $f$ is convex. Is it strictly convex?

Reflex: a piecewise formula — check the junctions $\pm\rho$ for continuity of $f$, $f'$, $f''$; convexity of a $C^1$ function on $\R$ = $f'$ nondecreasing; strict convexity dies on any segment where $f$ is affine.

Hint

Compute $f'$ on each piece and compare the one-sided values at $\pm\rho$. Then do the same for $f''$. For convexity use convex $\iff$ $f'$ nondecreasing.

Solution

1. Domain. Both formulas are finite for every real $x$, so $\dom f=\R$: $f$ is finite valued (and proper).

2. Continuity at the junctions. At $x=\pm\rho$ the two formulas agree: $\frac{\rho^2}{2}=\rho\cdot\rho-\frac{\rho^2}{2}$. So $f$ is continuous on $\R$.

3. First derivative. On $]-\rho,\rho[$, $f'(x)=x$; on $]\rho,+\infty[$, $f'(x)=\rho$; on $]-\infty,-\rho[$, $f'(x)=-\rho$. At $x=\rho$ the left derivative is $\rho$ and the right derivative is $\rho$; at $-\rho$ both equal $-\rho$. Hence $f$ is differentiable on $\R$ with $$f'(x)=\max(-\rho,\min(x,\rho))\quad(\text{the clipping of }x\text{ to }[-\rho,\rho]),$$ which is continuous: $f$ is $C^1$.

4. Second derivative. $f''(x)=1$ on $]-\rho,\rho[$ and $f''(x)=0$ on $\abs{x}\gt\rho$. At $x=\rho$ the left derivative of $f'$ is $1$ and the right derivative is $0$: $f'$ is not differentiable at $\pm\rho$. So $f$ is not twice differentiable (on $\R$); it is $C^1$ and piecewise $C^2$ with a jump of $f''$ at $\pm\rho$.

5. Convexity. $f$ is Gâteaux differentiable on $\dom f=\R$ (open convex) and $f'$ is nondecreasing (it is $x$ clipped to $[-\rho,\rho]$: constant, then increasing, then constant). By the monotone gradient criterion, $f$ is convex. (The second-order criterion of page 1 cannot be used directly, since $f$ is not $C^2$: this is precisely why the first-order criterion is the right tool here.)

6. Not strictly convex. On $[\rho,+\infty[$, $f(x)=\rho x-\rho^2/2$ is affine: for $x\ne y$ in $[\rho,+\infty[$ and $\alpha\in\,]0,1[$, $f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)=\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y)$ with equality, which violates the strict inequality of strict convexity. So $f$ is convex but not strictly convex.

Exercise 2

Minimizers of $f+\ind_C$

Part III · slide 16★★☆Exam#

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space. Let $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ and let $C\subset\H$ such that $\dom f\cap C\ne\emptyset$.

  1. Give a sufficient condition for $x\in\H$ to be a global minimizer of $f+\ind_C$.
  2. When $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $C$ is a closed convex set, and $f$ is Gâteaux-differentiable on $\H$, give a necessary and sufficient condition for $x\in\H$ to be a minimizer of $f+\ind_C$.

Reflex: minimizer $\iff$ $0\in\partial(f+\ind_C)(x)$ (Fermat); then replace $\partial(f+\ind_C)$ by $\partial f+N_C$ — with "$\supset$" for free (sufficient condition), with "$=$" under Moreau–Rockafellar (necessary and sufficient).

Hint

$\partial f(x)+\partial\ind_C(x)\subset\partial(f+\ind_C)(x)$ always, and $\partial\ind_C=N_C$. For question 2, which particular case of Moreau–Rockafellar applies when $f$ is Gâteaux differentiable on all of $\H$?

Solution (TD correction, C. Cosserat)

1. A sufficient condition. The hypothesis $\dom f\cap C\ne\emptyset$ makes $f+\ind_C$ proper. By Fermat's rule, $x$ is a global minimizer of $f+\ind_C$ iff $0\in\partial(f+\ind_C)(x)$. In general we only have the inclusion $\partial f(x)+\partial\ind_C(x)\subset\partial(f+\ind_C)(x)$ (easy inclusion, with $L=\Id$; its hypothesis is $\dom\ind_C\cap\dom f=C\cap\dom f\ne\emptyset$). So it suffices that $0\in\partial f(x)+\partial\ind_C(x)$, i.e. that there exists $z\in\partial f(x)$ with $-z\in\partial\ind_C(x)=N_C(x)$ (normal cone). The sufficient condition is $$N_C(x)\cap\big(-\partial f(x)\big)\ne\emptyset\qquad(\text{equivalently }0\in\partial f(x)+N_C(x)).$$

2. Interpretation (TA). If $x\in\inte C$, then $N_C(x)=\{0\}$ and the condition becomes $0\in\partial f(x)$: Fermat's rule for the unconstrained problem — an interior point can only be certified this way if it minimizes $f$ on the whole space, which is consistent with "a convex function minimized over a set at an interior point is minimized everywhere". If $x$ is on the boundary of $C$ and the condition holds with $u\in N_C(x)$, $-u\in\partial f(x)$, then for any $y$ with $f(y)\lt f(x)$ the subgradient inequality gives $f(y)-f(x)\ge\ps{-u}{y-x}$, so $\ps{u}{y-x}\ge f(x)-f(y)\gt0$ and therefore $y\notin C$: every point where $f$ is lower lies outside $C$. When $f$ is differentiable this reads $-\nabla f(x)\in N_C(x)$: to decrease $f$ you must leave $C$, the steepest descent direction points outward.

3. Question 2: check the hypotheses of Moreau–Rockafellar. $f$ Gâteaux differentiable on $\H$ implies $\dom f=\H$, so $f$ is finite valued; $C$ closed $\Rightarrow$ $\ind_C$ lsc, $C$ convex $\Rightarrow$ $\ind_C$ convex, $C\ne\emptyset$ (since $\dom f\cap C\ne\emptyset$) $\Rightarrow$ $\ind_C$ proper: $\ind_C\in\Gz{\H}$ (page 1). The first particular case of Moreau–Rockafellar (one of the two functions finite valued) applies: $(\forall x\in\H)\ \partial(f+\ind_C)(x)=\partial f(x)+\partial\ind_C(x)$.

4. Necessary and sufficient condition. Since $f$ is convex and Gâteaux differentiable, $\partial f(x)=\{\nabla f(x)\}$ (theorem). Fermat's rule now gives: $x$ minimizes $f+\ind_C$ $\iff$ $0\in\nabla f(x)+N_C(x)$ $\iff$ $$-\nabla f(x)\in N_C(x)\iff x\in C\ \text{ and }\ (\forall y\in C)\ \ps{\nabla f(x)}{y-x}\ge0,$$ which is the variational inequality of page 1, now obtained from the general machinery.

The Legendre–Fenchel conjugate

Legendre (1752–1833) traded positions for slopes to simplify differential equations; Fenchel (1905–1988) made the trade work for every convex function, with a supremum instead of a derivative. The conjugate $f^*$ encodes $f$ by its affine minorants — and inverts its subdifferential.

Definition

The Legendre–Fenchel conjugate

Part III · slide 18SlidesKey#
Definition

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space and $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$. The conjugate of $f$ is the function $f^*:\H\to[-\infty,+\infty]$ such that $$(\forall u\in\H)\qquad f^*(u)=\sup_{x\in\H}\big(\ps{x}{u}-f(x)\big)=\sup_{x\in\dom f}\big(\ps{x}{u}-f(x)\big).$$

In one sentence

$f^*(u)$ is the largest signed vertical gap $\ps{x}{u}-f(x)$ between the linear function $\ps{\cdot}{u}$ and $f$ (negative when that line stays below the graph); equivalently $-f^*(u)$ is the intercept of the highest affine function of slope $u$ that stays below $f$.

Why the definition makes sense

Fix a slope $u$, start with the line $x\mapsto\ps{x}{u}+c$ far below the graph of $f$ and raise it until it first touches the graph. The admissible intercepts are those $c$ with $\ps{x}{u}+c\le f(x)$ for all $x$, i.e. $c\le-(\ps{x}{u}-f(x))$ for all $x$, i.e. $c\le-f^*(u)$. So $-f^*(u)$ is the best intercept, and $$(\forall x,u)\qquad\ps{x}{u}-f^*(u)\le f(x)$$ is the best affine minorant of slope $u$ (this is the Fenchel–Young inequality, proved below). When no line of slope $u$ fits under $f$, $f^*(u)=+\infty$ (for $f=\abs{\cdot}$ and $\abs{u}\gt1$: the gap $xu-\abs{x}$ is unbounded). When $f$ is proper, $f^*\gt-\infty$ everywhere (pick any $x\in\dom f$). The passage from $f$ to $f^*$ is a change of coordinates: $f$ is described by points $(x,f(x))$, $f^*$ by lines (slope $u$, intercept $-f^*(u)$). For a smooth strictly convex $f$ the supremum is attained at the point $x$ where $u=\nabla f(x)$, when there is one ($u\in\ran\nabla f$ is needed: for $e^x$ and $u\le0$ there is none), and then $f^*(u)=\ps{x}{\nabla f(x)}-f(x)$: Legendre's original transform.

Worked examples (see the widget)
  • $f=\tfrac12x^2$: $f^*(u)=\tfrac12u^2$ (the best line of slope $u$ touches at $x=u$, intercept $-u^2/2$).
  • $f=\abs{x}$: $f^*=\ind_{[-1,1]}$ — slopes in $[-1,1]$ fit under the V with intercept $0$, others do not fit at all.
  • $f=e^x$: $f^*(u)=u\ln u-u$ for $u\gt0$, $f^*(0)=0$ (supremum not attained), $f^*(u)=+\infty$ for $u\lt0$.
  • $f=\ind_{[-1,1]}$: $f^*(u)=\abs{u}$ — the support function of the interval.
  • $f=\ps{c}{\cdot}$: $f^*=\ind_{\{c\}}$ (Exercise 3).

Proposition

Conjugates of $\tfrac12\norm{\cdot}^2$, of radial functions, of $\tfrac1q\norm{\cdot}_q^q$

Part III · slide 18SlidesKey#
Statement
  • $f=\tfrac12\norm{\cdot}^2\ \Rightarrow\ f^*=\tfrac12\norm{\cdot}^2$.
  • Let $\varphi:\R\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be an even function. Then $(\varphi\circ\norm{\cdot})^*=\varphi^*\circ\norm{\cdot}$.
  • $(\forall x\in\R^N)\ f(x)=\tfrac1q\norm{x}_q^q$ with $q\in\,]1,+\infty[$ $\ \Rightarrow\ $ $(\forall u\in\R^N)\ f^*(u)=\tfrac1{q^*}\norm{u}_{q^*}^{q^*}$ with $\tfrac1q+\tfrac1{q^*}=1$.
In one sentence

The half-squared norm is its own conjugate; a radial function has a radial conjugate; and the power $q$ is exchanged with its Hölder conjugate $q^*$.

Why it is true

Half-squared norm. Complete the square: $\ps{x}{u}-\tfrac12\norm{x}^2=\tfrac12\norm{u}^2-\tfrac12\norm{u-x}^2$, which is maximal when $x=u$ and then equals $\tfrac12\norm{u}^2$. Radial functions. On the sphere $\norm{x}=r$, the linear form $\ps{\cdot}{u}$ is maximal at $x=ru/\norm{u}$ with value $r\norm{u}$ (Cauchy–Schwarz), so maximizing over $\H$ reduces to maximizing $r\norm{u}-\varphi(r)$ over $r\ge0$; evenness of $\varphi$ lets you extend the supremum to $r\in\R$ and recognize $\varphi^*(\norm{u})$. The norm. Take $\varphi=\abs{\cdot}$ (even), whose conjugate is $\ind_{[-1,1]}$: then $\norm{\cdot}^*=\ind_{[-1,1]}\circ\norm{\cdot}=\ind_{\overline B(0,1)}$. Geometrically, a linear function of slope $u$ fits under the cone $\norm{\cdot}$ (intercept $0$) iff $\norm{u}\le1$, and no line of slope $u$ fits otherwise — the $\abs{x}$ picture of the widget above, in every direction. Powers. The function is separable, so by the separable-sum rule it suffices to conjugate $\tfrac1q\abs{\cdot}^q$ on $\R$; there the supremum of the concave function $t\mapsto tu-\tfrac1q\abs{t}^q$ is found by cancelling its derivative, $\abs{t}^{q-1}\sgn t=u$, and the exponent $q^*$ appears because $\frac1{q-1}=q^*-1$.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $\varphi$ even: so that negative radii never beat positive ones in the 1-D supremum. Only the values of $\varphi$ on $[0,+\infty[$ enter $\varphi\circ\norm{\cdot}$, and in general $(\varphi\circ\norm{\cdot})^*(u)=\sup_{r\ge0}(r\norm{u}-\varphi(r))=\tilde\varphi^*(\norm{u})$, where $\tilde\varphi=\varphi(\abs{\cdot})$ is the even function that agrees with $\varphi$ on $[0,+\infty[$. Without evenness this may differ from $\varphi^*(\norm{u})$: for $\varphi=\exp$ and $\norm{u}=\tfrac12$, $(e^{\norm{\cdot}})^*(u)=-1$ (supremum at $r=0$), whereas $\varphi^*(\tfrac12)=\tfrac12\ln\tfrac12-\tfrac12\approx-0.85$.
  • $q\in\,]1,+\infty[$: $q^*=q/(q-1)$ is finite and the 1-D supremum is attained. Limiting cases: for $q=1$, $\norm{\cdot}_1^*=\ind_{[-1,1]^N}$, the indicator of the dual ($\ell^\infty$) unit ball; as $q\to+\infty$, $\tfrac1q\norm{\cdot}_q^q$ tends pointwise to $\ind_{[-1,1]^N}$, whose conjugate is $\norm{\cdot}_1$ ($q^*\to1$). The right-hand side is $\le\tfrac12\norm{u}^2$ with equality iff $x=u$. Consequently $f^*(u)=\sup_x(\ldots)=\tfrac12\norm{u}^2$.

2. Radial functions, reduction to the radius. Let $u\in\H$ and $f=\varphi\circ\norm{\cdot}$. Group the $x$'s by their norm $r=\norm{x}\ge0$: $f^*(u)=\sup_{r\ge0}\ \sup_{\norm{x}=r}\big(\ps{x}{u}-\varphi(r)\big)=\sup_{r\ge0}\big(r\norm{u}-\varphi(r)\big)$, because by Cauchy–Schwarz $\sup_{\norm{x}=r}\ps{x}{u}=r\norm{u}$ (attained at $x=ru/\norm{u}$ if $u\ne0$; trivially $0$ if $u=0$).

3. Radial functions, use evenness. For $r\lt0$, $r\norm{u}-\varphi(r)=r\norm{u}-\varphi(-r)\le(-r)\norm{u}-\varphi(-r)$, which is one of the values with a nonnegative radius. Hence $\sup_{r\ge0}(r\norm{u}-\varphi(r))=\sup_{r\in\R}(r\norm{u}-\varphi(r))=\varphi^*(\norm{u})$. So $(\varphi\circ\norm{\cdot})^*=\varphi^*\circ\norm{\cdot}$.

4. Powers, one coordinate. Let $\psi(t)=\tfrac1q\abs{t}^q$ on $\R$ and $u\in\R$. The function $t\mapsto tu-\psi(t)$ is differentiable, concave (as $\psi$ is convex), and tends to $-\infty$ as $\abs{t}\to\infty$ (since $q\gt1$); its supremum is attained where the derivative vanishes: $u=\psi'(t)=\abs{t}^{q-1}\sgn(t)$, i.e. $t=\sgn(u)\abs{u}^{1/(q-1)}=\sgn(u)\abs{u}^{q^*-1}$ (because $q^*=\frac{q}{q-1}$ gives $q^*-1=\frac1{q-1}$). At that point $\abs{t}^q=\abs{u}^{q/(q-1)}=\abs{u}^{q^*}$ and $tu=\abs{u}^{q^*}$, so $$\psi^*(u)=\abs{u}^{q^*}-\tfrac1q\abs{u}^{q^*}=\big(1-\tfrac1q\big)\abs{u}^{q^*}=\tfrac1{q^*}\abs{u}^{q^*}.$$

5. Powers, sum over coordinates. $f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^N\psi(x_i)$ is separable, so by the conjugate of a separable sum, $f^*(u)=\sum_i\psi^*(u_i)=\tfrac1{q^*}\sum_i\abs{u_i}^{q^*}=\tfrac1{q^*}\norm{u}_{q^*}^{q^*}$.

Proposition

Conjugate calculus: parity, scaling, translation, isomorphism, lsc convexity

Statement

Let $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$.

  • If $f$ is even, then $f^*$ is even.
  • For every $\alpha\in\,]0,+\infty[$, $(\alpha f)^*=\alpha f^*(\cdot/\alpha)$.
  • For every $(y,v)\in\H^2$ and $\alpha\in\R$, $\big(f(\cdot-y)+\ps{\cdot}{v}+\alpha\big)^*=f^*(\cdot-v)+\ps{y}{\cdot-v}-\alpha$.
  • Let $\G$ be a Hilbert space and $L\in\B(\G,\H)$ be an isomorphism. Then $(f\circ L)^*=f^*\circ(L^{-1})^*$.
  • $f^*$ is lsc and convex.
In one sentence

Each rule is a change of variable inside the supremum; the last one says that a supremum of affine functions is always lsc and convex, whatever $f$ is.

Why it is true

The conjugate is a supremum over $x$ of $\ps{x}{u}-f(x)$. Substituting $x\to-x$ (parity), factoring $\alpha$ out (scaling), substituting $z=x-y$ and absorbing the linear term into the slope (translation), or substituting $z=Lx$ (isomorphism) are all bijective reparametrizations of the index set of the supremum, so the supremum is unchanged and only the expression changes. The last property has a different nature: for each fixed $x\in\dom f$, $u\mapsto\ps{x}{u}-f(x)$ is affine and continuous; a pointwise supremum of such functions has a closed convex epigraph (the intersection of the closed convex epigraphs — page 1's stability of $\Gz{}$ under suprema).

What each hypothesis buys
  • $\alpha\gt0$ in the scaling rule: $\alpha$ must be factored out of a supremum without flipping it.
  • $L$ isomorphism (bounded, bijective, bounded inverse): the substitution $z=Lx$ runs over all of $\H$ and $(L^{-1})^*$ exists. For a general $L$ the conjugate of $f\circ L$ is an infimal convolution-like formula, not on the slides.
  • Nothing for lsc convexity: it holds for every $f$, even nonconvex ones. If $\dom f=\emptyset$, $f^*\equiv-\infty$, which is (by convention) lsc and convex.

Proof (five changes of variable)

1. Parity. As on the slides: for every $u\in\H$, substituting $x\to-x$ (a bijection of $\H$), $$f^*(-u)=\sup_{x\in\H}\big(\ps{x}{-u}-f(x)\big)=\sup_{x\in\H}\big(\ps{-x}{-u}-f(-x)\big)=\sup_{x\in\H}\big(\ps{x}{u}-f(x)\big)=f^*(u),$$ where $f(-x)=f(x)$ was used in the last step.

2. Scaling. For $\alpha\gt0$: $(\alpha f)^*(u)=\sup_x\big(\ps{x}{u}-\alpha f(x)\big)=\alpha\sup_x\big(\ps{x}{u/\alpha}-f(x)\big)=\alpha f^*(u/\alpha)$.

3. Translation and affine perturbation. Let $g=f(\cdot-y)+\ps{\cdot}{v}+\alpha$. Then $g^*(u)=\sup_x\big(\ps{x}{u-v}-f(x-y)\big)-\alpha$. Substitute $z=x-y$: $$g^*(u)=\sup_{z}\big(\ps{z+y}{u-v}-f(z)\big)-\alpha=\ps{y}{u-v}+\sup_z\big(\ps{z}{u-v}-f(z)\big)-\alpha=f^*(u-v)+\ps{y}{u-v}-\alpha.$$

4. Isomorphism. For $u\in\G$: $(f\circ L)^*(u)=\sup_{x\in\G}\big(\ps{x}{u}-f(Lx)\big)$. Substitute $z=Lx$, which runs over all of $\H$ as $x$ runs over $\G$, with $x=L^{-1}z$: $$(f\circ L)^*(u)=\sup_{z\in\H}\big(\ps{L^{-1}z}{u}-f(z)\big)=\sup_{z\in\H}\big(\ps{z}{(L^{-1})^*u}-f(z)\big)=f^*\big((L^{-1})^*u\big).$$

5. lsc and convex. For each $x\in\dom f$, $a_x:u\mapsto\ps{x}{u}-f(x)$ is affine and continuous, hence lsc and convex. $f^*=\sup_{x\in\dom f}a_x$ and $\epi f^*=\bigcap_{x\in\dom f}\epi a_x$: an intersection of closed convex sets, hence closed and convex, which means $f^*$ is lsc (closed epigraph) and convex (convex epigraph). (If $\dom f=\emptyset$ the supremum is $-\infty$ everywhere.)

Theorem

Moreau–Fenchel: $f$ lsc convex $\iff$ $f^{**}=f$

Part III · slide 18SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space and $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be a proper function. Then $$f\text{ is lsc and convex}\iff f^{**}=f.$$ Consequence. If $f\in\Gz{\H}$, then $f^*$ is proper, hence $f^*\in\Gz{\H}$.

In one sentence

A closed convex function is the supremum of its affine minorants, and $f^{**}$ is exactly that supremum; conjugating twice loses nothing precisely when there was nothing nonconvex to lose.

Why it is true

Unfold $f^{**}(x)=\sup_u\big(\ps{x}{u}-f^*(u)\big)$: for each slope $u$, $\ps{\cdot}{u}-f^*(u)$ is the best affine minorant of $f$ with that slope, so $f^{**}$ is the supremum of all affine minorants of $f$. This is always $\le f$. The question is whether the affine minorants reach up to $f$ at every point. If $f$ is not convex, they cannot (they are all below the convex envelope); if $f$ is not lsc, they cannot either (a supremum of continuous functions is lsc). Conversely, when $\epi f$ is closed and convex, any point $(x_0,t)$ strictly below the graph can be separated from $\epi f$ by a hyperplane — the Hahn–Banach separation theorem, which in a Hilbert space follows from the projection onto a closed convex set. A non-vertical separating hyperplane is the graph of an affine minorant passing above $t$ at $x_0$, so $f^{**}(x_0)\ge t$; letting $t\uparrow f(x_0)$ gives $f^{**}(x_0)=f(x_0)$. The one subtle point is a vertical hyperplane (possible only when $x_0\notin\dom f$), handled by tilting it with any affine minorant. The consequence: $f^*\gt-\infty$ since $f$ is proper, and $f^*\not\equiv+\infty$ since otherwise $f=f^{**}\equiv-\infty$.

What each hypothesis buys
  • Convex: affine minorants can only build convex functions; lsc: they can only build lsc functions. So both are necessary; together they make $\epi f$ closed convex, which is what separation needs.
  • Proper: excludes $f\equiv+\infty$ (then $f^*\equiv-\infty$ and $f^{**}\equiv+\infty$ anyway) and guarantees the existence of at least one affine minorant, used in the "vertical hyperplane" case.
Note

The slides state the theorem without proof. The proof below is the standard one; it uses the strict separation of a point from a closed convex set in a Hilbert space (a corollary of the projection theorem). Retain the mechanism — "closed convex = sup of affine minorants" — more than the details.

Proof (not on the slides; separation theorem)

1. ($\Leftarrow$) If $f=f^{**}=(f^*)^*$, then $f$ is the conjugate of $f^*$, hence lsc and convex by the previous proposition.

2. $f^{**}\le f$ always. For every $u$ and every $x$, $f^*(u)\ge\ps{x}{u}-f(x)$ by definition of the supremum, i.e. $\ps{x}{u}-f^*(u)\le f(x)$ (Fenchel–Young, which uses only the definition). Taking the supremum over $u$: $f^{**}(x)\le f(x)$.

3. ($\Rightarrow$) Separation. Assume $f$ proper, lsc and convex, so that $\epi f$ is a nonempty closed convex subset of $\H\times\R$. Fix $x_0\in\H$ and $t\lt f(x_0)$: the point $(x_0,t)\notin\epi f$. By strict separation of a point from a closed convex set, there exist $(a,\beta)\in\H\times\R$ and $\gamma\in\R$ such that $$(\forall(y,s)\in\epi f)\quad\ps{a}{y}+\beta s\gt\gamma\gt\ps{a}{x_0}+\beta t.$$ Since $(y,s)\in\epi f$ implies $(y,s')\in\epi f$ for all $s'\ge s$, letting $s'\to+\infty$ forces $\beta\ge0$.

4. Non-vertical case $\beta\gt0$. Normalize $\beta=1$ and set $u=-a$. For $y\in\dom f$, take $s=f(y)$: $f(y)\gt\gamma+\ps{y}{u}$, so $\ps{y}{u}-f(y)\lt-\gamma$ for all $y$, i.e. $f^*(u)\le-\gamma$. Hence $$f^{**}(x_0)\ge\ps{x_0}{u}-f^*(u)\ge\ps{x_0}{u}+\gamma\gt t,$$ the last inequality being $\gamma\gt\ps{a}{x_0}+t=-\ps{x_0}{u}+t$.

5. Vertical case $\beta=0$. Then $\ps{a}{y}\gt\gamma\gt\ps{a}{x_0}$ for all $y\in\dom f$ (so $x_0\notin\dom f$ and $f(x_0)=+\infty$). Pick any affine minorant of $f$: step 4 applied at some $x_1\in\dom f$ with $t_1\lt f(x_1)$ produces $u_1$ with $f\ge\ps{\cdot}{u_1}+\gamma_1$. For $\lambda\gt0$ the function $y\mapsto\lambda(\gamma-\ps{a}{y})$ is $\lt0$ on $\dom f$, so $f(y)\ge\ps{y}{u_1-\lambda a}+\gamma_1+\lambda\gamma$ for all $y$ (trivially off $\dom f$). Thus $f^*(u_1-\lambda a)\le-\gamma_1-\lambda\gamma$ and $$f^{**}(x_0)\ge\ps{x_0}{u_1-\lambda a}-f^*(u_1-\lambda a)\ge\ps{x_0}{u_1}+\gamma_1+\lambda\big(\gamma-\ps{a}{x_0}\big)\xrightarrow[\lambda\to+\infty]{}+\infty,$$ since $\gamma-\ps{a}{x_0}\gt0$. So $f^{**}(x_0)=+\infty=f(x_0)$.

6. Conclude. In the case $\beta\gt0$, $f^{**}(x_0)\gt t$ for every $t\lt f(x_0)$ gives $f^{**}(x_0)\ge f(x_0)$; with step 2, $f^{**}=f$.

7. Consequence. Let $f\in\Gz{\H}$. Since $f$ is proper, pick $x\in\dom f$: $f^*(u)\ge\ps{x}{u}-f(x)\gt-\infty$ for every $u$. If $f^*\equiv+\infty$, then $f^{**}\equiv-\infty\ne f$, contradicting $f^{**}=f$. So $f^*$ is proper; being lsc and convex, $f^*\in\Gz{\H}$.

Support functions

Conjugating an indicator gives the support function $\sigma_C$: "how far does $C$ reach in direction $u$". The theorem of this section says that every positively homogeneous $\Gz{}$ function — every norm in particular — is a support function, so that $\norm{x}_q$ is a supremum of linear forms over the dual ball. This is what makes norms tractable in duality and in the prox calculus.

Definition

Support function $\sigma_C=\ind_C^*$

Part III · slide 19SlidesKey#
Definition

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space and $C\subset\H$. The support function of $C$ is $$(\forall u\in\H)\qquad\sigma_C(u)=\sup_{x\in C}\ps{x}{u}=\ind_C^*(u).$$

In one sentence

$\sigma_C(u)$ is the largest value of the linear form $\ps{\cdot}{u}$ on $C$: for $\norm{u}=1$, the signed distance from the origin to the supporting hyperplane of $C$ orthogonal to $u$.

Why the definition makes sense

Conjugating $\ind_C$ restricts the supremum $\sup_x\ps{x}{u}-\ind_C(x)$ to $x\in C$ (the $+\infty$ penalty kills the others): $\ind_C^*(u)=\sup_{x\in C}\ps{x}{u}$. Geometrically the line $\{x:\ps{x}{u}=\sigma_C(u)\}$ is the supporting hyperplane of $C$ with outward normal $u$, and $\sigma_C$ describes $C$ by its supporting hyperplanes — for a closed convex $C$ this description is complete: $C=\{x:\ (\forall u)\ \ps{x}{u}\le\sigma_C(u)\}$ (Moreau–Fenchel applied to $\ind_C$). On the slides: for $C=[\delta_1,\delta_2]\subset\R$, $\sigma_C(u)=\delta_2u$ if $u\ge0$ and $\delta_1u$ if $u\le0$ — the maximum of $xu$ over the interval is at the endpoint in the direction of $u$; the graph has a kink at $0$, with slope $\delta_1$ on the left and $\delta_2$ on the right (a "V" when $\delta_1\lt0\lt\delta_2$). Being a conjugate, $\sigma_C$ is lsc and convex; being a supremum of linear forms, it is positively homogeneous, and $\sigma_C(0)=0$ when $C\ne\emptyset$.

Two readings to keep together: $\sigma_C(u)=\ps{x}{u}$ for $x\in C$ exactly when $u\in N_C(x)$ (compare the normal cone as "the $u$'s maximized at $x$"), which is Fenchel–Young's equality case for $\ind_C$.

Theorem

Positively homogeneous $\Gz{}$ functions are exactly the support functions

Part III · slide 20SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space. A function $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ is positively homogeneous if $$(\forall x\in\H)(\forall\alpha\in\,]0,+\infty[)\qquad f(\alpha x)=\alpha f(x).$$ Then: $f$ is positively homogeneous and belongs to $\Gz{\H}$ if and only if $f=\sigma_C$ where $C$ is a nonempty closed convex subset of $\H$.

In one sentence

Homogeneity forces the best affine minorants to be linear (intercept $0$ or $-\infty$), so $f^*$ is an indicator $\ind_C$, and Moreau–Fenchel gives $f=f^{**}=\ind_C^*=\sigma_C$. The set $C$ is the set of slopes of the linear minorants of $f$, i.e. $C=\partial f(0)$.

Why it is true

($\Leftarrow$) A support function is the conjugate of $\ind_C\in\Gz{\H}$, hence in $\Gz{\H}$ by the consequence of Moreau–Fenchel, and a supremum of linear forms is positively homogeneous. ($\Rightarrow$) The whole point is to compute $f^*$. For a slope $u$, the gap $\ps{x}{u}-f(x)$ scales linearly with $x$ ($\alpha x$ gives $\alpha$ times the gap). So either the gap is $\le0$ for every $x$ — then $f^*(u)=0$, attained at $x=0$ where $f(0)=0$ — or it is positive somewhere, and scaling that $x$ by $\alpha\to+\infty$ sends the gap to $+\infty$. Hence $f^*$ only takes the values $0$ and $+\infty$: it is the indicator of $C=\{u:\ \ps{\cdot}{u}\le f\}$. Since $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $f^*=\ind_C\in\Gz{\H}$, which forces $C$ nonempty closed convex, and $f=f^{**}=\sigma_C$.

What each hypothesis buys
  • Positive homogeneity: makes the gap $\ps{x}{u}-f(x)$ homogeneous of degree $1$ in $x$, which is what makes $f^*$ take only the values $0$ and $+\infty$. It also gives $f(0)=0$ (with lsc + convexity).
  • $f\in\Gz{\H}$: needed for $f^{**}=f$ (Moreau–Fenchel) and for $f^*\in\Gz{\H}$, i.e. $C$ nonempty closed convex. Without lsc the equivalence fails: $f=\ind_{]0,+\infty[}$ on $\R$ is proper, convex and positively homogeneous, but $f(0)=+\infty$, whereas every support function of a nonempty set vanishes at $0$; its lsc hull $\ind_{[0,+\infty[}=\sigma_{]-\infty,0]}$ is a support function.

6. Conclude. Steps 4–5 say $f^*=\ind_C$. Since $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $f^*\in\Gz{\H}$ (Moreau–Fenchel consequence), so $\ind_C$ is proper, lsc and convex: $C$ is nonempty, closed and convex. Finally, Moreau–Fenchel gives $f=f^{**}=\ind_C^*=\sigma_C$.

Piecewise linear functions, $\ell^q$ norms and the $\ell^1$ norm as support functions

Part III · slide 21SlidesKey#
Statement
  • Let $f:\R\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]:\ x\mapsto\begin{cases}\delta_1x&\text{if }x\lt0\\ 0&\text{if }x=0\\ \delta_2x&\text{if }x\gt0\end{cases}$ with $-\infty\le\delta_1\lt\delta_2\le+\infty$. Then $f=\sigma_C$ where $C$ is the closed real interval such that $\inf C=\delta_1$ and $\sup C=\delta_2$.
  • Let $f$ be an $\ell^q$ norm of $\R^N$ with $q\in[1,+\infty]$. Then $f=\sigma_C$ where $C=\{y\in\R^N\ |\ \norm{y}_{q^*}\le1\}$ with $\tfrac1q+\tfrac1{q^*}=1$.
  • Particular case: the $\ell^1$ norm of $\R^N$: $C=[-1,1]^N$.
In one sentence

A function that is linear on each side of $0$ is the support function of the interval of its two slopes, and a norm is the support function of the dual unit ball: $\norm{x}_q=\max\{\ps{x}{y}:\norm{y}_{q^*}\le1\}$.

Why it is true

For the interval, $\sup_{c\in C}cx$ is reached at $\sup C$ when $x\gt0$ and at $\inf C$ when $x\lt0$ ($\delta_2x=+\infty$ for $x\gt0$ when $\delta_2=+\infty$, and $\delta_1x=+\infty$ for $x\lt0$ when $\delta_1=-\infty$, matching $f=+\infty$ there). For the norms, Hölder's inequality $\ps{x}{y}\le\norm{x}_q\norm{y}_{q^*}$ bounds the supremum over the dual ball by $\norm{x}_q$, and an explicit $y$ (the "dual vector" of $x$, obtained by raising $\abs{x_i}$ to the power $q-1$ and normalizing) attains it: a cleverly chosen test vector (T07). The $\ell^1$ case is the simplest: $\sum_i x_iy_i$ with $\abs{y_i}\le1$ is maximal when $y_i=\sgn(x_i)$. All these functions are positively homogeneous and in $\Gz{}$, so they are instances of the theorem above with $C=\partial f(0)$; the proof below identifies $C$ directly. Say it out loud: Hölder bounds, the dual vector attains.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $\delta_1\lt\delta_2$: $C$ is a genuine interval (possibly unbounded; for $\delta_1=-\infty$, $f=+\infty$ on $]-\infty,0[$ and $C=\,]-\infty,\delta_2]$). The case $\delta_1=\delta_2$ would give a linear function, $\sigma_{\{\delta\}}$.
  • $q\in[1,+\infty]$ with the conventions $q^*=\infty$ for $q=1$ and $q^*=1$ for $q=\infty$: Hölder covers all cases.

Proof T07 the dual vector as test vector (Hölder + an explicit maximizer)

1. The interval. Let $C$ be the closed interval with $\inf C=\delta_1$, $\sup C=\delta_2$ (so $C=[\delta_1,\delta_2]\cap\R$). For $x\gt0$, $\sigma_C(x)=\sup_{c\in C}cx=x\sup C=\delta_2x$ (equal to $+\infty$ if $\delta_2=+\infty$); for $x\lt0$, $\sigma_C(x)=x\inf C=\delta_1x$ (equal to $+\infty$ if $\delta_1=-\infty$); $\sigma_C(0)=0$. This is $f$.

2. Norms, upper bound. Let $x\in\R^N$ and $y\in C$, i.e. $\norm{y}_{q^*}\le1$. By Hölder's inequality, $\ps{x}{y}\le\norm{x}_q\norm{y}_{q^*}\le\norm{x}_q$. Hence $\sigma_C(x)\le\norm{x}_q$.

3. Norms, the maximizer, $1\lt q\lt\infty$. If $x=0$ both sides are $0$. Otherwise define $y_i=\sgn(x_i)\abs{x_i}^{q-1}/\norm{x}_q^{q-1}$. Since $(q-1)q^*=q$, $$\norm{y}_{q^*}^{q^*}=\frac{\sum_i\abs{x_i}^{(q-1)q^*}}{\norm{x}_q^{(q-1)q^*}}=\frac{\sum_i\abs{x_i}^q}{\norm{x}_q^q}=1,\qquad\ps{x}{y}=\frac{\sum_i\abs{x_i}^q}{\norm{x}_q^{q-1}}=\norm{x}_q.$$ So $y\in C$ and $\sigma_C(x)\ge\norm{x}_q$.

4. Norms, the limiting cases. $q=1$, $q^*=\infty$, $C=[-1,1]^N$: take $y_i=\sgn(x_i)$; then $\norm{y}_\infty\le1$ and $\ps{x}{y}=\sum_i\abs{x_i}=\norm{x}_1$. $q=\infty$, $q^*=1$, $C=\{\norm{y}_1\le1\}$: take $y=\sgn(x_j)e_j$ for an index $j$ with $\abs{x_j}=\max_i\abs{x_i}$; then $\norm{y}_1=1$ and $\ps{x}{y}=\abs{x_j}=\norm{x}_\infty$.

5. Conclude. In every case $\sigma_C=\norm{\cdot}_q$, and for $q=1$, $C=\{y:\norm{y}_\infty\le1\}=[-1,1]^N$.

Fenchel–Young and the inversion $\partial f^*=(\partial f)^{-1}$

The two halves of the page meet here. The conjugate gives the best affine minorant of each slope; the subdifferential gives the slopes supporting at each point. Fenchel–Young says these are the same data read in two directions, and for $f\in\Gz{\H}$ it makes $\partial f^*$ the inverse of $\partial f$.

Theorem

Fenchel–Young inequality, its equality case, and $\partial f^*=(\partial f)^{-1}$

Part III · slide 22SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Fenchel–Young inequality. If $f$ is proper, then

  1. $\big(\forall(x,u)\in\H^2\big)\qquad f(x)+f^*(u)\ge\ps{x}{u}$;
  2. $\big(\forall(x,u)\in\H^2\big)\qquad u\in\partial f(x)\iff f(x)+f^*(u)=\ps{x}{u}$.

If $f\in\Gz{\H}$, then $$\big(\forall(x,u)\in\H^2\big)\qquad u\in\partial f(x)\iff x\in\partial f^*(u).$$

In one sentence

The gap $f(x)+f^*(u)-\ps{x}{u}$ is the vertical distance at $x$ between the graph of $f$ and the best affine minorant of slope $u$; it is $\ge0$ always, and it is $0$ exactly when that minorant touches the graph at $x$, i.e. when $u\in\partial f(x)$. Since the gap is symmetric in $(f,x)\leftrightarrow(f^*,u)$ when $f^{**}=f$, the roles of point and slope can be exchanged.

Why it is true

(1) is the definition of a supremum: $f^*(u)$ is at least the value $\ps{x}{u}-f(x)$ of the function being maximized. (2) rewrites the subgradient inequality $f(y)\ge f(x)+\ps{y-x}{u}$ as $\ps{y}{u}-f(y)\le\ps{x}{u}-f(x)$ for all $y$: "$x$ attains the supremum defining $f^*(u)$". That supremum is $f^*(u)$, so $u\in\partial f(x)$ iff $f^*(u)=\ps{x}{u}-f(x)$. (3) is (2) applied to $f^*$ instead of $f$: $x\in\partial f^*(u)\iff f^*(u)+f^{**}(x)=\ps{u}{x}$, and when $f\in\Gz{\H}$, Moreau–Fenchel replaces $f^{**}$ by $f$, giving the same equation as in (2). Say it this way: $u$ supports $f$ at $x$ $\iff$ $(x,u)$ is a Fenchel–Young equality pair $\iff$ $x$ supports $f^*$ at $u$.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $f$ proper: $f^*\gt-\infty$ everywhere, so the sums in (1)–(2) make sense (no $+\infty-\infty$); for $x\notin\dom f$ both sides of (2) are false ($\partial f(x)=\emptyset$ and $f(x)+f^*(u)=+\infty\ne\ps{x}{u}$).
  • $f\in\Gz{\H}$ in (3): needed for $f^{**}=f$. Without it, the correct statement is $x\in\partial f^*(u)\iff u\in\partial f^{**}(x)$; e.g. $f=\ind_{\{-1,1\}}$ has $f^*=\abs{\cdot}$, $0\in\partial f^*(0)=[-1,1]$ but $\partial f(0)=\emptyset$.

Proof T10 Fenchel–Young as a lever

1. The inequality. Let $(x,u)\in\H^2$. If $x\notin\dom f$, then $f(x)=+\infty$ and, since $f$ is proper, $f^*(u)\ge\ps{x_0}{u}-f(x_0)\gt-\infty$ for any $x_0\in\dom f$; so $f(x)+f^*(u)=+\infty\ge\ps{x}{u}$. If $x\in\dom f$, by definition of the supremum $f^*(u)\ge\ps{x}{u}-f(x)$, i.e. $f(x)+f^*(u)\ge\ps{x}{u}$.

2. Equality case, rewriting the subgradient inequality. Let $x\in\dom f$. Then $$u\in\partial f(x)\iff(\forall y\in\H)\ f(y)\ge f(x)+\ps{y-x}{u}\iff(\forall y\in\H)\ \ps{y}{u}-f(y)\le\ps{x}{u}-f(x)\iff f^*(u)\le\ps{x}{u}-f(x),$$ the last step because $f^*(u)$ is the least upper bound of the left-hand sides. By step 1 the reverse inequality $f^*(u)\ge\ps{x}{u}-f(x)$ always holds, so $u\in\partial f(x)\iff f^*(u)=\ps{x}{u}-f(x)\iff f(x)+f^*(u)=\ps{x}{u}$. If $x\notin\dom f$, both sides are false (see the hypotheses callout), so the equivalence holds for every $(x,u)$.

3. Inversion for $f\in\Gz{\H}$. By the consequence of Moreau–Fenchel, $f^*\in\Gz{\H}$ is proper, so step 2 applies to $f^*$: $x\in\partial f^*(u)\iff f^*(u)+f^{**}(x)=\ps{u}{x}$. Since $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $f^{**}=f$, and this reads $f(x)+f^*(u)=\ps{x}{u}$, which by step 2 is $u\in\partial f(x)$.

Proposition

Conjugate of a separable sum: $f^*(u)=\sum_i f_i^*(u_i)$

Part III · slide 23Slides#
Statement

Let $(\H_i)_{i\in I}$, $I\subset\N$, be Hilbert spaces and $\H=\bigoplus_{i\in I}\H_i$. For every $i\in I$ let $f_i:\H_i\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be proper (see the note), and set $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]:\ x=(x_i)_{i\in I}\mapsto\sum_{i\in I}f_i(x_i)$. Then $$\big(\forall u=(u_i)_{i\in I}\in\H\big)\qquad f^*(u)=\sum_{i\in I}f_i^*(u_i).$$

In one sentence

The supremum of a sum of functions of independent variables is the sum of the suprema: each coordinate is maximized on its own.

Why it is true

$\ps{x}{u}-f(x)=\sum_i\big(\ps{x_i}{u_i}-f_i(x_i)\big)$, and the $i$-th term depends on $x_i$ only. Choosing the coordinates independently to maximize each term gives the sum of the suprema; conversely no choice can beat the sum of the suprema since each term is bounded by its own supremum. The same mechanism (coordinates decouple) gave the separable subdifferential and will give the separable prox on page 3.

What each hypothesis buys
  • Each $f_i$ proper: then $f_i^*\gt-\infty$, so the right-hand side is a well-defined sum in $]-\infty,+\infty]$, and in the proof the coordinates $j\ne i$ can be frozen at points of $\dom f_j$, where their terms are finite.
  • Otherwise only the separable structure. For an infinite $I$ the exchange of $\sup$ and $\sum$ is taken as on the slides (for a finite $I$ it is elementary: see the proof).
Note

The slides do not write "proper" in this statement (they do on slide 14 for the separable subdifferential). It is needed: if some $f_j\equiv+\infty$, then $f\equiv+\infty$ and $f^*\equiv-\infty$, while $f_j^*\equiv-\infty$ and another term may be $+\infty$ (e.g. $f_1=0$, whose conjugate $\ind_{\{0\}}$ is $+\infty$ at $u_1\ne0$), so the right-hand side would read $+\infty-\infty$.

Proof (as on the slides)

1. Decouple. Let $u=(u_i)_{i\in I}\in\H$. Since $\ps{x}{u}=\sum_i\ps{x_i}{u_i}$, $$f^*(u)=\sup_{x\in\H}\big(\ps{x}{u}-f(x)\big)=\sup_{x=(x_i)_{i\in I}\in\H}\ \sum_{i\in I}\big(\ps{x_i}{u_i}-f_i(x_i)\big).$$

2. Exchange sup and sum. For every $x$, each term is $\le\sup_{x_i\in\H_i}\big(\ps{x_i}{u_i}-f_i(x_i)\big)=f_i^*(u_i)$, so $f^*(u)\le\sum_if_i^*(u_i)$. Conversely (finite $I$): if some $f_i^*(u_i)=+\infty$, choose $x_i$ making the $i$-th term arbitrarily large and fix the others at points $x_j\in\dom f_j$ (so that their terms are finite), so $f^*(u)=+\infty$; otherwise (all $f_i^*(u_i)$ finite, as $f_i^*\gt-\infty$ by properness) for $\varepsilon\gt0$ pick $x_i$ with $\ps{x_i}{u_i}-f_i(x_i)\ge f_i^*(u_i)-\varepsilon$ for each $i$, and $x=(x_i)_i$ gives $f^*(u)\ge\sum_if_i^*(u_i)-\abs{I}\varepsilon$. Hence $$f^*(u)=\sum_{i\in I}\ \sup_{x_i\in\H_i}\big(\ps{x_i}{u_i}-f_i(x_i)\big)=\sum_{i\in I}f_i^*(u_i).$$

Fenchel–Young provides the affine minorant that makes $f+\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-x}^2$ coercive (existence of the prox) and, through $\partial f^*=(\partial f)^{-1}$, the Moreau decomposition $\prox_{\gamma f^*}x=x-\gamma\prox_{\gamma^{-1}f}(\gamma^{-1}x)$; Moreau–Fenchel is what allows $f^{**}=f$ in that proof. The normal cone becomes the projection ($P_C=\prox_{\ind_C}$, with $x-P_Cx\in N_C(P_Cx)$), and support functions give $\prox_{\sigma_C}=\Id-P_C$, hence soft-thresholding. Pages 4–6 iterate operators whose fixed points are the zeros of $\partial f+\nabla g$, $\partial f+\partial g$ or $\partial f+L^*\partial g\,L$: by the easy inclusion these zeros are minimizers, by Moreau–Rockafellar they are all the minimizers — which is why each convergence theorem assumes either that such a zero exists, or a minimizer together with a finite-valued smooth term or a qualification condition. Later: monotonicity of $\partial f$ makes prox firmly nonexpansive (page 4), and conjugation is the engine of duality (page 6).

Self-check

Ten questions

Why does Fermat's rule $0\in\partial f(x)\iff x\in\Argmin f$ need no convexity, and why does it nevertheless help so little for a nonconvex function?

With $u=0$ the subgradient inequality is literally $f(x)\le f(y)$ for all $y$ — a tautology for any proper $f$. But for a nonconvex $f$ the rule is blind to local minimizers: at a local minimizer that is not global, $0\notin\partial f(x)$ (the horizontal line through $(x,f(x))$ crosses the graph elsewhere), even though other slopes may still support $f$ there. And $\partial f(x)=\emptyset$ wherever $f$ lies strictly above its closed convex envelope (on $]-1,1[$ for $(\abs{x}-1)^2$, everywhere for $-x^2$). So the rule only certifies global minimizers, which local reasoning cannot find, and the sum rule that makes it computable requires $\Gz{}$ functions.

Compute $\partial\abs{\cdot}(0)$ and $\partial\max(0,\cdot)(0)$ from the definition.

$u\in\partial\abs{\cdot}(0)\iff uy\le\abs{y}$ for all $y\iff\abs{u}\le1$: $[-1,1]$. $u\in\partial\max(0,\cdot)(0)\iff uy\le\max(0,y)$ for all $y$: $y\gt0$ gives $u\le1$, $y\lt0$ gives $u\ge0$: $[0,1]$.

Prove in two lines that $\partial f$ is monotone.

$f(x_2)\ge f(x_1)+\ps{x_2-x_1}{u_1}$ and $f(x_1)\ge f(x_2)+\ps{x_1-x_2}{u_2}$; add and cancel the finite values: $\ps{x_1-x_2}{u_1-u_2}\ge0$.

In the proof of $\partial f(x)\subset\{\nabla f(x)\}$, which test vector concludes, and where was convexity used?

From $u\in\partial f(x)$ one gets $\ps{\nabla f(x)-u}{y}\ge0$ for all $y$; the test vector $y=u-\nabla f(x)$ gives $-\norm{u-\nabla f(x)}^2\ge0$. Convexity is not used in this inclusion; it is used only for $\nabla f(x)\in\partial f(x)$ (chord above the graph).

State the Moreau–Rockafellar theorem and give an example where the inclusion $\partial f+\partial g\subset\partial(f+g)$ is strict.

$f\in\Gz{\H}$, $g\in\Gz{\G}$, $L\in\B(\H,\G)$, and $\inte(\dom g)\cap L(\dom f)\ne\emptyset$ or $\dom g\cap\inte L(\dom f)\ne\emptyset$ $\Rightarrow$ $\partial(f+g\circ L)=\partial f+L^*\partial g\,L$. Strict example: $f=-\sqrt{\cdot}$ on $[0,+\infty[$ and $g=\ind_{]-\infty,0]}$ at $0$: $\partial(f+g)(0)=\partial\ind_{\{0\}}(0)=\R$ but $\partial f(0)=\emptyset$. Or two closed discs touching at a point.

What is $N_C(x)$ for $x$ on the boundary of a closed ball, and how does it encode the optimality condition for minimizing a smooth convex $f$ over the ball?

$N_C(x)=\{\alpha(x-c):\alpha\ge0\}$ when $\norm{x-c}=\rho$. Optimality: $-\nabla f(\hat x)\in N_C(\hat x)$, i.e. either $\hat x$ is inside and $\nabla f(\hat x)=0$, or $\hat x$ is on the sphere and $-\nabla f(\hat x)$ is a nonnegative multiple of the outward radius $\hat x-c$.

Compute $(\tfrac12\norm{\cdot}^2)^*$ and $\norm{\cdot}^*$. Which of $\tfrac12\norm{\cdot}^2$, $\norm{\cdot}^2$, $\norm{\cdot}$ is self-conjugate?

Complete the square: $\ps{x}{u}-\tfrac12\norm{x}^2=\tfrac12\norm{u}^2-\tfrac12\norm{u-x}^2\le\tfrac12\norm{u}^2$ with equality at $x=u$, so $(\tfrac12\norm{\cdot}^2)^*=\tfrac12\norm{\cdot}^2$. Radial rule with $\varphi=\abs{\cdot}$, $\varphi^*=\ind_{[-1,1]}$: $\norm{\cdot}^*=\ind_{\overline B(0,1)}$. Scaling rule with $\alpha=2$: $(\norm{\cdot}^2)^*=\tfrac14\norm{\cdot}^2$. Only $\tfrac12\norm{\cdot}^2$ is self-conjugate — and it is the only function with $f=f^*$: Fenchel–Young at $u=x$ gives $2f(x)\ge\norm{x}^2$, i.e. $f\ge\tfrac12\norm{\cdot}^2$, and conjugation reverses inequalities, so $f=f^*\le(\tfrac12\norm{\cdot}^2)^*=\tfrac12\norm{\cdot}^2$. (Intuition: its gradient is the identity, so the Legendre change of variables $u=\nabla f(x)$ does nothing.)

Why is $f^*$ always lsc and convex, and what does Moreau–Fenchel add?

$f^*=\sup_{x\in\dom f}\big(\ps{x}{\cdot}-f(x)\big)$ is a supremum of continuous affine functions: its epigraph is an intersection of closed half-spaces, hence closed convex. Moreau–Fenchel says that for a proper $f$, $f^{**}=f$ exactly when $f$ is lsc convex — the affine minorants reconstruct $f$ — and hence that $f^*$ is proper (in $\Gz{\H}$) when $f\in\Gz{\H}$.

Why is every norm a support function, and of which set?

A norm is positively homogeneous and in $\Gz{\H}$ (finite, continuous, convex), so by the theorem it equals $\sigma_C$ with $C=\partial\norm{\cdot}(0)=\{u:\ \ps{x}{u}\le\norm{x}\ \forall x\}$, the dual unit ball. For $\norm{\cdot}_q$ on $\R^N$, Hölder identifies $C=\{\norm{y}_{q^*}\le1\}$; for $\ell^1$, $C=[-1,1]^N$.

Derive $u\in\partial f(x)\iff x\in\partial f^*(u)$ for $f\in\Gz{\H}$ from Fenchel–Young, and say where $\Gz{}$ is used.

Equality case for $f$: $u\in\partial f(x)\iff f(x)+f^*(u)=\ps{x}{u}$. Equality case for $f^*$ (proper since $f\in\Gz{}$): $x\in\partial f^*(u)\iff f^*(u)+f^{**}(x)=\ps{x}{u}$. Moreau–Fenchel ($f\in\Gz{\H}$) gives $f^{**}=f$, so the two right-hand sides coincide. Without $\Gz{}$ one only gets $x\in\partial f^*(u)\iff u\in\partial f^{**}(x)$.
