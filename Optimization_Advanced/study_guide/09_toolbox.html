The toolbox

The course proves dozens of results, and almost every proof is assembled from the same two dozen moves: put the constraint in the function, solve an inclusion instead of minimizing, expand a convex combination, telescope, add two inequalities, lift to a product space, dualize, majorize. This page names each move, shows why it works, and points to every place it is used. Then it turns the course into tools: which algorithm for which problem, every algorithm side by side, the proof recipes, the hypotheses and what they buy, the prox cookbook and the constants.

  1. Stuck in a proof? Scan the "you need … → try" table below, open the move, read its template and its worked example, then come back to your proof.
  2. Learn each move as a reflex: the template, the one identity behind it, the "muscle memory" line, and the pitfall that tells you when it does not apply.
  3. Use the tools: the decision tree picks the algorithm, the tables give its exact hypotheses and constants, the calculators check them for your numbers.

Overview

A dozen moves, used over and over

The whole course is one line of thought, and the same few moves carry it from one end to the other. Once you see them, a new proof stops being a new object: it is a familiar sequence of familiar steps.

Pages 1–6 follow the principle Fermat's rule → zeros of a sum of operators → fixed points of a nonexpansive composition → Fejér/Krasnosel'skii–Mann convergence; page 7 adds no prox? majorize instead. Each arrow is a move. Fermat's rule becomes computable when you solve an inclusion instead of minimizing (T08); the inclusion becomes a fixed-point equation through the resolvent dictionary (T15); the operator is shown averaged by counting (T22); and convergence is always the same three moves: the λ-identity gives a strict decrease (T12), the decreases telescope (T13), Fejér monotonicity and demiclosedness pin down the limit (T14). Around this spine, a handful of local moves do the analysis: constraints as indicators (T01), the direct method (T02, T03), chords and segments (T04, T05), "write it twice and add" (T06), squares and conjugates (T09, T10, T17), and the structural moves that create parallelism (T16, T21) or recognize an old algorithm in a new one (T18).

Every card below has the same anatomy: the move as a template you can apply, in one sentence, why it works (the identity or principle, with the formula), a worked example taken from the course, where it appears (every card of pages 1–8 that cites the code; hover a chip to see the statement), a muscle-memory line, and the pitfall that tells you when the move does not apply.

Moves T01–T03 · pages 1, 3, 7, labs

Setting up: constraints and existence

Before any algorithm: write the problem as one extended-valued function, and prove that its infimum is attained. Three moves do all of it.

Trick

T01 · Indicator trick: constraints become $+\infty$ values

pages 1–6 · labsKey#
The move

Whenever a problem carries a constraint $x\in C$, delete the constraint and add the term $\ind_C$ to the objective: $$\Argmin_{x\in C}f(x)=\Argmin_{x\in\H}\big(f+\ind_C\big)(x),\qquad \ind_C(x)=\begin{cases}0&x\in C\\+\infty&x\notin C.\end{cases}$$ Then treat $\ind_C$ like any other term of $\Gz{\H}$: subdifferentiate it ($\partial\ind_C=N_C$), prox it ($\prox_{\gamma\ind_C}=P_C$ for every $\gamma\gt0$), conjugate it ($\ind_C^*=\sigma_C$). Equalities are constraints too: $Lx=b$ is $\ind_{\{b\}}(Lx)$, consensus $x_1=\dots=x_m$ is $\ind_D$ of the diagonal.

In one sentence

A constraint is a wall of height $+\infty$; once it is a function it can be added, subdifferentiated, proxed and conjugated like every other term, and the unconstrained theory covers constrained problems for free.

Why it works

With the convention $a+(+\infty)=+\infty$, $f+\ind_C$ equals $f$ on $C$ and $+\infty$ outside, so it has the same infimum and the same minimizers as $f$ restricted to $C$. The four operations of the course are then explicit: $$\ind_C\in\Gz{\H}\iff C\text{ nonempty, closed, convex};\qquad\partial\ind_C(x)=N_C(x)=\{u\mid\ps{u}{y-x}\le0\ \forall y\in C\};\qquad\prox_{\gamma\ind_C}=P_C;\qquad\ind_C^*=\sigma_C.$$ The first because $\epi\ind_C=C\times[0,+\infty[$; the second is the definition of a subgradient with $f$ replaced by $\ind_C$ ("the affine function stays below $0$ on $C$"); the third because $\gamma\ind_C=\ind_C$, so the prox minimizes $\tfrac12\norm{y-x}^2$ over $C$; the fourth is the definition of the conjugate restricted to $C$. Say it out loud: a constraint is a function whose prox is a projection and whose subdifferential is the normal cone.

Worked example · projected gradient and LP duality
  1. Minimize a smooth $g$ (with $\nu$-Lipschitz gradient) over a closed convex $C$: write $g+\ind_C$. Forward–Backward with $f=\ind_C$ needs only $\prox_{\gamma\ind_C}=P_C$: $x_{n+1}=x_n+\lambda_n\big(P_C(x_n-\gamma\nabla g(x_n))-x_n\big)$, with the FB constants $\gamma\in\,]0,2/\nu[$, $\lambda_n\in[0,2-\gamma\nu/2]$ unchanged.
  2. Its fixed points satisfy $0\in N_C(\hat x)+\nabla g(\hat x)$, i.e. $-\nabla g(\hat x)\in N_C(\hat x)$: the variational inequality of page 1.
  3. Linear programming: $\min\ps{c}{x}$ s.t. $x\ge0$, $Lx\ge b$ is $f(x)+g(Lx)$ with $f=\ps{c}{\cdot}+\ind_{[0,+\infty[^N}$ and $g=\ind_{[0,+\infty[^K}(\cdot-b)$; conjugating the indicators produces the dual LP.

Muscle memory

Constraint $\leftrightarrow\ind_C$; then $\partial\ind_C=N_C$, $\prox_{\gamma\ind_C}=P_C$, $\ind_C^*=\sigma_C$, and $\ind_C\in\Gz{\H}\iff C$ nonempty closed convex.

When it does not apply

$\ind_C$ is convex only if $C$ is, lsc only if $C$ is closed: a finite label set (segmentation, page 1) gives a combinatorial problem outside the course's theorems. Do not confuse $\ind_C$ ($0$/$+\infty$) with the characteristic function $\mathbf 1_C$ ($1$/$0$). And adding indicators can destroy the qualification condition of the sum rule: when $\dom f\cap C$ reduces to a boundary point, $\partial(f+\ind_C)$ can be strictly larger than $\partial f+N_C$ (page 2: $f=-\sqrt{\cdot}$ on $[0,+\infty[$ and $C=\,]-\infty,0]$).

Trick

T02 · Direct method: minimizing sequence, compactness, lower semicontinuity

pages 1, 7 · labsKey#
The move

Whenever you must prove that $\inf f$ is attained: (1) take a minimizing sequence, $f(x_n)\to\inf f$; (2) extract a convergent subsequence $x_{n_k}\to\hat x$ (compactness); (3) pass to the limit with lower semicontinuity: $$\inf f\ \le\ f(\hat x)\ \le\ \liminf_{k\to+\infty}f(x_{n_k})=\inf f .$$ Hence $f(\hat x)=\inf f$ (and, in passing, $\inf f\gt-\infty$).

In one sentence

Three hypotheses, three words: minimizing (proper), extract (compact), pass to the limit (lsc).

Why it works

The infimum of a proper function is in $[-\infty,+\infty[$ and is, by definition, the limit of some sequence of values. Compactness is exactly the property "every sequence has a subsequence converging inside the set". Lower semicontinuity is exactly the inequality needed at the limit point, in the right direction: $f(\hat x)\le\liminf f(x_{n_k})$; the reverse inequality $f(\hat x)\ge\inf f$ is free. Each hypothesis is used once, and none can be dropped. Say it: a minimizing sequence has a limit point, and lsc says the limit point is at least as good as the sequence.

Worked example · Weierstrass, then MM convergence
  1. $S$ nonempty compact, $f:S\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ proper lsc, $\mu=\inf_Sf$. Take $x_n\in S$ with $f(x_n)\to\mu$; extract $x_{n_k}\to\hat x\in S$; then $\mu\le f(\hat x)\le\liminf f(x_{n_k})=\mu$.
  2. In the MM proofs the same three words identify the limit of an algorithm: $f(x_n)$ decreases, so $x_n$ stays in the compact level set $\lev_{\le f(x_0)}f$ (coercivity, T03); extract $x_{n_k}\to\tilde x$; continuity of $\nabla f$ passes $\nabla f(x_{n_k})\to0$ to the limit: $\nabla f(\tilde x)=0$.

Muscle memory

Minimize, extract, lsc. One hypothesis per word.

When it does not apply

Without lsc: $f(x)=x^2$ for $x\ne0$, $f(0)=1$ has $\inf f=0$, never attained (the limit point $0$ is "worse than the sequence"). Without compactness: $f=e^x$ on $\R$ (convex, continuous, $\inf f=0$ not attained: minimizing sequences run to $-\infty$). In infinite dimension bounded sets are only weakly sequentially compact, so step (3) needs lsc for weak convergence; convex + lsc gives it, because closed convex sets (the level sets) are weakly closed Extra.

Trick

T03 · Coercivity confines minimizing sequences to a ball

pages 1, 3, 7 · labsKey#
The move

Whenever the domain is unbounded, use coercivity to come back to a bounded set: pick $x_0\in\dom f$; coercivity gives $R\gt0$ such that $\norm x\gt R\Rightarrow f(x)\gt f(x_0)$; so every point outside $\overline B(0,R)$ is worse than $x_0$, the minimization can be restricted to $\overline B(0,R)$, and in finite dimension that ball is compact (then T02). Equivalently: the level set $\lev_{\le f(x_0)}f$ is bounded, and it contains every minimizing sequence (eventually) and every iterate of a descent method.

In one sentence

Far away the function is too expensive, so the search for the minimum, and the iterates of any method that decreases $f$, stay in a ball.

Why it works

Coercive means $\lim_{\norm x\to+\infty}f(x)=+\infty$: for every $M$ there is $R$ with $\norm x\gt R\Rightarrow f(x)\gt M$. Take $M=f(x_0)\lt+\infty$. If $\inf f\lt f(x_0)$, a minimizing sequence eventually has $f(x_n)\lt f(x_0)$, hence $\norm{x_n}\le R$; if $\inf f=f(x_0)$, $x_0$ is already a minimizer. Coercivity usually comes from one of three sources: a coercive term ($\norm\cdot_1$, $\norm\cdot^2$, the entropy of lab 2); a bounded constraint ($f+\ind_C$ is $+\infty$ outside a ball when $C$ is bounded); or a quadratic plus an affine minorant: $f\ge\ps{u}{\cdot}-f^*(u)$ by Fenchel–Young (T10) and $\ps{u}{y}+\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{y-x}^2\to+\infty$. Say it: coercive means bounded level sets, and bounded level sets mean a ball to work in.

Worked example · existence of the prox and existence in $\R^N$
  1. $f\in\Gz{\H}$, so $f^*\in\Gz{\H}$ is proper: pick $u$ with $f^*(u)\in\R$. For every $y$, $f(y)+\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{y-x}^2\ge\ps{u}{y}-f^*(u)+\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{y-x}^2\to+\infty$ as $\norm y\to+\infty$: the proximal objective is coercive.
  2. It is also strictly convex (the quadratic is), so it has exactly one minimizer: $\prox_{\gamma f}x$ is well defined for every $f\in\Gz{\H}$ and $\gamma\gt0$.
  3. In $\R^N$: proper + lsc + coercive ⇒ restrict to $\overline B(0,R)$ (compact) ⇒ Weierstrass ⇒ $\Argmin f\neq\emptyset$; it is closed (lsc) and bounded (coercive), hence compact.

Muscle memory

Coercive ⇒ bounded level sets ⇒ minimizing sequences and descent iterates live in a ball. Affine minorant + parabola = coercive.

When it does not apply

Coercivity gives a ball, not compactness: in infinite dimension the ball is only weakly compact. It is sufficient, not necessary: $x^2/(1+x^2)$ is not coercive but has the minimizer $0$. It does not imply "bounded below": $x^2-1/\abs x$ (with $f(0)=0$) is coercive with $\inf f=-\infty$ (it is not lsc at $0$, page 1). And it gives existence, never uniqueness: $\abs{\abs x-1}$ is coercive with two minimizers.

First-order calculus: segments, pairs, test vectors

How the course extracts derivatives, monotonicity and identifications from inequalities: move along a segment, integrate along it, write an inequality twice, and choose the right test point.

Trick

T04 · Chord argument: move along $x+\alpha(y-x)$ and let $\alpha\to0^+$

pages 1, 2#
The move

Whenever you know an inequality at a point $x$ (a minimum, a subgradient, convexity) and want a statement about slopes: compare $x$ with the point $x+\alpha(y-x)=(1-\alpha)x+\alpha y$ of the segment $[x,y]$ (it lies in $C$ when $C$ is convex), divide the inequality by $\alpha\gt0$, and let $\alpha\to0^+$: $$\frac{f(x+\alpha(y-x))-f(x)}{\alpha}\ \xrightarrow[\alpha\to0^+]{}\ \ps{\nabla f(x)}{y-x}.$$

In one sentence

On a segment the problem is one-dimensional: dividing a comparison of values by the step length and shrinking the step turns it into a comparison of slopes.

Why it works

Two inequalities can be divided by $\alpha\gt0$. Minimality: if $x$ minimizes $f$ over a convex $C$ (even locally), then $f(x+\alpha(y-x))-f(x)\ge0$ for small $\alpha$, and the Gâteaux derivative gives $\ps{\nabla f(x)}{y-x}\ge0$. Convexity: $f(x+\alpha(y-x))\le f(x)+\alpha\big(f(y)-f(x)\big)$, so the difference quotient is $\le f(y)-f(x)$, and in the limit $\ps{\nabla f(x)}{y-x}\le f(y)-f(x)$: the tangent is below the graph. The same chord used without a limit gives "local implies global" for convex functions. Say it: chord, divide by $\alpha$, let $\alpha\to0^+$: values become slopes.

Worked example · variational inequality, $\partial f=\{\nabla f\}$, local = global
  1. $\hat x$ local minimizer of $f$ over convex $C$, $y\in C$: for $\alpha$ small, $f(\hat x+\alpha(y-\hat x))\ge f(\hat x)$; divide by $\alpha\gt0$, let $\alpha\to0^+$: $\ps{\nabla f(\hat x)}{y-\hat x}\ge0$ for all $y\in C$.
  2. $f$ convex, Gâteaux differentiable at $x$: the convexity chord gives $\ps{\nabla f(x)}{y-x}\le f(y)-f(x)$, i.e. $\nabla f(x)\in\partial f(x)$. Conversely, for $u\in\partial f(x)$, $f(x+\alpha y)-f(x)\ge\alpha\ps{u}{y}$; divide by $\alpha\gt0$: $\ps{\nabla f(x)-u}{y}\ge0$ for all $y$ — then T07 finishes.
  3. Local = global: if $f(y)\lt f(\hat x)$, the chord gives $f(\hat x+\alpha(y-\hat x))\le(1-\alpha)f(\hat x)+\alpha f(y)\lt f(\hat x)$ for every $\alpha\in\,]0,1]$, including $\alpha$ small: $\hat x$ is not a local minimizer.
Where it appears1 · convex set1 · variational inequality1 · minimizers of a convex function2 · $\partial f=\{\nabla f\}$
Muscle memory

Segment, divide by $\alpha\gt0$, $\alpha\to0^+$. Minimality gives $\ps{\nabla f(\hat x)}{y-\hat x}\ge0$; convexity gives the tangent below the graph.

When it does not apply

Divide by $\alpha\gt0$ only: letting $\alpha\to0^-$ flips the inequality, and at a kink the two one-sided limits differ ($\abs\cdot$ at $0$). The intermediate point must be admissible, which is why $C$ must be convex (or $y-x$ a feasible direction). The conclusion is necessary only: without convexity of $f$, $f=-x^2$ on $[-1,1]$ satisfies it at its maximizer $0$.

Trick

T05 · Integrate along the segment: $\varphi(t)=f(x+t(y-x))$

pages 1, 2, 4, 7#
The move

Whenever you need a global inequality between $f(x)$, $f(y)$ and gradients, restrict $f$ to the segment: $\varphi(t)=f(x+t(y-x))$, $t\in[0,1]$, with $$\varphi'(t)=\ps{\nabla f(x+t(y-x))}{y-x},\qquad\varphi''(t)=\ps{y-x}{\nabla^2f(x+t(y-x))(y-x)},\qquad f(y)-f(x)=\int_0^1\varphi'(t)\,dt,$$ and do one-variable calculus: bound the integrand, or use the sign of $\varphi''$.

In one sentence

Every multivariate statement about smoothness or convexity is a statement about the one-variable functions obtained by restricting to lines.

Why it works

Chain rule plus the fundamental theorem of calculus: $$f(y)=f(x)+\ps{\nabla f(x)}{y-x}+\int_0^1\ps{\nabla f(x+t(y-x))-\nabla f(x)}{y-x}\,dt .$$ Bound the integrand from above by $\nu t\norm{y-x}^2$ (Cauchy–Schwarz and the $\nu$-Lipschitz gradient): descent lemma. Bound it from below by $0$ (monotone gradient): gradient inequality, hence convexity. $\varphi''\ge0$ on every segment ⇔ $\varphi'$ nondecreasing ⇔ $\varphi$ convex: the Hessian test. Say it: restrict, differentiate once, integrate, bound the integrand.

Worked example · descent lemma (= the quadratic majorant of page 7)

If $\nabla f$ is $\nu$-Lipschitz: $$f(y)-f(x)-\ps{\nabla f(x)}{y-x}=\int_0^1\ps{\nabla f(x+t(y-x))-\nabla f(x)}{y-x}dt\le\int_0^1\nu t\norm{y-x}^2dt=\frac\nu2\norm{y-x}^2 .$$ So $f(y)\le f(x)+\ps{\nabla f(x)}{y-x}+\frac\nu2\norm{y-x}^2$: a parabola tangent at $x$ lies above $f$. Its minimizer is the gradient step $x-\nabla f(x)/\nu$, which is why gradient descent with $\gamma=1/\nu$ is an MM algorithm.

Where it appears1 · Hessian test2 · monotone gradient4 · descent lemma7 · Lipschitz majorant7 · Hessian-bound majorant
Muscle memory

$f(y)-f(x)=\int_0^1\ps{\nabla f(x+t(y-x))}{y-x}dt$; Lipschitz ⇒ $\le\frac\nu2\norm{y-x}^2$ above the tangent.

When it does not apply

The whole segment must lie in the domain where $f$ is differentiable (the slides ask for $\dom f$ open and convex). A one-sided bound on the integrand gives a one-sided inequality: Lipschitz continuity of $\nabla f$ alone gives the upper parabola, never the lower bound; that one comes from convexity. And $\nabla^2f\succ0$ gives strict convexity but not conversely ($x^4$ at $0$).

Trick

T06 · Write the defining inequality twice and add

pages 2, 4, 6, 7Key#
The move

Whenever a definition gives an inequality "from the point of view of $x_1$" (a subgradient inequality, Fenchel–Young, a descent inequality), write it once at $x_1$ tested against $x_2$, once at $x_2$ tested against $x_1$, and add: the function values cancel, and an inequality between points and slopes remains.

In one sentence

Symmetrize: what holds from each end of a pair holds for the pair, and the unknown values $f(x_1)$, $f(x_2)$ drop out.

Why it works

With $u_1\in\partial f(x_1)$ and $u_2\in\partial f(x_2)$: $$f(x_2)\ge f(x_1)+\ps{x_2-x_1}{u_1},\qquad f(x_1)\ge f(x_2)+\ps{x_1-x_2}{u_2}.$$ Adding (both values are finite since $x_1,x_2\in\dom f$), $f(x_1)+f(x_2)$ cancels and $0\ge\ps{x_2-x_1}{u_1-u_2}$, i.e. $\ps{u_1-u_2}{x_1-x_2}\ge0$. The same addition applied to Fenchel–Young turns two primal-dual inequalities into weak duality, and applied to the conjugate inequality at two gradients gives Baillon–Haddad. Say it: twice, add, cancel.

Worked example · $\partial f$ monotone, weak duality, Baillon–Haddad
  1. Monotonicity of $\partial f$: the two lines above.
  2. Weak duality: Fenchel–Young for $f$ at $(x,-L^*v)$ and for $g$ at $(Lx,v)$, added: $f(x)+g(Lx)+f^*(-L^*v)+g^*(v)\ge\ps{x}{-L^*v}+\ps{Lx}{v}=0$. Taking the infimum over $x$ and over $v$: $\mu\ge-\mu^*$.
  3. Baillon–Haddad (slide 18 of Part V): $f^*(\nabla f(y))\ge f^*(\nabla f(x))+\ps{x}{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}+\frac1{2\nu}\norm{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}^2$ and the same with $x,y$ exchanged; adding gives $\ps{y-x}{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}\ge\frac1\nu\norm{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}^2$.
Where it appears2 · $\partial f$ monotone2 · gradient inequality2 · sum inclusion4 · Opial's lemma4 · demiclosedness4 · Baillon–Haddad6 · weak duality7 · majorant calculus7 · VMFB convergence
Muscle memory

Twice, add, cancel. Subgradient inequality ×2 = monotonicity; Fenchel–Young ×2 = weak duality.

When it does not apply

The values must be finite to cancel ($x_1,x_2\in\dom f$; never write $+\infty-\infty$). Adding loses information: monotonicity is weaker than the subgradient inequality (on an affine piece $\ps{u_1-u_2}{x_1-x_2}=0$ with $x_1\ne x_2$), so you cannot go back. And the cross terms cancel only when the same pair of points appears in both inequalities.

Trick

T07 · Choose the test vector cleverly ($y=u-\nabla f(x)$, $\alpha\to+\infty$, freeze all coordinates but one)

pages 1, 2, 3, 6, 7#
The move

Whenever an inequality holds for every $y$ (or every $\alpha\gt0$, every competitor), plug in the one test point that turns it into your conclusion: the vector you want to kill ($y=u-\nabla f(x)$ gives $-\norm{u-\nabla f(x)}^2\ge0$); a dilation $\alpha x$ with $\alpha\to+\infty$ in a positively homogeneous expression (forces a value into $\{0,+\infty\}$); the point equal to $x$ except in one coordinate (separates a sum); a competitor in one direction of a subspace (certifies a decrease).

In one sentence

A universally quantified inequality is a whole family of facts; pick the member of the family that says what you want.

Why it works

T08 · Fermat + sum rule, then rewrite $0\in\partial f(p)+p-x$ as $x\in(\Id+\partial f)p$

pages 2–7 · labsKeyExam#
The move

Whenever you must characterize or compute an argmin (a prox, a fixed point, a saddle point, a Kuhn–Tucker point): (1) Fermat: $p\in\Argmin F\iff0\in\partial F(p)$; (2) sum rule: split $\partial F$ into the subdifferentials of its pieces (free when the other pieces are finite and differentiable); (3) rewrite the inclusion so that the unknown sits inside a resolvent: $$p=\prox_{\gamma f}x\iff0\in\partial f(p)+\tfrac1\gamma(p-x)\iff\frac{x-p}{\gamma}\in\partial f(p)\iff x\in(\Id+\gamma\partial f)(p).$$ Then solve the inclusion case by case on the pieces of $\partial f$.

In one sentence

Never minimize: solve the inclusion. Optimization questions become (set-valued) equations, and the resolvent $(\Id+\gamma\partial f)^{-1}=\prox_{\gamma f}$ reads them back.

Why it works

$F=f+\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{\cdot-x}^2$ is a sum of a $\Gz{}$ function and a finite, differentiable convex function, so the sum rule holds without any qualification condition: $\partial F(p)=\partial f(p)+\tfrac1\gamma(p-x)$. Fermat's rule (valid for every proper $F$) gives $0\in\partial F(p)$. The subgradient is evaluated at the arrival point $p$: that is what "implicit step" means. The same three steps turn every composite optimality condition into a fixed-point equation, e.g. $0\in\partial f(x)+\nabla g(x)\iff x-\gamma\nabla g(x)\in(\Id+\gamma\partial f)x\iff x=\prox_{\gamma f}(x-\gamma\nabla g(x))$ (T15). Say it: prox is the inverse of "add a subgradient".

T09 · Complete the square: $\ps{x}{u}-\tfrac12\norm x^2=\tfrac12\norm u^2-\tfrac12\norm{u-x}^2$

pages 2, 3, 4, 6, 7#
The move

Whenever a linear term meets a quadratic, absorb it into a shifted square and keep the leftover constant: $$\ps{x}{u}-\tfrac12\norm x^2=\tfrac12\norm u^2-\tfrac12\norm{u-x}^2,\qquad a\ps{z}{w}+\tfrac a2\norm w^2=\tfrac a2\norm{w+z}^2-\tfrac a2\norm z^2 .$$ The square tells you where the minimum (or maximum) is; the constant does not move it. In reverse, expand squares $\norm{a-b}^2=\norm a^2-2\ps ab+\norm b^2$ to make inner products appear.

In one sentence

A linear term only moves the centre of a quadratic: complete the square and read off the new centre.

Why it works

Expand $\tfrac12\norm{u-x}^2=\tfrac12\norm u^2-\ps{x}{u}+\tfrac12\norm x^2$; that is the whole proof. It is used in four guises: (i) conjugates of quadratics, $\sup_x\ps{x}{u}-\tfrac12\norm x^2=\tfrac12\norm u^2$ attained at $x=u$; (ii) the prox calculus, where $f+\tfrac\alpha2\norm\cdot^2+\ps{z}{\cdot}$ plus $\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-x}^2$ becomes $f+\tfrac{\alpha+1}2\norm{\cdot-\frac{x-z}{\alpha+1}}^2+$ const; (iii) the augmented Lagrangian, where $\gamma\ps{z}{Lx-y}+\tfrac\gamma2\norm{Lx-y}^2=\tfrac\gamma2\norm{Lx-y+z}^2-\tfrac\gamma2\norm z^2$ turns each ADMM step into a prox; (iv) "expand the square two ways" in the demiclosedness, Opial and firm-nonexpansiveness proofs. Say it: linear plus quadratic is a shifted quadratic plus a constant.

T10 · Fenchel–Young as a lever: affine minorants, coercivity, weak duality in one line

pages 2, 3, 4, 6Key#
The move

Whenever you need a lower bound of $f$, an inequality between a primal and a dual quantity, or a way to invert $\partial f$, use $$f(x)+f^*(u)\ge\ps{x}{u}\quad(f\text{ proper}),\qquad\text{with equality}\iff u\in\partial f(x)\iff x\in\partial f^*(u)\quad(\text{last step: }f\in\Gz{\H}).$$

In one sentence

The inequality is the definition of $f^*$ read backwards, its equality case is the subdifferential, and it is the bridge between the primal and the dual worlds.

Why it works

$f^*(u)=\sup_y\ps{y}{u}-f(y)\ge\ps{x}{u}-f(x)$: choose $y=x$ in the supremum. Equality means that $x$ attains the supremum, i.e. $\ps{y}{u}-f(y)\le\ps{x}{u}-f(x)$ for all $y$, i.e. $f(y)\ge f(x)+\ps{y-x}{u}$: $u\in\partial f(x)$. For $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $f^{**}=f$, so the same argument applied to $f^*$ gives $x\in\partial f^*(u)$ under the same equality: $\partial f^*=(\partial f)^{-1}$. Say it: the gap $f(x)+f^*(u)-\ps xu$ is never negative, and it vanishes exactly on the graph of $\partial f$.

Three uses. (1) An affine minorant for free: for $u\in\dom f^*$, $f\ge\ps{u}{\cdot}-f^*(u)$; this is what makes $f+\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{\cdot-x}^2$ coercive (prox existence, T03). (2) Weak duality in one line: add the inequality for $f$ at $(x,-L^*v)$ and for $g$ at $(Lx,v)$; the right-hand sides cancel (T06). (3) The inversion $\partial f^*=(\partial f)^{-1}$: it evaluates the conjugate at gradients in Baillon–Haddad ($f(x)+f^*(\nabla f(x))=\ps{x}{\nabla f(x)}$), gives the prox of $f^*$ from that of $f$ (Moreau decomposition), and turns primal inclusions into dual ones (duality theorems, Kuhn–Tucker points, fixed points of the primal-dual algorithm: $v=\prox_{\sigma g^*}(v+\sigma Lx)\iff Lx\in\partial g^*(v)\iff v\in\partial g(Lx)$).

Worked example · duality theorem (1)

$0\in\partial f(x)+L^*\partial g(Lx)$ means: there is $v$ with $v\in\partial g(Lx)$ and $-L^*v\in\partial f(x)$. Invert both inclusions with $\partial f^*=(\partial f)^{-1}$: $Lx\in\partial g^*(v)$ and $x\in\partial f^*(-L^*v)$. Apply $-L$ to the second and add: $0\in-L\,\partial f^*(-L^*v)+\partial g^*(v)$. Primal zeros exist iff dual zeros exist: one system of two inclusions, read for $x$ or for $v$.

T11 · Reflect to compose: $2\prox-\Id$ is nonexpansive

pages 4, 5, 6Key#
The move

Whenever you must combine two proxes into one operator whose fixed points encode $0\in\partial f(x)+\partial g(x)$, compose their reflections $\rprox_{\gamma h}=2\prox_{\gamma h}-\Id$ (nonexpansive), never the proxes themselves: $$T=\rprox_{\gamma f}\circ\rprox_{\gamma g}\ \text{ is nonexpansive},\qquad\zer(\partial f+\partial g)=\prox_{\gamma g}(\Fix T),$$ and iterate the firmly nonexpansive average $\tfrac12(\Id+T)$ (Douglas–Rachford).

In one sentence

A firmly nonexpansive map is the midpoint of the identity and a nonexpansive map; that map is the reflection, and reflections compose without losing the right fixed points.

Why it works

Write $p=\prox_{\gamma h}x$, $q=\prox_{\gamma h}y$. Firm nonexpansiveness says $\ps{p-q}{x-y}\ge\norm{p-q}^2$, hence $$\norm{\rprox_{\gamma h}x-\rprox_{\gamma h}y}^2=\norm{2(p-q)-(x-y)}^2=4\norm{p-q}^2-4\ps{p-q}{x-y}+\norm{x-y}^2\le\norm{x-y}^2 .$$ Equivalently $\prox_{\gamma h}=\tfrac12(\Id+\rprox_{\gamma h})$ is $\tfrac12$-averaged. Compositions of nonexpansive maps are nonexpansive, and averaging with the identity gives back something firmly nonexpansive to iterate (relaxation $\lambda_n/2$, T22). Say it: prox = midpoint, rprox = mirror; compose mirrors, then average.

Worked example · DR fixed points

$0\in\gamma\big(\partial f(x)+\partial g(x)\big)\iff\exists y:\ x-y\in\gamma\partial f(x),\ y-x\in\gamma\partial g(x)$. The second inclusion is $x=\prox_{\gamma g}y$; the first is $2x-y\in(\Id+\gamma\partial f)x$, i.e. $x=\prox_{\gamma f}(2x-y)=\prox_{\gamma f}(\rprox_{\gamma g}y)$. Reflect once more: $\rprox_{\gamma f}\rprox_{\gamma g}y=2x-\rprox_{\gamma g}y=2x-(2x-y)=y$. So the solutions are the shadows $\prox_{\gamma g}y$ of the fixed points $y$ of $T$.

T12 · The λ-identity $\norm{(1-\lambda)a+\lambda b}^2=(1-\lambda)\norm a^2+\lambda\norm b^2-\lambda(1-\lambda)\norm{a-b}^2$

pages 1, 4, 5KeyExam#
The move

Whenever a relaxed step $x_n+\lambda(Tx_n-x_n)=(1-\lambda)x_n+\lambda Tx_n$ meets a squared distance, expand it with the identity, valid in every Hilbert space for all $a,b\in\H$ and every $\lambda\in\R$: $$\norm{(1-\lambda)a+\lambda b}^2=(1-\lambda)\norm a^2+\lambda\norm b^2-\lambda(1-\lambda)\norm{a-b}^2 .$$ With $a=x_n-x$, $b=Tx_n-x$ ($x$ a fixed point) it gives the strict Fejér decrease of Krasnosel'skii–Mann.

In one sentence

The square of a convex combination is the convex combination of the squares minus a "variance" term; that variance is exactly what averaging gains.

Why it works

It is a two-line expansion (proof below). Read it probabilistically: if $V$ equals $a$ with probability $1-\lambda$ and $b$ with probability $\lambda$, then $\mathbb E\norm V^2=\norm{\mathbb EV}^2+\operatorname{Var}V$ with $\operatorname{Var}V=\lambda(1-\lambda)\norm{a-b}^2$. For $\lambda\in\,]0,1[$ the correction is a gain: this is the strict convexity of $\norm\cdot^2$. For $\lambda\gt1$ it becomes a loss, which only an averaged operator can pay for (relaxation up to $1/\alpha$, T22). Say it: convex combination of the squares, minus the variance.

Proof — expand both sides

1. Left side. $\norm{(1-\lambda)a+\lambda b}^2=(1-\lambda)^2\norm a^2+\lambda^2\norm b^2+2\lambda(1-\lambda)\ps ab$.

2. The correction. $-\lambda(1-\lambda)\norm{a-b}^2=-\lambda(1-\lambda)\norm a^2-\lambda(1-\lambda)\norm b^2+2\lambda(1-\lambda)\ps ab$.

3. Right side. Add $(1-\lambda)\norm a^2+\lambda\norm b^2$: the coefficient of $\norm a^2$ is $(1-\lambda)-\lambda(1-\lambda)=(1-\lambda)^2$, that of $\norm b^2$ is $\lambda-\lambda(1-\lambda)=\lambda^2$, and the cross term is $2\lambda(1-\lambda)\ps ab$. Both sides coincide; no restriction on $\lambda$ was used.

T13 · Telescoping sum ⇒ summable residuals ⇒ residual $\to0$

pages 4, 5, 6, 7Key#
The move

Whenever each step decreases a nonnegative quantity by at least a multiple of a residual, $a_{n+1}\le a_n-c_nr_n$ with $a_n\ge0$, $c_n\ge0$, $r_n\ge0$, sum the inequalities: $$\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_nr_n\le a_0-a_{N+1}\le a_0\qquad\text{for every }N .$$ Then (i) if $c_n\ge c\gt0$: $\sum r_n\lt+\infty$, so $r_n\to0$; (ii) if only $\sum c_n=+\infty$: $\liminf r_n=0$, and $r_n\to0$ as soon as $(r_n)$ is nonincreasing.

In one sentence

A quantity that cannot go below zero can only pay a finite total; if every payment is proportional to the residual, the residual has to die.

Why it works

The partial sums of the decreases telescope to $a_0-a_{N+1}\le a_0$, so the series $\sum c_nr_n$ converges. With $c_n\ge c\gt0$, its terms dominate $cr_n$. With merely $\sum c_n=+\infty$: for every $n$, $\big(\inf_{k\ge n}r_k\big)\sum_{k\ge n}c_k\le\sum_{k\ge n}c_kr_k\lt+\infty$, so $\inf_{k\ge n}r_k=0$ for all $n$, i.e. $\liminf r_n=0$; a nonincreasing sequence converges to its $\liminf$. Say it: sum the decreases; a bounded-below quantity has a finite budget.

T14 · Fejér + demiclosedness: the recipe for weak convergence

pages 4, 5, 6, 7KeyExam#
The move

To prove that $x_n\wto$ a point of $D=\Fix T$: (1) Fejér: $\norm{x_{n+1}-x}\le\norm{x_n-x}$ for all $x\in D$ (so the distances converge and $(x_n)$ is bounded); (2) residual: $Tx_n-x_n\to0$ (T13); (3) demiclosedness: every weak cluster point $\hat x$ (one exists by boundedness) satisfies $\hat x\in\Fix T$; (4) Fejér convergence theorem (Opial): the whole sequence converges weakly to a point of $D$.

In one sentence

Fejér keeps every solution in sight, demiclosedness says that every accumulation point is a solution, and two different accumulation points cannot both be in sight.

Why it works

Bounded sequences have weak cluster points. If $\hat x,\hat x'\in D$ were two of them, both distances converge, so $$\norm{x_n-\hat x}^2-\norm{x_n-\hat x'}^2=2\ps{x_n}{\hat x'-\hat x}+\norm{\hat x}^2-\norm{\hat x'}^2$$ converges, hence $\ps{x_n}{\hat x'-\hat x}$ converges; its limits along the two subsequences, $\ps{\hat x}{\hat x'-\hat x}$ and $\ps{\hat x'}{\hat x'-\hat x}$, must agree, so $\norm{\hat x'-\hat x}^2=0$. Demiclosedness is the closedness of $\Id-T$ for (weak in, strong out) convergence: $x_{n_k}\wto\hat x$ and $(\Id-T)x_{n_k}\to0$ give $(\Id-T)\hat x=0$; it is proved by expanding $\norm{x_n-T\hat x}^2$ (T09). Say it: Fejér + residual $\to0$ + demiclosed ⇒ weak limit in $\Fix T$.

Worked example · the end of KM, inherited by FB, DR, ADMM

FB, DR and the primal-dual algorithm are KM (or averaged) iterations of their operators, so they inherit the conclusion; ADMM inherits it through DR on the dual; VMFB replaces demiclosedness by the closed graph of $\partial f_2$.

The move

Whenever an algorithm is a fixed-point iteration, translate its fixed-point equation into the optimality condition (and back) with the resolvent identity: for $\gamma\gt0$, $A$ single-valued and $B$ set-valued, $$x\in\zer(A+B)\iff x-\gamma Ax\in x+\gamma Bx\iff x=(\Id+\gamma B)^{-1}(\Id-\gamma A)x,\qquad(\Id+\gamma\partial f)^{-1}=\prox_{\gamma f}.$$

In one sentence

Optimality conditions are zeros of sums of operators, algorithms are fixed points of compositions, and the resolvent translates one into the other; the step size cancels.

Why it works

Unpack each prox with T08: $x=\prox_{\gamma f}(w)\iff w-x\in\gamma\partial f(x)$. With $w=x-\gamma\nabla g(x)$ this is $-\gamma\nabla g(x)\in\gamma\partial f(x)$, and $\gamma$ cancels. That cancellation is why the fixed points do not depend on $\gamma$ (and why the step size may vary along the iterations). Say it: $\zer(A+B)=\Fix$ of "forward on $A$, backward on $B$".

Structure: lift, dualize, recognize

Three moves change the problem rather than estimate it: copy the variable to split a sum, conjugate a composed term to move the linear operator into a coupling, and rename the variables until a new algorithm is an old one.

Trick

T16 · Product-space lifting: $m$ functions become one, plus a consensus constraint

pages 1, 3, 5, 6 · labsKey#
The move

Whenever you face $\sum_{i=1}^mg_i(x)$ (or $\sum_ig_i(L_iv)$) and only know each prox separately, give each term its own copy of the variable in $\H^m$: $$\operatorname*{minimize}_{x\in\H}\ \sum_{i=1}^mg_i(x)\iff\operatorname*{minimize}_{(x_1,\dots,x_m)\in\H^m}\ \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^mg_i(x_i)}_{G\ \text{separable}}+\ind_D(x_1,\dots,x_m),\qquad D=\{(x,\dots,x)\}=\ran L,$$ with $L:x\mapsto(x,\dots,x)$. Now there are only two functions: $G$, whose prox is $m$ independent proxes, and $\ind_D$, whose prox is an average.

In one sentence

Copy the variable once per term: each worker minimizes its own term, and the only coupling left is "all copies agree", which costs one average.

Why it works · the adjoint computation

Equip $\H^m$ with $\ps{x}{y}=\sum_{i=1}^m\ps{x_i}{y_i}$. For $x\in\H$ and $y\in\H^m$, $$\ps{Lx}{y}=\sum_{i=1}^m\ps{x}{y_i}=\Big\langle x\,\Big|\,\sum_{i=1}^my_i\Big\rangle\quad\Longrightarrow\quad L^*y=\sum_{i=1}^my_i,\qquad L^*L=m\Id,$$ $$P_D=L(L^*L)^{-1}L^*:\ y\mapsto(\bar y,\dots,\bar y),\quad\bar y=\tfrac1m\sum_iy_i,\qquad\prox_{\gamma G}(x)=\big(\prox_{\gamma g_i}x_i\big)_{1\le i\le m}.$$ For general operators $L:v\mapsto(L_1v,\dots,L_mv)$: $L^*y=\sum_iL_i^*y_i$ and $L^*L=\sum_iL_i^*L_i$, the only object that couples the blocks, inverted once (PPXA+). Duality reads the same picture on the other side: $\ind_D^*=\ind_{D^\perp}$ with $D^\perp=\{v:\sum_iv_i=0\}$, which is why the dual of consensus is sharing. Say it: duplicate, and the adjoint sums.

Trick

T17 · Dualize by conjugation: $g(Lx)=\sup_v\ps{v}{Lx}-g^*(v)$

pages 2, 3, 6 · labsKey#
The move

Whenever a nonsmooth $g$ is composed with a linear $L$ (so that $\prox_{g\circ L}$ is not available), replace $g$ by its biconjugate: $$g(Lx)=g^{**}(Lx)=\sup_{v\in\G}\ \ps{v}{Lx}-g^*(v)\qquad(g\in\Gz{\G}).$$ The operator $L$ leaves $g$ and enters a bilinear coupling $\ps{v}{Lx}=\ps{L^*v}{x}$; the dual variable $v$ lives in $\G$; the prox of $g^*$ is free by Moreau; and swapping $\inf_x$ and $\sup_v$ produces the dual problem.

In one sentence

Conjugation trades a function of $Lx$ for a supremum of linear functions of $Lx$, and linear functions of $Lx$ are linear functions of $x$: the operator moves from inside the hard term to the coupling.

Why it works

Fenchel–Moreau ($g\in\Gz{\G}\iff g^{**}=g$) gives the identity. Then $$\inf_x f(x)+g(Lx)=\inf_x\sup_v f(x)+\ps{L^*v}{x}-g^*(v)\ \ge\ \sup_v\Big(\inf_x f(x)+\ps{x}{L^*v}\Big)-g^*(v)=\sup_v-f^*(-L^*v)-g^*(v),$$ since $\inf_xf(x)+\ps{x}{L^*v}=-\sup_x\big(\ps{x}{-L^*v}-f(x)\big)=-f^*(-L^*v)$. That is weak duality; equality is strong duality (qualification condition). For computations, $\prox_{\sigma g^*}(w)=w-\sigma\prox_{g/\sigma}(w/\sigma)$ (Moreau decomposition, page 3), so you never need $g^*$ explicitly. Say it: $L$ goes to the coupling, $g$ goes to the dual.

Worked example · the dual problem and the primal-dual algorithm

  2. With a smooth $h$: $\inf_x\sup_v f(x)+h(x)+\ps{v}{Lx}-g^*(v)$. Descend in $x$ (prox of $f$, gradient of $h$, coupling $L^*v$), ascend in $v$ (prox of $g^*$, coupling $Lx$): that is the primal-dual algorithm, which never inverts $L$.
  3. Total variation: $\lambda\norm{Dx}_1=\sup_{\norm{v}_\infty\le\lambda}\ps{v}{Dx}$, since $(\lambda\norm\cdot_1)^*=\ind_{[-\lambda,\lambda]^N}$; the dual step is a clipping.

Trick

T18 · Change variables to recognize an old algorithm (DR ↔ ADMM, PD = FB in a metric)

pages 4, 5, 6, 7 · labsKey#
The move

Whenever a new iteration looks unfamiliar, look for a change of variables, of parameters or of metric under which it becomes an algorithm whose convergence you already know (KM, FB, DR): rescale the relaxation ($\mu_n=\alpha\lambda_n$), rename the iterates (ADMM's $y_n,z_n$ in terms of DR's $u_n,v_n$), or rewrite the updates as $V(w_n-w_{n+1})\in Aw_{n+1}+Bw_n$ with $V$ self-adjoint positive definite (Forward–Backward in the $V$-metric).

In one sentence

Do not prove convergence twice: find the dictionary in which the new algorithm is an old one, and every conclusion transfers.

Why it works

Convergence statements are invariant under bijective continuous linear changes of variables (they preserve weak convergence, by the weak continuity of bounded linear maps) and under equivalent Hilbert norms ($\norm\cdot_V$ with $V\succ0$ bounded and boundedly invertible). Fixed points correspond through the dictionary, and so do limits. Say it: new algorithm? find the variables in which it is an old one.

  • Averaged iteration: $T=(1-\alpha)\Id+\alpha R$ ⇒ $x_n+\lambda_n(Tx_n-x_n)=x_n+\alpha\lambda_n(Rx_n-x_n)$: KM on $R$ with $\mu_n=\alpha\lambda_n\in[0,1]$, i.e. $\lambda_n\in[0,1/\alpha]$.
  • DR on the dual = ADMM: with $y_n=\gamma^{-1}(u_n-v_n)$, $z_n=\gamma^{-1}v_n$, the dual DR iteration becomes ADMM.
  • PD = FB in a metric: $w=(x,v)$, $V=\begin{pmatrix}\tau^{-1}\Id&-L^*\\-L&\sigma^{-1}\Id\end{pmatrix}$, $V\succ0\iff\tau\sigma\norm L^2\lt1$.
  • VMFB = MM: with $\theta_n\equiv1$, $x_{n+1}=\argmin h(\cdot,x_n)+f_2$; with $A\equiv\beta\Id$ it is FB with step $\theta_n/\beta$.
Worked example · the $y$- and $z$-steps of ADMM

Dual DR: $v_n=\prox_{\gamma g^*}u_n$, $u_{n+1}=u_n+w_n-v_n$. Moreau: $v_n=u_n-\gamma\prox_{g/\gamma}(u_n/\gamma)$, i.e. $y_n:=\frac{u_n-v_n}\gamma=\prox_{g/\gamma}\big(\tfrac{u_n}\gamma\big)$. The $w$-step, read through the chain rule (valid because $L^*L$ is invertible), is $2v_n-u_n-w_n=-\gamma Lx_n$ with $-L^*w_n\in\partial f(x_n)$ (this defines $x_n$); hence $u_{n+1}=u_n+w_n-v_n=v_n+\gamma Lx_n$, i.e. $\frac{u_{n+1}}\gamma=z_n+Lx_n$ with $z_n:=v_n/\gamma$. Hence $y_{n+1}=\prox_{g/\gamma}(z_n+Lx_n)$ and $z_{n+1}=\frac{u_{n+1}}\gamma-y_{n+1}=z_n+Lx_n-y_{n+1}$: the ADMM updates. The convergence of DR (on the dual) becomes the convergence of ADMM, with $\gamma z_n\wto\hat v$.

Trick

T19 · Majorize then minimize: tangent majorants give monotone decrease and sufficient descent

page 7 · lab 4Key#
The move

Whenever $f$ is hard to minimize but easy to bound from above near the current point, build a majorant $h(\cdot,x_n)$ ($f\le h(\cdot,x_n)$ everywhere, $f(x_n)=h(x_n,x_n)$) and set $x_{n+1}\in\Argmin h(\cdot,x_n)$. Decrease is free: $$f(x_{n+1})\ \le\ h(x_{n+1},x_n)\ \le\ h(x_n,x_n)=f(x_n).$$ With the quadratic majorant $h(x,y)=f(y)+\ps{\nabla f(y)}{x-y}+\tfrac12\norm{x-y}^2_{A(y)}$ and the step $x_{n+1}=x_n-\theta_nA(x_n)^{-1}\nabla f(x_n)$, $\theta_n\in\,]0,2[$, the decrease is sufficient: $f(x_n)-f(x_{n+1})\ge(\theta_n^{-1}-\tfrac12)\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2_{A(x_n)}$.

In one sentence

Going downhill on a surrogate that lies above $f$ and touches it here forces $f$ downhill too; a quadratic surrogate turns this into a quantified decrease.

Why it works

The first "$\le$" is majorization, the second is minimization (or merely "not worse than $x_n$"), the equality is the contact condition. For the quadratic majorant, the step satisfies $\nabla f(x_n)=-\theta_n^{-1}A(x_n)(x_{n+1}-x_n)$; inserting it in $h(x_{n+1},x_n)$, $$f(x_{n+1})\le f(x_n)+\ps{\nabla f(x_n)}{x_{n+1}-x_n}+\tfrac12\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2_{A(x_n)}=f(x_n)-\big(\theta_n^{-1}-\tfrac12\big)\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2_{A(x_n)} .$$ The coefficient is positive exactly when $\theta_n\lt2$; with $\theta_n\le2-\bar\theta$ and $\nu\Id\preccurlyeq A(x_n)$ it is at least $\mu_1=\big(\frac1{2-\bar\theta}-\frac12\big)\nu$; with $A(x_n)\preccurlyeq\bar\nu\Id$ and $\theta_n\ge\underline\theta$, $\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}\le\underline\theta^{-1}\bar\nu\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}$, which turns it into $\mu_2\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2$. Say it: above everywhere, equal here; minimize; $f$ goes down.

  2. $\nabla f$ $\beta$-Lipschitz: the descent lemma says $A\equiv\beta\Id$ gives a majorant; its minimizer is $x_n-\nabla f(x_n)/\beta$: gradient descent with $\gamma=1/\beta$ is MM.

T20 · Concavity gives tangent majorants; $x^2-y^2=(x-y)^2+2y(x-y)$

page 7 · lab 4#
The move

Whenever a term is concave (in its argument, or in a transformed argument), its tangent is a majorant: $\varphi$ concave ⇒ $\varphi(u)\le\varphi(v)+\varphi'(v)(u-v)$. For an even penalty $f(x)=\psi(\abs x)$ with $\psi(\sqrt\cdot)$ concave, take the tangent in the variable $u=x^2$ and come back to $x$ with $$x^2-y^2=(x-y)^2+2y(x-y):$$ the tangent in $x^2$ becomes a parabola in $x$, tangent to $f$ at $y$, with curvature $\omega(\abs y)=\dot\psi(\abs y)/\abs y$.

In one sentence

A concave function lies below its tangents; a change of variable makes "concave in $x^2$" produce a quadratic majorant in $x$, and the $x^2-y^2$ identity is the change of variable.

Why it works

A differentiable concave function is below its tangents (the gradient inequality with the sign reversed). With $\varphi=\psi(\sqrt\cdot)$ on $]0,+\infty[$, $\dot\varphi(v)=\dot\psi(\sqrt v)/(2\sqrt v)$, so $\dot\varphi(y^2)=\tfrac12\omega(\abs y)$ and $$\psi(\abs x)=\varphi(x^2)\le\varphi(y^2)+\tfrac12\omega(\abs y)(x^2-y^2)=\psi(\abs y)+\underbrace{\omega(\abs y)\,y}_{=\ \sgn(y)\dot\psi(\abs y)\ =\ \dot f(y)}(x-y)+\tfrac12\omega(\abs y)(x-y)^2 .$$ Say it: concave lies under its tangents; $\psi(\sqrt\cdot)$ concave ⇒ a parabola of curvature $\dot\psi(\abs y)/\abs y$ majorizes $\psi(\abs\cdot)$ at $y$.

Proof of the identity, and of the half-quadratic majorant

1. The identity. $(x-y)^2+2y(x-y)=x^2-2xy+y^2+2xy-2y^2=x^2-y^2$.

2. Tangent in $x^2$. For $x,y\ne0$, concavity of $\varphi$ at $v=y^2$, evaluated at $u=x^2$: $\varphi(x^2)\le\varphi(y^2)+\tfrac12\omega(\abs y)(x^2-y^2)$.

3. Back to $x$. By step 1, $\tfrac12\omega(\abs y)(x^2-y^2)=\omega(\abs y)y(x-y)+\tfrac12\omega(\abs y)(x-y)^2$, and $\omega(\abs y)y=\dot\psi(\abs y)\sgn(y)=\dot f(y)$ (chain rule). The points $x=0$ or $y=0$ follow by continuity, using that $\omega$ has a finite limit at $0^+$.

Trick

T21 · Bound the curvature by a diagonal (Jensen) to make updates parallel

page 7 · lab 4Lab#
The move

Whenever a quadratic majorant couples the coordinates (curvature $A(x)=H^\top H+\dots$), replace it by a diagonal $B(x)\succcurlyeq A(x)$ obtained from Jensen's inequality for the convex square: with $r_m=\sum_p\abs{H_{mp}}$, $$\Big(\sum_iH_{mi}d_i\Big)^2\le r_m\sum_i\abs{H_{mi}}\,d_i^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad H^\top H\preccurlyeq\operatorname{Diag}\big(\abs H^\top\abs H\mathbf1\big).$$ The surrogate becomes separable and the MM step is coordinatewise: $x_{n+1,i}=x_{n,i}-\theta_n\,\partial_if(x_n)/b_i(x_n)$, all $i$ in parallel.

In one sentence

Jensen pushes the square inside each row sum: the cross terms disappear, the majorant gets a little looser, and every coordinate can be updated on its own.

Why it works

For weights $\omega_i\ge0$ with $\sum_i\omega_i=1$, $\big(\sum_i\omega_it_i\big)^2\le\sum_i\omega_it_i^2$. Write $\sum_iH_{mi}d_i=\sum_i\omega_{mi}\frac{H_{mi}d_i}{\omega_{mi}}$ with $\omega_{mi}=\abs{H_{mi}}/r_m$ (terms with $H_{mi}=0$ drop out): the bound is $\sum_i\frac{H_{mi}^2d_i^2}{\omega_{mi}}=r_m\sum_i\abs{H_{mi}}d_i^2$. Summing over the rows, $$d^\top H^\top Hd=\sum_m\Big(\sum_iH_{mi}d_i\Big)^2\le\sum_id_i^2\underbrace{\sum_m\abs{H_{mi}}r_m}_{b_i=[\abs H^\top\abs H\mathbf1]_i}.$$ Say it: Jensen per row, sum the rows: a diagonal dominates $H^\top H$.

A looser majorant means shorter steps ($B\succcurlyeq A$): you trade iterations for parallelism, and on strongly coupled problems the trade can be bad. The weights must be nonnegative and sum to one, and Jensen needs a convex function. For the MM theorem the diagonal must stay uniformly positive and bounded ($\nu\Id\preccurlyeq B(x_n)\preccurlyeq\bar\nu\Id$).

Count: where the constants come from

The step-size and relaxation ranges of every splitting algorithm are not assumptions to memorize: they are the output of one computation. Count the averagedness of each factor, compose, and relax up to the inverse.

Trick

T22 · Count averagedness: the composition formula and the relaxation range

pages 4, 5, 6KeyExam#
The move

Whenever an algorithm reads $x_{n+1}=x_n+\lambda_n(Sx_n-x_n)$ with $S$ a composition of averaged maps: (1) count each factor ($\prox_{\gamma f}$: $\tfrac12$; $\Id-\gamma\nabla g$: $\gamma\nu/2$ for $\gamma\in\,]0,2/\nu[$); (2) compose, $$\gamma_{12}=\frac{\alpha_1+\alpha_2-2\alpha_1\alpha_2}{1-\alpha_1\alpha_2}\iff\frac{\gamma_{12}}{1-\gamma_{12}}=\frac{\alpha_1}{1-\alpha_1}+\frac{\alpha_2}{1-\alpha_2};$$ (3) relax in $[0,1/\gamma_{12}]$: $\lambda_n\in[0,\delta]$ with $\sum_n\lambda_n(\delta-\lambda_n)=+\infty$, $\delta=1/\gamma_{12}$.

In one sentence

Averagedness is a budget: in the "resistance" scale $\rho=\alpha/(1-\alpha)$ compositions add up, and the admissible relaxation is the inverse of the total.

Why it works

An $\alpha$-averaged $S=(1-\alpha)\Id+\alpha R$ relaxed with $\lambda_n$ is KM on the nonexpansive $R$ with $\mu_n=\alpha\lambda_n$ (T18), so $\mu_n\in[0,1]$ means $\lambda_n\in[0,1/\alpha]$, and $\sum\mu_n(1-\mu_n)=\alpha\sum\lambda_n(1-\alpha\lambda_n)=+\infty$ is the condition $\sum\lambda_n(\delta-\lambda_n)=+\infty$ up to the factor $\alpha^2$. The composition formula is slide 16 of Part V (the slides give no proof; page 4 does). Say it: prox one half, gradient $\gamma\nu/2$, compose, relax up to the inverse.

Sanity checks · every one verified numerically
  • Symmetric in $(\alpha_1,\alpha_2)$, as it must be for a formula that only sees the two constants.
  • Composing never helps, never destroys: $\gamma_{12}-\alpha_1=\frac{\alpha_2(1-\alpha_1)^2}{1-\alpha_1\alpha_2}\ge0$ and $1-\gamma_{12}=\frac{(1-\alpha_1)(1-\alpha_2)}{1-\alpha_1\alpha_2}\gt0$, so $\max(\alpha_1,\alpha_2)\le\gamma_{12}\lt1$.
  • Limits: $\alpha_2\to0$ ($A_2\to\Id$) gives $\gamma_{12}\to\alpha_1$; $\alpha_2\to1$ (merely nonexpansive) gives $\gamma_{12}\to1$: no relaxation room left.
  • Two firmly nonexpansive maps ($\alpha_1=\alpha_2=\tfrac12$): $\gamma_{12}=\frac{1/2}{3/4}=\tfrac23$, relaxation up to $\tfrac32$ (resistances $1+1=2$).
  • Forward–Backward ($\alpha_1=\tfrac12$, $\alpha_2=\gamma\nu/2$): $\gamma_{12}=\frac{1/2}{1-\gamma\nu/4}=\frac2{4-\gamma\nu}\in\,]\tfrac12,1[$ and $\delta=1/\gamma_{12}=2-\gamma\nu/2\in\,]1,2[$ (slide 25).
  • Douglas–Rachford: $S=\tfrac12(\Id+T)$ with $T$ nonexpansive is $\tfrac12$-averaged, so $\lambda_n\in[0,2]$; equivalently KM on $T$ with $\mu_n=\lambda_n/2$.
  • Primal-dual: in the $V$-metric the forward operator is $\frac\beta2(\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm L^2)^{-1}$-averaged, so $\delta=2-\frac\beta2(\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm L^2)^{-1}\in[1,2[$ (slide 35 of Part II.5); with $L=0$ it is FB's $2-\tau\beta/2$.
Worked example · Forward–Backward and composition

$T=\prox_{\gamma f}\circ(\Id-\gamma\nabla g)$ with $\alpha_1=\tfrac12$, $\alpha_2=\tfrac{\gamma\nu}2$: $$\alpha=\frac{\tfrac12+\tfrac{\gamma\nu}2-2\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{\gamma\nu}2}{1-\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{\gamma\nu}2}=\frac{1/2}{1-\gamma\nu/4}=\frac{2}{4-\gamma\nu}\iff\alpha^{-1}=2-\frac{\gamma\nu}2=\delta .$$ In resistances: $1+\frac{\gamma\nu/2}{1-\gamma\nu/2}=\frac1{1-\gamma\nu/2}$, and $1/\alpha=1+1/\text{(total resistance)}=2-\gamma\nu/2$.

Muscle memory

Prox $\tfrac12$, gradient $\tfrac{\gamma\nu}2$; $\frac{\gamma_{12}}{1-\gamma_{12}}=\frac{\alpha_1}{1-\alpha_1}+\frac{\alpha_2}{1-\alpha_2}$; relax up to $1/\gamma_{12}$. FB: $\delta=2-\gamma\nu/2$.

When it does not apply

The formula is an upper bound on the averagedness of the composition, so $[0,\delta]$ is a safe range, not necessarily the largest one. It needs $\alpha_1,\alpha_2\lt1$: a merely nonexpansive factor (a reflection) leaves no margin, which is why DR averages $T$ with $\Id$ explicitly. Do not add the constants: $\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1$ would wrongly say "only nonexpansive", while the right answer is $\tfrac23$. And the convex combination rule is different: $\lambda A_1+(1-\lambda)A_2$ is $\big(\lambda\alpha_1+(1-\lambda)\alpha_2\big)$-averaged.

    The MM recipe (page 7)

    1. Majorant. $h(\cdot,x_n)\ge f$, $h(x_n,x_n)=f(x_n)$, quadratic with curvature $A(x_n)$: from a Lipschitz gradient, a Hessian bound, the half-quadratic construction, or a Jensen diagonal T19 T20 T21 T05
    2. Monotone decrease. $f(x_{n+1})\le h(x_{n+1},x_n)\le h(x_n,x_n)=f(x_n)$ T19
    3. Sufficient descent. Insert the update $\nabla f(x_n)=-\theta_n^{-1}A(x_n)(x_{n+1}-x_n)$: $f(x_n)-f(x_{n+1})\ge(\theta_n^{-1}-\tfrac12)\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2_{A(x_n)}\ge\mu_1\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2$, using $\theta_n\le2-\bar\theta$ and $\nu\Id\preccurlyeq A(x_n)$
    4. Gradient control. $\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}\le\underline\theta^{-1}\bar\nu\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}$ (from $A(x_n)\preccurlyeq\bar\nu\Id$, $\theta_n\ge\underline\theta$), so the decrease is $\ge\mu_2\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2$
    5. Telescope. $\sum_n\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2\lt\infty$, $\sum_n\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2\lt\infty$ ⇒ $\nabla f(x_n)\to0$, $x_{n+1}-x_n\to0$ T13
    6. Compactness. $f$ coercive and $f(x_n)\le f(x_0)$ ⇒ $x_n$ in a compact level set ⇒ cluster points $\tilde x$; continuity of $\nabla f$ ⇒ $\nabla f(\tilde x)=0$ T03 T02
    7. Convexity upgrades. Convex ⇒ stationary points are minimizers; strictly convex ⇒ one minimizer, so every subsequence converges to it and $x_n\to\hat x$

    Instances: MM quadratic (the template); 3MG (step 3 by comparing with the best step along $d_n^1$ alone, T07: $\mu_1=\nu/2$, $\mu_2=\nu\gamma_0^2/(2\bar\nu^2\gamma_1^2)$); block MM (step 4 controls only $\nabla_{j_n}f$; quasi-cyclicity + vanishing steps + uniform continuity of $\nabla f$ give $\nabla f(x_n)\to0$); VMFB (step 3 adds the subgradient inequality of $f_2$ at the prox output, T06; the residual is $\nabla f_1(x_n)+r_n$ and the closed graph of $\partial f_2$ identifies the limit).

    The duality recipe (page 6)

    1. Dualize. $g(Lx)=\sup_v\ps{v}{Lx}-g^*(v)$, or introduce $y=Lx$ and the Lagrangian $\Lag(x,y,v)=f(x)+g(y)+\ps{v}{Lx-y}$ T17 T01
    2. Primal = inf sup, dual = sup inf. The dual problem is $\min_vf^*(-L^*v)+g^*(v)$; weak duality is Fenchel–Young twice T10 T06
    3. Optimality as a pair. Saddle point ⇔ Kuhn–Tucker point $-L^*\hat v\in\partial f(\hat x)$, $L\hat x\in\partial g^*(\hat v)$ ⇔ primal and dual solutions (qualification condition for the converse, strong duality) T08 T10 T15
    4. Run a known algorithm on the dual or on the pair. DR on the dual = ADMM; FB in the $V$-metric on $(x,v)$ = primal-dual; FB on a smooth dual when $f$ is strongly convex T18
    5. Recover the primal. $\hat x=\prox_\varphi(z-L^*\hat v)$ when $f=\varphi+\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-z}^2$ (prox form of KT), $x_n=(L^*L)^{-1}L^*s_n$ in ADMM, or directly $x_n$ in PD

    The existence recipe (pages 1, 3, labs)

    1. Put every constraint in the function: $F=f+\ind_C$ T01
    2. Check $F\in\Gz{\H}$: convex pieces, lsc pieces, closed convex $C$, and $\dom f\cap C\ne\emptyset$ (proper)
    3. Get a bounded minimizing sequence: $C$ bounded, or $F$ coercive (a coercive term, or a quadratic plus the Fenchel–Young affine minorant) T03 T10
    4. Conclude by the direct method (compact ball in $\R^N$; weak compactness and weak lsc for $\Gz{}$ functions in a Hilbert space) T02
    5. Uniqueness: strict convexity of $F$ (a strictly convex data term, the entropy, a quadratic); $\norm\cdot^2$ is strictly convex by the λ-identity T12
    e

    The compute-a-prox recipe (page 3, labs)

    1. Write the inclusion $x-p\in\gamma\partial f(p)$ and solve it on each piece of $\partial f$ (kinks give flat zones) T08
    2. Separable $f$ or orthonormal basis: work coordinate by coordinate T07 T16
    3. A conjugate or a support function: Moreau, $\prox_{\gamma f^*}x=x-\gamma\prox_{\gamma^{-1}f}(\gamma^{-1}x)$; $\prox_{\sigma_C}=\Id-P_C$ T17
    4. Translations, scalings, reflections, quadratic perturbations: the calculus table (complete the square) T09
    5. Composition with $L$: if $LL^*=\mu\Id$, $\prox_{f\circ L}=\Id-\mu^{-1}L^*(\Id-\prox_{\mu f})L$; otherwise do not compute it, go primal-dual
    6. Check: the output satisfies the inclusion, and agrees with a brute-force minimization on a few numbers (the prox calculator below does this check live)

    What to retain: the ten moves at your fingertips

    • Solve, don't minimize (T08): $p=\prox_{\gamma f}x\iff\frac{x-p}\gamma\in\partial f(p)$; constraints are functions (T01): $\prox_{\gamma\ind_C}=P_C$, $\partial\ind_C=N_C$.
    • The dictionary (T15): $\zer(A+B)=\Fix\big((\Id+\gamma B)^{-1}(\Id-\gamma A)\big)$, the step cancels; DR: solutions $=\prox_{\gamma g}(\Fix\rprox_{\gamma f}\rprox_{\gamma g})$ (T11).
    • The λ-identity (T12): $\norm{(1-\lambda)a+\lambda b}^2=(1-\lambda)\norm a^2+\lambda\norm b^2-\lambda(1-\lambda)\norm{a-b}^2$.
    • Telescope (T13): decrease $\ge c\cdot$residual and bounded below ⇒ residual $\to0$ (or $\liminf=0$ + monotone).
    • Fejér + demiclosed (T14): bounded, every cluster point is a fixed point, only one cluster point ⇒ weak convergence.
    • Count (T22): prox $\tfrac12$, gradient $\frac{\gamma\nu}2$, $\frac{\gamma_{12}}{1-\gamma_{12}}=\sum\frac{\alpha_i}{1-\alpha_i}$, relax up to $1/\gamma_{12}$; FB $\delta=2-\gamma\nu/2$, DR $2$, PD $2-\frac\beta2(\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm L^2)^{-1}$.
    • Twice and add (T06) with Fenchel–Young (T10): monotonicity, weak duality, Baillon–Haddad; $f(x)+f^*(u)\ge\ps xu$ with equality iff $u\in\partial f(x)$.
    • Lift and dualize (T16, T17): duplicate ⇒ the adjoint sums and the projection averages; $g(Lx)=\sup_v\ps{v}{Lx}-g^*(v)$, $\prox_{\sigma g^*}=\Id-\sigma\prox_{g/\sigma}(\cdot/\sigma)$.
    • Recognize (T18): DR on the dual is ADMM; PD is FB in the metric $V$; VMFB is MM; an averaged iteration is KM with $\mu_n=\alpha\lambda_n$.
    • Majorize (T19–T21): $f(x_{n+1})\le h(x_{n+1},x_n)\le f(x_n)$; quadratic + $\theta_n\lt2$ ⇒ sufficient descent; concave ⇒ tangent majorant; Jensen ⇒ diagonal curvature, parallel steps.

    Self-check

    Which move proves it?

    Which move proves that $\partial f$ is monotone, and what cancels?

    T06: write the subgradient inequality at $(x_1,u_1)$ against $x_2$ and at $(x_2,u_2)$ against $x_1$, add; the values $f(x_1)+f(x_2)$ cancel and $\ps{u_1-u_2}{x_1-x_2}\ge0$ remains.

    In the Krasnosel'skii–Mann proof, where does the strict decrease come from, and how big is it?

    T12, the λ-identity with $a=x_n-x$, $b=Tx_n-x$: $\norm{x_{n+1}-x}^2\le\norm{x_n-x}^2-\lambda_n(1-\lambda_n)\norm{Tx_n-x_n}^2$. Then T13 (telescoping) and T14 (demiclosedness + Fejér).

    How do you compute $\prox_{\gamma\abs\cdot}$ without minimizing anything?

    T08: solve $x-p\in\gamma\,\partial\abs\cdot(p)$ case by case: $p=x-\gamma$ if $x\gt\gamma$, $p=x+\gamma$ if $x\lt-\gamma$, $p=0$ if $\abs x\le\gamma$ (because $\partial\abs\cdot(0)=[-1,1]$). Soft-thresholding.

    Why may Forward–Backward be relaxed up to $2-\gamma\nu/2$, and Douglas–Rachford up to $2$?

    T22. FB composes a $\tfrac12$-averaged prox with the $\frac{\gamma\nu}2$-averaged gradient step: $\alpha=\frac2{4-\gamma\nu}$, range $1/\alpha=2-\gamma\nu/2$. DR iterates $\tfrac12(\Id+T)$ with $T$ nonexpansive: $\alpha=\tfrac12$, range $2$.

    Which two moves make $\prox_{\gamma f}x$ exist for every $f\in\Gz{\H}$?

    T10 then T03: Fenchel–Young gives an affine minorant $f\ge\ps u\cdot-f^*(u)$, so $f+\frac1{2\gamma}\norm{\cdot-x}^2$ is coercive; it is also strictly convex and lsc, so it has exactly one minimizer (T02 for existence in finite dimension, weak compactness in general).

    Which move produces the dual problem $\min_vf^*(-L^*v)+g^*(v)$, and which one proves weak duality?

    T17 (write $g(Lx)=\sup_v\ps v{Lx}-g^*(v)$ and swap $\inf$ and $\sup$) for the dual; T10 + T06 (Fenchel–Young for $f$ and for $g$, added: the inner products cancel) for $\mu\ge-\mu^*$.

    How is the convergence of ADMM proved without a new Fejér argument?

    T18: ADMM is the unrelaxed Douglas–Rachford algorithm applied to the dual problem, with $y_n=(u_n-v_n)/\gamma$ and $z_n=v_n/\gamma$; DR converges (T14), and $L^*L$ invertible plus the closed graph of $\partial f$ recover the primal limit.

    Which move turns "minimize $\sum_{i=1}^mg_i$" into an algorithm with $m$ proxes in parallel?

    T16: lift to $\H^m$, minimize $\sum_ig_i(x_i)+\ind_D(x)$ with $D$ the diagonal; the prox of the separable part is $m$ independent proxes, $P_D$ is the average (the adjoint of "duplicate" is "sum"); run DR: consensus PPXA.

    In the half-quadratic theorem, which identity turns the tangent of $\psi(\sqrt\cdot)$ into a parabola in $x$?

    T20: $x^2-y^2=(x-y)^2+2y(x-y)$. The tangent $\varphi(x^2)\le\varphi(y^2)+\tfrac12\omega(\abs y)(x^2-y^2)$ becomes $f(y)+\dot f(y)(x-y)+\tfrac12\omega(\abs y)(x-y)^2$.

    Which move makes the MM step of lab 4 parallel over the pixels, and what does it cost?

    T21: Jensen's inequality bounds the curvature by a diagonal, $H^\top H\preccurlyeq\operatorname{Diag}(\abs H^\top\abs H\mathbf1)$; the surrogate is separable and the step is a coordinatewise division. The price is a looser majorant, hence shorter steps.
