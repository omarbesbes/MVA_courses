Fixed points & Forward–Backward

The algorithmic half of the course's one principle: a minimizer is a fixed point of a prox-type map $T$, so iterate $T$. Picard's theorem needs a strict contraction and $T$ is only nonexpansive; this page builds the replacement, Fejér monotonicity + demiclosedness + relaxation, and harvests Forward–Backward, projected gradient, gradient descent and the proximal point algorithm.

Overview

Where we are

Pages 1–3 gave the static half of the principle: $\hat x$ minimizes $f+g$ iff $0\in\partial f(\hat x)+\nabla g(\hat x)$, and $\prox_{\gamma f}=(\Id+\gamma\partial f)^{-1}$ turns such inclusions into equations. This page gives the dynamic half: rewrite the inclusion as $\hat x=T\hat x$ and iterate $T$.

The dictionary is one line long (T15 zeros ⇔ fixed points): $$0\in\partial f(\hat x)+\nabla g(\hat x)\iff \hat x-\gamma\nabla g(\hat x)\in(\Id+\gamma\partial f)(\hat x)\iff \hat x=\underbrace{\prox_{\gamma f}\big(\hat x-\gamma\nabla g(\hat x)\big)}_{T\hat x}.$$ So minimizing $f+g$ means finding a fixed point of $T=\prox_{\gamma f}\circ(\Id-\gamma\nabla g)$, and the natural algorithm is $x_{n+1}=Tx_n$. The only theorem you know about such iterations is Picard's: it needs $T$ to be a strict contraction. Neither factor of $T$ is one: $\prox_{\gamma f}$ and $\Id-\gamma\nabla g$ are merely nonexpansive (they do not increase distances, but may keep them). A rotation of the plane is nonexpansive too, and its Picard iteration circles forever. Something has to replace contraction.

The replacement has three layers, and the whole page is the story of fitting them together:

  1. Fejér monotonicity replaces "the error shrinks by a factor $\rho$" with "the distance to every solution never increases". It costs nothing (every nonexpansive iteration has it) and already gives boundedness and convergence of the distances. Lemma 1 says that this plus "all weak cluster points are solutions" forces weak convergence.
  2. Demiclosedness produces cluster points that are fixed points, provided the residual $Tx_n-x_n$ goes to $0$.
  3. Relaxation $x_{n+1}=x_n+\lambda_n(Tx_n-x_n)$ with $\lambda_n\in\,]0,1[$ makes the residual go to $0$: the T12 λ-identity turns "non-increasing" into "decreasing by $\lambda_n(1-\lambda_n)\norm{Tx_n-x_n}^2$", and a telescoping sum does the rest. This is the Krasnosel'skii–Mann theorem, the central result of the page.

Then comes the second idea: some operators carry their relaxation inside. An $\alpha$-averaged operator is $(1-\alpha)\Id+\alpha R$ with $R$ nonexpansive, so iterating it is a KM iteration on $R$. Gradient steps $\Id-\gamma\nabla g$ (Baillon–Haddad) and $\prox$ (firmly nonexpansive) are averaged, averagedness survives composition with an explicit constant (T22), and the Forward–Backward theorem falls out with its exact constants $\gamma\lt 2/\nu$, $\delta=2-\gamma\nu/2$.

The one sentence of the page

Nonexpansive is not enough to converge, but nonexpansive + relaxation is: every relaxed iterate is at least as close to every fixed point as the previous one (Fejér), the residual $Tx_n-x_n$ is summable (λ-identity), and a weak limit with vanishing residual is a fixed point (demiclosedness). Averaged operators are those whose relaxation is already built in, and Forward–Backward is the averaged operator $\prox_{\gamma f}\circ(\Id-\gamma\nabla g)$.

A first answer: Picard's theorem, and why it is not enough

The oldest fixed point theorem gives everything one could wish for: existence, uniqueness, convergence, and a linear rate. Its price is a strict contraction, and that is exactly what optimization operators do not provide.

Theorem

Picard's fixed point theorem (strict contractions)

Part V · slides 2–3Slides#
Statement

Let $T:\H\to\H$ be such that

  • $\hat x\in\H$ is a fixed point of $T$, i.e. $\hat x=T\hat x$;
  • $T$ is a strict contraction: there exists $\rho\in[0,1[$ such that $\big(\forall (x,x')\in\H^2\big)\ \norm{Tx-Tx'}\le\rho\norm{x-x'}$.

Then the sequence defined by $x_0\in\H$ and $(\forall n\in\N)\ x_{n+1}=Tx_n$ converges to $\hat x$, and $\norm{x_n-\hat x}\le\rho^n\norm{x_0-\hat x}$ (linear convergence).

In one sentence

Each application of $T$ multiplies the error by at most $\rho\lt1$, so the error dies geometrically.

Why it is true

The fixed point is invisible to the iteration ($T\hat x=\hat x$), so $x_{n+1}-\hat x=Tx_n-T\hat x$ is the image of the error $x_n-\hat x$ by "one step of $T$ between two points", and a strict contraction shrinks the distance between any two points by the factor $\rho$. Nothing else is used: no convexity, no finite dimension, no structure of $T$. The sentence to say out loud: the error is a difference of two images, and $T$ contracts differences.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $\rho\lt1$ is the whole theorem: with $\rho=1$ (nonexpansive) the error is only non-increasing, and a rotation shows that it may never decrease at all (widget below).
  • A fixed point exists is assumed on the slides. It is in fact automatic (Banach): the iterates form a Cauchy sequence because $\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}\le\rho^n\norm{x_1-x_0}$ is summable, and $\H$ is complete; the limit is a fixed point by continuity of $T$. The fixed point is also unique: two fixed points $\hat x,\hat x'$ satisfy $\norm{\hat x-\hat x'}\le\rho\norm{\hat x-\hat x'}$. Extra
Hint — try it first

Write $x_{n+1}-\hat x$ as $Tx_n-T\hat x$ and apply the contraction inequality; then induct.

Proof (as on the slides)

1. One step. For every $n\in\N$, since $\hat x=T\hat x$, $$\norm{x_{n+1}-\hat x}=\norm{Tx_n-T\hat x}\le\rho\norm{x_n-\hat x}.$$

2. Induction. Applying step 1 $n$ times gives $\norm{x_n-\hat x}\le\rho^n\norm{x_0-\hat x}$. Since $\rho\in[0,1[$, $\rho^n\to0$, hence $x_n\to\hat x$, and the rate is linear (the error is bounded by a geometric sequence; see convergence rates).

□
Pitfall: what optimization gives you is $\rho=1$

$\prox_{\gamma f}$, projections $P_C$, gradient steps $\Id-\gamma\nabla g$ with $\gamma\le 2/\nu$: all are nonexpansive and none is a strict contraction in general. Soft-thresholding ($\prox$ of $\gamma\abs{\cdot}$) has slope exactly $1$ outside $[-\gamma,\gamma]$; $P_C$ is the identity on $C$; $\Id-\gamma\nabla g$ is the identity along any flat direction of $g$. The constant $\rho$ you would need equals $1$, and Picard's argument gives nothing. The rest of the page is the theory that replaces it.

Programme of Part V

Extend Picard's theorem to operators that are

  • not necessarily strictly contractive (only nonexpansive, or "averaged");
  • possibly dependent on the iteration number $n$ (varying step sizes $\gamma_n$, relaxation parameters $\lambda_n$);
  • built from compositions of simpler operators — the splitting idea: $T=\prox_{\gamma f}\circ(\Id-\gamma\nabla g)$ handles $f$ and $g$ separately, each with the tool that suits it;

and then apply this to minimization: how do we relate $T$ to the objective $f$? Answer: through Fermat's rule and the resolvent, $\Argmin f=\zer\partial f=\Fix\prox_{\gamma f}$ (T15).

In one sentence

Replace "contraction" by a property that is stable under composition and cheap to verify, and that still forces convergence of the relaxed iterates: that property will be averagedness.

Why "splitting" is the whole game

You can rarely compute $\prox_{\gamma(f+g)}$, but you can often compute $\prox_{\gamma f}$ and $\nabla g$ separately. An algorithm that only ever calls these two black boxes and still minimizes $f+g$ is a splitting algorithm. Forward–Backward (this page) splits a smooth $g$ from a proximable $f$; Douglas–Rachford (page 5) splits two proximable functions; the primal-dual algorithms (page 6) also split a linear operator $L$ off. The convergence theory is the same for all of them, which is why this page matters so much.

To remember

Three wishes: no contraction, $n$-dependent parameters, compositions. One tool answers all three: Fejér monotonicity with respect to $\Fix T$.

Convergence tools: weak limits and Fejér monotonicity

In infinite dimension the iterates will only converge weakly, so we need a criterion for weak convergence that does not mention the limit. Lemma 1 is that criterion; Fejér monotonicity is the cheap way to satisfy its first half.

Definition

Strong and weak convergence (recall)

Part V · slide 6Slides#
Definition

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space, $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ a sequence in $\H$ and $\hat x\in\H$.

  • $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ converges strongly to $\hat x$ if $\lim_{n\to+\infty}\norm{x_n-\hat x}=0$. Notation: $x_n\to\hat x$.
  • $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ converges weakly to $\hat x$ if $(\forall y\in\H)\ \lim_{n\to+\infty}\ps{y}{x_n-\hat x}=0$. Notation: $x_n\wto\hat x$.

Remark. In a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, strong and weak convergence are equivalent.

In one sentence

Weak convergence is convergence of every coordinate $\ps{y}{x_n}$, strong convergence is convergence of the length of the error; in $\R^d$ finitely many coordinates control the length, in $\ell^2$ they do not.

Example

In $\ell^2(\N)$ the basis vectors $e_n$ satisfy $\ps{y}{e_n}=y_n\to0$ for every $y$ (so $e_n\wto0$) while $\norm{e_n-0}=1$ (no strong convergence). Strong always implies weak by Cauchy–Schwarz: $\abs{\ps{y}{x_n-\hat x}}\le\norm{y}\norm{x_n-\hat x}$. See page 1 for the full discussion.

To remember

$\to$: norm of the error $\to0$. $\wto$: every inner product $\to$. Finite dimension: same thing. All convergence theorems of this page are weak; they become strong in $\R^d$, which is where you compute.

Proposition

Weak convergence ⇔ bounded with at most one weak sequential cluster point

Part V · slides 6–7SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ be a sequence in $\H$. Then $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ converges weakly if and only if

  • $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ is bounded, and
  • $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ possesses at most one sequential cluster point in the weak topology.

($\hat x$ is a sequential cluster point in the weak topology — a weak cluster point for short — if some subsequence $(x_{n_k})_{k\in\N}$ converges weakly to $\hat x$.)

Illustration (slide 7). $x_n=(-1)^n$ in $\R$: bounded, but two cluster points $-1$ and $1$, hence no convergence.

In one sentence

Boundedness guarantees that cluster points exist (weak compactness of balls); uniqueness of the cluster point then leaves the sequence no choice but to converge to it.

Why it is true

The direct implication is a sanity check (a weakly convergent sequence is bounded and its limit is the only possible cluster point). The useful direction is the converse, and the mechanism is the sub-subsequence argument: a sequence that does not converge to $\hat x$ has a subsequence staying away from $\hat x$ in some coordinate $\ps{y}{\cdot}$; this subsequence is bounded, so it has a weakly convergent sub-subsequence (bounded sets of a Hilbert space are weakly sequentially compact, page 1); its limit is a cluster point of the whole sequence, hence equals $\hat x$, which contradicts "staying away". The sentence to say out loud: bounded gives cluster points, uniqueness of the cluster point gives the limit.

What each hypothesis buys
  • Bounded: without it there may be no cluster point at all ($x_n=n\,e_0$ has none, yet "at most one" holds vacuously).
  • At most one cluster point: the $(-1)^n$ example has two; Fejér-monotone sequences that are not convergent are exactly those with several cluster points (widget below).
Proof (the slides state this without proof; here is a short one)

1. (⇒) Suppose $x_n\wto\hat x$. Every subsequence converges weakly to $\hat x$, so $\hat x$ is the only weak cluster point. Boundedness is the uniform boundedness principle (Banach–Steinhaus) applied to the functionals $\ps{x_n}{\cdot}$, which are pointwise bounded since $\ps{y}{x_n}$ converges for every $y$. Extra

2. (⇐) Set-up. Suppose $(x_n)$ bounded with at most one weak cluster point. Being bounded, $(x_n)$ has at least one weakly convergent subsequence (weak sequential compactness of bounded sets), so it has exactly one weak cluster point, call it $\hat x$.

3. Sub-subsequence argument. If $x_n\not\wto\hat x$, there exist $y\in\H$, $\eps\gt0$ and a subsequence $(x_{n_k})$ with $\abs{\ps{y}{x_{n_k}-\hat x}}\ge\eps$ for all $k$. This subsequence is bounded, so it has a further subsequence converging weakly to some $\tilde x$; $\tilde x$ is a weak cluster point of $(x_n)$, hence $\tilde x=\hat x$ by uniqueness. But along that sub-subsequence $\ps{y}{x_{n_{k_j}}-\hat x}\to\ps{y}{\tilde x-\hat x}=0$, contradicting $\abs{\ps{y}{x_{n_{k_j}}-\hat x}}\ge\eps$. Hence $x_n\wto\hat x$.

To prove weak convergence of an algorithm you never exhibit the limit: you prove bounded and all cluster points coincide (or, with Lemma 1, all cluster points lie in a set $D$ and the distances to $D$ converge). This is the pattern T14 Fejér + demiclosedness.

Lemma 1

Converging distances + cluster points in $D$ ⇒ weak convergence to a point of $D$ (Opial's lemma)

Part V · slides 7–8SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Let $D$ be a nonempty subset of $\H$ and $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ a sequence in $\H$. Then $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ converges weakly to a point in $D$ if

  • for every $x\in D$, $\big(\norm{x_n-x}\big)_{n\in\N}$ converges, and
  • every weak sequential cluster point of $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ lies in $D$.

Extra The slides call it Lemma 1; in the literature it is Opial's lemma (1967).

In one sentence

If the sequence had two different cluster points $\hat x,\hat x'$ in $D$, the converging distances to $\hat x$ and $\hat x'$ would force $\ps{x_n}{\hat x'-\hat x}$ to converge, and its limit along the two subsequences would differ by $\norm{\hat x'-\hat x}^2$.

Why it is true

The first hypothesis says the sequence cannot wander: its distance to any point of $D$ settles. The second says every place it may accumulate is in $D$. Suppose it accumulates at two points $\hat x\ne\hat x'$ of $D$. The quantity $\ps{x_n}{\hat x'-\hat x}$ — the coordinate of $x_n$ along the direction joining the two candidates — is a combination of $\norm{x_n-\hat x}^2$ and $\norm{x_n-\hat x'}^2$ (polarization), so it converges to some $\alpha$. Along the subsequence going to $\hat x$ it tends to $\ps{\hat x}{\hat x'-\hat x}$, along the other to $\ps{\hat x'}{\hat x'-\hat x}$; both equal $\alpha$, so their difference $\norm{\hat x'-\hat x}^2$ is $0$. The sentence to say out loud: two cluster points would have to project to the same place on the line joining them, which is absurd.

What each hypothesis buys
  • Distances converge: gives boundedness (needed to have cluster points at all) and the convergence of $\ps{x_n}{\hat x'-\hat x}$, which is the heart of the uniqueness argument. Fejér monotonicity provides it for free.
  • Cluster points in $D$: without it the two candidate limits need not be in $D$ and the first hypothesis says nothing about them. The $(-1)^n$ example with $D=\{0\}$: distances to $0$ converge (constant), cluster points $\pm1\notin D$, no convergence.
  • $D\neq\emptyset$: otherwise there is no $x$ to measure distances from (and no conclusion to reach).
Hint — try it first

Use the criterion: show boundedness, then that two weak cluster points $\hat x,\hat x'\in D$ must coincide. For the latter, expand $\norm{x_n-\hat x}^2-\norm{x_n-\hat x'}^2$.

Proof T09 expand the squaresT14 cluster points

1. Boundedness. Pick any $x\in D$ (nonempty). Since $\big(\norm{x_n-x}\big)_n$ converges it is bounded, say by $M$, hence $\norm{x_n}\le\norm{x_n-x}+\norm{x}\le M+\norm{x}$: $(x_n)_n$ is bounded.

2. Two cluster points. Let $(x_{n_k})_k$ and $(x_{n_\ell})_\ell$ be subsequences with $x_{n_k}\wto\hat x$ and $x_{n_\ell}\wto\hat x'$. By the second hypothesis $(\hat x,\hat x')\in D^2$. For every $n\in\N$, expanding $\norm{x_n-\hat x}^2=\norm{x_n}^2-2\ps{x_n}{\hat x}+\norm{\hat x}^2$ and the same with $\hat x'$, and subtracting: $$2\ps{x_n}{\hat x'-\hat x}=\norm{x_n-\hat x}^2-\norm{x_n-\hat x'}^2-\norm{\hat x}^2+\norm{\hat x'}^2.$$

3. The coordinate along $\hat x'-\hat x$ converges. Because $\hat x,\hat x'\in D$, both $\big(\norm{x_n-\hat x}\big)_n$ and $\big(\norm{x_n-\hat x'}\big)_n$ converge, so the right-hand side converges: there exists $\alpha\in\R$ with $\ps{x_n}{\hat x'-\hat x}\to\alpha$.

4. Evaluate $\alpha$ twice. A convergent sequence has the same limit along every subsequence. Along $(n_k)$, weak convergence gives $\ps{x_{n_k}}{\hat x'-\hat x}\to\ps{\hat x}{\hat x'-\hat x}$, so $\ps{\hat x}{\hat x'-\hat x}=\alpha$. Along $(n_\ell)$, similarly $\ps{\hat x'}{\hat x'-\hat x}=\alpha$.

5. Conclude. Subtracting, $\ps{\hat x'-\hat x}{\hat x'-\hat x}=\norm{\hat x'-\hat x}^2=0$, i.e. $\hat x=\hat x'$. Thus $(x_n)_n$ is bounded with at most one weak cluster point: by the criterion it converges weakly, and its limit (a cluster point) lies in $D$.

□
Trick: polarization

An inner product $\ps{x_n}{u}$ is a difference of two squared distances. Whenever you control $\norm{x_n-x}$ for several $x$, you control the coordinates of $x_n$ along the differences of those $x$. The identity $2\ps{x_n}{\hat x'-\hat x}=\norm{x_n-\hat x}^2-\norm{x_n-\hat x'}^2-\norm{\hat x}^2+\norm{\hat x'}^2$ is worth knowing by heart.

Definition + properties

Fejér-monotone sequences and their two properties

Part V · slide 9SlidesKey#
Definition

Let $D$ be a nonempty subset of a Hilbert space $\H$ and $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ a sequence in $\H$. $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ is Fejér-monotone with respect to $D$ if $$(\forall x\in D)(\forall n\in\N)\qquad\norm{x_{n+1}-x}\le\norm{x_n-x}.$$

Properties. If $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ is Fejér-monotone with respect to $D$ then

  • for every $x\in D$, $\big(\norm{x_n-x}\big)_{n\in\N}$ converges;
  • $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ is bounded.
In one sentence

Each step brings you no farther from any point of $D$ — $D$ will be the solution set — so the iterates stay in the ball $B(x,\norm{x_0-x})$ around every solution $x$, and in the intersection of all these balls.

Why the properties hold

For a fixed $x\in D$ the real sequence $\norm{x_n-x}$ is non-increasing and bounded below by $0$, hence convergent (to $\inf_n\norm{x_n-x}$, not necessarily to $0$). Boundedness: $\norm{x_n}\le\norm{x_n-x}+\norm{x}\le\norm{x_0-x}+\norm{x}$ for any single $x\in D$. The sentence to say out loud: Fejér monotone = trapped in shrinking balls around every solution.

Pitfall: Fejér-monotone does not mean convergent

Rotations: $x_{n+1}=R_\theta x_n$ in $\R^2$ is Fejér-monotone with respect to $D=\{0\}$ ($\norm{x_n}$ constant) and never converges. Nor does Fejér monotonicity give $\norm{x_n-x}\to0$: the limit of the distances is in general positive, and different for different $x\in D$. What it gives is the first hypothesis of Lemma 1; the second (cluster points in $D$) must come from somewhere else, and that is the job of demiclosedness.

Theorem

Fejér-monotone convergence: Fejér w.r.t. $D$ + weak cluster points in $D$ ⇒ $x_n\wto$ a point of $D$

Part V · slides 9–10SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Let $D$ be a nonempty subset of a Hilbert space $\H$ and $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ a sequence in $\H$. Then $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ converges weakly to a point in $D$ if

  • $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ is Fejér-monotone with respect to $D$, and
  • every weak sequential cluster point of $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ lies in $D$.
In one sentence

Fejér monotonicity supplies the first hypothesis of Lemma 1 (distances converge); the second hypothesis is the same; so this is Lemma 1 with a cheaper first bullet.

Why it is true

There is no new mechanism: a non-increasing sequence of distances converges, which is all Lemma 1 asked for. The value of restating it is methodological. The two hypotheses are exactly the two things an algorithm proof must deliver: (a) a Fejér inequality, usually obtained in one line from nonexpansiveness; (b) "a weak cluster point is a solution", usually obtained from demiclosedness once the residual $Tx_n-x_n$ tends to $0$. This split is the recipe T14 used by every convergence proof of pages 4, 5 and 6.

What each hypothesis buys
  • Fejér w.r.t. $D$: boundedness (so cluster points exist) and convergence of $\norm{x_n-x}$ for $x\in D$ (so cluster points are unique, by polarization).
  • Cluster points in $D$: the only hypothesis that involves the structure of the algorithm; it is where $\Fix T$ enters.
Proof T14 Fejér + cluster points

1. By the properties of Fejér-monotone sequences, for every $x\in D$ the sequence $\big(\norm{x_n-x}\big)_n$ converges.

2. Every weak sequential cluster point lies in $D$ by hypothesis. Lemma 1 applies: $(x_n)_n$ converges weakly to a point of $D$.

Lemma 2

Closed convex sets are weakly sequentially closed

Part V · slide 10Slides#
Statement

Let $C$ be a nonempty closed convex subset of $\H$. If $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ is a sequence in $C$ that converges weakly to $\hat x$, then $\hat x\in C$.

In one sentence

A closed convex set is an intersection of closed half-spaces, and a half-space $\{x:\ps{u}{x}\le c\}$ is weakly closed because $\ps{u}{x_n}\to\ps{u}{\hat x}$.

Why it is true

Weak convergence only controls linear functionals, so the sets that are preserved in the weak limit are those defined by linear inequalities: half-spaces. Convexity + closedness is exactly what makes a set an intersection of half-spaces (Hahn–Banach separation, or in a Hilbert space the projection theorem: $C=\{x:\ps{y-P_Cy}{x-P_Cy}\le0\ \forall y\}$). Without convexity the lemma fails: the unit sphere of $\ell^2$ is closed, contains $e_n$, and $e_n\wto0$, which is not on the sphere. The sentence to say out loud: weak limits respect linear inequalities, and closed convex sets are made of linear inequalities.

Proof (not on the slides; one paragraph using the projection theorem) Extra

1. Separate $\hat x$ from $C$ with the projection. Let $p=P_C\hat x$ (exists since $C$ is nonempty closed convex) and $u=\hat x-p$. The characterization of the projection (page 3, or the variational inequality) gives $(\forall y\in C)\ \ps{u}{y-p}\le0$.

Definition

Part V · slide 11Slides#
Definition

  • For $T:C\to\H$, the set of fixed points of $T$ is $\Fix T=\{x\in C\mid x=Tx\}$.
  • For a set-valued $S:C\to2^{\H}$, the set of zeros of $S$ is $\zer S=\{x\in C\mid 0\in Sx\}$.
In one sentence

  • $\Fix R_\theta=\{0\}$ for a rotation of angle $\theta\in\,]0,2\pi[$ of $\R^2$.

Definition

Nonexpansive operator

Part V · slide 11SlidesKey#
Definition

Let $C\subset\H$ be a nonempty set and $T:C\to\H$. $T$ is nonexpansive if $$\big(\forall(x,y)\in C^2\big)\qquad\norm{Tx-Ty}\le\norm{x-y}.$$

In one sentence

$1$-Lipschitz: distances never grow; a strict contraction is the case $\rho\lt1$, and all our operators sit exactly at $\rho=1$.

Examples and non-examples
  • Nonexpansive: $\Id$, $-\Id$, every isometry (rotations, reflections, unitary maps), $P_C$, $\prox_{\gamma f}$ (below), $\Id-\gamma\nabla g$ for $\gamma\in\,]0,2/\nu]$, $2\prox_{\gamma f}-\Id$ (page 5), convex combinations and compositions of nonexpansive operators.
  • Not nonexpansive: $x\mapsto2x$; $\Id-\gamma\nabla g$ for $\gamma\gt2/\nu$ (take $g=\tfrac\nu2\norm{\cdot}^2$: $\Id-\gamma\nabla g=(1-\gamma\nu)\Id$ has Lipschitz constant $\abs{1-\gamma\nu}\gt1$).

Nonexpansive operators are continuous, which is used silently in several proofs (e.g. "$\prox$ is continuous since nonexpansive" on page 5).

To remember

Nonexpansive = 1-Lipschitz. Stable under composition and convex combination; $\Fix$ of a nonexpansive operator on a closed convex set is closed and convex (not needed here, but true).

Theorem

Demiclosedness principle

Part V · slides 11–12SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Let $C$ be a nonempty closed convex subset of a Hilbert space $\H$ and $T:C\to\H$ a nonexpansive operator. If $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ is a sequence in $C$ that converges weakly to $\hat x$ and if $Tx_n-x_n\to0$, then $\hat x\in\Fix T$.

In one sentence

"Weak limit" + "residual tends to $0$ strongly" ⇒ "the limit is a fixed point": the operator $\Id-T$ is closed for the mixed (weak in, strong out) convergence.

Why it is true

You would like to write $T\hat x=\lim Tx_n=\lim x_n=\hat x$, but $T$ is not weakly continuous (a rotation of $\ell^2$ shows weak limits can be moved by nonexpansive maps). The way around is to never let $T$ act on a weak limit: measure $\norm{x_n-T\hat x}^2$ in two ways, once through $\hat x$ and once through $Tx_n$. Comparing the expansions, the term $\norm{Tx_n-T\hat x}^2$ is killed by nonexpansiveness against $\norm{x_n-\hat x}^2$, the cross terms contain either the residual $x_n-Tx_n\to0$ (strongly, so it beats the bounded factor next to it) or $\ps{x_n-\hat x}{\hat x-T\hat x}\to0$ (weak convergence against a fixed vector). What survives is $\norm{\hat x-T\hat x}^2\le0$. The sentence to say out loud: expand the same distance twice; nonexpansiveness eats the only term that could hurt.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $C$ closed convex: by Lemma 2, $\hat x\in C$, so $T\hat x$ is defined at all.
  • $T$ nonexpansive: replaces $\norm{Tx_n-T\hat x}$ by $\norm{x_n-\hat x}$, which is bounded and cancels. A merely continuous $T$ does not work (in infinite dimension, weak limits are not respected by nonlinear continuous maps).
  • $Tx_n-x_n\to0$ strongly: it multiplies $\norm{Tx_n-T\hat x}$, which is only bounded, so it must tend to $0$ in norm. Weak convergence of the residual would not suffice.
  • $x_n\wto\hat x$: enough, because the other factor $\hat x-T\hat x$ is a fixed vector.

Proof T09 expand twice (as on the slides)

0. $T\hat x$ makes sense. $x_n\in C$, $x_n\wto\hat x$ and $C$ is closed convex, so $\hat x\in C$ by Lemma 2; thus $T\hat x$ is defined.

1. One distance, two expansions. For every $n\in\N$, inserting $\hat x$ then inserting $Tx_n$: $$\begin{aligned}\norm{x_n-T\hat x}^2&=\norm{x_n-\hat x}^2+\norm{\hat x-T\hat x}^2+2\ps{x_n-\hat x}{\hat x-T\hat x},\\ \norm{x_n-T\hat x}^2&=\norm{x_n-Tx_n}^2+\norm{Tx_n-T\hat x}^2+2\ps{x_n-Tx_n}{Tx_n-T\hat x}.\end{aligned}$$ Subtracting and isolating the quantity we care about: $$\norm{\hat x-T\hat x}^2=\norm{x_n-Tx_n}^2+\norm{Tx_n-T\hat x}^2-\norm{x_n-\hat x}^2+2\ps{x_n-Tx_n}{Tx_n-T\hat x}-2\ps{x_n-\hat x}{\hat x-T\hat x}.$$

2. Nonexpansiveness and Cauchy–Schwarz. Since $T$ is nonexpansive, $\norm{Tx_n-T\hat x}^2-\norm{x_n-\hat x}^2\le0$, and by Cauchy–Schwarz then nonexpansiveness again, $$2\ps{x_n-Tx_n}{Tx_n-T\hat x}\le2\norm{x_n-Tx_n}\,\norm{Tx_n-T\hat x}\le2\norm{x_n-Tx_n}\,\norm{x_n-\hat x}.$$ Hence $$\norm{\hat x-T\hat x}^2\le\norm{x_n-Tx_n}^2+2\norm{x_n-Tx_n}\,\norm{x_n-\hat x}-2\ps{x_n-\hat x}{\hat x-T\hat x}.$$

3. Pass to the limit. $x_n\wto\hat x$ implies that $(x_n)_n$ is bounded (criterion), so $\norm{x_n-\hat x}\le M$; $\norm{x_n-Tx_n}\to0$ by hypothesis, so the first two terms tend to $0$; and $\ps{x_n-\hat x}{\hat x-T\hat x}\to0$ by the definition of weak convergence applied to the fixed vector $y=\hat x-T\hat x$. The left-hand side does not depend on $n$: $\norm{\hat x-T\hat x}^2\le0$, i.e. $\hat x=T\hat x$.

Proposition

Picard iteration of a nonexpansive map converges weakly when the residual vanishes

Part V · slides 12–13Slides#
Statement

Let $C$ be a nonempty closed convex subset of a Hilbert space $\H$ and $T:C\to C$ a nonexpansive operator such that $\Fix T\neq\emptyset$. Let $x_0\in C$ and $(\forall n\in\N)\ x_{n+1}=Tx_n$. If $x_n-Tx_n\to0$, then $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ converges weakly to a point in $\Fix T$.

Exercise (slide 13): prove this result by showing that $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ is Fejér-monotone with respect to $\Fix T$. Solution in exercise 1 below.

In one sentence

Nonexpansiveness gives Fejér monotonicity w.r.t. $\Fix T$ in one line, demiclosedness puts every cluster point in $\Fix T$, and the Fejér convergence theorem concludes — provided someone hands you $x_n-Tx_n\to0$.

Why it is true

For $x\in\Fix T$: $\norm{x_{n+1}-x}=\norm{Tx_n-Tx}\le\norm{x_n-x}$. That is Picard's one-line computation with $\rho=1$: no decrease, but no increase either, for every fixed point simultaneously. The residual hypothesis is exactly what feeds demiclosedness. The proposition is honest about what nonexpansiveness alone cannot do: it cannot make the residual vanish (rotation), so the hypothesis $x_n-Tx_n\to0$ has to be assumed. KM below removes it by relaxing.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $T:C\to C$: the iterates stay in $C$, where $T$ is defined and nonexpansive.
  • $\Fix T\neq\emptyset$: Fejér monotonicity with respect to the empty set is vacuous; some fixed point is needed to pin the sequence down.
  • $x_n-Tx_n\to0$: the only ingredient of demiclosedness that nonexpansiveness does not supply. It fails for a rotation.

3. Conclusion. Fejér-monotone convergence with $D=\Fix T$: $x_n\wto$ a point of $\Fix T$.

Theorem

Krasnosel'skii–Mann algorithm

Part V · slides 14–15SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Let $C$ be a nonempty closed convex subset of a Hilbert space $\H$ and $T:C\to C$ a nonexpansive operator such that $\Fix T\neq\emptyset$. Let $(\lambda_n)_{n\in\N}$ be a sequence in $[0,1]$ such that $$\sum_{n\in\N}\lambda_n(1-\lambda_n)=+\infty.$$ Let $x_0\in C$ and $(\forall n\in\N)\ x_{n+1}=x_n+\lambda_n(Tx_n-x_n)$. Then

  • $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ is Fejér-monotone with respect to $\Fix T$;
  • $(Tx_n-x_n)_{n\in\N}$ converges strongly to $0$;
  • $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ converges weakly to a point in $\Fix T$.

Typical choice: $(\forall n\in\N)\ \lambda_n=\lambda\in\,]0,1[$.

In one sentence

Averaging $x_n$ with $Tx_n$ turns "no increase" into a strict decrease of $\lambda_n(1-\lambda_n)\norm{Tx_n-x_n}^2$ per step, the decreases are summable, so the residual must go to $0$ — and the rest is the previous proposition.

Why it is true

Picture $x_n$, $Tx_n$ and a fixed point $x$. Nonexpansiveness says $Tx_n$ is no farther from $x$ than $x_n$ is: both lie in the ball $B(x,\norm{x_n-x})$. A point strictly between them, $x_{n+1}=(1-\lambda_n)x_n+\lambda_nTx_n$, lies inside the ball, and by how much is quantified by the T12 λ-identity $\norm{(1-\lambda)a+\lambda b}^2=(1-\lambda)\norm a^2+\lambda\norm b^2-\lambda(1-\lambda)\norm{a-b}^2$: the gain is $\lambda_n(1-\lambda_n)\norm{Tx_n-x_n}^2$, the squared chord between the two points. The gains are summable (they are bounded by the initial squared distance, by telescoping, T13), and since the weights $\lambda_n(1-\lambda_n)$ have an infinite sum, the residual $\norm{Tx_n-x_n}$ cannot stay away from $0$; nonexpansiveness makes it non-increasing, so it tends to $0$. Now demiclosedness turns weak cluster points into fixed points and Fejér convergence finishes. The sentence to say out loud: a strict convex combination of two points of a ball is strictly inside; the λ-identity measures by how much, and that much is summable.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $\lambda_n\in[0,1]$: $x_{n+1}$ is a convex combination of $x_n$ and $Tx_n$, so it stays in $C$, and the λ-identity has a non-negative gain.
  • $\sum\lambda_n(1-\lambda_n)=+\infty$: the gains $\lambda_n(1-\lambda_n)\norm{Tx_n-x_n}^2$ are summable; infinite total weight forces $\liminf\norm{Tx_n-x_n}=0$. With $\lambda_n=1$ for all $n$ (Picard) the weight is $0$ and nothing is forced: the rotation (widget) keeps $\norm{Tx_n-x_n}$ constant. With $\lambda_n=1/n^2$ the sum is finite and the iterates may freeze before reaching a fixed point.
  • $\Fix T\neq\emptyset$: Fejér monotonicity needs a point to be monotone with respect to; and the theorem would be false without it (translation $x\mapsto x+u$ is nonexpansive with no fixed point, KM drifts to infinity).
  • $C$ closed convex, $T$ nonexpansive: the demiclosedness principle.

Proof in four steps T12 λ-identityT13 telescopingT14 Fejér + demiclosed

0. Iterates stay in $C$. $x_{n+1}=(1-\lambda_n)x_n+\lambda_nTx_n$ with $\lambda_n\in[0,1]$, $x_n\in C$, $Tx_n\in C$ and $C$ convex, so $x_{n+1}\in C$ (induction from $x_0\in C$).

(i) Fejér monotonicity w.r.t. $\Fix T$. Let $x\in\Fix T$ and $n\in\N$. Then $$\begin{aligned}\norm{x_{n+1}-x}^2&=\norm{x_n+\lambda_n(Tx_n-x_n)-x}^2=\norm{(1-\lambda_n)(x_n-x)+\lambda_n(Tx_n-x)}^2\\&=(1-\lambda_n)^2\norm{x_n-x}^2+\lambda_n^2\norm{Tx_n-x}^2-2\lambda_n(1-\lambda_n)\ps{x-x_n}{Tx_n-x}\\&=(1-\lambda_n)\norm{x_n-x}^2+\lambda_n\norm{Tx_n-x}^2-\lambda_n(1-\lambda_n)\norm{Tx_n-x+x-x_n}^2\qquad\text{(λ-identity)}\\&=(1-\lambda_n)\norm{x_n-x}^2+\lambda_n\norm{Tx_n-Tx}^2-\lambda_n(1-\lambda_n)\norm{Tx_n-x_n}^2\qquad(x=Tx)\\&\le(1-\lambda_n)\norm{x_n-x}^2+\lambda_n\norm{x_n-x}^2-\lambda_n(1-\lambda_n)\norm{Tx_n-x_n}^2\qquad\text{(nonexpansive)}\\&=\norm{x_n-x}^2-\lambda_n(1-\lambda_n)\norm{Tx_n-x_n}^2\ \le\ \norm{x_n-x}^2.\end{aligned}$$ (The third line is the λ-identity: $(1-\lambda)^2a^2+\lambda^2b^2-2\lambda(1-\lambda)\ps{-a}{b}$ with $a=x_n-x$, $b=Tx_n-x$ equals $(1-\lambda)a^2+\lambda b^2-\lambda(1-\lambda)\norm{a-b}^2$ because $(1-\lambda)^2=(1-\lambda)-\lambda(1-\lambda)$ and $\lambda^2=\lambda-\lambda(1-\lambda)$.) So $(x_n)$ is Fejér-monotone with respect to $\Fix T$, and we have the stronger inequality $$\norm{x_{n+1}-x}^2\le\norm{x_n-x}^2-\lambda_n(1-\lambda_n)\norm{Tx_n-x_n}^2.\tag{$\ast$}$$

(ii) Summability and $\liminf\norm{Tx_n-x_n}=0$. Summing $(\ast)$ from $0$ to $N$ telescopes: $$\sum_{n=0}^{N}\lambda_n(1-\lambda_n)\norm{Tx_n-x_n}^2\le\norm{x_0-x}^2-\norm{x_{N+1}-x}^2\le\norm{x_0-x}^2,$$ so $\sum_{n\in\N}\lambda_n(1-\lambda_n)\norm{Tx_n-x_n}^2\le\norm{x_0-x}^2\lt+\infty$. The tails of a convergent series tend to $0$, and for every $n$, $$\Big(\inf_{k\ge n}\norm{Tx_k-x_k}^2\Big)\sum_{k=n}^{+\infty}\lambda_k(1-\lambda_k)\ \le\ \sum_{k=n}^{+\infty}\lambda_k(1-\lambda_k)\norm{Tx_k-x_k}^2\ \xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}\ 0.$$ By hypothesis $\sum_{k\ge n}\lambda_k(1-\lambda_k)=+\infty$ for every $n$ (a divergent series of non-negative terms has divergent tails), so the infimum must vanish: $\inf_{k\ge n}\norm{Tx_k-x_k}=0$ for every $n$, i.e. $\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\norm{Tx_n-x_n}=0$.

(iii) $\norm{Tx_n-x_n}$ is non-increasing, hence $\to0$. Since $x_{n+1}-Tx_n=(1-\lambda_n)(x_n-Tx_n)$, we have $Tx_n-x_{n+1}=(1-\lambda_n)(Tx_n-x_n)$, and by nonexpansiveness $$\norm{Tx_{n+1}-x_{n+1}}=\norm{Tx_{n+1}-Tx_n+(1-\lambda_n)(Tx_n-x_n)}\le\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}+(1-\lambda_n)\norm{Tx_n-x_n}=\lambda_n\norm{Tx_n-x_n}+(1-\lambda_n)\norm{Tx_n-x_n}=\norm{Tx_n-x_n}.$$ A non-increasing non-negative sequence converges, and its limit equals its $\liminf$, which is $0$: $Tx_n-x_n\to0$ strongly.

(iv) Weak convergence. $(x_n)$ is bounded (Fejér), so it has weak cluster points. Let $x_{n_k}\wto\hat x$. The $x_{n_k}$ lie in $C$ and $Tx_{n_k}-x_{n_k}\to0$, so the demiclosedness principle gives $\hat x\in\Fix T$. Every weak cluster point lies in $\Fix T$ and the sequence is Fejér-monotone with respect to $\Fix T$: by Fejér-monotone convergence, $x_n\wto$ a point of $\Fix T$.

Averaged operators: relaxation built into the operator

KM relaxes a nonexpansive $T$ by hand. Some operators are already of the form $(1-\alpha)\Id+\alpha R$: for them the plain iteration converges, and — the decisive point for splitting — the property survives composition with a computable constant.

Definition + characterization

$\alpha$-averaged operators; the inequality characterization; firmly nonexpansive

Part V · slide 16SlidesKey#
Definition

Let $C\subset\H$ be a nonempty set, $A:C\to\H$ and $\alpha\in\,]0,1[$. $A$ is an $\alpha$-averaged operator if there exists a nonexpansive operator $R:C\to\H$ such that $$A=(1-\alpha)\Id+\alpha R.$$

Characterization. $A$ is $\alpha$-averaged if and only if $$\big(\forall(x,y)\in C^2\big)\qquad\norm{Ax-Ay}^2+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\norm{(\Id-A)x-(\Id-A)y}^2\le\norm{x-y}^2.$$

Remark. When $\alpha=\tfrac12$, we say that $A$ is firmly nonexpansive: $\norm{Ax-Ay}^2+\norm{(\Id-A)x-(\Id-A)y}^2\le\norm{x-y}^2$, equivalently $\ps{Ax-Ay}{x-y}\ge\norm{Ax-Ay}^2$.

In one sentence

An averaged operator is a KM step of a nonexpansive one: $Ax=x+\alpha(Rx-x)$; the inequality says that $A$ not only does not expand, it loses $\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\norm{(\Id-A)x-(\Id-A)y}^2$ — the smaller $\alpha$, the more it loses.

Why the characterization holds, and the picture

Given $A$ and $\alpha$, the candidate $R$ is forced: $R=\Id+\frac1\alpha(A-\Id)$. Write $Ax-Ay=(1-\alpha)(x-y)+\alpha(Rx-Ry)$ and apply the λ-identity with $\lambda=\alpha$: $\norm{Ax-Ay}^2=(1-\alpha)\norm{x-y}^2+\alpha\norm{Rx-Ry}^2-\alpha(1-\alpha)\norm{(x-y)-(Rx-Ry)}^2$. Since $(\Id-R)=\frac1\alpha(\Id-A)$, the last norm is $\frac1{\alpha^2}\norm{(\Id-A)x-(\Id-A)y}^2$, and "$R$ nonexpansive" becomes exactly the displayed inequality. Picture: fix $x,y$ and $d=x-y$. The vector $Ax-Ay=(1-\alpha)d+\alpha(Rx-Ry)$ with $\norm{Rx-Ry}\le\norm d$ lies in the closed ball of centre $(1-\alpha)d$ and radius $\alpha\norm d$. All these balls contain $d$ (the "$A=\Id$" case) and are nested: $\alpha=1$ gives the whole ball $B(0,\norm d)$ (nonexpansive), $\alpha=\tfrac12$ the ball of diameter $[0,d]$ (firmly nonexpansive), $\alpha\to0$ the single point $d$. The sentence to say out loud: averaged means the difference $Ax-Ay$ is confined to a ball tangent at $d$, strictly inside the nonexpansive ball.

Reading the inequality
  • $\alpha\to1$: the coefficient $\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\to0$ and the inequality degenerates to nonexpansiveness: averaged ⊂ nonexpansive, with a quantitative surplus.
  • $\alpha=\tfrac12$: coefficient $1$. Expanding $\norm{(\Id-A)x-(\Id-A)y}^2=\norm{x-y}^2-2\ps{x-y}{Ax-Ay}+\norm{Ax-Ay}^2$, the inequality becomes $\norm{Ax-Ay}^2\le\ps{Ax-Ay}{x-y}$: this is the form in which $\prox$ will be proved firmly nonexpansive.
  • Averaged with a smaller $\alpha$ implies averaged with a larger one (the coefficient decreases): $\alpha$-averaged ⇒ $\beta$-averaged for $\alpha\le\beta\lt1$. In particular firmly nonexpansive ⇒ $\alpha$-averaged for all $\alpha\ge\tfrac12$.
  • A strict contraction with constant $\rho$ is $\alpha$-averaged for every $\alpha\ge\frac{1+\rho}{2}$. Extra
Proof of the characterization T12 λ-identity

1. The only possible $R$. If $A=(1-\alpha)\Id+\alpha R$ then $R=\Id+\frac1\alpha(A-\Id)$. Conversely define $R$ by this formula; then $A=(1-\alpha)\Id+\alpha R$ holds, and $A$ is $\alpha$-averaged iff this $R$ is nonexpansive. Note $\Id-R=\frac1\alpha(\Id-A)$.

2. λ-identity. For $x,y\in C$, with $a=x-y$ and $b=Rx-Ry$, $Ax-Ay=(1-\alpha)a+\alpha b$ and $$\norm{Ax-Ay}^2=(1-\alpha)\norm{a}^2+\alpha\norm{b}^2-\alpha(1-\alpha)\norm{a-b}^2,\qquad a-b=(\Id-R)x-(\Id-R)y=\tfrac1\alpha\big((\Id-A)x-(\Id-A)y\big).$$ Hence $\alpha\norm{Rx-Ry}^2=\norm{Ax-Ay}^2-(1-\alpha)\norm{x-y}^2+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\norm{(\Id-A)x-(\Id-A)y}^2$.

3. Translate. $R$ nonexpansive $\iff\alpha\norm{Rx-Ry}^2\le\alpha\norm{x-y}^2$ for all $x,y$ $\iff\norm{Ax-Ay}^2+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\norm{(\Id-A)x-(\Id-A)y}^2\le(1-\alpha)\norm{x-y}^2+\alpha\norm{x-y}^2=\norm{x-y}^2$.

Proposition

Convex combinations and compositions of averaged operators

Part V · slide 17SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $C\subset\H$ be a nonempty set, $A_1:C\to\H$ be $\alpha_1$-averaged and $A_2:C\to\H$ be $\alpha_2$-averaged, with $(\alpha_1,\alpha_2)\in\,]0,1[^2$.

  • Convex combination. Let $\lambda\in\,]0,1[$. $\lambda A_1+(1-\lambda)A_2$ is $\gamma$-averaged with $$\gamma=\lambda\alpha_1+(1-\lambda)\alpha_2.$$
  • Composition. Assume that $A_2(C)\subset C$. Then $A_1\circ A_2$ is $\gamma$-averaged with $$\gamma=\frac{\alpha_1+\alpha_2-2\alpha_1\alpha_2}{1-\alpha_1\alpha_2}.$$

Exercise (slide 17): prove the first property. Solution in exercise 2.

In one sentence

Averagedness is a budget: convex combinations average the budgets, compositions add them in the "resistance" scale $\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}$ — the composed $\gamma$ satisfies $\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}=\frac{\alpha_1}{1-\alpha_1}+\frac{\alpha_2}{1-\alpha_2}$, and is always $\lt1$.

Why it is true

Convex combination: write each $A_i=(1-\alpha_i)\Id+\alpha_iR_i$ and collect the $\Id$ terms; what multiplies the non-identity part is a convex combination of nonexpansive operators, hence nonexpansive. Composition: use the inequality characterization twice, at $(x,y)$ for $A_2$ and at $(A_2x,A_2y)$ for $A_1$, and add: you get $\norm{A_1A_2x-A_1A_2y}^2+\kappa_1\norm{\cdot}^2+\kappa_2\norm{\cdot}^2\le\norm{x-y}^2$ with $\kappa_i=\frac{1-\alpha_i}{\alpha_i}$ and two different residuals $b-c$ and $a-b$ whose sum is the residual $a-c$ of the composition. Convexity of $\norm{\cdot}^2$ (the λ-identity once more) lets you trade the two weighted residuals for one weighted residual $\kappa\norm{a-c}^2$ with $\frac1\kappa=\frac1{\kappa_1}+\frac1{\kappa_2}$, i.e. the resistance rule above. The sentence to say out loud: compose the inequalities, then merge the two residual terms with the λ-identity; the price is the harmonic rule for $\kappa$.

Sanity checks on the composition formula
  • $\alpha_1=\alpha_2=\tfrac12$ (two firmly nonexpansive operators, e.g. two projections or two prox): $\gamma=\dfrac{1-\tfrac12}{1-\tfrac14}=\dfrac23$. In the resistance scale: $1+1=2=\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}$. So $P_{C_2}P_{C_1}$ is $\tfrac23$-averaged and can be relaxed up to $\lambda_n\lt\tfrac32$.
  • $\gamma\lt1$ always: $1-\gamma=\dfrac{(1-\alpha_1)(1-\alpha_2)}{1-\alpha_1\alpha_2}\gt0$. Compositions of averaged operators are averaged, never merely nonexpansive.
  • $\gamma\gt\max(\alpha_1,\alpha_2)$: composing costs budget. For $k$ operators, by induction $\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}=\sum_i\frac{\alpha_i}{1-\alpha_i}$.
  • $A_2(C)\subset C$ is only there so that $A_1\circ A_2$ is defined on $C$; on $C=\H$ it is automatic.
  • Forward–Backward: $\alpha_1=\tfrac12$ ($\prox$), $\alpha_2=\gamma\nu/2$ (gradient step) give $\frac{\gamma_{\rm FB}}{1-\gamma_{\rm FB}}=1+\frac{\gamma\nu/2}{1-\gamma\nu/2}=\frac1{1-\gamma\nu/2}$, so $\gamma_{\rm FB}=\frac1{2-\gamma\nu/2}$ and the relaxation range is $1/\gamma_{\rm FB}=2-\gamma\nu/2=\delta$ (T22).

Lemma

Descent lemma: a $\nu$-Lipschitz gradient gives a quadratic upper bound

Part V · slide 18 (proof, part 1)SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Let $f\in\Gz{\H}$ be differentiable with a $\nu$-Lipschitzian gradient ($\nu\gt0$): $(\forall x,y)\ \norm{\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)}\le\nu\norm{x-y}$. Then $$\big(\forall(x,y)\in\H^2\big)\qquad f(y)\le f(x)+\ps{y-x}{\nabla f(x)}+\frac\nu2\norm{y-x}^2.$$

In one sentence

The gradient cannot change faster than $\nu$ per unit length, so along the segment from $x$ to $y$ the function cannot rise above its tangent by more than $\int_0^1\nu t\norm{y-x}^2dt=\frac\nu2\norm{y-x}^2$.

Why it is true

Restrict $f$ to the segment: $\varphi(t)=f(x+t(y-x))$ has derivative $\varphi'(t)=\ps{y-x}{\nabla f(x+t(y-x))}$. The gap between $f(y)$ and the tangent value $f(x)+\ps{y-x}{\nabla f(x)}$ is $\int_0^1\big(\varphi'(t)-\varphi'(0)\big)dt$, and $\varphi'(t)-\varphi'(0)$ is an inner product of $y-x$ with a gradient difference over a distance $t\norm{y-x}$, hence at most $\nu t\norm{y-x}^2$ by Cauchy–Schwarz and the Lipschitz bound. Integrating $\nu t$ gives $\nu/2$. Convexity is not used; combined with convexity (tangent below), $f$ is squeezed between its tangent and the tangent $+\frac\nu2\norm{y-x}^2$. The sentence to say out loud: integrate the Lipschitz bound of the derivative along the segment.

What each hypothesis buys
  • Differentiability: $\varphi$ is differentiable with the chain rule formula.
  • $\nu$-Lipschitz gradient: the bound $\norm{\nabla f(x+t(y-x))-\nabla f(x)}\le\nu t\norm{y-x}$. With a smaller constant the parabola dips below $f$ somewhere (widget).
  • Convexity ($f\in\Gz{\H}$): not needed for this lemma; it is needed for the next step (Baillon–Haddad), where the lemma is fed into the conjugate.

Proof T05 integrate along the segment (as on the slides)

1. Restrict to the segment. For $(x,y)\in\H^2$ and $t\in\R$ let $\varphi(t)=f(x+t(y-x))$. $\varphi$ is differentiable and $\varphi'(t)=\ps{y-x}{\nabla f(x+t(y-x))}$ (chain rule). By the fundamental theorem of calculus $\varphi(1)-\varphi(0)=\int_0^1\varphi'(t)\,dt$, i.e., subtracting $\ps{y-x}{\nabla f(x)}=\int_0^1\varphi'(0)dt$ on both sides, $$f(y)-f(x)-\ps{y-x}{\nabla f(x)}=\int_0^1\ps{y-x}{\nabla f(x+t(y-x))-\nabla f(x)}\,dt.$$

2. Cauchy–Schwarz and Lipschitz. For every $t\in[0,1]$, $$\ps{y-x}{\nabla f(x+t(y-x))-\nabla f(x)}\le\norm{y-x}\,\norm{\nabla f(x+t(y-x))-\nabla f(x)}\le\norm{y-x}\cdot\nu\norm{t(y-x)}=t\nu\norm{y-x}^2.$$

3. Integrate. $\int_0^1t\nu\norm{y-x}^2dt=\frac\nu2\norm{y-x}^2$, hence $f(y)\le f(x)+\ps{y-x}{\nabla f(x)}+\frac\nu2\norm{y-x}^2$.

Theorem

Baillon–Haddad: $\Id-\nabla f$ is $\nu/2$-averaged (co-coercivity of the gradient)

Part V · slides 18–19SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Let $f\in\Gz{\H}$ and $\nu\in\,]0,2[$. If $f$ is differentiable with a $\nu$-Lipschitzian gradient then $\Id-\nabla f$ is a $\nu/2$-averaged operator.

Along the way (and valid for every $\nu\gt0$) one proves the Baillon–Haddad inequality (co-coercivity): $$\big(\forall(x,y)\in\H^2\big)\qquad\ps{y-x}{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}\ \ge\ \frac1\nu\norm{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}^2.$$

In one sentence

For a convex function, "gradient $\nu$-Lipschitz" upgrades itself to "gradient $\frac1\nu$-co-coercive", which is a Lipschitz bound and a monotonicity bound at once; written for $\Id-\nabla f$, co-coercivity is exactly the $\nu/2$-averaged inequality.

Why it is true

The surprise is that a Lipschitz condition (which only bounds $\norm{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}$) produces an inner-product inequality. Convexity is the lever, through the conjugate: the descent lemma bounds $f$ from above by a parabola, and taking the conjugate flips upper bounds on $f$ into lower bounds on $f^*$ — and the conjugate of $\frac\nu2\norm\cdot^2$ is $\frac1{2\nu}\norm\cdot^2$. One obtains that $f^*$, evaluated at gradients, satisfies a strong convexity-type inequality with modulus $1/\nu$: $f^*(\nabla f(y))\ge f^*(\nabla f(x))+\ps{x}{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}+\frac1{2\nu}\norm{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}^2$ (note that $x\in\partial f^*(\nabla f(x))$, so $x$ plays the role of the subgradient of $f^*$). Writing this inequality twice with $x,y$ swapped and adding (T06) kills the values of $f^*$ and leaves co-coercivity. Finally expanding $\norm{(\Id-\nabla f)x-(\Id-\nabla f)y}^2$ and inserting co-coercivity is the averaged inequality with $\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}=\frac{2-\nu}{\nu}$, i.e. $\alpha=\nu/2$. The sentence to say out loud: Lipschitz gradient ⇒ quadratic majorant of $f$ ⇒ quadratic minorant of $f^*$ at gradients ⇒ (symmetrize) co-coercivity ⇒ averaged.

What each hypothesis buys
  • Convexity: the equality case of Fenchel–Young $f^*(\nabla f(x))=\ps{x}{\nabla f(x)}-f(x)$ (because $\nabla f(x)\in\partial f(x)$) and the very definition of $f^*$ as a supremum. Without convexity co-coercivity fails: $f=-\frac12\norm\cdot^2$ has a $1$-Lipschitz gradient and $\ps{y-x}{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}=-\norm{y-x}^2\lt0$.
  • $\nu$-Lipschitz gradient: the descent lemma, i.e. the parabola, i.e. the constant $\frac1{2\nu}$ after conjugation.
  • $\nu\lt2$: only to have $\alpha=\nu/2\in\,]0,1[$ so that "averaged" makes sense; co-coercivity itself holds for every $\nu\gt0$. The general step size version is the next corollary.
  • $f$ differentiable everywhere: $\dom f=\H$, so all values $f(x)$, $f^*(\nabla f(x))$ are finite and can be subtracted.

Proof T10 Fenchel–Young as a leverT06 write twice and addT09 complete the square (as on the slides, every step filled in)

1. Descent lemma. By the descent lemma, for all $x,z\in\H$: $f(z)\le f(x)+\ps{z-x}{\nabla f(x)}+\frac\nu2\norm{z-x}^2$.

2. Lower bound on $f^*$ at a gradient. Fix $x,y\in\H$. By definition of the conjugate, for every $z\in\H$, $f^*(\nabla f(y))=\sup_w\big(\ps{w}{\nabla f(y)}-f(w)\big)\ge\ps{z}{\nabla f(y)}-f(z)$. Insert step 1 (which bounds $f(z)$ from above, so $-f(z)$ from below): $$f^*(\nabla f(y))\ \ge\ \ps{z}{\nabla f(y)}-f(x)-\ps{z-x}{\nabla f(x)}-\frac\nu2\norm{z-x}^2\ =\ \ps{z}{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}+\ps{x}{\nabla f(x)}-f(x)-\frac\nu2\norm{z-x}^2.$$

3. Fenchel–Young with equality. Since $f$ is convex and differentiable at $x$, $\nabla f(x)\in\partial f(x)$ and the Fenchel–Young inequality is an equality: $\ps{x}{\nabla f(x)}-f(x)=f^*(\nabla f(x))$ (finite, as $f(x)\in\R$). Hence, for every $z$, $$f^*(\nabla f(y))\ge\ps{z}{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}+f^*(\nabla f(x))-\frac\nu2\norm{z-x}^2.$$

4. Split $z=x+(z-x)$ and take the supremum over $z$. Writing $\ps{z}{u}=\ps{x}{u}+\ps{z-x}{u}$ with $u=\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)$, $$f^*(\nabla f(y))\ge f^*(\nabla f(x))+\ps{x}{u}+\Big[\ps{z-x}{u}-\frac\nu2\norm{z-x}^2\Big]\qquad\text{for every }z\in\H.$$ The bracket, as a function of $w=z-x\in\H$, has supremum $\sup_w\big(\ps{w}{u}-\frac\nu2\norm w^2\big)=\big(\tfrac\nu2\norm\cdot^2\big)^*(u)=\frac1{2\nu}\norm u^2$ (conjugate of a quadratic; directly: complete the square, $\ps wu-\frac\nu2\norm w^2=\frac1{2\nu}\norm u^2-\frac\nu2\norm{w-u/\nu}^2$, maximal at $w=u/\nu$). Taking the supremum over $z$: $$f^*(\nabla f(y))\ \ge\ f^*(\nabla f(x))+\ps{x}{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}+\frac1{2\nu}\norm{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}^2.\tag{1}$$

5. Symmetrize and add (Baillon–Haddad). Exchanging the roles of $x$ and $y$ in (1): $$f^*(\nabla f(x))\ \ge\ f^*(\nabla f(y))+\ps{y}{\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)}+\frac1{2\nu}\norm{\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)}^2.\tag{2}$$ Adding (1) and (2), the finite values $f^*(\nabla f(x))$, $f^*(\nabla f(y))$ cancel: $$0\ \ge\ \ps{x}{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}-\ps{y}{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}+\frac1\nu\norm{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}^2=-\ps{y-x}{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}+\frac1\nu\norm{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}^2,$$ i.e. $\ps{y-x}{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}\ge\frac1\nu\norm{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}^2$.

6. The averaged inequality. Let $A=\Id-\nabla f$, so $\Id-A=\nabla f$. Expanding, $$\norm{Ax-Ay}^2=\norm{(x-y)-(\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y))}^2=\norm{x-y}^2-2\ps{x-y}{\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)}+\norm{\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)}^2\le\norm{x-y}^2-\Big(\frac2\nu-1\Big)\norm{\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)}^2$$ by step 5. Since $\frac2\nu-1=\frac{2-\nu}{\nu}=\frac{1-\nu/2}{\nu/2}$, this is $$\norm{(\Id-\nabla f)x-(\Id-\nabla f)y}^2+\frac{1-\nu/2}{\nu/2}\norm{\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)}^2\le\norm{x-y}^2,$$ the characterization of a $\nu/2$-averaged operator (here $\nu/2\in\,]0,1[$ because $\nu\lt2$).

□
Trick: conjugation flips bounds, symmetrization kills values

An upper bound $f\le q$ gives a lower bound $f^*\ge q^*$, and $q^*$ is explicit when $q$ is a quadratic ($\big(\tfrac\nu2\norm\cdot^2\big)^*=\tfrac1{2\nu}\norm\cdot^2$, T10). Then write the inequality at $(x,y)$ and at $(y,x)$ and add (T06): the function values disappear and an inequality between $\ps{y-x}{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}$ and $\norm{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}^2$ remains. The same two moves prove that $\nabla f$ is monotone (page 2) and the strong-convexity bounds of page 7.

Pitfall

Co-coercivity is stronger than Lipschitz continuity (Cauchy–Schwarz: $\frac1\nu\norm{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}^2\le\norm{y-x}\norm{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}$ gives back $\nu$-Lipschitz) and it is false without convexity. The theorem is a statement about convex functions; do not quote it for a nonconvex $g$.

Statement

Let $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $\nu\in\,]0,+\infty[$ and $\gamma\in\,]0,2/\nu[$. If $f$ is differentiable with a $\nu$-Lipschitzian gradient then $\Id-\gamma\nabla f$ is a $\gamma\nu/2$-averaged operator.

Remark. $\Id-\gamma\nabla f$ is the gradient descent operator.

In one sentence

Apply the theorem to $\gamma f$, whose gradient is $\gamma\nu$-Lipschitz with $\gamma\nu\in\,]0,2[$.

Why it is true

Scaling $f$ by $\gamma$ scales the gradient and its Lipschitz constant by $\gamma$; the theorem with $\nu$ replaced by $\gamma\nu$ is exactly the claim. The condition $\gamma\lt2/\nu$ is the condition $\gamma\nu\lt2$ that makes $\alpha=\gamma\nu/2$ smaller than $1$. The fixed points do not change: $\Fix(\Id-\gamma\nabla f)=\zer\nabla f=\Argmin f$ for every $\gamma\gt0$. The sentence to say out loud: the step size $\gamma$ buys averagedness $\gamma\nu/2$; the classical bound $2/\nu$ is where averagedness is lost.

Proof

1. $\gamma f\in\Gz{\H}$ is differentiable with $\nabla(\gamma f)=\gamma\nabla f$, and $\norm{\gamma\nabla f(x)-\gamma\nabla f(y)}\le\gamma\nu\norm{x-y}$: its gradient is $\gamma\nu$-Lipschitz, with $\gamma\nu\in\,]0,2[$.

Theorem

$\prox_f$ is firmly nonexpansive (i.e. $\tfrac12$-averaged)

Part V · slides 21–22SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Let $f\in\Gz{\H}$. Then $\prox_f$ is firmly nonexpansive, i.e. a $\tfrac12$-averaged operator: $$\big(\forall(u_1,u_2)\in\H^2\big)\qquad\norm{u_1-u_2}^2\ \ge\ \norm{\prox_fu_1-\prox_fu_2}^2+\norm{(\Id-\prox_f)u_1-(\Id-\prox_f)u_2}^2,$$ equivalently $\ps{\prox_fu_1-\prox_fu_2}{u_1-u_2}\ge\norm{\prox_fu_1-\prox_fu_2}^2$. In particular $\prox_f$ is nonexpansive, and so is $\prox_{\gamma f}$ for every $\gamma\gt0$.

In one sentence

Monotonicity of $\partial f$ says $\ps{x_1-x_2}{u_1-u_2}\ge0$ for subgradients; adding $\norm{x_1-x_2}^2$ to both sides turns it into a statement about $\Id+\partial f$, and reading it through the resolvent $\prox_f=(\Id+\partial f)^{-1}$ gives firm nonexpansiveness.

Why it is true

$\prox_f$ inverts $\Id+\partial f$. The operator $\Id+\partial f$ is "monotone plus identity": $\ps{x_1-x_2}{u'_1-u'_2}\ge\norm{x_1-x_2}^2$ whenever $u'_i\in(\Id+\partial f)x_i$, because the $\partial f$ part contributes $\ge0$ and the identity part contributes exactly $\norm{x_1-x_2}^2$. Now rename: $x_i=\prox_fu'_i$. The inequality says that the inner product of the input difference with the output difference dominates the squared output difference — which is firm nonexpansiveness. The sentence to say out loud: prox is the inverse of a strongly monotone operator, and inverting "identity plus monotone" gives firmly nonexpansive.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $f$ convex: monotonicity of $\partial f$, the only ingredient.
  • $f$ proper lsc: $\prox_f$ is defined everywhere (existence and uniqueness), so that every $u'\in\H$ can be written $u'\in(\Id+\partial f)(\prox_fu')$: the inequality obtained for pairs in the graph of $\Id+\partial f$ is an inequality for all pairs of points of $\H$.
  • Any $\gamma\gt0$: $\gamma f\in\Gz{\H}$ too, so $\prox_{\gamma f}$ is firmly nonexpansive with no condition on $\gamma$ — the implicit step is unconditionally stable, unlike the explicit gradient step.

Proof T08 resolvent readingT09 expand the square (as on the slides)

1. Monotonicity, shifted by the identity. Let $x_1,x_2\in\H$, $u_1\in\partial f(x_1)$ and $u_2\in\partial f(x_2)$. By monotonicity of $\partial f$, $\ps{x_1-x_2}{u_1-u_2}\ge0$, which is equivalent to $$\ps{x_1-x_2}{(x_1+u_1)-(x_2+u_2)}=\norm{x_1-x_2}^2+\ps{x_1-x_2}{u_1-u_2}\ge\norm{x_1-x_2}^2.$$

2. Rename through the resolvent. Set $u'_1=x_1+u_1\in(\Id+\partial f)x_1$ and $u'_2=x_2+u_2\in(\Id+\partial f)x_2$. Then $\ps{x_1-x_2}{u'_1-u'_2}\ge\norm{x_1-x_2}^2$. By the characterization of the proximity operator, $u'_i-x_i\in\partial f(x_i)$ means $x_i=\prox_fu'_i$. Hence $$\ps{\prox_fu'_1-\prox_fu'_2}{u'_1-u'_2}\ge\norm{\prox_fu'_1-\prox_fu'_2}^2.$$ Every $u'\in\H$ arises this way (take $x=\prox_fu'$, $u=u'-x\in\partial f(x)$), so the inequality holds for all $(u'_1,u'_2)\in\H^2$.

Therefore $$\norm{p_1-p_2}^2+\norm{(\Id-\prox_f)u'_1-(\Id-\prox_f)u'_2}^2=\norm{u'_1-u'_2}^2-2\big(\ps{u'_1-u'_2}{p_1-p_2}-\norm{p_1-p_2}^2\big)\le\norm{u'_1-u'_2}^2$$ by step 2: this is the characterization with $\alpha=\tfrac12$, i.e. $\prox_f$ is $\tfrac12$-averaged (firmly nonexpansive). Theorem

Relaxed iteration of an $\alpha$-averaged operator ($\lambda_n\in[0,1/\alpha]$)

Part V · slides 23–24SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $T:\H\to\H$ be an $\alpha$-averaged operator with $\alpha\in\,]0,1[$ such that $\Fix T\neq\emptyset$. Let $(\lambda_n)_{n\in\N}$ be a sequence in $[0,1/\alpha]$ such that $$\sum_{n\in\N}\lambda_n(1-\alpha\lambda_n)=+\infty.$$ Let $x_0\in\H$ and $(\forall n\in\N)\ x_{n+1}=x_n+\lambda_n(Tx_n-x_n)$. Then

  • $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ is Fejér-monotone with respect to $\Fix T$;
  • $(Tx_n-x_n)_{n\in\N}$ converges strongly to $0$;
  • $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ converges weakly to a point in $\Fix T$.
In one sentence

$T=(1-\alpha)\Id+\alpha R$ means $Tx-x=\alpha(Rx-x)$: the relaxed iteration of $T$ with parameter $\lambda_n$ is the KM iteration of $R$ with parameter $\mu_n=\alpha\lambda_n\in[0,1]$, and $\Fix R=\Fix T$.

Why it is true

There is no new analysis: the theorem is KM read through the change of parameter $\mu_n=\alpha\lambda_n$. The reward is the enlarged relaxation range: the unrelaxed iteration $\lambda_n=1$ is admissible (since $1\le1/\alpha$ and $1-\alpha\gt0$), and even over-relaxation up to $1/\alpha\gt1$ is allowed. That is what averagedness buys over mere nonexpansiveness. The sentence to say out loud: an averaged operator is a pre-relaxed nonexpansive one; relaxing it further by $\lambda_n$ relaxes $R$ by $\alpha\lambda_n$.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $\lambda_n\le1/\alpha$: $\mu_n=\alpha\lambda_n\le1$, the KM range. Beyond it the λ-identity gain becomes a loss and divergence is possible (FB widget with $\lambda\gt\delta$).
  • $\sum\lambda_n(1-\alpha\lambda_n)=+\infty$: this is $\frac1\alpha\sum\mu_n(1-\mu_n)=+\infty$, the KM condition. The constant choice $\lambda_n=1$ satisfies it.
  • $T:\H\to\H$: $C=\H$ is closed convex and $T(\H)\subset\H$, so KM applies to $R$ on $C=\H$.

Optimization algorithms: Forward–Backward and its special cases

Everything is in place. The operator $\prox_{\gamma f}\circ(\Id-\gamma\nabla g)$ has the right fixed points (dictionary), is averaged (two averaged factors, composition formula), so the averaged iteration theorem converges. The constants $2/\nu$ and $\delta=2-\gamma\nu/2$ are not assumptions: they are computed.

Theorem + algorithm

Forward–Backward algorithm

Part V · slides 25–26SlidesKeyExamLab#
Statement

Let $f\in\Gz{\H}$. Let $g\in\Gz{\H}$ be differentiable with a $\nu$-Lipschitzian gradient, $\nu\in\,]0,+\infty[$. Let $\gamma\in\,]0,2/\nu[$ and $\delta=2-\gamma\nu/2\in\,]1,2[$. Let $(\lambda_n)_{n\in\N}$ be a sequence in $[0,\delta]$ such that $\sum_{n\in\N}\lambda_n(\delta-\lambda_n)=+\infty$. We assume that $\Argmin(f+g)\neq\emptyset$. Let $x_0\in\H$ and $$(\forall n\in\N)\qquad\begin{cases}y_n=x_n-\gamma\nabla g(x_n)\\ x_{n+1}=x_n+\lambda_n\big(\prox_{\gamma f}y_n-x_n\big).\end{cases}$$ Then $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ converges weakly to a minimizer of $f+g$.

Forward–Backward (proximal gradient)

Choose $\gamma\in\,]0,2/\nu[$, $\lambda_n\in[0,\delta]$ with $\delta=2-\gamma\nu/2$ (e.g. $\lambda_n\equiv1$). Let $x_0\in\H$. For $n=0,1,\dots$

  1. $y_n=x_n-\gamma\nabla g(x_n)$ (forward / explicit gradient step on the smooth part $g$)
  2. $x_{n+1}=x_n+\lambda_n(\prox_{\gamma f}y_n-x_n)$ (backward / implicit proximal step on the nonsmooth part $f$, relaxed)

Cost per iteration: one evaluation of $\nabla g$ and one of $\prox_{\gamma f}$. Parallelizable: the prox when $f$ is separable ($\prox$ acts coordinate-wise, e.g. soft-thresholding), the gradient when $g=\tfrac12\norm{A\cdot-b}^2$ (matrix–vector products). "Forward": the gradient is evaluated at the current point $x_n$ (explicit, like forward Euler). "Backward": the subgradient of $f$ is evaluated at the new point, since $x_{n+1}$ (for $\lambda_n=1$) satisfies $x_{n+1}\in y_n-\gamma\partial f(x_{n+1})$ (implicit, like backward Euler).

In one sentence

One explicit step on $g$, one implicit step on $f$: the composed operator $T=\prox_{\gamma f}\circ(\Id-\gamma\nabla g)$ has $\Fix T=\Argmin(f+g)$ and is $\frac{1}{2-\gamma\nu/2}$-averaged, so its relaxed iteration converges by the averaged iteration theorem with $1/\alpha=\delta$.

Why it is true

Two facts, one for each conclusion. Where it goes: $x=\prox_{\gamma f}(x-\gamma\nabla g(x))$ unpacks, by the characterization of prox, to $-\gamma\nabla g(x)\in\gamma\partial f(x)$, i.e. $0\in\partial f(x)+\nabla g(x)=\partial(f+g)(x)$ (sum rule, legitimate because $g$ is finite everywhere), i.e. $x$ minimizes $f+g$: the fixed points of $T$ are exactly the minimizers. Why it gets there: $\prox_{\gamma f}$ is $\tfrac12$-averaged (for any $\gamma$), $\Id-\gamma\nabla g$ is $\frac{\gamma\nu}2$-averaged (for $\gamma\lt2/\nu$), and averagedness is stable under composition with $\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}=1+\frac{\gamma\nu/2}{1-\gamma\nu/2}=\frac1{1-\gamma\nu/2}$, giving $\alpha=\frac1{2-\gamma\nu/2}$. The averaged iteration theorem then allows $\lambda_n\in[0,1/\alpha]=[0,\delta]$ with $\sum\lambda_n(1-\alpha\lambda_n)=\frac1\delta\sum\lambda_n(\delta-\lambda_n)=+\infty$. The sentence to say out loud: fixed points of prox∘gradient-step are minimizers of the sum, and the composition is averaged with constant $1/\delta$.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $g$ differentiable with $\nu$-Lipschitz gradient: Baillon–Haddad, i.e. the gradient step is averaged. For a nonsmooth $g$ the explicit step $x-\gamma u$, $u\in\partial g(x)$, is not even nonexpansive (subgradient methods need vanishing steps and converge slowly): use Douglas–Rachford instead (page 5).
  • $g$ finite-valued (differentiable on all of $\H$): the sum rule $\partial(f+g)=\partial f+\nabla g$ without any qualification condition (Moreau–Rockafellar).
  • $\gamma\lt2/\nu$: $\gamma\nu/2\lt1$, the gradient step is averaged, not merely nonexpansive. At $\gamma=2/\nu$ the plain iteration may oscillate, beyond it diverges (widget).
  • $f\in\Gz{\H}$: $\prox_{\gamma f}$ exists and is firmly nonexpansive; $f$ may be nonsmooth and take the value $+\infty$ (constraints: next corollary).
  • $\Argmin(f+g)\neq\emptyset$: $\Fix T\ne\emptyset$, needed for Fejér monotonicity. (Use the existence theorems of page 1: coercivity, or a compact constraint.)
  • $\lambda_n\in[0,\delta]$, $\sum\lambda_n(\delta-\lambda_n)=+\infty$: the relaxation range of an $\frac1\delta$-averaged operator. $\lambda_n\equiv1$ is allowed since $\delta\gt1$; so is over-relaxation up to $\delta\lt2$, which can accelerate in practice.

Each $T_n=\prox_{\gamma_nf}\circ(\Id-\gamma_n\nabla g)$ has the same fixed point set $\Argmin(f+g)$ and is uniformly averaged (constant $\le\frac1{2-\overline\gamma\nu/2}\lt1$), so the Fejér inequality still holds step by step; the only new work is to pass from "$T_nx_n-x_n\to0$" to "a fixed operator has vanishing residual" in order to use demiclosedness.

Why it is true

The fixed-point set does not depend on $\gamma$ (step 1 of the FB proof holds for every $\gamma\gt0$), so Fejér monotonicity with respect to $\Argmin(f+g)$ is inherited from the direct Fejér inequality of the averaged iteration card, with a gain $\lambda_n(\delta_n-\lambda_n)\ge\underline\lambda(1-\overline\gamma\nu/2)\gt0$ bounded away from zero because $\lambda_n\le1\lt\delta_n$ and $\lambda_n\ge\underline\lambda$. The residuals $T_nx_n-x_n$ are therefore square-summable. Demiclosedness needs one operator: the residual of $T_{\underline\gamma}$ is controlled by that of $T_{\gamma_n}$ because $\gamma\mapsto\norm{x-T_\gamma x}$ is nondecreasing (a short monotonicity argument on $\partial f$). The sentence to say out loud: same fixed points, uniform averagedness, and the residual is monotone in the step.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $\overline\gamma\lt2/\nu$: uniform averagedness $\alpha_n\le\frac1{2-\overline\gamma\nu/2}\lt1$, so the Fejér gain does not vanish.
  • $\underline\gamma\gt0$: a fixed comparison operator $T_{\underline\gamma}$ for demiclosedness (and it prevents $\gamma_n\to0$, which would freeze the iteration).
  • $\lambda_n\in[\underline\lambda,1]$: $\lambda_n\le1$ keeps all $\lambda_n$ below every $\delta_n\in\,]1,2[$ without knowing $\gamma_n$; $\lambda_n\ge\underline\lambda\gt0$ keeps the gain away from $0$. (The slide's "$0\lt\underline\gamma\lt\overline\gamma$" can be read with $\le$; the constant-step case is the previous theorem.)
Proof (the slides state this without proof; here is one, reusing the FB proof) T14T13 Extra

1. Fixed points and averagedness, uniformly. For $\gamma\gt0$ let $T_\gamma=\prox_{\gamma f}\circ(\Id-\gamma\nabla g)$. Step 1 of the FB proof gives $\Fix T_\gamma=\Argmin(f+g)=:D\neq\emptyset$ for every $\gamma\gt0$; step 3 gives, for $\gamma\in\,]0,2/\nu[$, that $T_\gamma$ is $\alpha_\gamma$-averaged with $1/\alpha_\gamma=\delta_\gamma=2-\gamma\nu/2$. For $\gamma_n\le\overline\gamma$, $\delta_{\gamma_n}\ge\underline\delta:=2-\overline\gamma\nu/2\gt1$.

2. Fejér inequality with a uniform gain. Let $x\in D$ and $n\in\N$. $x_{n+1}=x_n+\lambda_n(T_{\gamma_n}x_n-x_n)$ with $\lambda_n\le1\le1/\alpha_{\gamma_n}$, so the direct Fejér inequality of the averaged iteration card applies: $$\norm{x_{n+1}-x}^2\le\norm{x_n-x}^2-\lambda_n(\delta_{\gamma_n}-\lambda_n)\norm{T_{\gamma_n}x_n-x_n}^2\le\norm{x_n-x}^2-\underline\lambda\,(\underline\delta-1)\norm{T_{\gamma_n}x_n-x_n}^2,$$ since $\lambda_n\ge\underline\lambda$ and $\delta_{\gamma_n}-\lambda_n\ge\underline\delta-1\gt0$. Hence $(x_n)$ is Fejér-monotone with respect to $D$ and, telescoping, $\sum_n\norm{T_{\gamma_n}x_n-x_n}^2\le\frac{\norm{x_0-x}^2}{\underline\lambda(\underline\delta-1)}\lt+\infty$: $T_{\gamma_n}x_n-x_n\to0$.

3. Lemma: $\gamma\mapsto\norm{x-T_\gamma x}$ is nondecreasing. Fix $x$ and $0\lt\gamma\le\mu$; let $p_\gamma=T_\gamma x$, $p_\mu=T_\mu x$. By the prox characterization, $\frac{x-p_\gamma}{\gamma}-\nabla g(x)\in\partial f(p_\gamma)$ and $\frac{x-p_\mu}{\mu}-\nabla g(x)\in\partial f(p_\mu)$. Monotonicity of $\partial f$ (the $\nabla g(x)$ terms cancel) with $a=x-p_\gamma$, $b=x-p_\mu$, $p_\gamma-p_\mu=b-a$: $$\Big\langle b-a\,\Big|\,\frac a\gamma-\frac b\mu\Big\rangle\ge0\iff\Big(\frac1\gamma+\frac1\mu\Big)\ps ab\ge\frac{\norm a^2}\gamma+\frac{\norm b^2}\mu.$$ With Cauchy–Schwarz $\ps ab\le\norm a\norm b$ this gives $\big(\norm b-\norm a\big)\big(\tfrac{\norm a}\gamma-\tfrac{\norm b}\mu\big)\ge0$. If $\norm b\lt\norm a$ then $\frac{\norm a}\gamma\le\frac{\norm b}\mu\lt\frac{\norm a}\mu\le\frac{\norm a}\gamma$, a contradiction; so $\norm{x-T_\mu x}=\norm b\ge\norm a=\norm{x-T_\gamma x}$.

4. Residual of the fixed operator $T_{\underline\gamma}$. By step 3 with $\gamma=\underline\gamma\le\gamma_n=\mu$: $\norm{x_n-T_{\underline\gamma}x_n}\le\norm{x_n-T_{\gamma_n}x_n}\to0$.

5. Cluster points and conclusion. $T_{\underline\gamma}$ is averaged, hence nonexpansive, on $C=\H$ with $\Fix T_{\underline\gamma}=D$. If $x_{n_k}\wto\hat x$, then $T_{\underline\gamma}x_{n_k}-x_{n_k}\to0$ and the demiclosedness principle gives $\hat x\in D$. With Fejér monotonicity (step 2), Fejér-monotone convergence yields $x_n\wto$ a point of $D=\Argmin(f+g)$.

Projected gradient algorithm ($f=\ind_C$)

Part V · slide 28SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $C$ be a nonempty closed convex subset of $\H$. Let $g\in\Gz{\H}$ be differentiable with a $\nu$-Lipschitzian gradient, $\nu\in\,]0,+\infty[$. Let $\gamma\in\,]0,2/\nu[$ and $\delta=2-\gamma\nu/2\in\,]1,2[$. Let $(\lambda_n)_{n\in\N}$ be a sequence in $[0,\delta]$ such that $\sum_{n\in\N}\lambda_n(\delta-\lambda_n)=+\infty$. We assume that $\Argmin_{x\in C}g(x)\neq\emptyset$. Let $x_0\in\H$ and $$(\forall n\in\N)\qquad\begin{cases}y_n=x_n-\gamma\nabla g(x_n)\\ x_{n+1}=x_n+\lambda_n\big(P_Cy_n-x_n\big).\end{cases}$$ Then $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ converges weakly to a minimizer of $g$ over $C$.

Projected gradient
  1. $y_n=x_n-\gamma\nabla g(x_n)$ (gradient step, ignoring the constraint)
  2. $x_{n+1}=x_n+\lambda_n(P_Cy_n-x_n)$ (project back onto $C$, relaxed)

Cost per iteration: one gradient, one projection (cheap for boxes, balls, half-spaces, simplices; expensive for general polyhedra). With $\lambda_n=1$, the iterate stays in $C$.

In one sentence

Forward–Backward with $f=\ind_C$: the constraint is a $+\infty$ penalty, and $\prox_{\gamma\ind_C}=P_C$ for every $\gamma$.

Why it is true

$\ind_C\in\Gz{\H}$ exactly when $C$ is nonempty closed convex (page 1), $\Argmin(\ind_C+g)=\Argmin_Cg$ (T01), and $\prox_{\gamma\ind_C}=\prox_{\ind_C}=P_C$ (page 3). Nothing else changes. The fixed point equation $x=P_C(x-\gamma\nabla g(x))$ is the variational inequality $\ps{\nabla g(x)}{y-x}\ge0$ for all $y\in C$, i.e. $-\nabla g(x)\in N_C(x)$.

Proof

1. $f=\ind_C\in\Gz{\H}$ ($C$ nonempty closed convex), and $\Argmin(f+g)=\Argmin_Cg\ne\emptyset$ since $f+g=g$ on $C$ and $+\infty$ outside.

2. $\gamma\ind_C=\ind_C$, and $\prox_{\ind_C}=P_C$: so the FB iteration with this $f$ is exactly the displayed one. Forward–Backward applies.

Gradient descent ($f=0$)

Part V · slide 29SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $g\in\Gz{\H}$ be a differentiable function with a $\nu$-Lipschitzian gradient, $\nu\in\,]0,+\infty[$. Let $(\gamma_n)_{n\in\N}$ be a sequence in $[\underline\gamma,\overline\gamma]$ where $0\lt\underline\gamma\lt\overline\gamma\lt2/\nu$. We assume that $\Argmin g\neq\emptyset$. Let $x_0\in\H$ and $$(\forall n\in\N)\qquad x_{n+1}=x_n-\gamma_n\nabla g(x_n).$$ Then $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ converges weakly to a minimizer of $g$.

Gradient descent
  1. $x_{n+1}=x_n-\gamma_n\nabla g(x_n)$, $\gamma_n\in[\underline\gamma,\overline\gamma]\subset\,]0,2/\nu[$

Cost per iteration: one gradient. The classical step range $]0,2/\nu[$ is explained by averagedness: $\Id-\gamma\nabla g$ is $\frac{\gamma\nu}2$-averaged iff $\gamma\lt2/\nu$. The "textbook" choice $\gamma=1/\nu$ makes the operator firmly nonexpansive and is the MM step of page 7.

In one sentence

Forward–Backward with $f=0$ ($\prox_{\gamma\cdot0}=\Id$) and $\lambda_n=1$; the iteration is the unrelaxed iteration of the averaged operators $\Id-\gamma_n\nabla g$, all with fixed point set $\Argmin g$.

Why it is true

With $f=0$, $\prox_{\gamma f}=\Id$ and the varying-stepsize theorem with $\lambda_n=1$ is exactly the statement. A direct proof is just as short and worth knowing: the gradient operator $G_n=\Id-\gamma_n\nabla g$ is $\frac{\gamma_n\nu}2$-averaged with $\Fix G_n=\zer\nabla g=\Argmin g$, so the averaged inequality at a minimizer $x$ gives $\norm{x_{n+1}-x}^2\le\norm{x_n-x}^2-\big(\frac2{\gamma_n\nu}-1\big)\gamma_n^2\norm{\nabla g(x_n)}^2$: Fejér, and $\nabla g(x_n)\to0$; a weak cluster point $\hat x$ then satisfies $\hat x\in\Fix(\Id-\underline\gamma\nabla g)$ by demiclosedness. The sentence to say out loud: a gradient step with $\gamma\lt2/\nu$ is averaged with fixed points the minimizers; iterate.

Proof

1. As a special case. Take $f=0\in\Gz{\H}$: $\prox_{\gamma_nf}=\Id$, $\Argmin(f+g)=\Argmin g\ne\emptyset$, and with $\lambda_n=1\in[\underline\lambda,1]$ (any $\underline\lambda\in\,]0,1]$) the iteration of FB with varying stepsize reads $x_{n+1}=x_n+1\cdot\big(y_n-x_n\big)=x_n-\gamma_n\nabla g(x_n)$. That theorem concludes.

2. Direct proof (optional). For $x\in\Argmin g$, the averaged inequality for $G_n=\Id-\gamma_n\nabla g$ ($\alpha_n=\gamma_n\nu/2$, $(\Id-G_n)x=\gamma_n\nabla g(x)=0$) gives $\norm{x_{n+1}-x}^2+\frac{1-\alpha_n}{\alpha_n}\gamma_n^2\norm{\nabla g(x_n)}^2\le\norm{x_n-x}^2$ with $\frac{1-\alpha_n}{\alpha_n}\gamma_n^2=\gamma_n\big(\frac2\nu-\gamma_n\big)\ge\underline\gamma\big(\frac2\nu-\overline\gamma\big)\gt0$. Telescoping: $\nabla g(x_n)\to0$, hence $(\Id-\underline\gamma\nabla g)x_n-x_n\to0$; demiclosedness of the nonexpansive $\Id-\underline\gamma\nabla g$ puts every weak cluster point in $\zer\nabla g=\Argmin g$; Fejér convergence concludes.

□
Pitfall

"Gradient descent converges for small steps" is only half of the truth: the admissible range is the open interval $]0,2/\nu[$ and the optimal fixed step for a quadratic is $2/(\nu+\mu)$ with $\mu$ the strong convexity constant, not $1/\nu$. At $\gamma=2/\nu$ the iteration can oscillate forever ($g=\frac\nu2\norm\cdot^2$).

Gradient descent = FB with $f=0$: $\gamma_n\in[\underline\gamma,\overline\gamma]\subset\,]0,2/\nu[$ ⇒ $x_n\wto$ a minimizer. "$2/\nu$" is "averagedness constant $\lt1$".

Proximal point algorithm ($g=0$)

Part V · slide 30SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $f\in\Gz{\H}$. Let $(\gamma_n)_{n\in\N}$ be a sequence in $]0,+\infty[$ such that $\sum_{n\in\N}\gamma_n=+\infty$. We assume that $\Argmin f\neq\emptyset$. Let $x_0\in\H$ and $$(\forall n\in\N)\qquad x_{n+1}=\prox_{\gamma_nf}x_n.$$ Then $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ converges weakly to a minimizer of $f$.

Proximal point algorithm (PPA)
  1. $x_{n+1}=\prox_{\gamma_nf}x_n=\argmin_y\ f(y)+\frac1{2\gamma_n}\norm{y-x_n}^2$, with $\gamma_n\gt0$, $\sum\gamma_n=+\infty$

Cost per iteration: one prox of $f$ — this is only useful when $\prox_{\gamma f}$ is much cheaper than minimizing $f$ (it is the building block of splitting methods rather than a method in itself). Step size: any $\gamma_n\gt0$, however large — there is no Lipschitz constant to respect, because $\prox_{\gamma f}$ is firmly nonexpansive for every $\gamma$.

In one sentence

Forward–Backward with $g=0$: no forward step, only the implicit step, which is unconditionally stable; $\sum\gamma_n=+\infty$ is needed because each prox step moves $x_n$ by a "time" $\gamma_n$ along the subgradient flow, and finite total time may not reach the minimizer.

Why it is true

Every $\prox_{\gamma_nf}$ is firmly nonexpansive with the same fixed point set $\zer\partial f=\Argmin f$, so the Fejér inequality $\norm{x_{n+1}-x}^2\le\norm{x_n-x}^2-\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2$ holds at each minimizer $x$. The condition $\sum\gamma_n=+\infty$ enters when identifying cluster points: since $\frac{x_n-x_{n+1}}{\gamma_n}\in\partial f(x_{n+1})$, the subgradient inequality gives $\gamma_n\big(f(x_{n+1})-\inf f\big)\le\frac12\big(\norm{x_n-x}^2-\norm{x_{n+1}-x}^2\big)$, so the weighted sum $\sum\gamma_n(f(x_{n+1})-\inf f)$ is finite while $\sum\gamma_n$ is infinite, which forces $f(x_n)\to\inf f$ (the values are non-increasing). A weak cluster point then has $f\le\inf f$ by weak lower semicontinuity, hence is a minimizer. The sentence to say out loud: Fejér from firm nonexpansiveness; $\sum\gamma_n=\infty$ drives the values down to the infimum; lsc identifies the limit.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $\gamma_n\gt0$ arbitrary: $\gamma_nf\in\Gz{\H}$, so $\prox_{\gamma_nf}$ is firmly nonexpansive and well defined whatever the size of $\gamma_n$ (theorem). Compare with the explicit step, which needs $\gamma\lt2/\nu$.
  • $\sum\gamma_n=+\infty$ Extra: without it the iterates can stop short. Example: $f=\abs\cdot$ on $\R$, $x_0=1$, $\gamma_n=2^{-n-2}$: $\prox_{\gamma\abs\cdot}$ is soft-thresholding, so $x_{n+1}=x_n-\gamma_n$ as long as $x_n\gt\gamma_n$, and $x_n=1-\sum_{k\lt n}\gamma_k\to\tfrac12\neq0=\argmin\abs\cdot$. The sequence is Fejér-monotone and convergent, but to the wrong point: finite total step length.
  • $\Argmin f\neq\emptyset$: a point to be Fejér-monotone with respect to.
Proof (the slides state this without proof; here is one) T14T13 Extra

1. Fejér monotonicity. Let $x\in\Argmin f=\zer\partial f=\Fix\prox_{\gamma_nf}$ (for every $n$). Since $\prox_{\gamma_nf}$ is $\tfrac12$-averaged and $(\Id-\prox_{\gamma_nf})x=0$, the characterization gives $\norm{x_{n+1}-x}^2+\norm{x_n-x_{n+1}}^2\le\norm{x_n-x}^2$: Fejér monotonicity w.r.t. $\Argmin f$, and $\sum_n\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2\lt+\infty$.

2. Values decrease to the infimum. By the prox characterization, $u_n:=\frac{x_n-x_{n+1}}{\gamma_n}\in\partial f(x_{n+1})$. The subgradient inequality at $x_{n+1}$ in the direction of a minimizer $x$: $f(x)\ge f(x_{n+1})+\ps{u_n}{x-x_{n+1}}$, i.e. $$\gamma_n\big(f(x_{n+1})-\inf f\big)\le\ps{x_n-x_{n+1}}{x_{n+1}-x}=\tfrac12\big(\norm{x_n-x}^2-\norm{x_{n+1}-x}^2-\norm{x_n-x_{n+1}}^2\big)\le\tfrac12\big(\norm{x_n-x}^2-\norm{x_{n+1}-x}^2\big)$$ (the middle identity is $2\ps{a}{b}=\norm{a+b}^2-\norm a^2-\norm b^2$ with $a=x_n-x_{n+1}$, $b=x_{n+1}-x$). Summing, $\sum_n\gamma_n\big(f(x_{n+1})-\inf f\big)\le\tfrac12\norm{x_0-x}^2\lt+\infty$. Moreover the values are non-increasing: by definition of $x_{n+1}$ as a minimizer, $f(x_{n+1})+\frac1{2\gamma_n}\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2\le f(x_n)$. If $f(x_n)-\inf f$ did not tend to $0$, it would stay $\ge\eps\gt0$ for all $n$ (non-increasing), and $\sum_n\gamma_n\eps\lt+\infty$ would contradict $\sum\gamma_n=+\infty$. Hence $f(x_n)\to\inf f$.

3. Cluster points are minimizers. Let $x_{n_k}\wto\hat x$. $f$ is convex and lsc, hence weakly lsc (page 1): $f(\hat x)\le\liminf_kf(x_{n_k})=\inf f$, so $\hat x\in\Argmin f$.

4. Conclusion. Fejér-monotone convergence with $D=\Argmin f$.

Why the implicit step is so robust

$x_{n+1}=\prox_{\gamma f}x_n$ solves $x_{n+1}=x_n-\gamma u_{n+1}$ with $u_{n+1}\in\partial f(x_{n+1})$: it is the backward Euler discretization of the subgradient flow $\dot x\in-\partial f(x)$, and backward Euler is unconditionally stable. The explicit (forward) step $x_n-\gamma\nabla g(x_n)$ is forward Euler, stable only for $\gamma\lt2/\nu$. Forward–Backward mixes the two, each where it is affordable.
