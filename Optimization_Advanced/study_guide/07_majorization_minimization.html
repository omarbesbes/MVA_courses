Majorization–Minimization

When no proximity operator is available, or when $f$ is not even convex, replace $f$ by a surrogate that touches it at the current point and lies above it everywhere; minimizing the surrogate can only decrease $f$. From this one idea: half-quadratic majorants, the MM quadratic algorithm, its subspace (3MG), block-coordinate and parallel versions, and the variable metric forward–backward algorithm.

Overview

Where we are

Pages 3–6 lived in the prox world: every algorithm there was a fixed-point iteration of a nonexpansive map built from proximity operators, and convergence came from Fejér monotonicity. This page is for the problems that world cannot reach: functions with no computable prox, and functions that are not convex at all.

The one principle of pages 1–6 was Fermat's rule → zeros of a sum of operators → fixed points of a nonexpansive composition → Fejér/Krasnosel'skii–Mann convergence. It needs two things: convexity (to make the operators monotone and the maps nonexpansive) and a computable prox for every nonsmooth piece. Part VI replaces both by a single, more primitive idea, T19 majorize then minimize:

The principle of this page

At the current point $x_n$, replace $f$ by a surrogate $h(\cdot,x_n)$ that touches $f$ at $x_n$ and lies above $f$ everywhere (when both are smooth, touching from above forces tangency: $\nabla_xh(x_n,x_n)=\nabla f(x_n)$), and is easy to minimize. Then $f(x_{n+1})\le h(x_{n+1},x_n)\le h(x_n,x_n)=f(x_n)$: the value of $f$ can only go down. No convexity of $f$ is used; no prox of $f$ is needed; all the work goes into building good surrogates.

Most of the deck is about quadratic surrogates $h(x,y)=f(y)+\ps{\nabla f(y)}{x-y}+\tfrac12\norm{x-y}^2_{A(y)}$, whose minimizer is one linear solve away. Seen through them, the familiar algorithms line up as special choices of the curvature operator $A$:

What you lose by leaving the prox world: there is no Fejér monotonicity any more, only the monotone decrease of $f(x_n)$. What you gain: the decrease comes with a sufficient descent inequality $f(x_n)-f(x_{n+1})\ge\mu_1\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2\ge\mu_2\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2$, and summing it (T13 telescoping) forces $\nabla f(x_n)\to0$: when $\nabla f$ is continuous, every cluster point is a stationary point, even for nonconvex $f$. Convexity, when it holds, upgrades "stationary" to "minimizer"; strict convexity upgrades cluster points to convergence of the whole sequence.

Part VI · slides 2–6

The Majorization–Minimization principle

Two definitions and one three-line theorem carry the whole page: what a majorant function is, how majorants combine, and why minimizing them one after the other makes $f$ decrease.

Principle

The Majorization–Minimization principle

Part VI · slides 2–3SlidesKey#
Statement

"When it is successful, the MM algorithm substitutes a simple optimization problem for a difficult optimization problem." — K. Lange.

Majorization–Minimization (MM; also called optimization transfer, iterative majorization, auxiliary function method, surrogate minimization) solves a minimization problem by alternating two steps:

  1. Majorize the criterion at the current iterate with a majorant function;
  2. Minimize the majorant function to define the next iterate.

Building an MM algorithm therefore requires (i) a strategy for building majorant functions and (ii) a strategy for minimizing them.

In one sentence

Never minimize $f$ itself: at each step minimize a simpler function that dominates $f$ and agrees with it at the current point; the difficulty is transferred from the minimization to the design of the surrogate.

Why it makes sense

The surrogate is tangent to $f$ from above at $x_n$. Going downhill on the surrogate necessarily goes downhill on $f$, because $f$ sits below the surrogate everywhere and equals it at the starting point: the surrogate's decrease is a certificate of $f$'s decrease (the sandwich inequality). The two "strategies" of the statement are the two design axes of the whole deck: how tight the majorant is (tighter ⇒ bigger steps, but harder to minimize) and how cheap its minimization is (quadratic ⇒ one linear solve; separable quadratic ⇒ parallel; restricted to a subspace ⇒ a tiny system). Say it out loud: MM trades one hard minimization for many easy ones, and each easy one is guaranteed not to hurt.

Where you have already met MM without the name Extra

Gradient descent with step $1/\beta$ is MM with the descent-lemma parabola; the EM algorithm of statistics is MM with the Jensen majorant of a log-likelihood; iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS, Weiszfeld's algorithm) is MM with half-quadratic majorants. The deck's own examples are the tomography lab (data term + edge-preserving penalty) and the variable metric forward–backward algorithm.

To remember

Majorize, minimize, repeat. Two ingredients: a way to build majorants, a way to minimize them.

Definition

Majorant function of $f$ at $y$

Part VI · slide 4SlidesKey#
Definition

Let $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ where $\H$ is a Hilbert space, and let $y\in\H$. A function $h(\cdot,y):\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ is a majorant function of $f$ at $y$ if $$\begin{cases}(\forall x\in\H)\quad f(x)\le h(x,y),\\ f(y)=h(y,y).\end{cases}$$

In one sentence

Above $f$ everywhere, equal to $f$ at $y$: the graph of $h(\cdot,y)$ rests on the graph of $f$ at the single point $y$.

Why the definition is what it is

Two conditions, two roles. Above everywhere is what makes the decrease of $h$ a certificate for $f$: whatever point you move to, $f$ there is at most $h$ there. Equal at $y$ is what makes the certificate start at the true value $f(y)$: without it, $h(x,y)$ could be far above $f$ and minimizing $h$ would say nothing about $f(x)\le f(y)$. The second condition also forces tangency when things are smooth (insight below), so a majorant is a "parabola resting on the curve", not any parabola above it.

Worked examples
  • The tangent parabola. If $\nabla f$ is $\beta$-Lipschitz, $h(x,y)=f(y)+\ps{\nabla f(y)}{x-y}+\tfrac{\beta}{2}\norm{x-y}^2$ is a majorant at $y$ (descent lemma). This is the picture above.
  • $f$ is its own majorant ($h(\cdot,y)=f$): the MM step is then the exact minimization of $f$. Legal, useless.
  • The useless majorant at the other extreme: $h(x,y)=f(y)+\ind_{\{y\}}(x)$ (value $f(y)$ at $x=y$, $+\infty$ elsewhere) is a majorant at $y$, and its only minimizer is $y$: MM never moves. Between these two extremes lies the art: tight enough to move, loose enough to be minimizable.
  • In $\R^N$, majorants add up coordinatewise: if $f(x)=\sum_i\psi(|x_i|)$ and each $\psi(|\cdot|)$ has the half-quadratic majorant of slide 13, their sum is a separable quadratic majorant of $f$ (sum rule below).
Tangency is automatic Extra

If $f$ and $h(\cdot,y)$ are both differentiable at $y$, then $\nabla_x h(y,y)=\nabla f(y)$. Indeed $x\mapsto h(x,y)-f(x)$ is $\ge0$ with value $0$ at $y$, so $y$ minimizes it and Fermat's rule gives $\nabla_x h(y,y)-\nabla f(y)=0$ (Fermat). So every smooth majorant has the form $f(y)+\ps{\nabla f(y)}{x-y}+r(x)$, with a remainder $r$ vanishing to first order at $y$: $r$ is nonnegative when $f$ is convex, and equal to $\tfrac12\norm{x-y}^2_A$ for the deck's quadratic majorants. (For a nonconvex $f$ it can be negative: with $f(x)=-x^2$, $h(x,y)=f(y)+f'(y)(x-y)-\tfrac12(x-y)^2$ is a majorant, since $h(x,y)-f(x)=\tfrac12(x-y)^2$.) The deck's quadratic majorants are therefore not a special choice of the linear term, only of the curvature.

To remember

Above everywhere, equal at $y$: $f\le h(\cdot,y)$ and $f(y)=h(y,y)$.

Proposition

Sums, products and compositions of majorants

Part VI · slide 5Slides#
Statement

Let $f_1,f_2:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ and $y\in\H$. Let $h_1(\cdot,y)$ be a majorant function of $f_1$ at $y$ and $h_2(\cdot,y)$ a majorant function of $f_2$ at $y$.

  • Sum. $h_1(\cdot,y)+h_2(\cdot,y)$ is a majorant function of $f_1+f_2$ at $y$.
  • Product. If, for all $x\in\H$, $f_1(x)\ge0$ and $f_2(x)\ge0$, then $h_1(\cdot,y)\,h_2(\cdot,y)$ is a majorant function of $f_1f_2$ at $y$.
  • Composition. If $\varphi:\R\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ is an increasing function, then $\varphi\big(h_1(\cdot,y)\big)$ is a majorant function of $\varphi(f_1)$ at $y$.
In one sentence

Majorization is preserved by every operation that preserves "$\le$" and "$=$": adding, multiplying nonnegative quantities, applying an increasing map.

Why it is true

Each rule is an inequality rule you already know, applied pointwise, plus the trivial check that equalities at $y$ stay equalities. The only subtlety is the product: multiplying inequalities requires nonnegativity ("$a\le b$ and $c\le d$ imply $ac\le bd$" needs $a,d\ge0$), which is why the slide restricts to $f_1,f_2\ge0$; the majorants are then automatically nonnegative too, since $h_i\ge f_i\ge0$.

What each hypothesis buys
  • Sum: nothing is needed. The two inequalities add, and so do the two equalities at $y$ (values in $]-\infty,+\infty]$ never produce $\infty-\infty$).
  • Product, $f_1,f_2\ge0$: this is what allows multiplying the two inequalities (and then $h_i\ge f_i\ge0$ automatically). Drop it and the pitfall below shows $(x+x^2)^2$ failing to majorize $x^2$ at $x=-\tfrac12$.
  • Composition, $\varphi$ increasing: it transports "$\le$" from $f_1\le h_1$ to $\varphi(f_1)\le\varphi(h_1)$. A decreasing $\varphi$ (e.g. $\varphi(t)=-t$) turns a majorant into a minorant.
Hint — try it first

Each proof is one line: check "$\le$ everywhere" and "$=$ at $y$". For the product, insert $h_1f_2$ between $f_1f_2$ and $h_1h_2$.

Proof T06 write the inequalities and add (one line each)

Sum. For every $x$, $f_1(x)+f_2(x)\le h_1(x,y)+h_2(x,y)$ by adding the two majorization inequalities, and at $x=y$ both are equalities, so the sum is an equality. (Values $+\infty$ are harmless: nothing is ever $-\infty$.)

Product. For every $x$: $f_1(x)f_2(x)\le h_1(x,y)f_2(x)$ because $f_1\le h_1$ and $f_2(x)\ge0$; then $h_1(x,y)f_2(x)\le h_1(x,y)h_2(x,y)$ because $f_2\le h_2$ and $h_1(x,y)\ge f_1(x)\ge0$. At $y$: $h_1(y,y)h_2(y,y)=f_1(y)f_2(y)$.

Composition. $\varphi$ increasing means $u\le v\Rightarrow\varphi(u)\le\varphi(v)$; apply it to $f_1(x)\le h_1(x,y)$. At $y$: $\varphi(h_1(y,y))=\varphi(f_1(y))$.

□
Pitfall: drop the sign condition and the product rule fails

Take $f_1=f_2=\operatorname{id}$ on $\R$, $y=0$, and the (legitimate) majorants $h_1(x,0)=h_2(x,0)=x+x^2$. Then $f_1f_2(x)=x^2$ but $h_1h_2(x,0)=(x+x^2)^2$, which at $x=-\tfrac12$ equals $\tfrac1{16}\lt\tfrac14=f_1f_2(-\tfrac12)$: not a majorant. Likewise a decreasing $\varphi$ turns majorants into minorants.

Trick: build big majorants from small ones

Every majorant of the deck is assembled with these rules: the tomography criterion $\tfrac12\norm{Hx-y}^2+\lambda\sum_n\psi([Gx]_n)$ is majorized term by term (exact quadratic for the first term, half-quadratic for each $\psi$), then summed. The sum rule is literally "write the two inequalities and add" (T06); the product rule inserts the middle term $h_1f_2$ between $f_1f_2$ and $h_1h_2$. A linear change of variable costs nothing: if $h(\cdot,Gy)$ majorizes $\psi$ at $Gy$, then $h(G\cdot,Gy)$ majorizes $\psi\circ G$ at $y$ (just substitute $x\to Gx$).

To remember

Sum: always. Product: when both are $\ge0$. Composition: with an increasing $\varphi$.

Algorithm

The MM algorithm and the monotone decrease of $f(x_n)$

Part VI · slide 6SlidesKey#
Problem and algorithm

Minimize $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$.

MM algorithm

Let $x_0\in\H$. For $n=0,1,\dots$

  1. choose a majorant function $h(\cdot,x_n)$ of $f$ at $x_n$ (majorization)
  2. $x_{n+1}\in\Argmin_{x\in\H} h(x,x_n)$ (minimization)

Monotone decrease. The sequence $(f(x_n))_{n\in\N}$ is decreasing: $$(\forall n\in\N)\qquad f(x_{n+1})\;\underset{\text{M}}{\le}\;h(x_{n+1},x_n)\;\underset{\text{M}}{\le}\;h(x_n,x_n)=f(x_n).$$

In one sentence

Each step sits between two "M"s: majorization puts $f(x_{n+1})$ under the surrogate, minimization puts the surrogate's new value under its old one, and the old one is $f(x_n)$.

Why it is true

The three relations use exactly the three facts available: "$h\ge f$ everywhere" (evaluated at $x_{n+1}$), "$x_{n+1}$ minimizes $h(\cdot,x_n)$" (compared with the candidate $x_n$), and "$h=f$ at the touching point $x_n$". Nothing else. In particular no convexity, no differentiability, no Lipschitz constant: the decrease is a purely structural consequence of the definition of a majorant. This is why MM applies to nonconvex $f$ at all, and also why it promises so little: a decrease, not a limit.

Proof T19 majorize then minimize

1. Majorization at the new point. $h(\cdot,x_n)$ majorizes $f$, so $f(x_{n+1})\le h(x_{n+1},x_n)$.

2. Minimality of $x_{n+1}$. $x_{n+1}$ minimizes $h(\cdot,x_n)$ over $\H$, and $x_n$ is an admissible competitor: $h(x_{n+1},x_n)\le h(x_n,x_n)$.

3. Touching. $h(x_n,x_n)=f(x_n)$ by the second condition of the definition. Chain the three. □

What is needed, what is not
  • Only the existence of a minimizer of $h(\cdot,x_n)$ (so that step 2 is defined). If $h(\cdot,x_n)$ is a strongly convex quadratic, the minimizer is unique and explicit; this is the case of all the algorithms of this page.
  • No convexity, continuity or differentiability of $f$ is used for the decrease. They enter only when one wants to say something about the limit.

Cost per iteration. One minimization of the surrogate: the whole point of the method is that this is cheap (one linear solve for a quadratic surrogate; one coordinatewise formula for a separable one). What is parallelizable depends only on the surrogate: a separable $h(\cdot,x_n)$ is minimized coordinate by coordinate in parallel (parallel MM).

Pitfall: monotone decrease is not convergence

"$f(x_n)$ decreases" does not give $x_n\to$ anything, nor that the limit of $f(x_n)$ is a stationary value. Example: $f(x)=\tfrac12x^2$ on $\R$, $x_0=1$, with the perfectly legal majorants $h(x,x_n)=f(x_n)+x_n(x-x_n)+\tfrac{\mu_n}{2}(x-x_n)^2$ where $\mu_n=2^{\,n+1}\ge1$. The MM step is $x_{n+1}=x_n(1-2^{-n-1})$, so $x_n\to\prod_{k\ge1}(1-2^{-k})\approx0.289\ne0$: $f(x_n)$ decreases to a value that is not the minimum, because the majorants become arbitrarily loose and the steps die out. The convergence theorems of this page rule this out with a uniform curvature bound $A(x_n)\preccurlyeq\bar\nu\,\Id$, which turns the plain decrease into a sufficient descent $f(x_n)-f(x_{n+1})\ge\mu_2\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2$ (mm-quadratic-convergence).

To remember

$f(x_{n+1})\le h(x_{n+1},x_n)\le h(x_n,x_n)=f(x_n)$: majorize, minimize, touch. Decrease is free; convergence has to be paid for with uniform curvature bounds.

Majorization techniques

Four generic ways to manufacture a majorant: the tangent of a concave function (from above!), the descent-lemma parabola of a Lipschitz gradient, a global Hessian bound, and Jensen's inequality to push a convex function inside a weighted sum. Then the five exercise inequalities, which are these four techniques in disguise.

Proposition

Tangent majorant of a concave function

Part VI · slide 7SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $f:\H\to[-\infty,+\infty[$ be a concave function. Let $y\in\H$ and $(-t)\in\partial(-f)(y)$. A majorant function for $f$ at $y$ is $$(\forall x\in\H)\qquad h(x,y)=f(y)+\ps{t}{x-y}.$$

In one sentence

A concave function lies below all its tangent (supporting) hyperplanes: the tangent at $y$ is an affine majorant.

Why it is true

The subgradient inequality of the convex function $-f$ says that $-f$ lies above its supporting hyperplane at $y$; multiply by $-1$ and $f$ lies below the mirrored hyperplane. The vector $t$ (a "supergradient" of $f$: $t=\nabla f(y)$ when $f$ is differentiable) is the slope of that hyperplane. What makes this useful is not that the majorant is affine, which is too loose to be minimized alone, but that it is linear in $x$: a concave piece of a criterion can be replaced by a linear term, which then costs nothing inside a quadratic surrogate. This is the engine behind the half-quadratic theorem and behind exercises 1, 2 and 4.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $f$ concave, with values in $[-\infty,+\infty[$: so that $-f$ is a convex function with values in $]-\infty,+\infty]$, the setting of the subdifferential.
  • $(-t)\in\partial(-f)(y)$: a subgradient of $-f$ at $y$ must exist (true for instance when $f$ is finite and differentiable at $y$, with $t=\nabla f(y)$; in general $y$ must be in the domain of $\partial(-f)$).
Hint — try it first

Write the subgradient inequality for $-f$ at $y$ with the subgradient $-t$, and change signs.

Proof T20 concavity gives tangent majorants

1. Subgradient inequality for $-f$. By definition of $\partial(-f)(y)\ni-t$: for every $x\in\H$, $-f(x)\ge-f(y)+\ps{-t}{x-y}$.

2. Change signs. Multiply by $-1$: $f(x)\le f(y)+\ps{t}{x-y}=h(x,y)$.

3. Touching. $h(y,y)=f(y)$ trivially. □

Trick (T20): "concave ⇒ replace by its tangent"

Whenever a criterion contains a concave piece $g$ (in $x$, or in a function of $x$ such as $x^2$ in the half-quadratic theorem), majorize that piece by $g(y)+\ps{\nabla g(y)}{x-y}$ and keep the rest. If the rest is convex, the surrogate is convex; if the rest is quadratic, the surrogate is quadratic. In DC programming (difference of convex functions $f=u-v$) this is the whole algorithm: linearize $-v$ at $x_n$, minimize $u-\text{(affine)}$ — the "convex–concave procedure" Extra.

Pitfall: tangent above or below?

For a convex $g$ the tangent is a minorant (gradient inequality): $g(x)\ge g(y)+\ps{\nabla g(y)}{x-y}$. For a concave $f$ it is a majorant. Same picture, mirrored. Mixing the two is the classic sign error in exercise 4 ($-\norm{\cdot}$ is concave, $\norm{\cdot}$ is convex).

To remember

Concave: under its tangents. $f(x)\le f(y)+\ps{t}{x-y}$ with $-t\in\partial(-f)(y)$.

Proposition

Quadratic majorant of a $\beta$-Lipschitz differentiable function

Part VI · slide 7SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be a $\beta$-Lipschitz differentiable function on $\H$. Then, for every $y\in\H$ and every $\mu\in[\beta,+\infty[$, a majorant function for $f$ at $y$ is $$(\forall x\in\H)\qquad h(x,y)=f(y)+\ps{\nabla f(y)}{x-y}+\frac{\mu}{2}\norm{x-y}^2.$$

In one sentence

The descent lemma of page 4 is a majorization statement: the parabola of curvature $\beta$ tangent at $y$ lies above $f$, and so does any steeper one.

Why it is true

A $\beta$-Lipschitz gradient cannot grow faster than $\beta$ per unit length, so along the segment from $y$ to $x$ the slope of $f$ exceeds its initial value $\ps{\nabla f(y)}{x-y}$ by at most $\beta t\norm{x-y}^2$ at time $t$; integrating over $t\in[0,1]$ gives the extra $\tfrac\beta2\norm{x-y}^2$. Raising $\beta$ to $\mu\ge\beta$ only lifts the parabola. The MM step with this majorant is $x_{n+1}=x_n-\mu^{-1}\nabla f(x_n)$: gradient descent with step $1/\mu\le1/\beta$ is an MM algorithm.

What each hypothesis buys
  • "Differentiable on $\H$" with values in $]-\infty,+\infty]$: $f$ is in fact finite everywhere (a differentiable function is finite at every point).
  • $\nabla f$ $\beta$-Lipschitz: the integral bound of the proof. Without it a parabola of fixed curvature need not dominate $f$ (think of $f(x)=x^4$: at any $y$, $x^4$ eventually exceeds every parabola, so there is no quadratic majorant at all). Strictly, only a one-sided curvature bound is used (for a twice differentiable $f$, $\nabla^2f\preccurlyeq\beta\Id$, as in the Hessian-bound card: $-x^4$ has no Lipschitz gradient, yet every tangent parabola with curvature $\ge0$ majorizes it); the Lipschitz assumption is the slide's convenient sufficient condition.
  • $\mu\ge\beta$: the majorant must be at least as curved as the descent-lemma bound; $\mu\lt\beta$ gives a parabola that $f$ can cross.
Hint — try it first

Integrate $t\mapsto\frac{d}{dt}f(y+t(x-y))=\ps{\nabla f(y+t(x-y))}{x-y}$ on $[0,1]$ and bound $\ps{\nabla f(y+t(x-y))-\nabla f(y)}{x-y}$ with Cauchy–Schwarz and the Lipschitz constant.

Proof T05 integrate along the segment

1. Restrict to the segment. Let $\varphi(t)=f(y+t(x-y))$, $t\in[0,1]$; $\varphi$ is differentiable with $\varphi'(t)=\ps{\nabla f(y+t(x-y))}{x-y}$, and $\varphi'$ is continuous (because $\nabla f$ is Lipschitz, hence continuous), so $f(x)-f(y)=\varphi(1)-\varphi(0)=\int_0^1\varphi'(t)\,dt$.

2. Compare the slope with its initial value. $\varphi'(t)-\varphi'(0)=\ps{\nabla f(y+t(x-y))-\nabla f(y)}{x-y}\le\norm{\nabla f(y+t(x-y))-\nabla f(y)}\,\norm{x-y}\le\beta t\norm{x-y}^2$ (Cauchy–Schwarz, then the Lipschitz property with $\norm{t(x-y)}=t\norm{x-y}$).

3. Integrate. $f(x)-f(y)=\varphi'(0)+\int_0^1(\varphi'(t)-\varphi'(0))\,dt\le\ps{\nabla f(y)}{x-y}+\beta\norm{x-y}^2\int_0^1t\,dt=\ps{\nabla f(y)}{x-y}+\tfrac\beta2\norm{x-y}^2$. This is the descent lemma.

4. Loosen to $\mu$. Since $\mu\ge\beta$, $\tfrac\beta2\norm{x-y}^2\le\tfrac\mu2\norm{x-y}^2$, hence $f(x)\le h(x,y)$; and $h(y,y)=f(y)$. □

Pitfall: $1/\beta$ versus $2/\beta$

As a majorant, the parabola needs $\mu\ge\beta$, i.e. gradient steps $\le1/\beta$. Gradient descent is known to converge for steps up to $2/\beta$ (page 4). There is no contradiction: the MM quadratic algorithm recovers the full range through its relaxation parameter $\theta_n\in\,]0,2[$ — a step $\theta_n/\beta$ — and its proof shows exactly where $\theta_n\lt2$ is used (mm-quadratic-convergence).

Proposition

Quadratic majorant from a Hessian bound $A\succcurlyeq\nabla^2f(x)$

Part VI · slide 8SlidesLab#
Statement

Let $f:\R^N\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be a twice differentiable function on $\R^N$ with Hessian $\nabla^2f$. Let $A\in\R^{N\times N}$ be a positive semidefinite matrix such that, for every $x\in\R^N$, $A-\nabla^2f(x)$ is positive semidefinite. Then, for every $y\in\R^N$, a majorant function for $f$ at $y$ is $$(\forall x\in\R^N)\qquad h(x,y)=f(y)+\ps{\nabla f(y)}{x-y}+\tfrac12\underbrace{\ps{x-y}{A(x-y)}}_{\norm{x-y}^2_A}.$$

In one sentence

If one fixed quadratic form dominates the curvature of $f$ in every direction at every point, the parabola with that curvature, tangent at $y$, lies above $f$.

Why it is true

Second-order Taylor along the segment $[y,x]$: $f(x)=f(y)+\ps{\nabla f(y)}{x-y}+\tfrac12\ps{x-y}{\nabla^2f(\xi)(x-y)}$ for some $\xi$ on the segment, and the last term is at most $\tfrac12\ps{x-y}{A(x-y)}$ by the Hessian bound. Compared with the Lipschitz majorant this is the anisotropic version: $A=\beta\Id$ gives back slide 7 (in finite dimension, $\nabla^2f\preccurlyeq\beta\Id$ everywhere is the same as $\nabla f$ being $\beta$-Lipschitz for a convex $f$), but a non-scalar $A$ can follow the geometry of $f$, e.g. $A=H^\top H$ for a least-squares term, with exactly the right curvature in every direction.

Note

The slides state this without proof; here is a short one. The notation $\norm{x-y}^2_A:=\ps{x-y}{A(x-y)}$ introduced on this slide is used for the rest of the deck.

Hint — try it first

Proof T05 restrict to the segment

1. Restrict to the segment. Let $d=x-y$ and $\varphi(t)=f(y+td)$ for $t\in[0,1]$. Since $f$ is twice differentiable, $\varphi$ is twice differentiable with $\varphi'(t)=\ps{\nabla f(y+td)}{d}$ (continuous, since $\nabla f$ is differentiable hence continuous) and $\varphi''(t)=\ps{d}{\nabla^2f(y+td)\,d}$.

2. Taylor–Lagrange in one variable. There exists $\xi\in\,]0,1[$ with $\varphi(1)=\varphi(0)+\varphi'(0)+\tfrac12\varphi''(\xi)$, i.e. $f(x)=f(y)+\ps{\nabla f(y)}{x-y}+\tfrac12\ps{d}{\nabla^2f(y+\xi d)\,d}$.

3. Hessian bound. $A-\nabla^2f(y+\xi d)$ is positive semidefinite, so $\ps{d}{\nabla^2f(y+\xi d)\,d}\le\ps{d}{Ad}=\norm{x-y}^2_A$. Hence $f(x)\le h(x,y)$, and $h(y,y)=f(y)$. □

What each hypothesis buys
  • Twice differentiable: Taylor's formula to second order along every segment.
  • $A\succcurlyeq\nabla^2f(x)$ for every $x$, not only at $y$: the point $\xi$ of the proof is unknown. $A$ positive semidefinite then makes $h(\cdot,y)$ a convex quadratic (for the algorithms one wants more: $A$ strongly positive, so that the minimizer exists and is unique).
Pitfall: Newton's method is not an MM method

Choosing $A=\nabla^2f(y)$ (Newton) satisfies the hypothesis only if the Hessian at $y$ dominates the Hessian everywhere, which is false in general: for $f(x)=-\cos x$ at $y=\pi$, $\nabla^2f(\pi)=-1$ is not even positive. Newton steps can increase $f$; MM steps cannot. The lab's $A(x)=H^\top H+\lambda G^\top\operatorname{Diag}(\omega([Gx]_n))G$ is not obtained from this proposition either (it depends on $x$): it comes from the half-quadratic theorem, which gives a local curvature that is nevertheless a global majorant. So MM quadratic is "Newton with a majorizing curvature": the price of a dominating $A(x_n)$ is a more cautious step (when $\nabla^2f(x_n)\succ0$, $A(x_n)\succcurlyeq\nabla^2f(x_n)$ gives $\ps{\nabla f(x_n)}{A(x_n)^{-1}\nabla f(x_n)}\le\ps{\nabla f(x_n)}{\nabla^2f(x_n)^{-1}\nabla f(x_n)}$, a smaller predicted decrease; in 1-D, a shorter step), the gain is a guaranteed decrease, which Newton does not have.

Examples Lab

Proposition

Jensen's inequality

Part VI · slide 8SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $\psi:\R\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be a convex function and let $\omega=(\omega^{(i)})_{1\le i\le N}\in[0,+\infty[^N$ be such that $\sum_{i=1}^N\omega^{(i)}=1$. Then $$\big(\forall(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(N)})\big)\qquad\psi\Big(\sum_{i=1}^N\omega^{(i)}x^{(i)}\Big)\le\sum_{i=1}^N\omega^{(i)}\psi\big(x^{(i)}\big).$$

In one sentence

A convex function of an average is at most the average of the function: "the convex function goes inside the weighted sum, the inequality goes the right way".

Why it is true

Convexity is the $N=2$ case by definition ($\psi(\omega x+(1-\omega)x')\le\omega\psi(x)+(1-\omega)\psi(x')$). For more points, peel off one weight at a time: a weighted average of $N$ points is a two-point average between the last point and the weighted average of the first $N-1$. Geometrically: the epigraph is convex, so the convex combination of $N$ points on the graph lies in the epigraph, i.e. above the graph.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $\psi$ convex: the claim for $N=2$ is convexity. For a concave $\psi$ the inequality reverses: $\log\big(\sum_i\omega^{(i)}x^{(i)}\big)\ge\sum_i\omega^{(i)}\log x^{(i)}$.
  • $\omega^{(i)}\ge0$: negative weights break it. With $\psi=(\cdot)^2$, $\omega=(2,-1)$ (sum $1$) and $x=(0,1)$: $\psi(2\cdot0-1\cdot1)=1\gt-1=2\psi(0)-\psi(1)$.
  • $\sum_i\omega^{(i)}=1$: with a total mass $s\ne1$ it can fail. With $\psi=(\cdot)^2$, $\omega=(1,1)$ and $x=(1,1)$: $\psi(2)=4\gt2=\psi(1)+\psi(1)$. Renormalize first (pitfall below).
Note

The slide writes the points as $(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(N)})\in\H^N$ while $\psi$ is defined on $\R$; read $x^{(i)}\in\R$ (or $\psi$ convex on $\H$ — the statement and the proof are identical). The slides give no proof; here is the induction. Extra

Hint — try it first

Induction on $N$; if $\omega^{(N)}=1$ there is nothing to prove, otherwise write $\sum_{i\le N}\omega^{(i)}x^{(i)}=(1-\omega^{(N)})\bar x+\omega^{(N)}x^{(N)}$ with $\bar x$ a weighted average of the first $N-1$ points.

Proof T21 the inequality behind the diagonal bound (induction on $N$: peel off one weight)

1. $N=1$ and $N=2$. $N=1$: $\omega^{(1)}=1$, equality. $N=2$: this is the definition of convexity of $\psi$ with $\omega^{(1)}\in[0,1]$, $\omega^{(2)}=1-\omega^{(1)}$ (if a value is $+\infty$, the inequality holds trivially).

2. Induction step. Assume the inequality for $N-1$ points and take $N$ points with weights $\omega$. If $\omega^{(N)}=1$ all other weights vanish and both sides equal $\psi(x^{(N)})$. Otherwise set $\lambda=1-\omega^{(N)}\in\,]0,1]$ and $\tilde\omega^{(i)}=\omega^{(i)}/\lambda$ for $i\le N-1$: these are nonnegative and sum to $1$. Then $\sum_{i=1}^N\omega^{(i)}x^{(i)}=\lambda\bar x+(1-\lambda)x^{(N)}$ with $\bar x=\sum_{i\le N-1}\tilde\omega^{(i)}x^{(i)}$.

3. Two applications. Convexity: $\psi(\lambda\bar x+(1-\lambda)x^{(N)})\le\lambda\psi(\bar x)+(1-\lambda)\psi(x^{(N)})$. Induction hypothesis: $\psi(\bar x)\le\sum_{i\le N-1}\tilde\omega^{(i)}\psi(x^{(i)})$. Multiply the latter by $\lambda$ and substitute: the right-hand side becomes $\sum_{i\le N-1}\omega^{(i)}\psi(x^{(i)})+\omega^{(N)}\psi(x^{(N)})$. □

Trick (T21): separate a coupled term by Jensen

A square of a sum, $(\sum_ia_i)^2$, couples all the $a_i$. Write $\sum_ia_i=\sum_i\omega_i\,(a_i/\omega_i)$ with weights $\omega_i\ge0$ summing to one, and Jensen for the convex square gives $(\sum_ia_i)^2\le\sum_i\omega_i(a_i/\omega_i)^2=\sum_ia_i^2/\omega_i$: a sum of separate squares, with equality when all $a_i/\omega_i$ are equal. Choosing $\omega_i$ proportional to $|H_{mi}|$ gives the diagonal majorant $H^\top H\preccurlyeq\operatorname{Diag}(|H|^\top|H|\mathbf1)$ of the lab (parallel MM). The same move with $\psi=\exp$ is exercise 3, and with $\psi=-\log$ it is the E-step of EM Extra.

Pitfall

The weights must be nonnegative and sum to one. If they only sum to $s\ne1$, renormalize first: $\psi(\sum_i\omega_ix_i)=\psi(s\sum_i(\omega_i/s)x_i)\le\sum_i(\omega_i/s)\psi(sx_i)$. Forgetting the renormalization is the usual way to get a "diagonal majorant" that is not one.

To remember

$\psi(\sum\omega_ix_i)\le\sum\omega_i\psi(x_i)$ for convex $\psi$, $\omega_i\ge0$, $\sum\omega_i=1$. Use it to push a convex function inside a sum (T21).

Prove the following majorizing properties:

  1. $\big(\forall(x,y)\in(\R_+)^2\big)(\forall q\in\,]0,1[)\qquad x^q\le q\,y^{q-1}x+(1-q)y^q$
  2. $\big(\forall(x,y)\in(\R_+^*)^2\big)\qquad\log x\le\dfrac{x}{y}+\log y-1$
  3. $(\forall x\in\R^N)\qquad\exp\Big(\dfrac1N\sum_{i=1}^Nx^{(i)}\Big)\le\dfrac1N\sum_{i=1}^Ne^{x^{(i)}}$
  4. $(\forall x\in\R^N)(\forall y\in\R^N\setminus\{0\})\qquad-\norm{x}\le-\dfrac{\ps{x}{y}}{\norm{y}}$
  5. $\big(\forall(x,z)\in\R^2\big)\big(\forall(y,t)\in(\R_+^*)^2\big)\qquad 2xz\le\dfrac{x^2t}{y}+\dfrac{z^2y}{t}$

Reflex: in exercises 1, 2, 4 and 5 the right-hand side is a majorant at the current point $y$ (or $(y,t)$): it touches the left-hand side at $x=y$ (for 5: at $(x,z)=(y,t)$, and in fact on the whole line $x/y=z/t$, i.e. at every real multiple of $(y,t)$); exercise 3 is Jensen, with equality when all the $x^{(i)}$ coincide. So ask "which function is concave or convex here, and what is the tangent at $y$?" — the slide's hints are exactly that: concavity of $x^q$, of $\log$, of $-\norm\cdot$; Jensen for $\exp$; a square for the last one.

Solution T20 tangent majorants T21 Jensen

1. $x^q$, $q\in\,]0,1[$. $g(x)=x^q$ is concave on $\R_+$ ($g''(x)=q(q-1)x^{q-2}\lt0$ on $\R_+^*$, and $g$ is continuous at $0$), differentiable on $\R_+^*$ with $g'(y)=qy^{q-1}$. Its tangent at $y\gt0$ is a majorant (concave ⇒ under its tangents): $x^q\le y^q+qy^{q-1}(x-y)=qy^{q-1}x+(1-q)y^q$ for every $x\ge0$. Equality iff $x=y$.

2. $\log$. $\log$ is concave on $\R_+^*$ with derivative $1/y$ at $y$: $\log x\le\log y+\dfrac{x-y}{y}=\dfrac xy+\log y-1$.

3. $\exp$. Jensen's inequality (slide 8) for the convex function $\exp$ with the weights $\omega^{(i)}=1/N$ (nonnegative, summing to $1$): $\exp\big(\sum_i\tfrac1Nx^{(i)}\big)\le\sum_i\tfrac1N\exp(x^{(i)})$.

4. $-\norm\cdot$. $\norm\cdot$ is convex (triangle inequality + positive homogeneity), so $g=-\norm\cdot$ is concave; it is differentiable at $y\ne0$ with $\nabla g(y)=-y/\norm y$ (differentiate $\sqrt{\ps yy}$). Tangent majorant at $y$: $-\norm x\le-\norm y-\ps{y/\norm y}{x-y}=-\norm y-\dfrac{\ps xy}{\norm y}+\norm y=-\dfrac{\ps xy}{\norm y}$. (Equivalently: this is Cauchy–Schwarz $\ps xy\le\norm x\norm y$, read as a majorization statement.)

5. $2xz$. For $y,t\gt0$: $0\le\Big(\dfrac xy-\dfrac zt\Big)^2=\dfrac{x^2}{y^2}-\dfrac{2xz}{yt}+\dfrac{z^2}{t^2}$. Multiply by $yt\gt0$: $0\le\dfrac{x^2t}{y}-2xz+\dfrac{z^2y}{t}$, which is the claim. Equality iff $x/y=z/t$; in particular at $(x,z)=(y,t)$ (or any positive multiple), where both sides equal $2yt$: the right-hand side is a majorant of the product $2xz$ at the current point $(y,t)$. □

What each inequality is for in practice Extra
  • 1 ($x^q$): sparsity penalties $\sum_i|u_i|^q$ with $q\lt1$. With $x=|u_i|$ the majorant is a weighted $\ell^1$ term (iteratively reweighted $\ell^1$); with $x=u_i^2$ and exponent $q/2$ it is a weighted $\ell^2$ term $\tfrac q2|y_i|^{q-2}u_i^2+\text{const}$: the FOCUSS / IRLS algorithms.
  • 2 ($\log$): Poisson likelihoods and entropy terms contain $\log$; replacing $\log$ by its tangent turns them into linear terms (Richardson–Lucy / EM for Poisson data use this and Jensen).
  • 3 ($\exp$): separability. Jensen pushes a convex function inside a sum, so a coupled term becomes a sum of one-dimensional ones (log-sum-exp in logistic regression; the diagonal majorant of parallel MM). With $x^{(i)}=\log a_i$ it is the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality.
  • 4 ($-\norm\cdot$): maximizing a norm (or minimizing a DC criterion containing $-\norm{\cdot}$): the concave part is replaced by a linear one, $-\ps{x}{y_n}/\norm{y_n}$, at each iteration.
  • 5 ($2xz$): decoupling products. A bilinear term $xz$ (cross terms of a square, products of two unknowns in matrix factorization) is majorized by separate quadratics in $x$ and in $z$ with weights $t/y$ and $y/t$ taken from the current iterate; this is the "quadratic-over-linear" trick behind multiplicative NMF updates and another route to the diagonal bound of T21.

Half-quadratic majorants and the table of potentials

The workhorse of edge-preserving image reconstruction: for an even function $\psi(|x|)$ whose profile in $x^2$ is concave, the parabola tangent at $y$ with curvature $\omega(|y|)=\dot\psi(|y|)/|y|$ is a majorant. One theorem, one table of nine potentials, and the whole tomography lab follows.

Theorem

Half-quadratic majorant of $f=\psi(|\cdot|)$ when $\psi(\sqrt\cdot)$ is concave

Part VI · slides 13–14SlidesKeyLab#
Statement

Let $f$ be defined as $(\forall x\in\R)\ f(x)=\psi(|x|)$, where

  1. $\psi$ is differentiable on $]0,+\infty[$,
  2. $\psi(\sqrt\cdot)$ is concave on $]0,+\infty[$,
  3. $(\forall x\in[0,+\infty[)\ \dot\psi(x)\ge0$,
  4. $\displaystyle\lim_{\substack{x\to0\\x\gt0}}\Big(\omega(x):=\frac{\dot\psi(x)}{x}\Big)\in\R$.

Then, for all $y\in\R$, $$(\forall x\in\R)\qquad f(x)\le f(y)+\dot f(y)(x-y)+\tfrac12\,\omega(|y|)\,(x-y)^2.$$

In one sentence

Concavity of $\psi$ as a function of $x^2$ gives a tangent majorant in the variable $x^2$; rewriting $x^2-y^2$ around $y$ turns it into a parabola in $x$, tangent at $y$, with curvature $\omega(|y|)$.

Why it is true

Write $f(x)=\varphi(x^2)$ with $\varphi=\psi(\sqrt\cdot)$. Assumption (ii) says $\varphi$ is concave, so $\varphi(u)\le\varphi(v)+\dot\varphi(v)(u-v)$: in the variable $u=x^2$, the tangent at $v=y^2$ lies above (T20). The slope is $\dot\varphi(y^2)=\dot\psi(|y|)/(2|y|)=\tfrac12\omega(|y|)$. The whole theorem is then the algebraic identity $x^2-y^2=(x-y)^2+2y(x-y)$: the linear-in-$x^2$ majorant becomes a quadratic-in-$x$ one, whose linear part $y\,\omega(|y|)(x-y)=\dot f(y)(x-y)$ is forced by tangency and whose curvature is $\omega(|y|)$. Assumption (iii) makes that curvature nonnegative (a convex parabola, hence minimizable), and (iv) keeps it finite when $y\to0$. Say it out loud: a concave profile in $x^2$ is the same as a parabola in $x$ resting on the curve at $y$ with curvature $\dot\psi(|y|)/|y|$.

What each hypothesis buys
  • (i) $\psi$ differentiable on $]0,+\infty[$: $\omega$ and $\dot f$ exist away from $0$.
  • (ii) $\psi(\sqrt\cdot)$ concave: the engine. For a differentiable $\psi$ it is equivalent to $\omega$ non-increasing on $]0,+\infty[$ (because $\dot\varphi(v)=\tfrac12\omega(\sqrt v)$ and a differentiable function is concave iff its derivative is non-increasing). This is the quickest test to run on the table below.
  • (iii) $\dot\psi\ge0$ on $[0,+\infty[$: $\omega\ge0$, so the majorant is a convex parabola. It also says $\psi$ is non-decreasing: the penalty grows with $|x|$.
  • (iv) $\omega$ has a finite limit at $0^+$: the curvature does not blow up at the kink of $|\cdot|$. This is what fails for $\psi(u)=u$ ($\omega(u)=1/u$) and for $\psi(u)=u^q$, $q\lt1$: the majorant degenerates as $y\to0$ — the "numerical issues at nondifferentiability points" of slide 33. It also gives $\dot\psi(x)=x\,\omega(x)\to0$, so $f$ is differentiable at $0$ with $\dot f(0)=0$, which is how $\dot f(y)$ and $\omega(|y|)$ must be read at $y=0$ (with $\omega(0)$ the limit).

Proof T20 concavity + the $x^2-y^2$ identity

1. The concave profile. By (ii), $\varphi=\psi(\sqrt\cdot)$ is concave on $]0,+\infty[$, and by (i) it is differentiable there with, by the chain rule, $\dot\varphi(v)=\dfrac{\dot\psi(\sqrt v)}{2\sqrt v}$, which is $\ge0$ by (iii). A differentiable concave function lies below its tangents: for all $(u,v)\in\,]0,+\infty[^2$, $\varphi(u)\le\varphi(v)+(u-v)\dot\varphi(v)$.

2. Substitute $u=x^2$, $v=y^2$ for $(x,y)\in(\R^*)^2$. Since $\dot\varphi(y^2)=\dot\psi(|y|)/(2|y|)=\tfrac12\omega(|y|)$: $$\varphi(x^2)\le\varphi(y^2)+\tfrac12(x^2-y^2)\,\omega(|y|).$$

3. The identity. $x^2-y^2=(x-y)^2+2y(x-y)$ (expand the right-hand side). Hence $\tfrac12(x^2-y^2)\omega(|y|)=y\,\omega(|y|)(x-y)+\tfrac12\omega(|y|)(x-y)^2$, and $y\,\omega(|y|)=y\dot\psi(|y|)/|y|=\operatorname{sign}(y)\dot\psi(|y|)=\dot f(y)$ (chain rule for $f=\psi\circ|\cdot|$ at $y\ne0$). So $$f(x)\le f(y)+\operatorname{sign}(y)\dot\psi(|y|)(x-y)+\tfrac12\omega(|y|)(x-y)^2,$$ which is the claim for $x\ne0$ and $y\ne0$.

4. The points $x=0$ and/or $y=0$, by continuity. Here we use that $\psi$ is continuous at $0$, i.e. $\psi(0)=\lim_{x\to0^+}\psi(x)$. This is implicit in the slides (assumption (iii) even writes $\dot\psi(0)$), and the limit exists because $\dot\psi(x)=x\,\omega(x)$ stays bounded near $0^+$ by (iv), so $\psi$ is Lipschitz near $0^+$. Then $f=\psi(|\cdot|)$ is continuous on $\R$. For a fixed $y\ne0$, both sides are continuous in $x$, so the inequality passes to $x=0$. For $y\to0$ with $x$ fixed: $f(y)\to f(0)$, $\omega(|y|)\to\omega(0)\in\R$ by (iv), and $\dot f(y)=\operatorname{sign}(y)\,|y|\,\omega(|y|)\to0=\dot f(0)$. So the right-hand side converges to $f(0)+\tfrac12\omega(0)x^2$, and the inequality holds at $y=0$ too (with $\omega(0)$ read as the limit). □

Why "half-quadratic", and the extension to $\R^N$

The surrogate $h(x,y)=f(y)+\dot f(y)(x-y)+\tfrac12\omega(|y|)(x-y)^2$ is quadratic in $x$ but its curvature depends on $y$: as a function of the pair $(x,y)$ it is "half" quadratic. Steps 2–3 of the proof also show that it can be written $h(x,y)=f(y)+\tfrac12\omega(|y|)(x^2-y^2)$: an even parabola in $x$, centred at $0$ whatever $y$; the information about $y$ is entirely in the weight $\omega(|y|)$ (the name comes from the Geman–Reynolds / Geman–Yang augmented formulations of the 1990s, of which this is the MM reading Extra). In $\R^N$, for $r(x)=\sum_n\psi(|[Gx]_n|)$, apply the theorem to each term at $[Gy]_n$ and sum (sum rule + substitution $x\to Gx$): $$r(x)\le r(y)+\ps{\nabla r(y)}{x-y}+\tfrac12\norm{x-y}^2_{A_r(y)},\qquad A_r(y)=G^\top\operatorname{Diag}\big(\omega(|[Gy]_n|)\big)G.$$ Adding the exact quadratic of a least-squares term gives the lab's $A(x)=H^\top H+\lambda A_r(x)$.

Recipe (T20): the half-quadratic majorant of a new potential

(1) Compute $\dot\psi$ on $]0,+\infty[$ and check $\dot\psi\ge0$ (iii). (2) Set $\omega(u)=\dot\psi(u)/u$; check that $\omega$ is non-increasing on $]0,+\infty[$ (this is (ii)) and has a finite limit at $0^+$ (iv). (3) Write $h(x,y)=\psi(|y|)+\tfrac12\omega(|y|)(x^2-y^2)$, or equivalently $\psi(|y|)+\operatorname{sign}(y)\dot\psi(|y|)(x-y)+\tfrac12\omega(|y|)(x-y)^2$. (4) In $\R^N$, for $r(x)=\sum_n\psi(|[Gx]_n|)$, the curvature is $A_r(y)=G^\top\operatorname{Diag}\big(\omega(|[Gy]_n|)\big)G$. Example (row 9, Cauchy): $\psi(u)=\log(1+u^2/\delta^2)$, $\dot\psi(u)=2u/(\delta^2+u^2)\ge0$, $\omega(u)=2/(\delta^2+u^2)$, non-increasing, $\omega(0)=2/\delta^2$ (T20 concavity gives tangent majorants).

The MM quadratic algorithm

With a quadratic majorant the MM step is explicit: a preconditioned gradient step whose preconditioner is the majorant's curvature. The convergence proof is the model for every other proof of the page: sufficient descent, telescoping, compactness.

Algorithm

MM quadratic algorithm: $x_{n+1}=x_n-\theta_nA(x_n)^{-1}\nabla f(x_n)$

Part VI · slide 17SlidesKeyLab#
Problem, assumption, algorithm

Problem: minimization of a differentiable function $f:\H\to\R$.

Assumption: for every $y\in\H$, there exists a strongly positive self-adjoint operator $A(y)$ such that the quadratic function $$(\forall x\in\H)\qquad h(x,y)=f(y)+\ps{\nabla f(y)}{x-y}+\tfrac12\norm{x-y}^2_{A(y)}$$ is a majorant function of $f$ at $y$.

MM quadratic algorithm

Let $x_0\in\H$. For $n=0,1,\dots$

  1. $x_{n+1}=x_n-\theta_n\,A(x_n)^{-1}\nabla f(x_n)$, with $\theta_n\in\,]0,2[$ (solve $A(x_n)p_n=\nabla f(x_n)$, then step $-\theta_np_n$)

$(\theta_n)_n$ acts as a stepsize parameter. For $\theta_n\equiv1$ we recover the basic MM algorithm.

In one sentence

Minimizing the quadratic majorant is one linear solve; the result is a gradient step preconditioned by the majorant's curvature, and $\theta_n$ lets you under- or over-relax it.

Why the update is what it is

$h(\cdot,x_n)$ is a strongly convex quadratic, so its unique minimizer is where its gradient vanishes: $\nabla_xh(x,x_n)=\nabla f(x_n)+A(x_n)(x-x_n)=0$, i.e. $x=x_n-A(x_n)^{-1}\nabla f(x_n)$ ("strongly positive" means $\ps{x}{A(y)x}\ge\nu\norm x^2$ for some $\nu\gt0$, which makes $A(y)$ invertible with $\norm{A(y)^{-1}}\le1/\nu$). The relaxed step $\theta_n$ moves along the same direction by a fraction $\theta_n$ of the MM step. Along that direction $h$ is a parabola with minimum at $\theta=1$ and, by symmetry, back to the value $f(x_n)$ at $\theta=2$: this is why the admissible range is $]0,2[$ and not $]0,1]$, and the convergence proof uses exactly this (a coefficient $\theta_n^{-1}-\tfrac12\gt0$).

Cost per iteration. One gradient, one linear system $A(x_n)p=\nabla f(x_n)$ (conjugate gradient or bicg in the lab; trivial when $A(x_n)$ is diagonal). Parallelizable: the products $A(x_n)v$ (sparse matrix–vector products); the solve itself only when $A(x_n)$ is diagonal or block-diagonal (parallel MM).

Special cases: where the familiar algorithms sit
  • $A(y)\equiv\mu\Id$ with $\mu\ge\beta$ (Lipschitz majorant): $x_{n+1}=x_n-(\theta_n/\mu)\nabla f(x_n)$, gradient descent with step $\theta_n/\mu\in\,]0,2/\beta[$ — the full admissible range of page 4.
  • $A\succcurlyeq\nabla^2f$ everywhere (Hessian bound): a Newton-like step with a safe curvature; for a strictly convex quadratic $f$ ($\nabla^2f\succ0$) and $A=\nabla^2f$, $\theta_n=1$ reaches the minimizer in one step: Newton's method, which is an MM step exactly when its local quadratic model is a majorant.
  • $A(y)=H^\top H+\lambda G^\top\operatorname{Diag}(\omega([Gy]_n))G$ (half-quadratic): the lab's algorithm; the curvature follows the current edges of the image.

Theorem

Sufficient descent and convergence of the MM quadratic algorithm

Part VI · slides 18–20SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Assumptions.

  1. $f:\H\to\R$ is a coercive, differentiable function.
  2. There exists $(\nu,\bar\nu)\in\,]0,+\infty[^2$ such that $(\forall n\in\N)\ \nu\Id\preccurlyeq A(x_n)\preccurlyeq\bar\nu\Id$.
  3. There exists $(\underline\theta,\bar\theta)\in\,]0,+\infty[^2$ such that $(\forall n\in\N)\ \underline\theta\le\theta_n\le2-\bar\theta$.

Sufficient descent property. There exists $(\mu_1,\mu_2)\in\,]0,+\infty[^2$ such that $$(\forall n\in\N)\qquad f(x_n)-f(x_{n+1})\ \ge\ \mu_1\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2\ \ge\ \mu_2\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2 .$$

Convergence theorem (in finite dimension).

  1. $\nabla f(x_n)\to0$ and $f(x_n)\searrow f(\tilde x)$ for some $\tilde x\in\H$.
  2. If $f$ is continuously differentiable, any sequential cluster point of $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ is a stationary point of $f$.
  3. If $f$ is convex, any sequential cluster point of $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ is a minimizer of $f$.
  4. If $f$ is strictly convex, then $x_n\to\hat x$ where $\hat x$ is the unique minimizer of $f$.
In one sentence

Each step decreases $f$ by at least a fixed multiple of $\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2$; since $f$ is bounded below, the decreases are summable, so the gradients vanish, and coercivity confines the iterates to a compact set where cluster points exist and are stationary.

Why it is true

Three mechanisms. (a) Descent with a margin. The majorant evaluated at the new point is not just $\le f(x_n)$: because the step is $\theta_n$ times the minimizer of the parabola, the parabola's value there is $f(x_n)-(\theta_n^{-1}-\tfrac12)\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2_{A(x_n)}$, strictly below $f(x_n)$ as long as $\theta_n\lt2$; and since $A(x_n)\succcurlyeq\nu\Id$, this $A$-length is at least $\nu$ times the true squared length, which is where $\mu_1=\big(\tfrac1{2-\bar\theta}-\tfrac12\big)\nu$ comes from. (b) Step controls gradient. The update formula is $\nabla f(x_n)=-\theta_n^{-1}A(x_n)(x_{n+1}-x_n)$: a small step means a small gradient, because the curvature is bounded by $\bar\nu$ and $\theta_n$ is bounded below. (c) Compactness. The decreasing sequence $f(x_n)$ keeps the iterates in the bounded level set $\{f\le f(x_0)\}$ (coercivity, T03), compact in finite dimension; summing (a)+(b) (T13) gives $\sum\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2\lt\infty$. Convexity then upgrades "stationary" to "minimizer", strict convexity to uniqueness and hence convergence of the whole sequence.

What each hypothesis buys
  • Coercive $f$: the iterates stay in a bounded set (level set of $f(x_0)$), hence have cluster points in finite dimension, and $f$ is bounded below along the sequence. Drop it: the softplus $f(x)=\log(1+e^x)$ on $\R$ has $f''=\sigma(1-\sigma)\le\tfrac14$ ($\sigma$ the logistic function), so $A\equiv1$ is a valid curvature; with $\theta_n\equiv1$ the MM step is $x_{n+1}=x_n-\sigma(x_n)$, and from $x_0=0$ one gets $x_n\approx-\log n\to-\infty$, $f'(x_n)=\sigma(x_n)\to0$ and $f(x_n)\searrow0=\inf f$, but no cluster point.
  • $A(x_n)\succcurlyeq\nu\Id$: converts the descent in the $A$-norm into a descent in the true norm ($\mu_1\gt0$) and makes the step well defined ($\norm{A(x_n)^{-1}}\le1/\nu$). Drop it and the step is no longer controlled. With Tukey's potential (row 7 of the table, $\omega=0$ beyond $\sqrt6\,\delta$) the half-quadratic curvature can vanish while $h(\cdot,x_n)$ is still a perfectly valid majorant (constant, equal to the saturation value $1$). Then $A(x_n)$ is not invertible, every point of a flat direction minimizes $h(\cdot,x_n)$, and a decrease $\norm{d_n}^2_{A(x_n)}$ says nothing about $\norm{d_n}$.
  • $A(x_n)\preccurlyeq\bar\nu\Id$: makes the step at least proportional to the gradient ($\mu_2\gt0$). Drop it: the counterexample of mm-algorithm ($\mu_n=2^{n+1}$) has $f(x_n)\searrow$ but $\nabla f(x_n)\not\to0$.
  • $\theta_n\ge\underline\theta$: again a nonvanishing step; $\theta_n\le2-\bar\theta$: keeps the descent coefficient $\theta_n^{-1}-\tfrac12\ge\tfrac1{2-\bar\theta}-\tfrac12=\tfrac{\bar\theta}{2(2-\bar\theta)}\gt0$ uniformly. With $\theta_n=2$ on $f=\tfrac12x^2$, $A\equiv1$: $x_{n+1}=-x_n$, no decrease at all.
  • Finite dimension: bounded ⇒ relatively compact (strong cluster points). In infinite dimension one only gets weak cluster points and the argument breaks at "$\nabla f(\tilde x)=0$" without extra assumptions.
  • $\nabla f$ continuous (item 2): to pass $\nabla f(x_{n_k})\to0$ to the limit. For a convex differentiable $f$ on $\R^N$ this is automatic (convex differentiable functions are $C^1$ in finite dimension Extra), which is why items 3–4 do not repeat it.
Hint — try it first

Start from $f(x_{n+1})\le h(x_{n+1},x_n)$ and replace $\nabla f(x_n)$ in $h$ by $-\theta_n^{-1}A(x_n)(x_{n+1}-x_n)$ (the update). Then bound $\norm{x-y}^2_{A}$ between $\nu\norm{x-y}^2$ and $\bar\nu\norm{x-y}^2$.

Proof T19 majorize then minimize T13 telescoping T02 compactness T03 coercivity

1. Majorization at the new point. Let $n\in\N$ and write $A_n=A(x_n)$, $d_n=x_{n+1}-x_n$. By the majorization property, $f(x_{n+1})\le h(x_{n+1},x_n)=f(x_n)+\ps{\nabla f(x_n)}{d_n}+\tfrac12\norm{d_n}^2_{A_n}$.

2. Insert the update. The update reads $\nabla f(x_n)+\theta_n^{-1}A_nd_n=0$. Hence $\ps{\nabla f(x_n)}{d_n}=-\theta_n^{-1}\ps{A_nd_n}{d_n}=-\theta_n^{-1}\norm{d_n}^2_{A_n}$, and therefore $$f(x_{n+1})\le f(x_n)-\Big(\theta_n^{-1}-\frac12\Big)\norm{d_n}^2_{A_n}.$$

3. The coefficient is positive: this is where $\theta_n\lt2$ enters. From $\theta_n\le2-\bar\theta$: $\theta_n^{-1}\ge\frac1{2-\bar\theta}$, so $\theta_n^{-1}-\frac12\ge\frac1{2-\bar\theta}-\frac12=\frac{\bar\theta}{2(2-\bar\theta)}\gt0$. (With $\theta_n\to2$ this coefficient would tend to $0$: the parabola $\theta\mapsto h(x_n-\theta A_n^{-1}\nabla f(x_n),x_n)$ has its minimum at $\theta=1$ and comes back to the value $f(x_n)$ at $\theta=2$.) With $\norm{d_n}^2_{A_n}\ge\nu\norm{d_n}^2$: $$f(x_{n+1})\le f(x_n)-\underbrace{\Big(\frac1{2-\bar\theta}-\frac12\Big)\nu}_{\mu_1}\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2 .$$

4. The step controls the gradient. From the update, $\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}=\theta_n^{-1}\norm{A_nd_n}\le\underbrace{\underline\theta^{-1}\bar\nu}_{\sqrt{\mu_1/\mu_2}}\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}$, using $\theta_n\ge\underline\theta$ and $\norm{A_n}\le\bar\nu$. Squaring: $\mu_1\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2\ge\mu_1\underline\theta^2\bar\nu^{-2}\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2=:\mu_2\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2$. This proves the sufficient descent property with $\mu_1=\big(\tfrac1{2-\bar\theta}-\tfrac12\big)\nu$ and $\mu_2=\mu_1\underline\theta^2/\bar\nu^2$.

5. Compactness. $(f(x_n))_n$ is decreasing, so every $x_n$ lies in the level set $\lev_{\le f(x_0)}f$, which is bounded because $f$ is coercive and closed because $f$ is continuous (differentiable), hence compact in finite dimension. So $(x_n)_n$ belongs to a compact subset of $\H$ and $f$ is bounded below on it: $(f(x_n))_n$ is a decreasing bounded sequence, hence converges. There exists a subsequence $(x_{n_k})_k$ converging to some $\tilde x\in\H$; by continuity of $f$, $f(x_{n_k})\to f(\tilde x)$, so that the whole sequence satisfies $f(x_n)\searrow f(\tilde x)$.

6. Telescoping. Summing the descent inequality from $0$ to $N$: $\mu_2\sum_{n=0}^N\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2\le\sum_{n=0}^N\big(f(x_n)-f(x_{n+1})\big)=f(x_0)-f(x_{N+1})\le f(x_0)-f(\tilde x)$. The series converges, so $\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}\to0$; likewise $\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}\to0$. This is item 1.

7. Cluster points are stationary (item 2). If $\nabla f$ is continuous, for any cluster point $\tilde x=\lim x_{n_k}$: $\nabla f(\tilde x)=\lim\nabla f(x_{n_k})=0$, so $\tilde x$ is a critical point.

8. Convex case (item 3). If $f$ is convex, every critical point is a minimizer (Fermat for convex differentiable functions: $f(x)\ge f(\tilde x)+\ps{\nabla f(\tilde x)}{x-\tilde x}=f(\tilde x)$).

9. Strictly convex case (item 4). A strictly convex function has at most one minimizer; a coercive lsc function on $\R^N$ has at least one (existence under coercivity): call it $\hat x$. By step 8 every cluster point of $(x_n)_n$ is a minimizer, hence equals $\hat x$. A bounded sequence of $\R^N$ with a unique cluster point converges to it (otherwise some subsequence stays outside a ball around $\hat x$, and by compactness it has a cluster point $\ne\hat x$). So $x_n\to\hat x$. □

Note: "strictly" versus "strongly" convex in item 4

Slide 18 states item 4 for a strictly convex $f$, while the proof on slide 20 says "if $f$ is strongly convex, the set of critical points is reduced to a singleton". Strong convexity is more than needed: strict convexity already gives at most one minimizer, coercivity gives at least one, and the argument of step 9 (all cluster points of a bounded sequence coincide ⇒ convergence) finishes the proof without strong convexity. Strong convexity would additionally give a rate (linear convergence), which the slides do not claim.

Trick: the sufficient-descent recipe (used four times on this page)

(1) majorant at the new point; (2) replace the gradient by the update rule, so that the linear term becomes $-\theta_n^{-1}\norm{d_n}^2_A$; (3) $\theta_n\lt2$ ⇒ coefficient $\gt0$; $A\succcurlyeq\nu\Id$ ⇒ $\mu_1$; (4) $A\preccurlyeq\bar\nu\Id$ ⇒ $\norm{\nabla f}\le\underline\theta^{-1}\bar\nu\norm{d_n}$ ⇒ $\mu_2$; (5) coercivity ⇒ compact level set ⇒ bounded below; (6) telescope ⇒ $\nabla f(x_n)\to0$. The same six moves prove the subspace, block and VMFB theorems; only step (2) changes.

Pitfall

Item 1 does not say $x_n$ converges, nor that $f(\tilde x)$ is the minimum: for a nonconvex $f$ different $x_0$ lead to different local minimizers (widget w-mm-1d: from $x_0=2.2$ the iterates reach the global minimizer $0.93$, from $x_0=-1$ the local minimizer $-1.14$, whose value $4.31$ is higher than $3.01$), and in principle the whole sequence could even fail to converge while its cluster points are all stationary (ruled out in practice by the Kurdyka–Łojasiewicz theory, not covered Extra).

Acceleration via a subspace strategy (3MG)

In large scale, solving $A(x_n)d=\nabla f(x_n)$ exactly is the expensive part. Minimize the majorant only on a small subspace spanned by a few well-chosen directions: the subproblem becomes an $M_n\times M_n$ system, and with the memory-gradient subspace (gradient + previous step) you get the 3MG algorithm, a conjugate-gradient-like method with no line search.

Strategy

Subspace acceleration and the table of subspaces

Statement

Problem: minimization of a differentiable function $f:\H\to\R$. MM quadratic algorithm: $x_{n+1}\in\Argmin_{x\in\H}h(x,x_n)$.

Difficulty: in the context of large scale optimization, the minimization of $h$ over $\H$ may become intractable.

⇒ Subspace strategy: instead of minimizing $h$ over the whole set $\H$, restrict the minimization space to a subspace spanned by a small number of vectors.

In one sentence

Keep the majorant, shrink the search space: every row of the table starts with the steepest-descent direction and adds a little memory of the past (previous steps, previous gradients, or differences of gradients).

Why this is the right place to cut corners

The surrogate $h(\cdot,x_n)$ is only an approximation of $f$ away from $x_n$, so solving its minimization to machine precision in all $N$ directions is wasted effort; what matters is to find a point where $h$ (hence $f$) is markedly lower, and a handful of directions suffices for that. The first direction $-\nabla f(x_n)$ guarantees descent (the convergence theorem below uses only it); the memory directions make the method behave like conjugate gradient instead of steepest descent, removing the zig-zag. Notice the convention of slide 21: the MM quadratic algorithm written as $x_{n+1}\in\Argmin h(x,x_n)$ is the $\theta_n=1$ version.

MM quadratic subspace algorithm and 3MG

Problem, assumption, algorithm

Problem: minimization of $f:\H\to\R$ where $f$ is differentiable. Assumption: for all $y\in\H$, there exists a strongly positive self-adjoint operator $A(y)$ such that $h(x,y)=f(y)+\ps{\nabla f(y)}{x-y}+\tfrac12\norm{x-y}^2_{A(y)}$ is a majorant function of $f$ at $y$.

MM quadratic subspace algorithm

Let $x_0\in\H$. For $n=0,1,\dots$

  1. choose $D_n=[d_n^1|\dots|d_n^{M_n}]\in\H^{M_n}$ (a row of the table)
  2. $u_n\in\Argmin_{u\in\R^{M_n}}h\Big(x_n+\sum_{m=1}^{M_n}u^{(m)}d_n^m,\ x_n\Big)$ (an $M_n$-dimensional quadratic problem)
  3. $x_{n+1}=x_n+\sum_{m=1}^{M_n}u_n^{(m)}d_n^m$

⇒ the 3MG algorithm (MM Memory Gradient) is obtained when $(D_n)_n$ is the memory gradient subspace, $D_n=[-\nabla f(x_n)\,|\,x_n-x_{n-1}]$ (with $D_0=-\nabla f(x_0)$).

In one sentence

Write $x=x_n+D_nu$ and minimize the quadratic $h$ in the $M_n$ coefficients $u$: a tiny linear system $D_n^\top A(x_n)D_n\,u=-D_n^\top\nabla f(x_n)$ replaces the $N\times N$ solve.

Why it works and what it costs

Restricting a strongly convex quadratic to an affine subspace gives a convex quadratic in the coordinates $u$, strongly convex when the columns of $D_n$ are linearly independent; its minimizers are explicit (with a pseudo-inverse in general, see below). The expensive objects are never formed: in the lab, $D_n^\top H^\top HD_n$ is computed as $(HD_n)^\top(HD_n)$, i.e. $M_n$ products with $H$ and an $M_n\times M_n$ Gram matrix. Cost per iteration: one gradient, $M_n$ products $A(x_n)d_n^m$ (or $HD_n$, $GD_n$), $M_n^2$ inner products, one $M_n\times M_n$ (pseudo-)inverse — for 3MG, $M_n=2$. Memory: $D_n$ ($2$ vectors for 3MG). Parallelizable: the matrix–vector products. No line search is ever needed: the majorant decides the step lengths along all $M_n$ directions at once.

Whiteboard slides 25–26: the closed form Extra

Slides 25–26 are blank in the PDF; the lecturer presumably derived there the solution of step 2, which the lab states as $u_k=-(D_k^\top A(x_k)D_k)^\dagger D_k^\top\nabla f(x_k)$. Here is the derivation. Write $D_n:\R^{M_n}\to\H$, $u\mapsto\sum_mu^{(m)}d_n^m$, with adjoint $D_n^\top:\H\to\R^{M_n}$, $v\mapsto(\ps{d_n^m}{v})_m$. Then $$h(x_n+D_nu,x_n)=f(x_n)+\ps{D_n^\top\nabla f(x_n)}{u}+\tfrac12\ps{u}{D_n^\top A(x_n)D_n\,u},$$ a convex quadratic in $u\in\R^{M_n}$ whose minimizers are the solutions of $D_n^\top A(x_n)D_n\,u=-D_n^\top\nabla f(x_n)$. The $M_n\times M_n$ matrix $B_n=D_n^\top A(x_n)D_n$ is symmetric positive semidefinite; it is invertible when the columns of $D_n$ are linearly independent, and in general the system is always consistent: $\operatorname{ran}B_n=\operatorname{ran}(D_n^\top A(x_n)^{1/2})=\operatorname{ran}D_n^\top\ni D_n^\top\nabla f(x_n)$ because $A(x_n)^{1/2}$ is invertible. The pseudo-inverse picks the minimum-norm solution: $$u_n=-\big(D_n^\top A(x_n)D_n\big)^\dagger D_n^\top\nabla f(x_n),\qquad x_{n+1}=x_n+D_nu_n .$$ For $M_n=1$, $d_n^1=-\nabla f(x_n)$: $u_n=\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2/\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2_{A(x_n)}$, the exact minimization of the majorant along the steepest descent direction ("majorant line search").

3MG versus conjugate gradient and L-BFGS Extra

On a convex quadratic $f=\tfrac12\ps{x}{Qx}-\ps bx$ with $A\equiv Q$ the majorant is $f$ itself, and minimizing $f$ over $x_n+\operatorname{span}(-\nabla f(x_n),x_n-x_{n-1})$ is exactly the linear conjugate gradient iteration (the CG iterate is characterized by this two-dimensional minimization): 3MG terminates in at most $N$ steps, two in the plane. For a general $f$, 3MG is a nonlinear conjugate gradient method in which the majorant replaces the Wolfe line search and the conjugacy formula; L-BFGS keeps $m$ pairs $(d_{n-k},\delta_{n-k})$ — the quasi-Newton subspace of the table — but uses them to build an approximate inverse Hessian rather than to restrict a majorant, and still needs a line search. The MM point of view trades a bit of per-iteration optimality for a convergence guarantee that holds for nonconvex $f$ with no tuning.

Pitfall: a singular $2\times2$ system

$B_n=D_n^\top A(x_n)D_n$ is singular exactly when the columns of $D_n$ are linearly dependent: when $x_n-x_{n-1}$ is parallel to $\nabla f(x_n)$, when $\nabla f(x_n)=0$, or when a column is zero (e.g. $x_n-x_{n-1}$ at $n=0$ if one sets $x_{-1}=x_0$ instead of taking $D_0=-\nabla f(x_0)$). Use the pseudo-inverse, never a plain inverse. Nothing is lost: the system is always consistent, and two solutions differ by some $u$ with $D_nu=0$, since $\ker B_n=\ker\big(A(x_n)^{1/2}D_n\big)=\ker D_n$; so all solutions give the same $x_{n+1}=x_n+D_nu_n$, and the pseudo-inverse picks the minimum-norm one. When $\nabla f(x_n)=0$ it returns $u_n=0$ and the algorithm stops, as it should.

Theorem

Convergence of the MM quadratic subspace algorithm

Part VI · slide 24Slides#
Statement

Assumptions.

  1. $f:\H\to\R$ is a coercive, differentiable function.
  2. There exists $(\nu,\bar\nu)\in\,]0,+\infty[^2$ such that $(\forall n\in\N)\ \nu\Id\preccurlyeq A(x_n)\preccurlyeq\bar\nu\Id$.
  3. There exists $(\gamma_0,\gamma_1)\in\,]0,+\infty[^2$ such that $$(\forall n\in\N)\qquad\ps{\nabla f(x_n)}{d_n^1}\le-\gamma_0\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2\quad\text{and}\quad\norm{d_n^1}\le\gamma_1\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}.$$

Sufficient descent property. There exists $(\mu_1,\mu_2)\in\,]0,+\infty[^2$ such that $(\forall n\in\N)\ f(x_n)-f(x_{n+1})\ge\mu_1\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2\ge\mu_2\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2$.

Convergence theorem (in finite dimension).

  1. $\nabla f(x_n)\to0$ and $f(x_n)\searrow f(\tilde x)$ where $\tilde x$ is a critical point of $f$.
  2. If $f$ is convex, any sequential cluster point of $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ is a minimizer of $f$.
  3. If $f$ is strictly convex, then $x_n\to\hat x$ where $\hat x$ is the unique minimizer of $f$.
In one sentence

The first direction is a genuine descent direction (gradient-related), so the best step along it alone already lowers the majorant by a fixed multiple of $\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2$; the subspace minimizer does at least as well, and the MM quadratic proof takes over.

Why it is true

Two facts about the subspace minimizer $x_{n+1}$. First, the optimality condition in $u$ makes the linear term of $h$ equal to $-\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2_{A_n}$, so $h(x_{n+1},x_n)=f(x_n)-\tfrac12\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2_{A_n}$: the decrease of the majorant is half the squared $A$-length of the step (this is the $\theta_n=1$ case of the MM quadratic computation). Second, the line $x_n+td_n^1$ is inside the search space, so $h(x_{n+1},x_n)\le\min_th(x_n+td_n^1,x_n)=f(x_n)-\ps{\nabla f(x_n)}{d_n^1}^2/(2\norm{d_n^1}^2_{A_n})$, and the gradient-related condition turns the right-hand side into $f(x_n)-c\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2$. Combining the two gives the sufficient descent; coercivity and telescoping do the rest exactly as before.

What each hypothesis buys
  • Assumption 3 (gradient-related first direction): $\ps{\nabla f}{d^1}\le-\gamma_0\norm{\nabla f}^2$ says $d^1$ points downhill at least as much as $-\gamma_0\nabla f$; $\norm{d^1}\le\gamma_1\norm{\nabla f}$ forbids a huge direction that would make the one-dimensional minimum meaningless. The choice $d_n^1=-\nabla f(x_n)$ of every row of the table satisfies it with $\gamma_0=\gamma_1=1$. The other directions are free: they can only improve the subspace minimum.
  • Coercivity, $\nu,\bar\nu$, finite dimension: as in mm-quadratic-convergence. Item 1 calls $\tilde x$ a critical point, which needs $\nabla f$ continuous (implicit in the slide, as in item 2 of the quadratic theorem).

The optimality of $u_n$ reads $D_n^\top A_nD_nu_n=-D_n^\top g_n$. Taking the inner product with $u_n$: $\ps{g_n}{D_nu_n}=\ps{D_n^\top g_n}{u_n}=-\ps{D_n^\top A_nD_nu_n}{u_n}=-\norm{D_nu_n}^2_{A_n}=-\norm{s_n}^2_{A_n}$. Hence $$h(x_{n+1},x_n)=f(x_n)+\ps{g_n}{s_n}+\tfrac12\norm{s_n}^2_{A_n}=f(x_n)-\tfrac12\norm{s_n}^2_{A_n}.$$ With majorization, $f(x_{n+1})\le h(x_{n+1},x_n)\le f(x_n)-\tfrac\nu2\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2$: this is the first inequality with $\mu_1=\nu/2$.

Since $x_n+t^\star d_n^1$ belongs to the search space $x_n+\operatorname{span}(D_n)$ and $x_{n+1}$ minimizes $h(\cdot,x_n)$ there: $h(x_{n+1},x_n)\le f(x_n)-\dfrac{\ps{g_n}{d_n^1}^2}{2\norm{d_n^1}^2_{A_n}}$.

3. Gradient-related condition. $\ps{g_n}{d_n^1}^2\ge\gamma_0^2\norm{g_n}^4$ and $\norm{d_n^1}^2_{A_n}\le\bar\nu\norm{d_n^1}^2\le\bar\nu\gamma_1^2\norm{g_n}^2$. So $h(x_{n+1},x_n)\le f(x_n)-\dfrac{\gamma_0^2}{2\bar\nu\gamma_1^2}\norm{g_n}^2$.

4. Second inequality. Steps 1 and 3 give $\tfrac12\norm{s_n}^2_{A_n}=f(x_n)-h(x_{n+1},x_n)\ge\dfrac{\gamma_0^2}{2\bar\nu\gamma_1^2}\norm{g_n}^2$, and $\norm{s_n}^2_{A_n}\le\bar\nu\norm{s_n}^2$, hence $\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2\ge\dfrac{\gamma_0^2}{\bar\nu^2\gamma_1^2}\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2$ and $$\mu_1\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2\ge\underbrace{\frac{\nu\gamma_0^2}{2\bar\nu^2\gamma_1^2}}_{\mu_2}\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2 .$$

Acceleration via block-alternation, and parallel MM

Split the unknown into blocks and update one block per iteration with its own small quadratic majorant: smaller systems, less memory, the same convergence theorem provided every block is visited regularly. With a separable (diagonal) majorant the block updates decouple completely and the algorithm runs in parallel.

Strategy

Block-coordinate strategy

Part VI · slide 27Slides#
Statement

Problem: minimization of $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$, where $\H=\bigtimes_{j=1}^J\H_j$ and $\H_1,\dots,\H_J$ are separable real Hilbert spaces. Every $x\in\H$ is written $x=(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(J)})$ with $x^{(j)}\in\H_j$, and $f(x)=f(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(J)})$.

⇒ Block-coordinate strategy: instead of updating the whole vector $x$ at iteration $n\in\N$, restrict the update to a block $j_n\in\{1,\dots,J\}$.

In one sentence

Freeze all blocks but one, take an MM step on that block only, move on to another block.

Why one would do this

Two reasons, both about scale. The subproblem on block $j$ lives in $\H_j$: for a quadratic majorant it is an $N_j\times N_j$ system instead of $N\times N$, and the block curvature $A_j(y)$ is the $(j,j)$ diagonal block of the full $A(y)$ (lab, §3.4), much cheaper to form and to invert. And the freshest information is used immediately: the update of block $j_n$ sees the blocks already updated at previous iterations (Gauss–Seidel style), which is why block methods often need fewer passes over the data than full updates. The price: the iterations are inherently sequential, and the convergence proof must make sure that no block is forgotten (selection rules). Notation: $\nabla_jf(y)\in\H_j$ is the partial gradient of $f$ with respect to the block $x^{(j)}$ at $y$, so that $\nabla f(y)=(\nabla_1f(y),\dots,\nabla_Jf(y))$.

To remember

$\H=\bigtimes_j\H_j$; at iteration $n$ only $x^{(j_n)}$ moves. Smaller systems, sequential iterations.

In one sentence

The MM quadratic step, applied to the one-block function $x^{(j_n)}\mapsto f(\dots,x^{(j_n)},\dots)$ with its own curvature $A_{j_n}(x_n)$.

Why the block majorant is enough, and where it comes from

Only the restriction of $f$ to the block $j_n$ is minimized at iteration $n$, so only a majorant of that restriction is needed, and it is a function of $N_{j_n}$ variables. If a full quadratic majorant with curvature $A(y)$ is available, its restriction to the block is one: for $x$ differing from $y$ only in block $j$, $\norm{x-y}^2_{A(y)}=\norm{x^{(j)}-y^{(j)}}^2_{[A(y)]_{jj}}$, so $A_j(y)=[A(y)]_{jj}$, the $(j,j)$ diagonal block, works (this is the lab's "$A_j(x)$ containing only the lines and rows of $A(x)$ with indexes $\mathbb J_j$"). A block majorant can also be tighter than the diagonal block of a loose full majorant (e.g. $L\,\Id$, or the Jensen diagonal $B(x)$ of parallel MM): for a least-squares term the exact block curvature $H_j^\top H_j=[H^\top H]_{jj}$ is available, whatever the coupling with the other blocks ($H_j$ the columns of $H$ of block $j$).

Definition

Selection of blocks: cyclic, quasi-cyclic, random

Part VI · slide 29Slides#
Definition

At each iteration $n\in\N$, $j_n\in\{1,\dots,J\}$ can be chosen according to:

  • the cyclic rule: $(\forall n\in\N)\ j_n-1=n\bmod J$;
  • a quasi-cyclic rule: there exists a constant $K\ge J$ such that, for every $n\in\N$, $\{1,\dots,J\}\subset\{j_n,\dots,j_{n+K-1}\}$;
  • a random rule: for every $n\in\N$, $j_n$ is a realization of a random variable.

⇒ The convergence properties of the algorithm may depend on the block selection rule.

In one sentence

Quasi-cyclic means "every block is visited at least once in any window of $K$ consecutive iterations"; cyclic is the special case $K=J$.

Examples and non-examples
  • Cyclic $1,2,\dots,J,1,2,\dots$: quasi-cyclic with $K=J$. The lab uses it ($j=\bmod(k-1,J)+1$ in 1-based indexing).
  • $1,1,2,2,\dots,J,J,\dots$ or any pattern that repeats each block a bounded number of times: quasi-cyclic with a larger $K$ ("essentially cyclic").
  • Uniform i.i.d. random choice: not quasi-cyclic for any fixed $K$ (a block can be missed for arbitrarily long stretches with positive probability), yet convergence holds almost surely by a different argument Extra; this is why the slide warns that the convergence properties depend on the rule.
  • A rule that never selects block $2$: $f(x)=\tfrac12\norm x^2$ on $\R^2$, $j_n\equiv1$. Then $x_n^{(2)}\equiv x_0^{(2)}$, $f(x_n)\searrow\tfrac12(x_0^{(2)})^2\ne\min f$ and $\nabla f(x_n)\not\to0$. Quasi-cyclicity is exactly what rules this out.
To remember

Theorem

Convergence of block-coordinate MM quadratic ((quasi-)cyclic rule)

Part VI · slide 30Slides#
Statement

Assumptions.

  1. $f:\R^N\to\R$ is a coercive, differentiable function.
  2. There exists $(\nu,\bar\nu)\in\,]0,+\infty[^2$ such that $(\forall n\in\N)\ \nu\Id\preccurlyeq A_{j_n}(x_n)\preccurlyeq\bar\nu\Id$.
  3. There exists $(\underline\theta,\bar\theta)\in\,]0,+\infty[^2$ such that $(\forall n\in\N)\ \underline\theta\le\theta_n\le2-\bar\theta$.

Sufficient descent property. There exists $(\mu_1,\mu_2)\in\,]0,+\infty[^2$ such that $$(\forall n\in\N)\qquad f(x_n)-f(x_{n+1})\ge\mu_1\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2\ge\mu_2\norm{\nabla_{j_n}f(x_n)}^2 .$$

Convergence theorem (in finite dimension and (quasi-)cyclic rule).

  1. $\nabla f(x_n)\to0$ and $f(x_n)\searrow f(\tilde x)$ where $\tilde x$ is a critical point of $f$.
  2. If $f$ is convex, any sequential cluster point of $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ is a minimizer of $f$.
  3. If $f$ is strictly convex, then $x_n\to\hat x$ where $\hat x$ is the unique minimizer of $f$.
In one sentence

Each iteration kills a fixed fraction of the selected partial gradient and moves the iterate by a vanishing amount; within $K$ iterations every block is selected, and since the iterate has barely moved in between and $\nabla f$ is continuous, every partial gradient at $x_n$ is small: the full gradient vanishes.

Why it is true

The one-block computation is the MM quadratic computation with $\nabla f$ replaced by $\nabla_{j_n}f$ and $\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}=\norm{x_{n+1}^{(j_n)}-x_n^{(j_n)}}$, so the sufficient descent is immediate, but it only controls $\norm{\nabla_{j_n}f(x_n)}$, the gradient of the block that was just updated. The delicate point is to recover the whole gradient. Fix a block $j$ and an iteration $n$; quasi-cyclicity provides $k\in\{n,\dots,n+K-1\}$ with $j_k=j$, so $\nabla_jf(x_k)=\nabla_{j_k}f(x_k)$ is small. Between $n$ and $k$ there are fewer than $K$ steps, each of vanishing length (telescoping), so $x_k$ is close to $x_n$; the iterates live in a compact level set on which $\nabla f$ is uniformly continuous, so $\nabla_jf(x_n)$ is close to $\nabla_jf(x_k)$, hence small too. Since this holds for each of the $J$ blocks, $\nabla f(x_n)\to0$.

What each hypothesis buys
  • Quasi-cyclic rule with a fixed $K$: the gap between $n$ and the next visit of block $j$ is bounded, so the accumulated displacement $\sum_{i=n}^{k-1}\norm{x_{i+1}-x_i}\le K\max_{i\ge n}\norm{x_{i+1}-x_i}\to0$. With unbounded gaps the argument fails (and so can the conclusion: see the "never select block 2" example in block-selection).
  • Continuity of $\nabla f$ (implicit in "critical point", as in the previous theorems): needed to transport "$\nabla_jf(x_k)$ is small" back to $x_n$. Uniform continuity comes for free from compactness of the level set.
  • Coercivity, $\nu$, $\bar\nu$, $\theta$ bounds, finite dimension: as before, now for the block curvatures $A_{j_n}(x_n)$.
Note

The slides state this theorem without proof; here is one. The constants are those of the MM quadratic theorem, $\mu_1=\big(\tfrac1{2-\bar\theta}-\tfrac12\big)\nu$ and $\mu_2=\mu_1\underline\theta^2/\bar\nu^2$.

3. From the selected block to the whole gradient (this is where quasi-cyclicity enters). $\nabla f$ is continuous on the compact set $C$, hence uniformly continuous there: for every $\varepsilon\gt0$ there is $\eta\gt0$ such that $\norm{x-x'}\le\eta\Rightarrow\norm{\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(x')}\le\varepsilon$ for $x,x'\in C$. Choose $n_0$ such that for $i\ge n_0$, $\norm{x_{i+1}-x_i}\le\eta/K$ and $\norm{\nabla_{j_i}f(x_i)}\le\varepsilon$. Let $n\ge n_0$ and $j\in\{1,\dots,J\}$. By the quasi-cyclic rule there is $k\in\{n,\dots,n+K-1\}$ with $j_k=j$. Then $\norm{x_k-x_n}\le\sum_{i=n}^{k-1}\norm{x_{i+1}-x_i}\le K\cdot\eta/K=\eta$, so $$\norm{\nabla_jf(x_n)}\le\norm{\nabla_jf(x_n)-\nabla_jf(x_k)}+\norm{\nabla_{j_k}f(x_k)}\le\norm{\nabla f(x_n)-\nabla f(x_k)}+\varepsilon\le2\varepsilon .$$ Hence $\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2=\sum_{j=1}^J\norm{\nabla_jf(x_n)}^2\le4J\varepsilon^2$ for $n\ge n_0$: $\nabla f(x_n)\to0$.

4. Conclusion. With $\nabla f(x_n)\to0$ in hand, steps 7–9 of the MM quadratic proof give: $\nabla f(\tilde x)=0$ for every cluster point (continuity), minimizers in the convex case, and $x_n\to\hat x$ in the strictly convex case. □

Pitfall

The sufficient descent of a block method controls only the partial gradient of the block just updated; "$\norm{\nabla_{j_n}f(x_n)}\to0$" alone says nothing about the other blocks. Quasi-cyclicity + vanishing steps + continuity of $\nabla f$ are the three ingredients that convert it into $\nabla f(x_n)\to0$; remove any one and the conclusion can fail.

Proposition

Parallel MM: a diagonal majorant by Jensen, $A(x)\preccurlyeq B(x)$

Lab 4 · §3.5 (not on the slides)ExtraLabKey#
Statement

Let $H\in\R^{M\times N}$. For every $d\in\R^N$, $$d^\top H^\top Hd=\norm{Hd}^2\ \le\ \sum_{i=1}^Nb_i\,d_i^2,\qquad b_i=\sum_{m=1}^M|H_{mi}|\sum_{p=1}^N|H_{mp}|=\big[\,|H|^\top|H|\mathbf 1\,\big]_i ,$$ i.e. $H^\top H\preccurlyeq\operatorname{Diag}(b)$. Consequently, for the lab's criterion $f(x)=\tfrac12\norm{Hx-y}^2+\lambda\sum_n\psi([Gx]_n)$ with curvature $A(x)=H^\top H+\lambda G^\top\operatorname{Diag}(\omega(Gx))G$, the diagonal matrix $B(x)=\operatorname{Diag}(b(x))$ with $$b_i(x)=\sum_m|H_{mi}|\sum_p|H_{mp}|\ +\ \lambda\sum_n|G_{ni}|\,\omega([Gx]_n)\sum_p|G_{np}|$$ satisfies $A(x)\preccurlyeq B(x)$ for every $x$, so $x\mapsto f(y)+\ps{\nabla f(y)}{x-y}+\tfrac12\norm{x-y}^2_{B(y)}$ is a separable quadratic majorant of $f$ at $y$. The parallel MM quadratic algorithm is $x_{n+1}=x_n-\theta_nB(x_n)^{-1}\nabla f(x_n)$, i.e. $x_{n+1,i}=x_{n,i}-\theta_n\,\partial_if(x_n)/b_i(x_n)$ for all $i$ simultaneously.

In one sentence

Jensen pushes the square inside each row sum: $(\sum_iH_{mi}d_i)^2\le(\sum_p|H_{mp}|)\sum_i|H_{mi}|d_i^2$, and summing over the rows gives a diagonal matrix dominating $H^\top H$.

Why it is true and what it buys

A quadratic form $\norm{Hd}^2$ couples the coordinates through the cross terms $H_{mi}H_{mp}d_id_p$. Jensen's inequality for the convex square, with the weights $|H_{mi}|/r_m$ ($r_m$ the absolute row sum), bounds each row's square by a weighted sum of squares of the $d_i$: the coupling disappears at the price of replacing $H^\top H$ by a diagonal matrix with larger entries ($b_i\ge[H^\top H]_{ii}$). The MM step with a diagonal curvature is a coordinatewise formula: no linear system, perfectly parallel over the $N$ pixels, and only a few sparse matrix–vector products per iteration (with $H$, $H^\top$, $G$, $G^\top$ for $\nabla f$, and one with $|G|^\top$ for $b(x)$; the vectors $|H|^\top|H|\mathbf1$ and $|G|\mathbf1$ are computed once). The trade-off is explicit in the tomography lab: the parallel majorant is looser than $A(x)$, so more iterations are needed, but each iteration is cheap and parallel. This is the syllabus item "parallel approaches using block-coordinate strategies": a separable majorant is the extreme block decomposition, $J=N$ blocks of size one, all updated at once.

What each hypothesis buys
  • Weights $|H_{mi}|$: Jensen needs nonnegative weights, so the signs of $H_{mi}$ go into the points $t_i$, not into the weights.
  • Normalization per row by $r_m=\sum_p|H_{mp}|$: the weights must sum to one in each row; a zero row contributes nothing.
  • $A(x)\preccurlyeq B(x)$ at every point where a majorant is built: then $h_B\ge h_A\ge f$, so $B$ inherits the majorant property. The MM quadratic theorem then applies with $B(x_n)$ in place of $A(x_n)$, which needs $\nu\Id\preccurlyeq B(x_n)\preccurlyeq\bar\nu\Id$: for the lab's $\psi$, $0\lt\omega\le1/\delta^2$, so $[\,|H|^\top|H|\mathbf1\,]_i\le b_i(x)\le[\,|H|^\top|H|\mathbf1\,]_i+\tfrac\lambda{\delta^2}[\,|G|^\top|G|\mathbf1\,]_i$, and $\nu\gt0$ as soon as no column of $H$ is zero (every pixel is seen by at least one ray).

The nondifferentiable case: IRLS versus variable metric forward–backward

When $f=f_1+f_2$ with $f_2$ nonsmooth, either majorize everything by a quadratic (iteratively reweighted least squares, with trouble at the kinks) or majorize only $f_1$ and keep $f_2$: the surrogate is then "quadratic in a metric plus $f_2$", and its minimizer is a proximity operator in that metric. That is the variable metric forward–backward algorithm, and with a constant scalar metric it is exactly the forward–backward algorithm of page 4.

Discussion

Nondifferentiable $f=f_1+f_2$: two approaches

Part VI · slide 33Slides#
Statement

Problem: minimization of $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ where $f=f_1+f_2$ with $f_1$ differentiable and $f_2$ not necessarily differentiable. MM algorithm: $x_{n+1}\in\Argmin_{x\in\H}h(x,x_n)$.

Difficulty: how to majorize the nondifferentiable function $f$, so that the majorants remain easy to minimize?

⇒ Two main approaches:

  1. Use quadratic majorant functions for $f$ (but numerical issues at nondifferentiability points): Iterative Reweighted Least Squares algorithms (e.g. Weiszfeld, FOCUSS, IRLS, …).
  2. Use a quadratic majorant function for $f_1$ and keep $f_2$ untouched, $x_{n+1}\in\Argmin_{x\in\H}h(x,x_n)+f_2(x)$: the variable metric forward–backward algorithm.
In one sentence

Either smooth the kink into the majorant (cheap steps, singular weights at the kink) or leave the kink alone and pay a prox for it.

Why the first approach has "numerical issues", and what the second one costs

Approach 1 applies the half-quadratic theorem to a $\psi$ that violates assumption (iv). For $\psi(u)=u$ (i.e. $f_2=\norm\cdot_1$ or a sum of Euclidean norms), $\psi(\sqrt v)=\sqrt v$ is concave, $\dot\psi=1\ge0$, but $\omega(u)=\dot\psi(u)/u=1/u\to+\infty$: the majorant at $y$, $|y|/2+x^2/(2|y|)$, is a parabola whose curvature explodes as $y\to0$ and is undefined at $y=0$. The MM step is then a weighted least-squares problem with weights $1/|y_n|$ — hence the name IRLS — and coordinates that reach $0$ get an infinite weight and are frozen there for ever; implementations add an $\varepsilon$: $\psi_\varepsilon(u)=\sqrt{u^2+\varepsilon^2}$, which is exactly the lab's $\delta\sqrt{1+u^2/\delta^2}$. Approach 2 keeps $f_2$ exact; the surrogate $h(\cdot,x_n)+f_2$ is "a strongly convex quadratic in the metric $A(x_n)$ plus $f_2$", whose minimizer is by definition a proximity operator in that metric (next card). The cost moves from "solve a weighted least-squares system" to "compute a prox in a metric", which is explicit when $A(x_n)$ is diagonal and $f_2$ separable (slide 35) — one more reason to like the diagonal majorants of parallel MM.

The Fermat–Weber problem $\min_x\sum_{k=1}^K\norm{x-a_k}$ (geometric median). Majorize each norm at $x_n\ne a_k$ by $\tfrac{\norm{x_n-a_k}}2+\dfrac{\norm{x-a_k}^2}{2\norm{x_n-a_k}}$ (the half-quadratic parabola with $\omega=1/\norm{x_n-a_k}$; check: equality at $x=x_n$, and $\norm{x-a_k}\le\tfrac{s}2+\tfrac{\norm{x-a_k}^2}{2s}$ for every $s\gt0$ by $2st\le s^2+t^2$). Minimizing the sum of parabolas gives the weighted mean $$x_{n+1}=\frac{\sum_kw_ka_k}{\sum_kw_k},\qquad w_k=\frac1{\norm{x_n-a_k}},$$ Weiszfeld's 1937 iteration. It is undefined when $x_n$ hits some $a_k$ — the "numerical issue at nondifferentiability points" in its purest form.

Definition

Proximity operator within a metric

Part VI · slide 34SlidesKey#
Definition

Let $f\in\Gz{\H}$. Let $A:\H\to\H$ be a strongly positive self-adjoint operator. For all $x\in\H$, $\prox_{A,f}(x)$ is the proximity operator of $f$ in $(\H,\norm\cdot_A)$, i.e. the unique minimizer of $$y\mapsto f(y)+\tfrac12\norm{x-y}^2_A .$$

Remarks.

  • If $A=\alpha^{-1}\Id$ with $\alpha\gt0$, then $\prox_{\alpha^{-1}\Id,f}\equiv\prox_{\alpha f}$ corresponds to the usual proximity operator.
  • We have $(\forall x\in\R^N)\quad\prox_{A,f}(x)=A^{-1/2}\prox_{f\circ A^{-1/2}}\big(A^{1/2}x\big)$.
In one sentence

Same compromise as the usual prox — stay close to $x$, pay $f$ — but "close" is measured with the ellipsoidal norm $\norm\cdot_A$: cheap to move in directions where $A$ is small, expensive where $A$ is large.

Why it is well defined, and why the two remarks hold

$\ps{u}{v}_A=\ps{u}{Av}$ is an inner product whose norm is equivalent to the original one ($\nu\norm u^2\le\norm u^2_A\le\norm A\norm u^2$), so $(\H,\norm\cdot_A)$ is a Hilbert space with the same topology, $f$ is still proper, lsc and convex there, and the usual existence and uniqueness of the prox applies: $f+\tfrac12\norm{x-\cdot}^2_A$ is strongly convex, lsc and proper. First remark: $\tfrac12\norm{x-y}^2_{\alpha^{-1}\Id}=\tfrac1{2\alpha}\norm{x-y}^2$, and minimizing $f(y)+\tfrac1{2\alpha}\norm{x-y}^2$ is the same as minimizing $\alpha f(y)+\tfrac12\norm{x-y}^2$. Second remark: the change of variable $z=A^{1/2}y$ turns the $A$-norm into the Euclidean norm, $\norm{x-y}^2_A=\norm{A^{1/2}x-z}^2$, so the problem becomes a usual prox problem for the function $f\circ A^{-1/2}$ at the point $A^{1/2}x$ (T18 change variables).

Proof of the remarks T18 change of variable T08 Fermat

1. Scalar metric. For $A=\alpha^{-1}\Id$: $\Argmin_y f(y)+\tfrac1{2\alpha}\norm{x-y}^2=\Argmin_y\alpha f(y)+\tfrac12\norm{x-y}^2=\{\prox_{\alpha f}x\}$ (multiplying the objective by $\alpha\gt0$ does not change the minimizer).

2. Square root. $A$ strongly positive self-adjoint has a unique strongly positive self-adjoint square root $A^{1/2}$ (spectral theorem; in $\R^N$, diagonalize $A=U\operatorname{Diag}(a_i)U^\top$ and set $A^{1/2}=U\operatorname{Diag}(\sqrt{a_i})U^\top$), invertible with inverse $A^{-1/2}$, and $\norm{u}^2_A=\ps{u}{A^{1/2}A^{1/2}u}=\norm{A^{1/2}u}^2$.

3. Change of variable. Set $z=A^{1/2}y$, a bijection of $\H$. Then $f(y)+\tfrac12\norm{x-y}^2_A=f(A^{-1/2}z)+\tfrac12\norm{A^{1/2}x-z}^2$. The function $g=f\circ A^{-1/2}$ belongs to $\Gz{\H}$ (composition of $f\in\Gz{\H}$ with a linear bijection: proper, lsc, convex), so the right-hand side has the unique minimizer $z^\star=\prox_g(A^{1/2}x)$, and the left-hand side has the unique minimizer $y^\star=A^{-1/2}z^\star=A^{-1/2}\prox_{f\circ A^{-1/2}}(A^{1/2}x)$. □

4. Characterization (useful later). By Fermat and the sum rule, $p=\prox_{A,f}(x)\iff0\in\partial f(p)+A(p-x)\iff A(x-p)\in\partial f(p)$: the usual $x-p\in\partial f(p)$ with $x-p$ measured through $A$.

Worked examples
  • $f=\ind_C$: $\prox_{A,\ind_C}$ is the projection onto $C$ in the $A$-metric (an oblique projection: the level sets of $\norm{x-\cdot}_A$ are ellipsoids, and the projection is where the smallest ellipsoid around $x$ touches $C$).
  • $f=\lambda\norm\cdot_1$ and $A=\operatorname{Diag}(a_i)$: componentwise soft-thresholding with thresholds $\lambda/a_i$ (next card): a large curvature $a_i$ means a small threshold on coordinate $i$ — the metric rescales the regularization.
  • $f$ quadratic, $f=\tfrac12\norm{Lx-z}^2$: $\prox_{A,f}(x)=(A+L^\top L)^{-1}(Ax+L^\top z)$ (Fermat: $L^\top(Lp-z)+A(p-x)=0$).

Separable $f$ and diagonal $A$: componentwise prox

Part VI · slide 35SlidesLab#
Statement

Let $f\in\Gz{\R^N}$. Assume that $f$ is separable, i.e. $\big(\forall x=(x^{(i)})_{1\le i\le N}\in\R^N\big)\ f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^Nf_i(x^{(i)})$, and that $A$ is diagonal with (strictly) positive diagonal elements $(a_i)_{1\le i\le N}$. Then, for every $x\in\R^N$, $\prox_{A,f}(x)=p$ where $p=(p^{(i)})_{1\le i\le N}\in\R^N$ is given by $$(\forall i\in\{1,\dots,N\})\qquad p^{(i)}=\prox_{a_i^{-1}f_i}\big(x^{(i)}\big).$$

In one sentence

Everything is a sum over coordinates, so the minimization splits into $N$ one-dimensional prox problems, each with its own parameter $1/a_i$.

Why it is true

The objective $\sum_i\big[f_i(y^{(i)})+\tfrac{a_i}2(x^{(i)}-y^{(i)})^2\big]$ is a sum of functions of different variables; a sum of independent terms is minimized by minimizing each term, and the $i$-th term, divided by $a_i\gt0$, is the usual prox objective of $a_i^{-1}f_i$ at $x^{(i)}$. This is the metric version of the separable prox of page 3; it is what makes VMFB with a diagonal metric and $f_2=\lambda\norm\cdot_1$ (or an indicator of a box) fully explicit and parallel.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $f$ separable: the objective splits into $N$ independent one-dimensional problems. A coupling term ($f=\norm\cdot_2$, say) breaks the splitting even with a diagonal $A$.
  • $A$ diagonal: $\norm{x-y}^2_A=\sum_ia_i(x^{(i)}-y^{(i)})^2$ has no cross terms. A non-diagonal $A$ couples the coordinates even for a separable $f$: $\prox_{A,\lambda\norm\cdot_1}$ is then a LASSO problem (design $A^{1/2}$), not a soft-thresholding.
  • $a_i\gt0$: each one-dimensional problem gets a finite positive parameter $1/a_i$, and each term is strongly convex, so its minimizer exists and is unique (with $a_i=0$ the $i$-th problem would be $\min f_i$, which may have no or several solutions).

Expand $\norm{x-y}^2_A=\sum_ia_i(x^{(i)}-y^{(i)})^2$ and minimize term by term.

Proof T07 freeze all coordinates but one

1. Each $f_i$ is in $\Gz{\R}$. $f$ proper gives a point $\bar x$ with $f(\bar x)\lt+\infty$, hence every $f_i(\bar x^{(i)})\lt+\infty$, and no $f_i$ takes the value $-\infty$ (otherwise $f$ would); $f_i(t)=f(\bar x^{(1)},\dots,t,\dots,\bar x^{(N)})-\sum_{k\ne i}f_k(\bar x^{(k)})$ is lsc and convex as the restriction of $f$ to a line, up to a constant.

2. Split. For $y\in\R^N$: $f(y)+\tfrac12\norm{x-y}^2_A=\sum_{i=1}^N\Big[f_i(y^{(i)})+\tfrac{a_i}2\big(x^{(i)}-y^{(i)}\big)^2\Big]=\sum_{i=1}^Na_i\,\varphi_i(y^{(i)})$ with $\varphi_i(t)=a_i^{-1}f_i(t)+\tfrac12(x^{(i)}-t)^2$.

3. Minimize term by term. Each $\varphi_i$ has the unique minimizer $p^{(i)}=\prox_{a_i^{-1}f_i}(x^{(i)})$ (definition of the prox of $a_i^{-1}f_i\in\Gz\R$). For any $y$, $\sum_ia_i\varphi_i(y^{(i)})\ge\sum_ia_i\varphi_i(p^{(i)})$ with equality iff $y^{(i)}=p^{(i)}$ for all $i$ ($a_i\gt0$). Hence $p$ is the unique minimizer, i.e. $p=\prox_{A,f}(x)$. □

Example: weighted soft-thresholding

$f=\lambda\norm\cdot_1$, $A=\operatorname{Diag}(a_i)$: $p^{(i)}=\prox_{(\lambda/a_i)|\cdot|}(x^{(i)})=\operatorname{sign}(x^{(i)})\max(|x^{(i)}|-\lambda/a_i,0)$ (soft-thresholding). In VMFB the metric is $\theta_n^{-1}A(x_n)$, so the thresholds are $\theta_n\lambda/a_i(x_n)$: coordinates with a small curvature get a large step and a large threshold, consistently.

Variable metric forward–backward algorithm (VMFB)

Part VI · slide 36SlidesKey#
Problem, assumption, algorithm

Problem: minimization of $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ where $f=f_1+f_2$ with $f_1$ differentiable and $f_2$ convex, not necessarily differentiable.

Assumption: for every $y\in\H$, there exists a strongly positive self-adjoint operator $A(y):\H\to\H$ such that the quadratic function $h(x,y)=f_1(y)+\ps{\nabla f_1(y)}{x-y}+\tfrac12\norm{x-y}^2_{A(y)}$ is a majorant function of $f_1$ at $y$.

VMFB algorithm

Let $x_0\in\H$. For $n=0,1,\dots$

  1. $y_n=x_n-\theta_n\,A(x_n)^{-1}\nabla f_1(x_n)$ (forward step in the metric $A(x_n)$, stepsize $\theta_n$)
  2. $x_{n+1}=\prox_{\theta_n^{-1}A(x_n),\,f_2}(y_n)$ (backward step: prox of $f_2$ in the metric $\theta_n^{-1}A(x_n)$)

In one line: $x_{n+1}=\prox_{\theta_n^{-1}A(x_n),\,f_2}\big(x_n-\theta_nA(x_n)^{-1}\nabla f_1(x_n)\big)$.

⇒ $(\theta_n)_n$ acts as a stepsize parameter.

In one sentence

Forward–backward with the Euclidean geometry replaced, at every iteration, by the geometry of the current majorant of $f_1$: the gradient step is preconditioned by $A(x_n)^{-1}$ and the prox is taken in the matching metric.

Why the metric of the prox is $\theta_n^{-1}A(x_n)$

The prox must "undo" the same quadratic the forward step came from. The surrogate being minimized is $\ps{\nabla f_1(x_n)}{x-x_n}+\tfrac1{2\theta_n}\norm{x-x_n}^2_{A(x_n)}+f_2(x)$ (next card does the algebra for $\theta_n=1$); completing the square shows it equals $\tfrac1{2\theta_n}\norm{x-y_n}^2_{A(x_n)}+f_2(x)+\text{const}$ with $y_n$ the forward point, and $\tfrac1{2\theta_n}\norm\cdot^2_{A}=\tfrac12\norm\cdot^2_{\theta_n^{-1}A}$. A large $\theta_n$ means a long forward step and a weak quadratic in the prox (the prox trusts $f_2$ more); the two always move together, exactly as $\gamma$ appears both in $x_n-\gamma\nabla g(x_n)$ and in $\prox_{\gamma f}$ for FB.

Cost per iteration. One gradient of $f_1$, one solve with $A(x_n)$ and one prox in the metric $\theta_n^{-1}A(x_n)$. The prox is explicit when $A(x_n)$ is scalar (usual prox) or diagonal with $f_2$ separable (slide 35): then everything is coordinatewise and parallel. For a general $A(x_n)$ the prox in the metric has no closed form and must be approximated by inner iterations (inexact VMFB, with error conditions Extra); this is why the diagonal majorants of parallel MM are the natural partners of VMFB.

Statement

Let $\theta_n\equiv1$. According to the definition of the proximity operator, $$\begin{aligned}x_{n+1}&=\argmin_{x\in\H}\ \tfrac12\norm{x-x_n+A(x_n)^{-1}\nabla f_1(x_n)}^2_{A(x_n)}+f_2(x)\\&=\argmin_{x\in\H}\ \ps{x-x_n}{A(x_n)^{-1}\nabla f_1(x_n)}_{A(x_n)}+\tfrac12\norm{x-x_n}^2_{A(x_n)}+f_2(x)\\&=\argmin_{x\in\H}\ \ps{x-x_n}{\nabla f_1(x_n)}+\tfrac12\norm{x-x_n}^2_{A(x_n)}+f_2(x)\\&=\argmin_{x\in\H}\ h(x,x_n)+f_2(x).\end{aligned}$$

Particular case. Assume that $f_1$ is $\beta$-Lipschitz differentiable. According to the descent lemma, a possible choice for the metric is $A(x_n)\equiv\beta\,\Id$. Then the VMFB algorithm becomes equivalent to the usual forward–backward algorithm.

In one sentence

Completing the square in the $A$-norm shows that the prox step is the exact minimization of "quadratic majorant of $f_1$ plus $f_2$": VMFB is the MM algorithm for $f_1+f_2$ whose majorant leaves $f_2$ untouched.

Why it is true

Expanding $\norm{(x-x_n)+A^{-1}g}^2_A$ produces a cross term $2\ps{x-x_n}{A^{-1}g}_A$, a square $\norm{x-x_n}^2_A$ and a constant $\norm{A^{-1}g}^2_A$ which does not affect the argmin. The cross term is where the metric disappears: $\ps{u}{A^{-1}g}_A=\ps{u}{AA^{-1}g}=\ps ug$. What remains is the linear and quadratic part of the majorant $h(\cdot,x_n)$ of $f_1$ (up to the constant $f_1(x_n)$), plus $f_2$. By the sum rule of majorants, $h(\cdot,x_n)+f_2$ majorizes $f_1+f_2$ at $x_n$ ($f_2$ is its own majorant), so this is an MM step for $f$ and the sandwich inequality applies verbatim: $f(x_{n+1})\le f(x_n)$.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $\theta_n\equiv1$: the metric of the prox is exactly the curvature $A(x_n)$ of the majorant. With $\theta_n\lt1$ the surrogate $\ps{\nabla f_1(x_n)}{x-x_n}+\tfrac1{2\theta_n}\norm{x-x_n}^2_{A(x_n)}+f_2(x)$ is a more curved majorant (up to the constant $f_1(x_n)$), so the step is still MM. With $\theta_n\in\,]1,2[$ it need not be a majorant any more, and the decrease of $f$ comes from the sufficient-descent computation of vmfb-convergence (coefficient $\theta_n^{-1}-\tfrac12\gt0$), not from the sandwich.
  • $h(\cdot,x_n)$ is a majorant of $f_1$: this is what makes $h(\cdot,x_n)+f_2$ a majorant of $f$ (sum rule; $f_2$ is its own majorant).
  • Particular case, $\nabla f_1$ $\beta$-Lipschitz: this is what makes $A\equiv\beta\Id$ a legal curvature (descent lemma), and the prox in the metric $\beta\Id$ is $\prox_{\beta^{-1}f_2}$: FB with $\gamma=1/\beta$.

Convergence of VMFB

Part VI · slide 38 (whiteboard 39–40)Slides#
Statement

Assumptions.

  1. $f_1:\H\to\R$ is a coercive, differentiable function. $f_2\in\Gz{\H}$ is continuous on its domain.
  2. There exists $(\nu,\bar\nu)\in\,]0,+\infty[^2$ such that $(\forall n\in\N)\ \nu\Id\preccurlyeq A(x_n)\preccurlyeq\bar\nu\Id$.
  3. There exists $(\underline\theta,\bar\theta)\in\,]0,+\infty[^2$ such that $(\forall n\in\N)\ \underline\theta\le\theta_n\le2-\bar\theta$.

Sufficient descent property (in the form proved below; the slide's indexing is discussed in the note). There exists $(\mu_1,\mu_2)\in\,]0,+\infty[^2$ such that $$(\forall n\in\N)\qquad f(x_n)-f(x_{n+1})\ge\mu_1\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2\ge\mu_2\norm{\nabla f_1(x_{n+1})+r_{n+1}}^2,$$ where $r_{n+1}=-\nabla f_1(x_n)-\theta_n^{-1}A(x_n)(x_{n+1}-x_n)\in\partial f_2(x_{n+1})$ is the subgradient produced by the prox step.

Convergence theorem (in finite dimension).

  1. $\nabla f_1(x_n)+r_n\to0$, with $r_n\in\partial f_2(x_n)$ the subgradient produced by the prox step ($n\ge1$), and $f(x_n)\searrow f(\tilde x)$ where $\tilde x$ is a critical point of $f$ (i.e. $0\in\nabla f_1(\tilde x)+\partial f_2(\tilde x)$).
  2. If $f_1$ is convex, any sequential cluster point of $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ is a minimizer of $f$.
  3. If $f$ is strictly convex, then $(x_n)_n\to\hat x$ where $\hat x$ is a minimizer of $f$.
In one sentence

The MM quadratic proof goes through with one change: the optimality condition of the prox step is an inclusion, which produces a subgradient $r_{n+1}\in\partial f_2(x_{n+1})$ playing the role of the gradient, and the residual $\nabla f_1+r$ replaces $\nabla f$.

Why it is true

Two inequalities add up. The majorant of $f_1$ at $x_n$, evaluated at $x_{n+1}$, bounds $f_1(x_{n+1})$. The prox step says $x_{n+1}$ minimizes $f_2+\text{(affine)}+\tfrac1{2\theta_n}\norm{\cdot-x_n}^2_{A_n}$; Fermat gives a subgradient $r_{n+1}=-\nabla f_1(x_n)-\theta_n^{-1}A_n(x_{n+1}-x_n)\in\partial f_2(x_{n+1})$, and the subgradient inequality at $x_{n+1}$ tested at $x_n$ bounds $f_2(x_{n+1})$ by $f_2(x_n)$ minus the linear term $\ps{\nabla f_1(x_n)}{x_{n+1}-x_n}$ minus $\theta_n^{-1}\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2_{A_n}$. Summing, the linear terms cancel and the coefficient $\theta_n^{-1}-\tfrac12$ appears exactly as in the smooth case: this is the sufficient descent in $\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2$. The residual $\nabla f_1(x_{n+1})+r_{n+1}$ is then a combination of $\nabla f_1(x_{n+1})-\nabla f_1(x_n)$ and $A_n(x_{n+1}-x_n)$, both controlled by the step when $\nabla f_1$ is Lipschitz. The rest is compactness and telescoping, plus one new ingredient to identify the limit: the graph of $\partial f_2$ is closed, so the limit of $r_{n_k}\in\partial f_2(x_{n_k})$ is a subgradient at $\tilde x$.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $f_2\in\Gz\H$: the prox in a metric exists and is unique; $\partial f_2$ has a closed graph (lsc + convex), which identifies the limit as a critical point.
  • $f_2$ continuous on its domain: the slide's convenient way to get $f(x_{n_k})\to f(\tilde x)$ in step 7 (lower semicontinuity alone gives only $f(\tilde x)\le\lim f(x_n)$). It is not indispensable here: the subgradients $r_{n_k}\in\partial f_2(x_{n_k})$ produced by the prox steps are bounded on the compact level set, so the subgradient inequality tested at $\tilde x$, $f_2(\tilde x)\ge f_2(x_{n_k})+\ps{r_{n_k}}{\tilde x-x_{n_k}}$, gives $\limsup f_2(x_{n_k})\le f_2(\tilde x)$, and lsc gives the reverse inequality.
  • Coercivity: as before, the level set $\{f\le f(x_0)\}$ must be bounded. The slide says "$f_1$ coercive"; what the proof uses is coercivity of $f=f_1+f_2$, which follows when $f_2$ is bounded below (e.g. $\lambda\norm\cdot_1$, an indicator), the typical case. (Coercivity of $f_1$ alone does not imply that of $f_1+f_2$: $f_1=\sqrt{1+\norm\cdot^2}$, differentiable and coercive with a $1$-Lipschitz gradient, and $f_2=-2\ps u\cdot$ with $\norm u=1$, continuous and in $\Gz\H$, give $f_1(tu)+f_2(tu)=\sqrt{1+t^2}-2t\to-\infty$ as $t\to+\infty$.)
  • $\nu,\bar\nu,\underline\theta,\bar\theta$: same roles as in mm-quadratic-convergence; $\theta_n\lt2$ keeps $\theta_n^{-1}-\tfrac12\gt0$.
  • Lipschitz continuity of $\nabla f_1$ (not written on the slide, but needed for the second inequality): $\norm{\nabla f_1(x_{n+1})-\nabla f_1(x_n)}\le\beta\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}$. For a convex $f_1$ it follows from the uniform majorant bound $A(x_n)\preccurlyeq\bar\nu\Id$ (descent-lemma characterization), in general it is an extra assumption, standard in the VMFB literature Extra.
  • Item 2 says "$f_1$ convex": then $f=f_1+f_2$ is convex and critical points (in the sense $0\in\nabla f_1+\partial f_2$) are minimizers by Fermat. Item 3 asks $f$ strictly convex (so that the minimizer is unique and the whole sequence converges).

1. The prox step as an argmin, and its Fermat rule. Let $A_n=A(x_n)$, $g_n=\nabla f_1(x_n)$, $s_n=x_{n+1}-x_n$. By definition, $x_{n+1}$ minimizes $q_n(x)=f_2(x)+\tfrac12\norm{x-x_n+\theta_nA_n^{-1}g_n}^2_{\theta_n^{-1}A_n}$; completing the square as in vmfb-mm-link (with $\theta_n^{-1}A_n$ in place of $A_n$), $q_n(x)=f_2(x)+\ps{g_n}{x-x_n}+\tfrac1{2\theta_n}\norm{x-x_n}^2_{A_n}+\text{const}$. Fermat's rule with the sum rule ($f_2\in\Gz\H$, the rest finite and differentiable): $0\in\partial f_2(x_{n+1})+g_n+\theta_n^{-1}A_ns_n$, i.e. $$r_{n+1}:=-g_n-\theta_n^{-1}A_ns_n\in\partial f_2(x_{n+1}).$$

2. Subgradient inequality at $x_{n+1}$, tested at $x_n$. $f_2(x_n)\ge f_2(x_{n+1})+\ps{r_{n+1}}{x_n-x_{n+1}}=f_2(x_{n+1})+\ps{g_n}{s_n}+\theta_n^{-1}\norm{s_n}^2_{A_n}$.

3. Majorization of $f_1$ at $x_{n+1}$. $f_1(x_{n+1})\le f_1(x_n)+\ps{g_n}{s_n}+\tfrac12\norm{s_n}^2_{A_n}$.

4. Add. $f(x_{n+1})=f_1(x_{n+1})+f_2(x_{n+1})\le f_1(x_n)+f_2(x_n)+\ps{g_n}{s_n}-\ps{g_n}{s_n}+\big(\tfrac12-\theta_n^{-1}\big)\norm{s_n}^2_{A_n}$, i.e. $$f(x_n)-f(x_{n+1})\ge\Big(\theta_n^{-1}-\frac12\Big)\norm{s_n}^2_{A_n}\ge\underbrace{\Big(\frac1{2-\bar\theta}-\frac12\Big)\nu}_{\mu_1}\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2 .$$ Same constant $\mu_1$ as in the smooth case; the linear terms cancelled exactly (this is the T06 move: two inequalities in opposite directions, added).

5. The residual is controlled by the step. $\nabla f_1(x_{n+1})+r_{n+1}=\big(\nabla f_1(x_{n+1})-\nabla f_1(x_n)\big)-\theta_n^{-1}A_ns_n$, so with $\nabla f_1$ $\beta$-Lipschitz: $\norm{\nabla f_1(x_{n+1})+r_{n+1}}\le(\beta+\underline\theta^{-1}\bar\nu)\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}$, hence $\mu_1\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2\ge\mu_2\norm{\nabla f_1(x_{n+1})+r_{n+1}}^2$ with $\mu_2=\mu_1/(\beta+\underline\theta^{-1}\bar\nu)^2$.

6. Compactness. $(f(x_n))$ decreases, so $x_n\in\lev_{\le f(x_0)}f$, bounded by coercivity of $f$ and closed since $f$ is lsc: compact in finite dimension. $f$ is bounded below there (lsc on a compact set), so $f(x_n)$ converges, and a subsequence $x_{n_k}\to\tilde x$. Telescoping the descent inequality: $\sum_n\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2\lt\infty$, so $\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}\to0$ and $\nabla f_1(x_n)+r_n\to0$.

7. $f(x_n)\searrow f(\tilde x)$. By lsc, $f_2(\tilde x)\le\liminf f_2(x_{n_k})\lt+\infty$ (since $f_1$ is bounded on the compact set and $f(x_{n_k})\le f(x_0)$), so $\tilde x\in\dom f_2$; by continuity of $f_2$ on its domain, $f_2(x_{n_k})\to f_2(\tilde x)$, and $f_1(x_{n_k})\to f_1(\tilde x)$ by continuity of $f_1$. Hence $f(x_{n_k})\to f(\tilde x)$ and the whole decreasing sequence $f(x_n)$ converges to $f(\tilde x)$. (The continuity of $f_2$ can be avoided: the prox subgradients $r_{n_k}\in\partial f_2(x_{n_k})$ are bounded, because $\nabla f_1$ is continuous on the compact level set, $\norm{A_n}\le\bar\nu$, $\theta_n\ge\underline\theta$ and $x_{n+1}-x_n\to0$; the subgradient inequality $f_2(\tilde x)\ge f_2(x_{n_k})+\ps{r_{n_k}}{\tilde x-x_{n_k}}$ then gives $\limsup f_2(x_{n_k})\le f_2(\tilde x)$, and lsc gives the reverse.)

8. $\tilde x$ is a critical point. Along the subsequence, $r_{n_k}\in\partial f_2(x_{n_k})$ and $r_{n_k}=\big(\nabla f_1(x_{n_k})+r_{n_k}\big)-\nabla f_1(x_{n_k})\to-\nabla f_1(\tilde x)$ (continuity of $\nabla f_1$). The graph of $\partial f_2$ is closed: if $x_k\to x$, $r_k\to r$ with $r_k\in\partial f_2(x_k)$ and $f_2(x_k)\to f_2(x)$, then for every $y$, $f_2(y)\ge f_2(x_k)+\ps{r_k}{y-x_k}\to f_2(x)+\ps r{y-x}$, so $r\in\partial f_2(x)$ (lsc of $f_2$ would even suffice: $f_2(y)\ge\limsup\big(f_2(x_k)+\ps{r_k}{y-x_k}\big)\ge\liminf f_2(x_k)+\ps r{y-x}\ge f_2(x)+\ps r{y-x}$). Hence $-\nabla f_1(\tilde x)\in\partial f_2(\tilde x)$, i.e. $0\in\nabla f_1(\tilde x)+\partial f_2(\tilde x)$: item 1.

9. Convex cases. If $f_1$ is convex, $f$ is convex and $\partial f=\nabla f_1+\partial f_2$ (sum rule with $f_1$ finite and differentiable), so $0\in\partial f(\tilde x)$ is Fermat's rule: every cluster point is a minimizer (item 2). If $f$ is strictly convex, the minimizer $\hat x$ is unique, every cluster point of the bounded sequence equals $\hat x$, hence $x_n\to\hat x$ (item 3). □

Pitfall

"Critical point" for a nonsmooth $f$ means $0\in\nabla f_1(\tilde x)+\partial f_2(\tilde x)$, not $\nabla f_1(\tilde x)=0$. And the residual that goes to zero is $\nabla f_1(x_n)+r_n$ with the specific subgradient produced by the prox step; $\partial f_2(x_n)$ may contain other elements that do not go to zero.

What to retain

  • Majorant at $y$: $f\le h(\cdot,y)$ everywhere and $f(y)=h(y,y)$. Calculus: sums always, products of nonnegative functions, composition with an increasing $\varphi$.
  • MM step $x_{n+1}\in\Argmin h(\cdot,x_n)$ and the sandwich $f(x_{n+1})\le h(x_{n+1},x_n)\le h(x_n,x_n)=f(x_n)$: decrease is free, convergence needs uniform curvature bounds.
  • Concave ⇒ under its tangents (T20): $f(x)\le f(y)+\ps{t}{x-y}$, $-t\in\partial(-f)(y)$. Convex ⇒ above its tangents. Never mix them.
  • Lipschitz gradient ⇒ parabola $f(y)+\ps{\nabla f(y)}{x-y}+\tfrac\mu2\norm{x-y}^2$, $\mu\ge\beta$; Hessian bound $A\succcurlyeq\nabla^2f$ everywhere ⇒ curvature $A$. Special cases of MM quadratic: $A\equiv\beta\Id$ ⇒ gradient descent with step $\theta_n/\beta\in\,]0,2/\beta[$; $A=\nabla^2f(x_n)$ ⇒ Newton, an MM step only when this local model majorizes $f$ ($f$ quadratic with $\nabla^2f\succ0$: one step), so MM with a dominating curvature is a "safe Newton"; majorizing only $f_1$ with $A\equiv\beta\Id$ and keeping $f_2$ ⇒ forward–backward with $\gamma=1/\beta$.
  • Jensen $\psi(\sum\omega_ix_i)\le\sum\omega_i\psi(x_i)$ pushes a convex function inside a weighted sum (T21); it gives $H^\top H\preccurlyeq\operatorname{Diag}(|H|^\top|H|\mathbf1)$.
  • Five exercise inequalities: $x^q$, $\log$, $-\norm\cdot$ by tangents of concave functions; $\exp$ by Jensen; $2xz\le x^2t/y+z^2y/t$ by $(x/y-z/t)^2\ge0$.
  • Half-quadratic: $\psi(\sqrt\cdot)$ concave, $\dot\psi\ge0$, $\omega=\dot\psi/\mathrm{id}$ finite at $0$ ⇒ $\psi(|x|)\le\psi(|y|)+\dot f(y)(x-y)+\tfrac12\omega(|y|)(x-y)^2$. Proof: tangent in $x^2$, then $x^2-y^2=(x-y)^2+2y(x-y)$. Lab: $\psi=\sqrt{1+u^2/\delta^2}$, $\omega(u)=1/(\delta^2\sqrt{1+u^2/\delta^2})$.
  • MM quadratic: $x_{n+1}=x_n-\theta_nA(x_n)^{-1}\nabla f(x_n)$, $\theta_n\in\,]0,2[$. Sufficient descent $f(x_n)-f(x_{n+1})\ge(\tfrac1{2-\bar\theta}-\tfrac12)\nu\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2\ge\mu_2\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2$, $\sqrt{\mu_1/\mu_2}=\underline\theta^{-1}\bar\nu$.
  • Convergence recipe: majorize at the new point, insert the update, $\theta_n\lt2$ ⇒ positive coefficient, $\nu,\bar\nu$ ⇒ $\mu_1,\mu_2$, coercive ⇒ compact level set, telescope ⇒ $\nabla f(x_n)\to0$; convex ⇒ minimizers; strictly convex ⇒ $x_n\to\hat x$.
  • Subspace MM / 3MG: $u_n=-(D_n^\top A(x_n)D_n)^\dagger D_n^\top\nabla f(x_n)$, $D_n=[-\nabla f(x_n)\,|\,x_n-x_{n-1}]$; the gradient direction alone (gradient-related: $\ps{\nabla f}{d^1}\le-\gamma_0\norm{\nabla f}^2$, $\norm{d^1}\le\gamma_1\norm{\nabla f}$) certifies descent; on quadratics with $A=Q$ it is conjugate gradient.
  • Block MM: update block $j_n$ with $A_{j_n}(x_n)$; quasi-cyclic rule $\{1,\dots,J\}\subset\{j_n,\dots,j_{n+K-1}\}$; descent controls $\norm{\nabla_{j_n}f(x_n)}$ and quasi-cyclicity + vanishing steps + uniform continuity of $\nabla f$ give $\nabla f(x_n)\to0$.
  • Parallel MM: a diagonal (Jensen) majorant $B(x)\succcurlyeq A(x)$ ⇒ coordinatewise steps $x_i-\theta\,\partial_if/b_i$; looser, but embarrassingly parallel. What each variant buys in large scale: subspace (3MG) = a $2\times2$ system instead of an inner solver; block = a small system on one block, sequential; parallel = no system at all, looser majorant; VMFB = keeps $f_2$ exactly.
  • Prox in a metric: $\prox_{A,f}=\operatorname{argmin}f+\tfrac12\norm{x-\cdot}^2_A$; $\prox_{\alpha^{-1}\Id,f}=\prox_{\alpha f}$; $\prox_{A,f}(x)=A^{-1/2}\prox_{f\circ A^{-1/2}}(A^{1/2}x)$; separable $f$ + diagonal $A$ ⇒ $\prox_{a_i^{-1}f_i}$ componentwise.
  • VMFB: $x_{n+1}=\prox_{\theta_n^{-1}A(x_n),f_2}(x_n-\theta_nA(x_n)^{-1}\nabla f_1(x_n))$ = MM with $f_2$ kept ($\theta_n=1$: argmin $h(\cdot,x_n)+f_2$); $A\equiv\beta\Id$ gives FB with $\gamma=1/\beta$ (the slide's $\beta^{-1}\Id$ is a typo). Convergence: residual $\nabla f_1(x_n)+r_n\to0$ with $r_n\in\partial f_2(x_n)$ the subgradient produced by the prox step (the descent inequality of slide 38 must be read with $r_{n+1}\in\partial f_2(x_{n+1})$).
  • IRLS = half-quadratic majorant of a kink: weights $1/|y|$ blow up at $0$ (Weiszfeld); VMFB avoids it by keeping $f_2$ exact.

Self-check

Ten questions

Why does the MM algorithm decrease $f$ without any convexity assumption?

$f(x_{n+1})\le h(x_{n+1},x_n)$ (majorant above $f$), $h(x_{n+1},x_n)\le h(x_n,x_n)$ ($x_{n+1}$ minimizes $h(\cdot,x_n)$ and $x_n$ is a competitor), $h(x_n,x_n)=f(x_n)$ (touching). Only the two defining properties of a majorant are used.

Give a quadratic majorant of a function with $\beta$-Lipschitz gradient, and the MM step it produces.

$h(x,y)=f(y)+\ps{\nabla f(y)}{x-y}+\tfrac\mu2\norm{x-y}^2$ with $\mu\ge\beta$ (descent lemma). The MM step is $x_{n+1}=x_n-\mu^{-1}\nabla f(x_n)$: gradient descent with step $1/\mu$. With the relaxation $\theta_n\in\,]0,2[$ the step is $\theta_n/\mu$, covering $]0,2/\beta[$.

Which choices of the majorant turn MM into gradient descent, into Newton's method, into forward–backward? Is Newton an MM method?

The quadratic majorant with $A\equiv\beta\Id$ (descent lemma) gives $x_{n+1}=x_n-\tfrac{\theta_n}{\beta}\nabla f(x_n)$: gradient descent. Majorizing only $f_1$ with $A\equiv\beta\Id$ and keeping $f_2$ gives $x_{n+1}=\prox_{\beta^{-1}f_2}\big(x_n-\beta^{-1}\nabla f_1(x_n)\big)$: forward–backward. Newton uses $A=\nabla^2f(x_n)$, a legitimate MM curvature only when its local quadratic model $f(x_n)+\ps{\nabla f(x_n)}{x-x_n}+\tfrac12\norm{x-x_n}^2_{\nabla^2f(x_n)}$ majorizes $f$ (and $\nabla^2f(x_n)\succ0$, so that the step exists); a sufficient condition is that $\nabla^2f(x_n)$ dominates $\nabla^2f$ everywhere, as for a strictly convex quadratic $f$, where one step suffices. In general this fails, and Newton can increase $f$ ($f=-\cos$ near $\pi$). MM quadratic with a dominating $A(x_n)$ is the "safe Newton": more cautious steps, guaranteed decrease.

State the four assumptions of the half-quadratic theorem and the weight $\omega$. What does assumption (iv) prevent?

(i) $\psi$ differentiable on $]0,+\infty[$; (ii) $\psi(\sqrt\cdot)$ concave; (iii) $\dot\psi\ge0$; (iv) $\omega(x)=\dot\psi(x)/x$ has a finite limit at $0^+$. Then $\psi(|x|)\le\psi(|y|)+\dot f(y)(x-y)+\tfrac12\omega(|y|)(x-y)^2$. (iv) prevents the curvature from blowing up at $y=0$, which is what happens for $\psi(u)=u$ ($\omega=1/u$): the IRLS/Weiszfeld singularity.

In the proof of the sufficient descent of MM quadratic, where exactly is $\theta_n\lt2$ used?

After inserting the update into the majorant, $f(x_{n+1})\le f(x_n)-(\theta_n^{-1}-\tfrac12)\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2_{A(x_n)}$. The coefficient $\theta_n^{-1}-\tfrac12$ is positive iff $\theta_n\lt2$, and uniformly so ($\ge\tfrac1{2-\bar\theta}-\tfrac12$) iff $\theta_n\le2-\bar\theta$. Along the step direction the majorant is a parabola minimal at $\theta=1$ and back to $f(x_n)$ at $\theta=2$.

Why do the gradients $\nabla f(x_n)$ tend to zero, and why is that not the same as $x_n$ converging?

Summing $f(x_n)-f(x_{n+1})\ge\mu_2\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2$ gives $\mu_2\sum\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2\le f(x_0)-\inf f\lt\infty$ (coercive $f$ is bounded below on the level set), so $\nabla f(x_n)\to0$. This only says that cluster points are stationary; for nonconvex $f$ different starting points give different limits, and convergence of the whole sequence needs strict convexity (unique minimizer) in the theorem.

Write the closed form of the subspace step and explain why the first direction $-\nabla f(x_n)$ alone guarantees sufficient descent.

$u_n=-(D_n^\top A(x_n)D_n)^\dagger D_n^\top\nabla f(x_n)$, $x_{n+1}=x_n+D_nu_n$. The line $x_n+td_n^1$ lies in the search space, so $h(x_{n+1},x_n)\le\min_th(x_n+td_n^1,x_n)=f(x_n)-\ps{\nabla f}{d^1}^2/(2\norm{d^1}^2_{A})\le f(x_n)-\tfrac{\gamma_0^2}{2\bar\nu\gamma_1^2}\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2$ by the gradient-related condition. The other directions can only lower the minimum further.

Block-coordinate MM with a quasi-cyclic rule: how does one pass from $\nabla_{j_n}f(x_n)\to0$ to $\nabla f(x_n)\to0$?

Fix a block $j$; within $K$ iterations after $n$ there is $k$ with $j_k=j$, so $\norm{\nabla_jf(x_k)}$ is small. The steps vanish (telescoping), so $\norm{x_k-x_n}\le K\max_{i\ge n}\norm{x_{i+1}-x_i}\to0$; $\nabla f$ is uniformly continuous on the compact level set, so $\norm{\nabla_jf(x_n)-\nabla_jf(x_k)}\to0$. Hence every partial gradient at $x_n$ tends to $0$.

Prove $H^\top H\preccurlyeq\operatorname{Diag}(b)$ with $b_i=\sum_m|H_{mi}|\sum_p|H_{mp}|$, and say what it is good for.

Row $m$: $(\sum_iH_{mi}d_i)^2=r_m^2(\sum_i\tfrac{|H_{mi}|}{r_m}\operatorname{sign}(H_{mi})d_i)^2\le r_m^2\sum_i\tfrac{|H_{mi}|}{r_m}d_i^2=r_m\sum_i|H_{mi}|d_i^2$ (Jensen for the square, weights $|H_{mi}|/r_m$). Sum over $m$: $\norm{Hd}^2\le\sum_ib_id_i^2$. A diagonal curvature gives a separable majorant, minimized coordinatewise in parallel (parallel MM, lab §3.5), and an explicit prox in VMFB when $f_2$ is separable.

Show that VMFB with $\theta_n=1$ is an MM algorithm, and identify the choice of metric that gives back forward–backward.

$x_{n+1}=\prox_{A,f_2}(x_n-A^{-1}\nabla f_1(x_n))=\operatorname{argmin}\tfrac12\norm{x-x_n+A^{-1}\nabla f_1(x_n)}^2_A+f_2(x)$; expand the square, drop the constant, use $\ps{u}{A^{-1}g}_A=\ps ug$: $=\operatorname{argmin}\ps{x-x_n}{\nabla f_1(x_n)}+\tfrac12\norm{x-x_n}^2_A+f_2(x)=\operatorname{argmin}h(x,x_n)+f_2(x)$, an MM step with the majorant $h(\cdot,x_n)+f_2$. With $\nabla f_1$ $\beta$-Lipschitz, $A\equiv\beta\Id$ (not $\beta^{-1}\Id$ as printed on slide 37) gives $x_{n+1}=\prox_{\beta^{-1}f_2}(x_n-\beta^{-1}\nabla f_1(x_n))$: FB with $\gamma=1/\beta$.
