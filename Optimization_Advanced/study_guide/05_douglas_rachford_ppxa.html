Douglas–Rachford and PPXA+

When neither $f$ nor $g$ is smooth, compose reflections instead of proxes: $T=\rprox_{\gamma f}\circ\rprox_{\gamma g}$ is nonexpansive, its fixed points are the solutions seen through $\prox_{\gamma g}$, and Krasnosel'skii–Mann does the rest. Lifting to a product space turns the same algorithm into a parallel one.

Overview

Where we are

Page 4 ended with Forward–Backward: it solves $0\in\partial f(x)+\nabla g(x)$ by composing a prox with a gradient step, and converges because that composition is averaged. This page removes the last smoothness assumption.

The chain of the course so far is Fermat's rule $0\in\partial(f+g)(\hat x)$ → a zero of a sum of operators $0\in\partial f(\hat x)+\partial g(\hat x)$ → a fixed point of a nonexpansive map → Fejér monotonicity and Krasnosel'skii–Mann. Every link is in place except one: when both $f$ and $g$ are nonsmooth, which nonexpansive map has the solutions as fixed points? The obvious candidate $\prox_{\gamma f}\circ\prox_{\gamma g}$ is nonexpansive but its fixed points are not the minimizers of $f+g$ (try $f=\abs{\cdot}$, $g=\tfrac12(\cdot-3)^2$, $\gamma=1$: the composition fixes $1$, the minimizer is $2$).

The invention of this page (Lions–Mercier 1979, after Douglas and Rachford 1956) has two parts, and both are worth learning by heart:

  • Reflect instead of projecting. $\rprox_{\gamma f}=2\prox_{\gamma f}-\Id$ is nonexpansive because $\prox_{\gamma f}$ is firmly nonexpansive (T11 reflect to compose). Reflections lose the averagedness of the prox, but their composition has the right fixed points (seen through $\prox_{\gamma g}$); averaging is restored at the end by the relaxation.
  • Solutions are shadows of fixed points. $\zer(\partial f+\partial g)=\prox_{\gamma g}(\Fix T)$ with $T=\rprox_{\gamma f}\circ\rprox_{\gamma g}$: a fixed point of $T$ is not a solution, its image under $\prox_{\gamma g}$ is (T15 zeros ⇔ fixed points).

Averaging $T$ with the identity (relaxation $\lambda_n/2$) gives the Douglas–Rachford algorithm, Krasnosel'skii–Mann in disguise (T22 count averagedness). The second half of the page applies it to $f=\ind_E$ with $E=\ran L$ a subspace: the projection $P_E$ becomes a least-squares solve, the algorithm becomes the parallel Douglas–Rachford, and on a product space it becomes PPXA+, where the $m$ proxes run simultaneously (T16 product-space lifting). Page 6 will show that Douglas–Rachford applied to a dual problem is ADMM.

The one picture

For two sets $A,B$ ($f=\ind_A$, $g=\ind_B$) one Douglas–Rachford step is: project $x$ on $B$ (get $y$), reflect through it ($2y-x$), project the reflection on $A$ (get $z$), then move $x$ by $z-y$. The point $x$ itself never has to be in $A$ or $B$: it is a bookkeeping variable whose shadow $y=P_Bx$ converges to $A\cap B$. The hero picture above shows exactly one such step.

Two nonsmooth functions: the Douglas–Rachford algorithm

Minimize $f+g$ with $f,g\in\Gz{\H}$, both possibly nonsmooth, each with a computable prox. Three results: the reflected prox is nonexpansive; solutions are shadows of the fixed points of $\rprox_{\gamma f}\circ\rprox_{\gamma g}$; the relaxed iteration converges.

Remark

Which algorithm needs what: a splitting algorithm for two nonsmooth functions

Motivation

Let $f\in\Gz{\H}$ and $g\in\Gz{\H}$. We want to $$\operatorname*{minimize}_{x\in\H}\ f(x)+g(x).$$ Possible solutions and what they require:

AlgorithmNeedsIteration
gradient descent$f+g$ smooth (Lipschitz gradient)$x_{n+1}=x_n-\gamma\nabla(f+g)(x_n)$

Can we find a splitting algorithm when both $f$ and $g$ are nonsmooth? Splitting means: the algorithm only ever calls $\prox_{\gamma f}$ and $\prox_{\gamma g}$ separately, never a prox of the sum.

In one sentence

Knowing the two proxes separately is not knowing the prox of the sum: a splitting algorithm has to combine them, and the right combination is a composition of reflections, not of proxes.

Why the question is not trivial

The prox of a sum is in general not computable from the two proxes: $\prox_{\gamma(f+g)}\neq\prox_{\gamma f}\circ\prox_{\gamma g}$ (page 3). Worse, the fixed points of $\prox_{\gamma f}\circ\prox_{\gamma g}$ are not minimizers of $f+g$. Take $f=\abs{\cdot}$, $g=\tfrac12(\cdot-3)^2$ on $\R$, $\gamma=1$: $\prox_g x=\tfrac{x+3}{2}$, $\prox_f=\operatorname{soft}_1$, and $\prox_f(\prox_g x)=x$ gives $\tfrac{x+3}{2}-1=x$, i.e. $x=1$, whereas the minimizer is $\hat x=\prox_{\abs\cdot}(3)=2$. So "alternate the two proxes" is the wrong algorithm for minimization. (It is the right one for feasibility, $f=\ind_A$, $g=\ind_B$: the fixed points of $P_AP_B$ are exactly $A\cap B$ when the intersection is nonempty, which is why alternating projections works there, and why the general case needs a new idea.)

A second motivation is the feasibility problem itself: find $x\in A\cap B$ for two closed convex sets, i.e. minimize $\ind_A+\ind_B$. The algorithm of this page then reads $y_n=P_Bx_n$, $z_n=P_A(2y_n-x_n)$, $x_{n+1}=x_n+\lambda_n(z_n-y_n)$: a projection, a reflection $2y_n-x_n$ through $B$, another projection. Reflections, not projections, are the geometric heart of Douglas–Rachford (widget in the algorithm card).

To remember

GD needs the sum smooth, PPA needs the sum proximable, FB needs one smooth part. DR needs only the two proxes, and it composes their reflections. No smoothness is ever needed, because each prox is firmly nonexpansive for every $\gamma\gt0$, whatever $f$ and $g$ are.

Proposition

The reflected prox $\rprox_{\gamma f}=2\prox_{\gamma f}-\Id$ is nonexpansive

Part V.2 · slide 3 · Part V · slide 31SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $\gamma\in\,]0,+\infty[$ and let $f\in\Gz{\H}$. The reflection of the proximity operator, defined as $$\rprox_{\gamma f}=2\prox_{\gamma f}-\Id,$$ is nonexpansive: $(\forall x,y\in\H)\ \norm{\rprox_{\gamma f}x-\rprox_{\gamma f}y}\le\norm{x-y}$.

In one sentence

$\prox_{\gamma f}x$ is the midpoint of $x$ and $\rprox_{\gamma f}x$; "firmly nonexpansive" means precisely "midpoint of the identity and a nonexpansive map", so the reflection is that nonexpansive map.

Why it is true

On page 4, $\prox_{\gamma f}$ was shown to be firmly nonexpansive, i.e. $\tfrac12$-averaged: by the definition of an $\alpha$-averaged operator there is a nonexpansive $R$ with $\prox_{\gamma f}=\tfrac12\Id+\tfrac12R$. Solve for $R$: $R=2\prox_{\gamma f}-\Id=\rprox_{\gamma f}$. That is the whole proof; the computation below shows that the two statements are in fact equivalent, which is the content of the averaged inequality with $\alpha=\tfrac12$.

The mechanism is a parallelogram. Write $p=\prox_{\gamma f}x$, $q=\prox_{\gamma f}y$ and split the displacement $x-y$ into its prox part and its residual part: $x-y=a+b$ with $a=p-q$ and $b=(x-p)-(y-q)$. The reflection keeps $a$ and flips $b$: $\rprox_{\gamma f}x-\rprox_{\gamma f}y=2a-(a+b)=a-b$. The two diagonals of the parallelogram built on $a$ and $b$ satisfy $\norm{a+b}^2-\norm{a-b}^2=4\ps{a}{b}$, so flipping $b$ cannot lengthen the displacement as soon as $\ps{a}{b}\ge0$. That is exactly firm nonexpansiveness: $x-p\in\gamma\partial f(p)$ and $y-q\in\gamma\partial f(q)$, so $b$ is a difference of subgradients, and the monotonicity of $\partial f$ makes it form a non-obtuse angle with $a=p-q$. For the projection onto a disc (widget below), $R_Cx=2P_Cx-x$ is the mirror image of $x$ in the tangent line at $P_Cx$.

The sentence to say out loud: the reflection flips the residual part of the displacement; flipping a vector that makes a non-obtuse angle with the other part never lengthens the sum.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $f\in\Gz{\H}$ and $\gamma\gt0$: $\gamma f\in\Gz{\H}$, so $\prox_{\gamma f}$ is well defined and single-valued everywhere, and page 4's firm nonexpansiveness applies to it.
  • Nothing else. The result is unconditional: any prox can be reflected.
Hint — try it first

Write $p=\prox_{\gamma f}x$, $q=\prox_{\gamma f}y$ and expand $\norm{(2p-x)-(2q-y)}^2$. The cross term is exactly what firm nonexpansiveness controls.

Proof T11 reflect to compose T12 λ-identity (α = ½)

1. Name the objects. Fix $x,y\in\H$, set $p=\prox_{\gamma f}x$, $q=\prox_{\gamma f}y$, $R=\rprox_{\gamma f}$. Then $Rx-Ry=2(p-q)-(x-y)$.

2. Expand. $$\norm{Rx-Ry}^2=4\norm{p-q}^2-4\ps{p-q}{x-y}+\norm{x-y}^2 .$$

3. Firm nonexpansiveness. Page 4 proved, from the monotonicity of $\partial(\gamma f)$, that $\ps{p-q}{x-y}\ge\norm{p-q}^2$ (this is the inequality $\ps{\prox u_1'-\prox u_2'}{u_1'-u_2'}\ge\norm{\prox u_1'-\prox u_2'}^2$ of the slides). Hence $4\norm{p-q}^2-4\ps{p-q}{x-y}\le0$.

4. Conclude. $\norm{Rx-Ry}^2\le\norm{x-y}^2$: $R$ is nonexpansive.

5. Equivalence (bonus). Step 2 is an identity, so $R$ nonexpansive $\iff$ $\ps{p-q}{x-y}\ge\norm{p-q}^2$ $\iff$ $\prox_{\gamma f}$ firmly nonexpansive. Equivalently, $\norm{\tfrac12(x-y)+\tfrac12(Rx-Ry)}^2=\tfrac12\norm{x-y}^2+\tfrac12\norm{Rx-Ry}^2-\tfrac14\norm{(x-y)-(Rx-Ry)}^2$ (the λ-identity with $\lambda=\tfrac12$) is the averaged inequality of page 4 with $\alpha=\tfrac12$.

Trick

Reflect to compose (T11). Composing two proxes is harmless for convergence (the composition is still averaged) but fatal for the target: $\Fix(\prox_{\gamma f}\prox_{\gamma g})\neq\zer(\partial f+\partial g)$ in general (overview example: $1$ instead of $2$; projections onto two intersecting sets are the lucky exception). Extra In fact $\Fix(\prox_{\gamma f}\prox_{\gamma g})=\Argmin\big(f+\env{\gamma}{g}\big)$: the composition minimizes $f$ plus the Moreau envelope of $g$, not $f+g$. Composing the reflections keeps nonexpansiveness and gets the target right, through the shadow: $\zer(\partial f+\partial g)=\prox_{\gamma g}\big(\Fix(\rprox_{\gamma f}\rprox_{\gamma g})\big)$. Reflections are only nonexpansive, so average back with the identity, $\tfrac12(\Id+T)$, and let Krasnosel'skii–Mann converge. Pattern: write each firmly nonexpansive $P$ as $\tfrac12(\Id+R)$ with $R=2P-\Id$, compose the $R$'s, average at the end. This is the entire design of Douglas–Rachford.

Pitfall

$\rprox_{\gamma f}$ is nonexpansive but not averaged in general: the reflection through a line is an isometry ($\norm{Rx-Ry}=\norm{x-y}$), and the Picard iteration of a composition of two such reflections is a rotation that never converges (see Peaceman–Rachford). Nonexpansive is enough to compose, not enough to iterate: averaging must come back at the end.

The readout checks the identity of the proof, $\norm{x-y}^2-\norm{R_Cx-R_Cy}^2=4\big(\ps{P_Cx-P_Cy}{x-y}-\norm{P_Cx-P_Cy}^2\big)\ge0$: firm nonexpansiveness of $P_C$ is exactly what keeps the reflection from expanding.

When both points are inside $C$, $P_C$ and $R_C$ both act as the identity. Distances never grow.

To remember

prox = midpoint, rprox = mirror. $\prox_{\gamma f}=\tfrac12(\Id+\rprox_{\gamma f})$; proxes are firmly nonexpansive, their reflections are nonexpansive, and firmly nonexpansive $\iff$ $2P-\Id$ nonexpansive.

Exercise

Establish that $\rprox_{\gamma f}$ is nonexpansive

Part V · slide 31★☆☆#

Let $\gamma\in\,]0,+\infty[$ and $f\in\Gz{\H}$. Establish that $\rprox_{\gamma f}=2\prox_{\gamma f}-\Id$ is nonexpansive.

Reflex: a statement about $2P-\Id$ is a statement about $P$ being $\tfrac12$-averaged. Expand the square and let firm nonexpansiveness kill the cross term.

Hint

Two routes. (a) Use the definition of a $\tfrac12$-averaged operator: $\prox_{\gamma f}=\tfrac12\Id+\tfrac12R$. (b) Direct computation: $\norm{(2p-x)-(2q-y)}^2$ with $p=\prox_{\gamma f}x$, $q=\prox_{\gamma f}y$, plus the inequality $\ps{p-q}{x-y}\ge\norm{p-q}^2$ proved on page 4.

Solution

Route (a). By page 4, $\prox_{\gamma f}$ is firmly nonexpansive, i.e. $\tfrac12$-averaged: there exists a nonexpansive $R:\H\to\H$ such that $\prox_{\gamma f}=(1-\tfrac12)\Id+\tfrac12R$. Multiplying by $2$ and subtracting $\Id$: $R=2\prox_{\gamma f}-\Id=\rprox_{\gamma f}$, which is therefore nonexpansive.

Route (b), from scratch. Let $x,y\in\H$, $p=\prox_{\gamma f}x$, $q=\prox_{\gamma f}y$. By the characterization of the prox, $x-p\in\gamma\partial f(p)$ and $y-q\in\gamma\partial f(q)$. The monotonicity of $\partial f$ gives $\ps{(x-p)-(y-q)}{p-q}\ge0$, i.e. $\ps{x-y}{p-q}\ge\norm{p-q}^2$. Then $$\norm{\rprox_{\gamma f}x-\rprox_{\gamma f}y}^2=\norm{2(p-q)-(x-y)}^2=\norm{x-y}^2-4\big(\ps{x-y}{p-q}-\norm{p-q}^2\big)\le\norm{x-y}^2 .$$

Remark. The identity in route (b) shows the converse too: if $2P-\Id$ is nonexpansive then $\ps{x-y}{Px-Py}\ge\norm{Px-Py}^2$, so $P$ is firmly nonexpansive. "Firmly nonexpansive" and "reflection nonexpansive" are the same property.

Theorem

Solutions are shadows of fixed points: $\zer(\partial f+\partial g)=\prox_{\gamma g}(\Fix T)$, $T=\rprox_{\gamma f}\circ\rprox_{\gamma g}$

Part V.2 · slide 4 · Part V · slide 32SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Let $\gamma\in\,]0,+\infty[$, let $f\in\Gz{\H}$ and let $g\in\Gz{\H}$. We have $$\zer(\partial f+\partial g)=\prox_{\gamma g}(\Fix T)\qquad\text{where }T=\rprox_{\gamma f}\circ\rprox_{\gamma g}.$$ In words: $x$ is a zero of $\partial f+\partial g$ if and only if $x=\prox_{\gamma g}y$ for some fixed point $y$ of $T$.

Letters: here, as on the slides, $x$ is the solution and $y$ the fixed point. In the algorithm the roles swap: the governing sequence $x_n$ tends to a fixed point $\hat x$, and the shadow $y_n=\prox_{\gamma g}x_n$ tends to the solution $\hat y$.

In one sentence

A solution $x$ carries a subgradient $u\in\partial g(x)$ that $f$ cancels ($-u\in\partial f(x)$); the point $y=x+\gamma u$ "above" $x$ is a fixed point of $T$, and $x$ is recovered as its shadow $\prox_{\gamma g}y$.

Why it is true

$0\in\partial f(x)+\partial g(x)$ says: some $u\in\partial g(x)$ satisfies $-u\in\partial f(x)$. Encode $u$ as a displacement: $y=x+\gamma u$. Then $y-x\in\gamma\partial g(x)$ is, by the prox characterization (T08), the statement $x=\prox_{\gamma g}y$: the point $x$ is the shadow of $y$. And $x-y\in\gamma\partial f(x)$ is $x=\prox_{\gamma f}(2x-y)$, where $2x-y=2\prox_{\gamma g}y-y=\rprox_{\gamma g}y$ is the reflection of $y$ through $x$. So a solution is a point $x$ that is simultaneously the $\prox_{\gamma g}$ of $y$ and the $\prox_{\gamma f}$ of the reflection of $y$. Reflecting once more, $\rprox_{\gamma f}(\rprox_{\gamma g}y)=2x-(2x-y)=y$: the two reflections bring $y$ back to itself, $y\in\Fix T$. Every step is an equivalence, so the fixed points of $T$ are exactly the points whose two shadows coincide, and the common shadow is the solution.

The sentence to say out loud: fixed points of $T$ live one subgradient above the solutions; $\prox_{\gamma g}$ brings them down.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $f,g\in\Gz{\H}$: the two proxes exist and the characterization $p=\prox_{\gamma h}y\iff y-p\in\gamma\partial h(p)$ holds for $h=f$ and $h=g$ (applied to $\gamma h\in\Gz{\H}$, using $\partial(\gamma h)=\gamma\partial h$).
  • $\gamma\gt0$ is free: it only rescales the subgradient $u$ into the displacement $y-x=\gamma u$. Different $\gamma$ give different fixed points $y$ for the same solution $x$ (widget below).
  • No qualification condition is needed: the theorem is about $\zer(\partial f+\partial g)$, not about $\Argmin(f+g)$. The inclusion $\partial f+\partial g\subset\partial(f+g)$ gives $\zer(\partial f+\partial g)\subset\Argmin(f+g)$ for free; equality needs the sum rule.
Hint — try it first

Introduce $y$ with $x-y\in\gamma\partial f(x)$ and $y-x\in\gamma\partial g(x)$, then translate each inclusion into a prox equation with $w\in(\Id+\gamma\partial h)x\iff x=\prox_{\gamma h}w$ (T08): here $w=y$ for $h=g$, and $w=2x-y$ for $h=f$. Watch the point at which the inclusion is evaluated: always $x$, so $x$ is the output of both proxes.

Proof T08 Fermat + rewrite T15 zeros ⇔ fixed points T11 reflect

Let $x\in\H$. We prove a chain of equivalences, exactly as on the slide, and justify each one.

1. Split the zero into two subgradients. Since $\gamma\gt0$ and $\partial(\gamma h)=\gamma\partial h$ (scaling), $$0\in\partial f(x)+\partial g(x)\iff 0\in\gamma\big(\partial f(x)+\partial g(x)\big)\iff(\exists y\in\H)\ \ x-y\in\gamma\partial f(x)\ \text{ and }\ y-x\in\gamma\partial g(x).\tag{1}$$ ($\Rightarrow$) pick $v\in\gamma\partial g(x)$ with $-v\in\gamma\partial f(x)$ and set $y=x+v$. ($\Leftarrow$) add the two inclusions: $0=(x-y)+(y-x)\in\gamma\partial f(x)+\gamma\partial g(x)$.

2. Rewrite each inclusion as "x is a prox". By the characterization applied to $\gamma g$: $y-x\in\gamma\partial g(x)\iff y\in(\Id+\gamma\partial g)x\iff x=\prox_{\gamma g}y$. For $f$: $x-y\in\gamma\partial f(x)\iff 2x-y\in x+\gamma\partial f(x)=(\Id+\gamma\partial f)x$ (add $x$ to both sides). So $$(1)\iff(\exists y\in\H)\ \ 2x-y\in(\Id+\gamma\partial f)x\ \text{ and }\ x=\prox_{\gamma g}y.\tag{2}$$

3. The second prox and the first reflection. $2x-y\in(\Id+\gamma\partial f)x\iff x=\prox_{\gamma f}(2x-y)$. Now use $x=\prox_{\gamma g}y$ inside: $2x-y=2\prox_{\gamma g}y-y=\rprox_{\gamma g}y$. Hence $$(2)\iff(\exists y\in\H)\ \ x=\prox_{\gamma f}\big(\rprox_{\gamma g}y\big)\ \text{ and }\ x=\prox_{\gamma g}y.\tag{3}$$

4. The second reflection closes the loop. Write $r=\rprox_{\gamma g}y$. For any $r$, $x=\prox_{\gamma f}r\iff\rprox_{\gamma f}r=2\prox_{\gamma f}r-r=2x-r$. With $r=\rprox_{\gamma g}y=2x-y$ (step 3) this reads $\rprox_{\gamma f}(\rprox_{\gamma g}y)=2x-(2x-y)=y$. Therefore $$(3)\iff(\exists y\in\H)\ \ \rprox_{\gamma f}\big(\rprox_{\gamma g}y\big)=2x-\rprox_{\gamma g}y=y\ \text{ and }\ x=\prox_{\gamma g}y .$$ (The equivalence is two-sided: given $x=\prox_{\gamma g}y$, the identity $Ty=2\prox_{\gamma f}(r)-r$ with $r=2x-y$ shows $Ty=y\iff\prox_{\gamma f}(r)=x$.)

5. Read off. The condition is "$y\in\Fix T$ and $x=\prox_{\gamma g}y$": $$0\in\partial f(x)+\partial g(x)\iff(\exists y\in\Fix T)\ x=\prox_{\gamma g}y\iff x\in\prox_{\gamma g}(\Fix T).$$

□
Explicit dictionary Extra

The proof gives more than the statement: $$\Fix T=\big\{\,x+\gamma u\ :\ x\in\zer(\partial f+\partial g),\ u\in\partial g(x),\ -u\in\partial f(x)\,\big\}.$$ One solution can have many fixed points above it (one per cancelling subgradient pair), and several fixed points can share the same shadow. When $g$ is differentiable, $u=\nabla g(x)$ is forced and each solution has exactly one fixed point $x+\gamma\nabla g(x)$ above it.

Trick

Zeros ⇔ fixed points (T15), DR flavour: to turn $0\in A x+Bx$ into a fixed-point equation, introduce the auxiliary point $y=x+\gamma u$ with $u\in Bx$, and read $y-x\in\gamma Bx$ as $x=(\Id+\gamma B)^{-1}y$. The FB flavour (page 4) used $x=\prox_{\gamma f}(x-\gamma\nabla g(x))$ with no auxiliary point because $\nabla g(x)$ is single-valued; here $\partial g(x)$ is a set, so the choice of $u$ must be stored somewhere, in $y$.

Pitfall

A fixed point of $T$ is not a solution. If you run an iteration converging to a point of $\Fix T$ and output that point, you are wrong by $\gamma u$. Always output the image of the fixed-point variable under $\prox_{\gamma g}$: $\prox_{\gamma g}y$ in the theorem's letters, $y_n=\prox_{\gamma g}x_n$ (never $x_n$) in the algorithm's letters. Also, $\Fix T$ depends on $\gamma$ even though the solution set $\zer(\partial f+\partial g)$ does not.

Theorem

Douglas–Rachford algorithm and its convergence

Part V.2 · slides 5–7 · Part V · slides 33–34SlidesKeyExamLab#
Statement

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space. Let $f\in\Gz{\H}$ and $g\in\Gz{\H}$. Let $\gamma\in\,]0,+\infty[$ and let $(\lambda_n)_{n\in\N}$ be a sequence in $[0,2]$ such that $\sum_{n\in\N}\lambda_n(2-\lambda_n)=+\infty$. We assume that $\zer(\partial f+\partial g)\neq\emptyset$. Let $x_0\in\H$ and

Douglas–Rachford

For $n=0,1,\dots$

  1. $y_n=\prox_{\gamma g}x_n$ (the shadow: the candidate solution)
  2. $z_n=\prox_{\gamma f}(2y_n-x_n)$ (prox of $f$ at the reflection of $x_n$ through $y_n$)
  3. $x_{n+1}=x_n+\lambda_n(z_n-y_n)$ (relaxed update of the governing variable)

The following properties are satisfied:

  1. $x_n\wto\hat x$;
  2. $\hat y=\prox_{\gamma g}\hat x\in\Argmin(f+g)$;
  3. $z_n-y_n\to0$;
  4. $y_n\wto\hat y$ and $z_n\wto\hat y$.
Careful: the letters swap

In the fixed-point theorem (as on the slides) $x$ is the solution and $y$ the fixed point: $x=\prox_{\gamma g}y$. In the algorithm it is the other way round: $x_n$ is the governing sequence, $x_n\wto\hat x\in\Fix T$, and $y_n=\prox_{\gamma g}x_n$ is the shadow, $y_n\wto\hat y\in\Argmin(f+g)$. A rule that never fails: what goes into $\prox_{\gamma g}$ lives in $\Fix T$; what comes out of $\prox_{\gamma g}$ is the candidate solution. The widgets of this page keep one colour code: black = the governing sequence $x_n$ (and the fixed points of $T$), orange = the shadow $y_n$, blue = $z_n$, green = solutions (in the PPXA widget, colours identify the three copies).

In one sentence

Douglas–Rachford is the Krasnosel'skii–Mann iteration of $T=\rprox_{\gamma f}\rprox_{\gamma g}$ with relaxation $\lambda_n/2$; the governing sequence $x_n$ converges to a fixed point, and the shadow $y_n=\prox_{\gamma g}x_n$ converges to the minimizer underneath it.

Why it is true

Three facts do all the work. (i) $T$ is nonexpansive because each reflection is (previous proposition), and $\Fix T\neq\emptyset$ because $\prox_{\gamma g}(\Fix T)=\zer(\partial f+\partial g)\neq\emptyset$ (fixed-point theorem). (ii) The update is, after a two-line computation, $x_{n+1}=x_n+\frac{\lambda_n}{2}(Tx_n-x_n)$, so it is Krasnosel'skii–Mann with relaxation $\mu_n=\lambda_n/2\in[0,1]$; the condition $\sum\lambda_n(2-\lambda_n)=+\infty$ is exactly $\sum\mu_n(1-\mu_n)=+\infty$ (factor $\tfrac14$). KM gives $Tx_n-x_n\to0$ and $x_n\wto\hat x\in\Fix T$ by the Fejér + demiclosedness recipe (T14). (iii) The shadow of the limit is a solution by the fixed-point theorem, and a solution of $0\in\partial f+\partial g$ is a minimizer of $f+g$ by the inclusion $\partial f+\partial g\subset\partial(f+g)$ and Fermat.

Why $x_n$ is called the governing sequence. It is the only state the algorithm carries from one iteration to the next: $y_n$ and $z_n$ are recomputed from it. It is bookkeeping: at the limit it sits one scaled subgradient $\gamma u$ away from the solution, it need not be feasible or optimal, and in the disjoint case of the feasibility widget below it even runs off to infinity while the shadows settle on the closest pair. The step moves it by $z_n-y_n$, the disagreement between its two shadows $\prox_{\gamma g}x_n$ and $\prox_{\gamma f}(\rprox_{\gamma g}x_n)$; it stops moving exactly when they agree, which is the fixed-point condition of the previous theorem.

Where did smoothness go? In Forward–Backward, $g$ entered through the forward step $\Id-\gamma\nabla g$, which is averaged only if $\nabla g$ is $\nu$-Lipschitz and $\gamma\lt2/\nu$ (Baillon–Haddad): that is the only place where the Lipschitz constant was used, and it is where the step-size bound came from. Douglas–Rachford never takes a gradient: $g$ enters through $\prox_{\gamma g}$, a backward step, and the prox of any $g\in\Gz{\H}$ is firmly nonexpansive for every $\gamma\gt0$, because all it uses is the monotonicity of $\partial g$. Monotonicity, not Lipschitz continuity, is what a prox needs. And the convergence argument is the Fejér monotonicity of $x_n$ with respect to $\Fix T$ (Krasnosel'skii–Mann), not a decrease of $f+g$, so no descent lemma is needed either.

The sentence to say out loud: reflect, reflect, average: that is Krasnosel'skii–Mann, and the solution is the shadow.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $f,g\in\Gz{\H}$: both proxes exist, both reflections are nonexpansive, the fixed-point dictionary holds.
  • $\zer(\partial f+\partial g)\neq\emptyset$: gives $\Fix T\neq\emptyset$, which KM needs (a nonexpansive map without fixed points, e.g. a translation, has divergent iterates: this is what happens for two disjoint sets, see the widget). Note that it is stronger than $\Argmin(f+g)\neq\emptyset$; the two coincide under a qualification condition (sum rule).
  • $\lambda_n\in[0,2]$, $\sum\lambda_n(2-\lambda_n)=+\infty$: the KM condition for $\mu_n=\lambda_n/2$. The range is twice that of KM because $T$ is only nonexpansive and the algorithm already contains the factor $\tfrac12$ (T22). $\lambda_n\equiv2$ is excluded (the series is $0$): see Peaceman–Rachford.
  • $\gamma\in\,]0,+\infty[$: any value works. Unlike Forward–Backward there is no Lipschitz constant to respect (FB's bound $\gamma\lt2/\nu$ came from its gradient step, which DR does not have). $\gamma$ changes $\Fix T$, the speed and, when $f+g$ has several minimizers, which minimizer the shadows converge to (e.g. $f=\norm{\cdot}_1$, $g=\ind_{\{x_1+x_2=1\}}$ on $\R^2$, $x_0=(3,-1)$, $\lambda_n\equiv1$: the limit shadow is $(0.95,0.05)$ for $\gamma=0.1$, $(0.75,0.25)$ for $\gamma=0.5$, $(0.5,0.5)$ for $\gamma\ge1$). Only the set $\prox_{\gamma g}(\Fix T)=\zer(\partial f+\partial g)$ is independent of $\gamma$: if the minimizer is unique, every $\gamma$ gives the same limit (as in the widgets).
  • $\H$ Hilbert: KM is a Hilbert-space theorem (weak convergence). Item 4 is proved on the slides in finite dimension only (see step 6 of the proof).

2. The update is a relaxed step on T (the algebra). For every $n\in\N$, substitute $y_n$ and $z_n$: $$\begin{aligned} x_{n+1}&=x_n+\lambda_n\Big(\prox_{\gamma f}\big(2\prox_{\gamma g}(x_n)-x_n\big)-\prox_{\gamma g}(x_n)\Big)\\ &=x_n+\frac{\lambda_n}{2}\Big(2\prox_{\gamma f}\big(2\prox_{\gamma g}(x_n)-x_n\big)-2\prox_{\gamma g}(x_n)+x_n-x_n\Big)\\ &=x_n+\frac{\lambda_n}{2}\Big(2\prox_{\gamma f}\big(\rprox_{\gamma g}(x_n)\big)-\rprox_{\gamma g}(x_n)-x_n\Big)\\ &=x_n+\frac{\lambda_n}{2}\big(Tx_n-x_n\big). \end{aligned}$$ Line 2 multiplies and divides by $2$ and adds $x_n-x_n$; line 3 recognizes $2\prox_{\gamma g}(x_n)-x_n=\rprox_{\gamma g}(x_n)$ twice; line 4 recognizes $2\prox_{\gamma f}(r)-r=\rprox_{\gamma f}(r)$ with $r=\rprox_{\gamma g}(x_n)$.

3. Krasnosel'skii–Mann. Set $\mu_n=\lambda_n/2$. Then $\mu_n\in[0,1]$ and $\sum_n\mu_n(1-\mu_n)=\tfrac14\sum_n\lambda_n(2-\lambda_n)=+\infty$. So $(x_n)$ is the Krasnosel'skii–Mann sequence of the nonexpansive $T:\H\to\H$ with $\Fix T\neq\emptyset$ (take $C=\H$). Hence $(x_n)$ is Fejér-monotone with respect to $\Fix T$, $Tx_n-x_n\to0$ and $x_n\wto\hat x\in\Fix T$. This is item 1.

4. The residual. From line 4 of step 2 read backwards, $Tx_n-x_n=2\big(\prox_{\gamma f}(2y_n-x_n)-y_n\big)=2(z_n-y_n)$, i.e. $$z_n-y_n=\prox_{\gamma f}\big(2\prox_{\gamma g}(x_n)-x_n\big)-\prox_{\gamma g}(x_n)=\tfrac12(Tx_n-x_n)\to0 .$$ This is item 3.

5. The shadow of the limit is a minimizer. Since $\hat x\in\Fix T$, $\hat y=\prox_{\gamma g}(\hat x)\in\prox_{\gamma g}(\Fix T)=\zer(\partial f+\partial g)$. This is item 2.

6. Convergence of the shadows (finite dimension, as on the slides). Assume $\H$ finite-dimensional. Then $x_n\wto\hat x$ is $x_n\to\hat x$, and $\prox_{\gamma g}$ is continuous (it is nonexpansive, hence $1$-Lipschitz), so $y_n=\prox_{\gamma g}x_n\to\prox_{\gamma g}\hat x=\hat y$. Because $z_n-y_n\to0$, also $z_n=y_n+(z_n-y_n)\to\hat y$. This is item 4 (strong convergence in that case).

What is proved where

Items 1–3 are proved in any Hilbert space. The slides prove item 4 "for simplicity" in finite dimension, where $\prox_{\gamma g}$ continuous and $x_n\to\hat x$ give $y_n\to\hat y$ directly. In infinite dimension the argument breaks because a nonexpansive map need not be weakly continuous, not even a prox: for the projection $P_B$ onto the closed unit ball of $\ell^2$, $x_n=e_1+e_n\wto e_1$ ($n\ge2$) but $P_Bx_n=(e_1+e_n)/\sqrt2\wto e_1/\sqrt2\neq P_Be_1=e_1$. Extra The conclusion $y_n\wto\hat y$ nevertheless holds in every Hilbert space, by a finer argument: Svaiter (2011, "On weak convergence of the Douglas–Rachford method") for $\lambda_n\equiv1$, Bauschke–Combettes (2nd ed. 2017, Thm 26.11) for the relaxed version stated here. The Part V deck (slide 34) assumes finite dimension from the start of its proof and writes $x_n\to\hat x$; Part V.2 keeps the Hilbert statement and localizes the assumption to item 4.

Trick

Count the averagedness (T22): the relaxation range of a scheme $x_{n+1}=x_n+\lambda_n(Sx_n-x_n)$ is $[0,1/\alpha]$ when $S$ is $\alpha$-averaged. Pitfalls

$x_n$ is not the solution. It converges to a fixed point $\hat x=\hat y+\gamma u$ of $T$, one subgradient above the minimizer; the output is $y_n=\prox_{\gamma g}x_n$ (or $z_n$, same limit). The widget below shows $x_n$ settling away from $\hat y$. Do not compose the proxes. $\prox_{\gamma f}\circ\prox_{\gamma g}$ converges, to the wrong point (motivation card). $\lambda_n=2$ for all $n$ is not covered. The series condition fails; convergence may fail (next card). $\gamma$ is free but not neutral. In the widget "the governing sequence and the shadow" below, one step multiplies $x_n-\hat x$ by $1-\lambda\gamma/(1+\gamma)$ near the limit: a small $\gamma$ is slow at every $\lambda$; for $\lambda\le1$ a larger $\gamma$ is always faster, but for $\lambda\in\,]1,2]$ the best value is $\gamma=1/(\lambda-1)$ ($\gamma=1$ at $\lambda=2$) and a larger $\gamma$ slows down again (try $\gamma=0.1$, $1$ and $4$ at $\lambda=1.9$). In general the best $\gamma$ depends on the problem.

Cost per iteration and parallelism

One $\prox_{\gamma g}$, one $\prox_{\gamma f}$ and two vector additions. The two proxes are sequential ($z_n$ needs $y_n$), so plain DR is not parallel; the parallel version below removes this by lifting to a product space. Memory: $x_n$ only (plus the temporaries $y_n$, $z_n$). Fejér monotonicity holds with respect to $\Fix T$, so $\norm{x_n-\hat x}$ decreases, but nothing is monotone about $f(y_n)+g(y_n)$: DR is not a descent method.

Peaceman–Rachford: the limit case $\lambda_n\equiv2$

Part V.2 · slide 7Slides#
Remark (slide)

The limit case when $(\forall n\in\N)\ \lambda_n=2$ is the Peaceman–Rachford algorithm. Its convergence is only guaranteed under some additional assumption (e.g. strong convexity of $g$).

In one sentence

With $\lambda_n=2$ the update is $x_{n+1}=Tx_n$, the plain Picard iteration of a map that is only nonexpansive: no averaging, no Fejér-strict decrease, no guarantee.

Why the guarantee is lost, and what restores it

From step 2 of the DR proof, $\lambda_n=2$ gives $x_{n+1}=x_n+(Tx_n-x_n)=Tx_n$. The series $\sum\lambda_n(2-\lambda_n)$ is identically $0$, and KM's mechanism, the strict decrease $\norm{x_{n+1}-\bar x}^2\le\norm{x_n-\bar x}^2-\mu_n(1-\mu_n)\norm{Tx_n-x_n}^2$ (T12), degenerates to a plain inequality: the residual is no longer forced to $0$. Page 4 showed that the Picard iteration of a rotation never converges; here the rotation is $R_AR_B$. For $f=\ind_A$, $g=\ind_B$ with $A,B$ two distinct lines through the origin, $T=R_AR_B$ is the rotation by twice the angle from $B$ to $A$ (the composition of two reflections is a rotation by twice the angle between the mirrors): by $-2\theta$ when $A$ is the horizontal axis and $B$ makes the angle $\theta$ with it, while $R_BR_A$ rotates by $+2\theta$. Its only fixed point is $0=A\cap B$, every iterate stays on the circle of radius $\norm{x_0}$ and never converges, while $\lambda\lt2$ spirals in (widget).

Extra Why strong convexity of $g$ rescues it: if $g$ is $\mu$-strongly convex then $\partial g$ is $\mu$-strongly monotone, and redoing the computation of the reflected prox with $p=\prox_{\gamma g}x$, $q=\prox_{\gamma g}\bar x$ gives $\ps{x-\bar x}{p-q}\ge(1+\gamma\mu)\norm{p-q}^2$, hence $$\norm{\rprox_{\gamma g}x-\rprox_{\gamma g}\bar x}^2\le\norm{x-\bar x}^2-4\gamma\mu\norm{p-q}^2 .$$ With $\bar x\in\Fix T$ and $T=\rprox_{\gamma f}\rprox_{\gamma g}$ nonexpansive, $\norm{x_{n+1}-\bar x}^2\le\norm{x_n-\bar x}^2-4\gamma\mu\norm{y_n-\hat y}^2$; telescoping (T13) gives $\sum_n\norm{y_n-\hat y}^2\lt+\infty$, so the shadows $y_n$ converge strongly to the unique minimizer $\hat y=\prox_{\gamma g}\bar x$. (Lions–Mercier 1979.) The governing sequence itself need not converge, but the shadow does, which is all one wants.

Pitfall

"$\lambda_n\in[0,2]$" in the theorem does not mean that the constant choice $\lambda_n\equiv2$ is allowed. Individual values $\lambda_n=2$ are fine as long as $\sum\lambda_n(2-\lambda_n)=+\infty$; the constant endpoint is excluded by the series condition, just as $\lambda_n\equiv1$ is excluded in KM. A sequence like $\lambda_n=2-\tfrac1{n+1}$ is fine ($\sum\lambda_n(2-\lambda_n)=\sum\tfrac{2n+1}{(n+1)^2}=+\infty$).

Lemma

Bounded linear operators are weakly continuous

Part V.2 · slide 8Slides#
Statement

Let $\H$ and $\G$ be two Hilbert spaces and let $L\in\B(\G,\H)$. Then $L$ is weakly continuous: for every sequence $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ of $\G$, $$x_n\wto x\ \Rightarrow\ Lx_n\wto Lx .$$

In one sentence

Testing $Lx_n$ against $y$ is testing $x_n$ against $L^*y$: the adjoint moves the operator onto the test vector, where weak convergence already does the job.

Why it is true

Weak convergence is convergence of every inner product $\ps{x_n}{w}$. The inner products that define "$Lx_n\wto Lx$" are $\ps{Lx_n}{y}=\ps{x_n}{L^*y}$, which are inner products of $x_n$ against the fixed vectors $L^*y$: they converge by assumption. Linearity and boundedness are used exactly once, to have an adjoint. This is why the parallel DR proof below is allowed to pass weak limits through $P_E$, $L$ and $(L^*L)^{-1}L^*$, but not through a nonlinear prox.

The sentence to say out loud: the adjoint carries the operator onto the test vector.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $L$ linear and bounded: existence of the adjoint $L^*\in\B(\H,\G)$ (page 1). Nothing else is used.
  • Contrast: a nonexpansive nonlinear map is strongly continuous but not weakly continuous in general, not even a prox. For the projection $P_B$ onto the closed unit ball of $\ell^2$, $x_n=e_1+e_n\wto e_1$ ($n\ge2$) but $P_Bx_n=(e_1+e_n)/\sqrt2\wto e_1/\sqrt2\neq P_Be_1=e_1$; another example is $x\mapsto\norm{x}e$ with $\norm{e}=1$, which sends $e_n\wto0$ to $e$. This is exactly why item 4 of DR needed finite dimension on the slides.

Proof T07 test vector $L^*y$ (two lines, via the adjoint)

1. Fix a test vector. Let $(x_n)$ be a sequence of $\G$ with $x_n\wto x\in\G$, and let $y\in\H$.

2. Move L to the other side. $L^*y\in\G$, so by weak convergence $\ps{x_n}{L^*y}\to\ps{x}{L^*y}$, i.e. by definition of the adjoint $\ps{Lx_n}{y}\to\ps{Lx}{y}$.

Parallel form of Douglas–Rachford for $g\circ L$

Statement

Let $\H$ and $\G$ be two Hilbert spaces. Let $g\in\Gz{\H}$ and $L\in\B(\G,\H)$ be such that $\ran L$ is closed and $L^*L$ is an isomorphism. Let $\gamma\in\,]0,+\infty[$ and let $(\lambda_n)_{n\in\N}$ be a sequence in $[0,2]$ such that $\sum_{n\in\N}\lambda_n(2-\lambda_n)=+\infty$. Assume that $\zer(L^*\circ\partial g\circ L)\neq\emptyset$. Let $x_0\in\H$, $v_0=(L^*L)^{-1}L^*x_0$ and

Parallel Douglas–Rachford

For $n=0,1,\dots$

  1. $y_n=\prox_{\gamma g}x_n$ (prox in the big space $\H$)
  2. $c_n=(L^*L)^{-1}L^*y_n$ (least squares: $Lc_n=P_Ey_n$, $E=\ran L$)
  3. $x_{n+1}=x_n+\lambda_n\big(L(2c_n-v_n)-y_n\big)$ ($L(2c_n-v_n)=P_E(2y_n-x_n)=z_n$)
  4. $v_{n+1}=v_n+\lambda_n(c_n-v_n)$ (the same update read in $\G$)

Then $v_n\wto\hat v$ where $\hat v\in\Argmin(g\circ L)$. (The proof also gives $c_n\wto\hat v$, as stated on slide 8.)

In one sentence

Minimizing $g(Lv)$ over $v$ is minimizing $\ind_E+g$ over $x\in\H$ with $E=\ran L$; run Douglas–Rachford with $f=\ind_E$, whose prox is the projection $P_E=L(L^*L)^{-1}L^*$, and translate the iterates back to $\G$ through $L$.

Why it is true

Why lift at all? Because $\prox_{\gamma(g\circ L)}$ is in general not computable (page 3 gives it only when $LL^*=\mu\Id$), whereas $\prox_{\gamma g}$ and $P_{\ran L}$ are. Writing $g\circ L$ as $\ind_{\ran L}+g$ on the big space trades one intractable prox for two tractable ones, exactly the input DR needs. Nothing is parallel yet: the name anticipates PPXA+ below, where $g$ is separable on a product space and $\prox_{\gamma g}$ splits into $m$ independent proxes.

Two ideas. Lifting the constraint (T01): the values $g(Lv)$, $v\in\G$, are exactly the values $g(x)$ for $x$ in the subspace $E=\ran L$, so the problem is "$g$ restricted to $E$", i.e. $\ind_E+g$: two nonsmooth functions, DR territory. The projection is a least-squares solve: $P_Ey$ is the point $Lc$ of $E$ closest to $y$, and the normal equations $L^*(Lc-y)=0$ give $c=(L^*L)^{-1}L^*y$; that is where the isomorphism hypothesis enters (it also makes $E$ closed, so that $\ind_E\in\Gz{\H}$, and makes $L$ injective, so that iterates in $E$ can be "divided by $L$"). Since $P_E$ is linear, $z_n=P_E(2y_n-x_n)=2P_Ey_n-P_Ex_n=L(2c_n-v_n)$ with $v_n=(L^*L)^{-1}L^*x_n$, and the $x$-update projected onto $E$ becomes $v_{n+1}=v_n+\lambda_n(c_n-v_n)$. Finally DR says $y_n\wto\hat y\in\Argmin(\ind_E+g)$, i.e. $\hat y=L\hat v$ with $\hat v$ minimizing $g\circ L$, and the lemma lets us push the weak limit through $P_E$ and $(L^*L)^{-1}L^*$ to get $c_n\wto\hat v$ and $v_n\wto\hat v$.

Sentence to say out loud: parallel DR is DR for $\ind_{\ran L}+g$, with the projection computed by least squares and the iteration read in $\G$.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $L^*L$ isomorphism (bijective, bounded, bounded inverse): $E=\ran L$ is closed (proved in step 1, so the explicit hypothesis "$\ran L$ closed" on the slide is redundant but harmless), $\ind_E\in\Gz{\H}$, the least-squares formula $P_E=L(L^*L)^{-1}L^*$ makes sense, and $L$ is injective ($Lw=0\Rightarrow L^*Lw=0\Rightarrow w=0$), which is needed to read $Lv_{n+1}=L(v_n+\lambda_n(c_n-v_n))$ as an equality in $\G$. Extra In Hilbert spaces, $L^*L$ is an isomorphism iff $L$ is injective with closed range iff $\norm{Lw}\ge\kappa\norm{w}$ for some $\kappa\gt0$. Drop the hypothesis and the least-squares step is ill-posed: for $L(v_1,v_2)=v_1+v_2$ from $\R^2$ to $\R$ (closed range, not injective), $L^*L=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ is singular, $c_n$ is not unique, and $g\circ L$ has a whole line of minimizers above each minimizer of $g$.
  • $\zer(L^*\circ\partial g\circ L)\neq\emptyset$: this is $\zer(\partial\ind_E+\partial g)\neq\emptyset$ in disguise (step 2), the DR hypothesis. It is stronger than $\Argmin(g\circ L)\neq\emptyset$ because only $L^*\partial g(L\cdot)\subset\partial(g\circ L)$ holds without qualification (page 2).
  • $\gamma$, $\lambda_n$: inherited from DR, unchanged.
  • $v_0=(L^*L)^{-1}L^*x_0$: makes $Lv_n=P_Ex_n$ true at $n=0$, and the $v$-update propagates it.

PPXA+: parallel proximal algorithm for $\sum_{i=1}^m g_i\circ L_i$

Statement (PPXA+)

Let $\H_1,\dots,\H_m$ and $\G$ be Hilbert spaces. For every $i\in\{1,\dots,m\}$, let $g_i\in\Gz{\H_i}$ and let $L_i\in\B(\G,\H_i)$. Assume that $\sum_{i=1}^mL_i^*L_i$ is an isomorphism. Let $\gamma\in\,]0,+\infty[$ and let $(\lambda_n)_{n\in\N}$ be a sequence in $[0,2]$ such that $\sum_{n\in\N}\lambda_n(2-\lambda_n)=+\infty$. Assume that $\zer\big(\sum_{i=1}^mL_i^*\circ\partial g_i\circ L_i\big)\neq\emptyset$. Let $(x_{0,i})_{1\le i\le m}\in\H_1\times\dots\times\H_m$, $v_0=\big(\sum_{i=1}^mL_i^*L_i\big)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^mL_i^*x_{0,i}$ and

PPXA+

For $n=0,1,\dots$

  1. $y_{n,i}=\prox_{\gamma g_i}(x_{n,i}),\quad i\in\{1,\dots,m\}$ (parallel: $m$ independent proxes)
  2. $c_n=\big(\sum_{i=1}^mL_i^*L_i\big)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^mL_i^*y_{n,i}$ (synchronization: gather, one linear solve)
  3. $x_{n+1,i}=x_{n,i}+\lambda_n\big(L_i(2c_n-v_n)-y_{n,i}\big),\quad i\in\{1,\dots,m\}$ (parallel: broadcast $2c_n-v_n$, local update)
  4. $v_{n+1}=v_n+\lambda_n(c_n-v_n)$ (the small variable)

Then $v_n\wto\hat v\in\Argmin\sum_{i=1}^mg_i\circ L_i$.

In one sentence

PPXA+ is the parallel Douglas–Rachford on the product space $\H=\H_1\times\dots\times\H_m$ with the separable $g(x)=\sum g_i(x_i)$ and the stacked $L:v\mapsto(L_1v,\dots,L_mv)$; separability makes the prox step $m$ independent proxes, and $L^*L=\sum L_i^*L_i$ is the only coupling.

Why it is the parallel DR (answer to the slide's "Question")

Take $\H=\H_1\times\dots\times\H_m$ with $\ps{x}{y}=\sum_i\ps{x_i}{y_i}$, $g(x)=\sum_{i=1}^mg_i(x_i)$, $L:\G\to\H$, $v\mapsto(L_1v,\dots,L_mv)$ (T16). Then $g(Lv)=\sum_ig_i(L_iv)$, so $\Argmin(g\circ L)=\Argmin\sum_ig_i\circ L_i$, and the five ingredients of the parallel DR become:

  • Adjoint: $\ps{Lv}{y}=\sum_i\ps{L_iv}{y_i}=\sum_i\ps{v}{L_i^*y_i}=\ps{v}{\sum_iL_i^*y_i}$, so $L^*y=\sum_{i=1}^mL_i^*y_i$ (the adjoint of a stacking is a sum). Hence $L^*L=\sum_{i=1}^mL_i^*L_i$, and "$L^*L$ isomorphism" is the stated assumption.
  • Prox: $g$ is separable, so $\prox_{\gamma g}(x)=\big(\prox_{\gamma g_i}(x_i)\big)_{1\le i\le m}$ (prox of a separable sum): line 1, computable in parallel.
  • Zeros: $\partial g(x)=\partial g_1(x_1)\times\dots\times\partial g_m(x_m)$ (page 2), so $L^*\partial g(Lv)=\sum_iL_i^*\partial g_i(L_iv)$ and the hypothesis $\zer(L^*\partial gL)\neq\emptyset$ is the stated one.
  • Least squares: $c_n=(L^*L)^{-1}L^*y_n=\big(\sum_iL_i^*L_i\big)^{-1}\sum_iL_i^*y_{n,i}$: line 2. Likewise $v_0=(L^*L)^{-1}L^*x_0$.
  • Update: $x_{n+1}=x_n+\lambda_n(L(2c_n-v_n)-y_n)$ read componentwise is line 3; $v_{n+1}=v_n+\lambda_n(c_n-v_n)$ is line 4.

The conclusion $v_n\wto\hat v\in\Argmin(g\circ L)=\Argmin\sum_ig_i\circ L_i$ is the parallel DR theorem verbatim. (Closedness of $\ran L$ follows from the isomorphism, step 1 of that proof.)

What each hypothesis buys
  • $\sum_iL_i^*L_i$ isomorphism: the synchronization step is a well-posed linear solve; it holds as soon as one $L_i^*L_i$ is an isomorphism (e.g. one $L_i=\Id$).
  • $\zer(\sum_iL_i^*\partial g_iL_i)\neq\emptyset$: existence of a minimizer plus a qualification condition (sum rule); this is what the exercise's condition $\dom g_1\cap\bigcap_{i\ge2}\inte(\dom g_i)\neq\emptyset$ is for.
  • $\gamma$, $\lambda_n$: as in DR.
Convergence proof (reduction to the parallel DR theorem)

1. Define $\H$, $g$, $L$ as in "Why". $g\in\Gz{\H}$ as a finite sum of functions of $\Gz{\H}$ of separate variables (proper: $\dom g=\prod\dom g_i\neq\emptyset$; lsc and convex: each term is). $L\in\B(\G,\H)$ with $\norm{Lv}^2=\sum_i\norm{L_iv}^2\le(\sum_i\norm{L_i}^2)\norm{v}^2$.

2. $L^*L=\sum_iL_i^*L_i$ is an isomorphism by assumption; hence $\ran L$ is closed (parallel DR, step 1).

3. $\zer(L^*\circ\partial g\circ L)=\zer(\sum_iL_i^*\circ\partial g_i\circ L_i)\neq\emptyset$ by separability of $\partial g$.

4. The PPXA+ iteration is the parallel DR iteration written componentwise (the five identifications in "Why"). The parallel DR theorem gives $v_n\wto\hat v\in\Argmin(g\circ L)=\Argmin\sum_ig_i\circ L_i$.

Cost per iteration, what is parallel, what synchronizes

Parallel: the $m$ proxes $\prox_{\gamma g_i}$ (line 1) and the $m$ local updates (line 3) are independent: one worker per $i$, each holding $x_{n,i}$, $y_{n,i}$ and applying $L_i$. Synchronization: line 2 gathers $\sum_iL_i^*y_{n,i}$ (a reduce), solves one linear system with the fixed operator $\sum_iL_i^*L_i$ (factorized once, or trivial when it is $\mu\Id$), and broadcasts $2c_n-v_n\in\G$. Communication per iteration: one reduce of a vector of $\G$ and one broadcast of a vector of $\G$. The variable $v_n$ is the consensus value; the solution is its limit.

Pitfall

Each $x_{n,i}$ lives in $\H_i$ and is not an estimate of the solution; neither is $y_{n,i}=\prox_{\gamma g_i}x_{n,i}$ alone. Only the consensus variable $v_n\in\G$ (equivalently $c_n$) converges to $\hat v$. Also: the proxes are of $\gamma g_i$ with the same $\gamma$ for all $i$ (no weights here; weighted versions change the metric).

  2. $c_n=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^my_{n,i}$ (the average)

  4. $v_{n+1}=v_n+\lambda_n(c_n-v_n)$

Then $v_n\wto\hat v\in\Argmin\sum_{i=1}^mg_i$.

In one sentence

With $L_i=\Id$, $\sum L_i^*L_i=m\Id$ and the synchronization step is just the average of the $m$ prox outputs: minimizing a sum of $m$ functions costs $m$ proxes in parallel and one mean.

Why it is true

Set $\H_i=\G$ and $L_i=\Id$ in PPXA+. Then $L^*L=\sum_i\Id=m\Id$, an isomorphism with inverse $\frac1m\Id$; $L^*y=\sum_iy_i$; so $c_n=\frac1m\sum_iy_{n,i}$, $v_0=\frac1m\sum_ix_{0,i}$ and $L_i(2c_n-v_n)=2c_n-v_n$. The hypothesis $\zer(\sum_iL_i^*\partial g_iL_i)\neq\emptyset$ is $\zer(\sum_i\partial g_i)\neq\emptyset$. Geometrically, $E=\ran L=\{(v,\dots,v)\}$ is the consensus subspace (the diagonal of $\G^m$) and $P_E$ replaces every component by the average: the product-space widget above shows exactly this for $m=2$.

Proof T16 lifting

1. Apply PPXA+ with $\H_i=\G$, $L_i=\Id\in\B(\G,\G)$: $\sum_iL_i^*L_i=m\Id$ is an isomorphism.

2. $(m\Id)^{-1}\sum_iL_i^*y_{n,i}=\frac1m\sum_iy_{n,i}=c_n$ and $(m\Id)^{-1}\sum_iL_i^*x_{0,i}=\frac1m\sum_ix_{0,i}=v_0$; line 3 of PPXA+ becomes $x_{n+1,i}=x_{n,i}+\lambda_n(2c_n-v_n-y_{n,i})$.

3. PPXA+ gives $v_n\wto\hat v\in\Argmin\sum_ig_i\circ\Id=\Argmin\sum_ig_i$.

□
Qualification

$\zer(\sum_i\partial g_i)\neq\emptyset$ follows from "$\Argmin\sum_ig_i\neq\emptyset$" when the sum rule $\partial(\sum_ig_i)=\sum_i\partial g_i$ holds, e.g. under $\dom g_1\cap\bigcap_{i=2}^m\inte(\dom g_i)\neq\emptyset$: see the exercise. Extra This is the PPXA algorithm of Combettes–Pesquet (2008); the "+" of PPXA+ (Pesquet–Pustelnik 2012) is the linear operators $L_i$.

To remember

Consensus PPXA: prox each, average, reflect through the average ($2c_n-v_n$), update each, move the consensus toward the average. The whole coupling is one mean.

Exercise

Parallel algorithms for $\sum g_i$, for projections + support functions, and for $\norm{\cdot}_1$ over an intersection

Part V.2 · slide 12 · Part V · slide 38★★☆Exam#

Let $\G$ be a real Hilbert space. Let $m\in\N$, $m\ge2$.

  1. For every $i\in\{1,\dots,m\}$, let $g_i$ be a function in $\Gz{\G}$ such that $\dom g_1\cap\bigcap_{i=2}^m\inte(\dom g_i)\neq\emptyset$. Propose a parallel algorithm for finding a minimizer of $\sum_{i=1}^mg_i$ when such a minimizer exists.
  2. Let $p\in\{1,\dots,m-1\}$ and let $(C_i)_{1\le i\le m}$ be nonempty closed convex subsets of $\G$. We assume that $\bigcap_{i=1}^p\inte(C_i)\neq\emptyset$, and for every $j\in\{p+1,\dots,m\}$, $C_j$ is bounded. Propose a parallel algorithm for solving the problem $$\operatorname*{minimize}_{x\in\bigcap_{i=1}^pC_i}\ \sum_{j=p+1}^m\sigma_{C_j}(x)$$ when this problem admits a solution.
  3. Let $\H=\R^N$. Deduce an algorithm to $$\operatorname*{minimize}_{x\in\bigcap_{i=1}^pC_i}\ \norm{x}_1 .$$

Part V version: item 1 without the qualification condition, item 2 without "$C_j$ bounded", no item 3.

Reflex: a sum of $m$ proximable terms is PPXA+ with $L_i=\Id$; each hypothesis of PPXA+ must be checked, and "a minimizer exists" is not yet "$\zer\sum\partial g_i\neq\emptyset$": that is what the qualification condition is for. Indicators and support functions are a Moreau pair: $\prox$ of one is $\Id$ minus a projection.

Hint

(1) Consensus PPXA; use the sum rule to turn $\Argmin\neq\emptyset$ into $\zer(\sum\partial g_i)\neq\emptyset$. (2) $g_i=\ind_{C_i}$ for $i\le p$, $g_j=\sigma_{C_j}$ for $j\gt p$; $\prox_{\gamma\sigma_C}$ from the Moreau decomposition with $f=\ind_C$; bounded $C_j$ makes $\dom\sigma_{C_j}=\G$. (3) $\norm{\cdot}_1=\sigma_{[-1,1]^N}$ and the projection on a box is a clip.

Solution

1. Consensus lifting. Apply PPXA+ with $\H_i=\G$ and $L_i=\Id$ for every $i$ (consensus case). Then $\sum_iL_i^*L_i=m\Id$ is an isomorphism with inverse $\frac1m\Id$, $\sum_iL_i^*y_i=\sum_iy_i$, and the algorithm is, for $(x_{0,i})_{1\le i\le m}\in\G^m$ and $v_0=\frac1m\sum_ix_{0,i}$:

Item 1 · parallel algorithm for $\sum_{i=1}^m g_i$
  1. $y_{n,i}=\prox_{\gamma g_i}(x_{n,i})$, $i=1,\dots,m$ (in parallel)
  2. $c_n=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^my_{n,i}$
  3. $x_{n+1,i}=x_{n,i}+\lambda_n(2c_n-v_n-y_{n,i})$, $i=1,\dots,m$ (in parallel)
  4. $v_{n+1}=v_n+\lambda_n(c_n-v_n)$

with $\gamma\gt0$, $\lambda_n\in[0,2]$, $\sum\lambda_n(2-\lambda_n)=+\infty$. Checking the hypotheses. Each $g_i\in\Gz{\G}$ is given. The remaining one is $\zer(\sum_{i=1}^m\partial g_i)\neq\emptyset$. We know $\Argmin\sum_ig_i\neq\emptyset$, i.e. by Fermat $\zer\,\partial(\sum_ig_i)\neq\emptyset$. In general only $\sum_i\partial g_i\subset\partial(\sum_ig_i)$ holds; the qualification condition $\dom g_1\cap\bigcap_{i\ge2}\inte(\dom g_i)\neq\emptyset$ is exactly the hypothesis of the Moreau–Rockafellar sum rule (applied $m-1$ times), which gives $\partial(\sum_ig_i)=\sum_i\partial g_i$. Hence $\zer(\sum_i\partial g_i)=\Argmin\sum_ig_i\neq\emptyset$, PPXA+ applies, and $v_n\wto\hat v\in\Argmin\sum_ig_i$. (Without the condition, as in the Part V version, one must simply assume $\zer(\sum_i\partial g_i)\neq\emptyset$ directly.)

2. Indicators and support functions. Write the problem as $\operatorname*{minimize}_{x\in\G}\sum_{i=1}^m g_i(x)$ with (T01) $$g_i=\ind_{C_i}\ (1\le i\le p),\qquad g_j=\sigma_{C_j}\ (p+1\le j\le m).$$ Each $g_i$ is in $\Gz{\G}$: $\ind_{C_i}$ because $C_i$ is nonempty closed convex (page 1); $\sigma_{C_j}=\ind_{C_j}^*$ is a conjugate, hence lsc convex, and proper since $\sigma_{C_j}(0)=0$ and $\sigma_{C_j}\gt-\infty$ ($C_j\neq\emptyset$) (support function). Qualification condition of item 1: $\dom\ind_{C_1}=C_1$, $\inte(\dom\ind_{C_i})=\inte C_i$ for $2\le i\le p$, and for $j\gt p$, since $C_j$ is bounded by some $\rho_j$, $\sigma_{C_j}(x)=\sup_{c\in C_j}\ps{c}{x}\le\rho_j\norm{x}\lt+\infty$: $\dom\sigma_{C_j}=\G$, so $\inte(\dom\sigma_{C_j})=\G$. Therefore $$\dom g_1\cap\bigcap_{i=2}^m\inte(\dom g_i)=C_1\cap\bigcap_{i=2}^p\inte C_i\ \supset\ \bigcap_{i=1}^p\inte C_i\neq\emptyset .$$ This is why "$C_j$ bounded" and "$\bigcap_{i\le p}\inte C_i\neq\emptyset$" are assumed: they make the qualification condition of item 1 hold (an unbounded $C_j$ gives a support function with $+\infty$ values, whose domain may have empty interior and may break the sum rule). The problem admits a solution by assumption, so item 1 applies.

The proxes. For $i\le p$: $\prox_{\gamma\ind_{C_i}}=P_{C_i}$ (page 3). For $j\gt p$: by the Moreau decomposition $\prox_{\gamma f^*}x=x-\gamma\prox_{\gamma^{-1}f}(\gamma^{-1}x)$ with $f=\ind_{C_j}$, $f^*=\sigma_{C_j}$ and $\prox_{\gamma^{-1}\ind_{C_j}}=P_{C_j}$: $$\prox_{\gamma\sigma_{C_j}}x=x-\gamma P_{C_j}(x/\gamma)=x-P_{\gamma C_j}(x).$$ For $\gamma=1$ this is the slide formula $\prox_{\sigma_C}=\Id-P_C$ (page 3); the identity $\gamma P_C(x/\gamma)=P_{\gamma C}(x)$ is the scaling of a projection. The algorithm, with $x_{0,i}\in\G$ and $v_0=\frac1m\sum_ix_{0,i}$:

Item 2 · parallel algorithm for $\min_{x\in\cap_{i\le p}C_i}\sum_{j\gt p}\sigma_{C_j}(x)$
  1. $y_{n,i}=P_{C_i}(x_{n,i})$ for $i=1,\dots,p$; $\ y_{n,j}=x_{n,j}-\gamma P_{C_j}(x_{n,j}/\gamma)$ for $j=p+1,\dots,m$ (all $m$ in parallel)
  2. $c_n=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^my_{n,i}$
  3. $x_{n+1,i}=x_{n,i}+\lambda_n(2c_n-v_n-y_{n,i})$, $i=1,\dots,m$ (in parallel)
  4. $v_{n+1}=v_n+\lambda_n(c_n-v_n)$

and $v_n\wto\hat v$, a solution of the problem. Only projections onto the $C_i$ are ever needed.

3. The $\ell^1$ norm over an intersection. On $\R^N$, $\norm{x}_1=\sum_k\abs{x_k}=\sup_{c\in[-1,1]^N}\ps{c}{x}=\sigma_{[-1,1]^N}(x)$ (page 2: the $\ell^1$ norm is the support function of the $\ell^\infty$ unit ball). So the problem is item 2 with $m=p+1$ and $C_{p+1}=[-1,1]^N$, which is bounded, as required. $P_{[-1,1]^N}$ is the componentwise clip $(P_{[-1,1]^N}x)_k=\max(-1,\min(1,x_k))$, and the prox of the support function is soft-thresholding: $$\big(\prox_{\gamma\norm{\cdot}_1}x\big)_k=x_k-\gamma\max\big(-1,\min(1,x_k/\gamma)\big)=\operatorname{sign}(x_k)\max(\abs{x_k}-\gamma,0),$$ recovering the formula of page 3 (soft-thresholding). The algorithm, with $x_{0,1},\dots,x_{0,p+1}\in\R^N$ and $v_0=\frac1{p+1}\sum_ix_{0,i}$:

Item 3 · parallel algorithm for $\min_{x\in\cap_{i\le p}C_i}\norm{x}_1$
  1. $y_{n,i}=P_{C_i}(x_{n,i})$ for $i=1,\dots,p$; $\ y_{n,p+1}=\operatorname{soft}_\gamma(x_{n,p+1})$ (componentwise) (in parallel)
  2. $c_n=\frac1{p+1}\sum_{i=1}^{p+1}y_{n,i}$
  3. $x_{n+1,i}=x_{n,i}+\lambda_n(2c_n-v_n-y_{n,i})$, $i=1,\dots,p+1$ (in parallel)
  4. $v_{n+1}=v_n+\lambda_n(c_n-v_n)$

Then $v_n\to\hat v\in\Argmin_{x\in\cap_{i\le p}C_i}\norm{x}_1$ (strong convergence: finite dimension). Here "the problem admits a solution" is automatic: $\ind_{\cap C_i}+\norm{\cdot}_1$ is proper (the intersection contains $\bigcap\inte C_i\neq\emptyset$), lsc and coercive on $\R^N$, so a minimizer exists (page 1).

For $\operatorname*{minimize}_x f(x)+g(Lx)$, page 6 writes the Fenchel–Rockafellar dual $\operatorname*{minimize}_v f^*(-L^*v)+g^*(v)$, applies the unrelaxed DR ($\lambda_n\equiv1$) to the two dual functions, and then changes variables (T18): the dual proxes become primal minimizations of the augmented Lagrangian, and the iteration is exactly the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers. Retain

What to retain

  • Motivation. GD needs $f+g$ smooth, PPA needs the sum proximable, FB needs $g$ smooth; DR needs only $\prox_{\gamma f}$ and $\prox_{\gamma g}$.
  • Reflected prox. $\rprox_{\gamma f}=2\prox_{\gamma f}-\Id$ is nonexpansive $\iff$ $\prox_{\gamma f}$ is firmly nonexpansive ($\tfrac12$-averaged). Proof: expand $\norm{2(p-q)-(x-y)}^2$ and use $\ps{p-q}{x-y}\ge\norm{p-q}^2$.
  • Never compose proxes to minimize $f+g$: the fixed points are wrong ($\abs\cdot+\tfrac12(\cdot-3)^2$: $1$ instead of $2$). Compose reflections.
  • Dictionary. $\zer(\partial f+\partial g)=\prox_{\gamma g}(\Fix T)$, $T=\rprox_{\gamma f}\rprox_{\gamma g}$. Fixed point $y=\hat x+\gamma u$ with $u\in\partial g(\hat x)$, $-u\in\partial f(\hat x)$; $\prox_{\gamma g}$ brings it down.
  • Proof route of the dictionary: $x-y\in\gamma\partial f(x)$ and $y-x\in\gamma\partial g(x)$ → $x=\prox_{\gamma g}y$ and $x=\prox_{\gamma f}(\rprox_{\gamma g}y)$ → $Ty=2x-\rprox_{\gamma g}y=y$.
  • DR algorithm. $y_n=\prox_{\gamma g}x_n$, $z_n=\prox_{\gamma f}(2y_n-x_n)$, $x_{n+1}=x_n+\lambda_n(z_n-y_n)$; $\gamma\gt0$ any, $\lambda_n\in[0,2]$, $\sum\lambda_n(2-\lambda_n)=+\infty$, $\zer(\partial f+\partial g)\neq\emptyset$.
  • Why it converges: $x_{n+1}=x_n+\tfrac{\lambda_n}2(Tx_n-x_n)$ is KM with $\mu_n=\lambda_n/2$; $z_n-y_n=\tfrac12(Tx_n-x_n)\to0$; $x_n\wto\hat x\in\Fix T$; $\hat y=\prox_{\gamma g}\hat x\in\zer(\partial f+\partial g)\subset\Argmin(f+g)$.
  • Output the shadow $y_n$ (or $z_n$), never $x_n$; $y_n\wto\hat y$ (proved in finite dimension on the slides, true in general).
  • Peaceman–Rachford ($\lambda_n\equiv2$) is Picard on $T$: two lines → rotation → no convergence; strong convexity of $g$ restores convergence of the shadows.
  • Weak continuity lemma: $\ps{Lx_n}{y}=\ps{x_n}{L^*y}$, so bounded linear maps preserve weak convergence (nonlinear nonexpansive maps do not).
  • Parallel DR $=$ DR for $\ind_{\ran L}+g$; $P_{\ran L}y=Lc$ with $c=(L^*L)^{-1}L^*y$ (normal equations); $x_{n+1}=x_n+\lambda_n(L(2c_n-v_n)-y_n)$, $v_{n+1}=v_n+\lambda_n(c_n-v_n)$; $v_n\wto\hat v\in\Argmin(g\circ L)$.
  • Hypotheses of parallel DR: $L^*L$ isomorphism (gives $\ran L$ closed, least squares, $L$ injective), $\zer(L^*\partial gL)\neq\emptyset$ ($=\zer(\partial\ind_E+\partial g)\neq\emptyset$ via $N_E=E^\perp=\Ker L^*$).
  • PPXA+ $=$ parallel DR on $\H_1\times\dots\times\H_m$: $L^*y=\sum L_i^*y_i$, $L^*L=\sum L_i^*L_i$, prox separable. Prox / gather / scatter / consensus. $L_i=\Id$: $c_n$ is the average.
  • Exercise formulas: $\prox_{\gamma\ind_C}=P_C$; $\prox_{\gamma\sigma_C}=\Id-\gamma P_C(\cdot/\gamma)=\Id-P_{\gamma C}$; $\norm\cdot_1=\sigma_{[-1,1]^N}$ ⇒ soft-thresholding. "$C_j$ bounded" makes $\dom\sigma_{C_j}=\G$ for the qualification condition.
  • Next: DR on the dual of $f+g\circ L$ is ADMM; the primal-dual algorithm is FB in a product space.

Self-check

Ten questions

Why does the Douglas–Rachford algorithm converge without any smoothness assumption?

Because it is Krasnosel'skii–Mann on $T=\rprox_{\gamma f}\rprox_{\gamma g}$, which is nonexpansive as a composition of reflections of firmly nonexpansive proxes; KM only needs nonexpansiveness, a fixed point, and $\mu_n=\lambda_n/2\in[0,1]$ with $\sum\mu_n(1-\mu_n)=+\infty$. Smoothness entered FB only through the averagedness of $\Id-\gamma\nabla g$; here the averaging is done by the relaxation itself.

State the fixed-point dictionary of DR and explain where the fixed point sits relative to the solution.

$\zer(\partial f+\partial g)=\prox_{\gamma g}(\Fix T)$ with $T=\rprox_{\gamma f}\rprox_{\gamma g}$. If $\hat x$ is a solution with $u\in\partial g(\hat x)$, $-u\in\partial f(\hat x)$, then $y=\hat x+\gamma u$ is a fixed point and $\prox_{\gamma g}y=\hat x$: the fixed point is one scaled subgradient above the solution.

Why is $\rprox_{\gamma f}$ nonexpansive? Give the one-line computation.

$\norm{(2p-x)-(2q-y)}^2=\norm{x-y}^2-4(\ps{x-y}{p-q}-\norm{p-q}^2)\le\norm{x-y}^2$ because $\ps{x-y}{p-q}\ge\norm{p-q}^2$ (firm nonexpansiveness of the prox, from monotonicity of $\partial f$).

In the DR theorem, what is the exact condition on $(\lambda_n)$ and where does it come from?

$\lambda_n\in[0,2]$ and $\sum_n\lambda_n(2-\lambda_n)=+\infty$. Since $x_{n+1}=x_n+\frac{\lambda_n}2(Tx_n-x_n)$, it is KM's condition on $\mu_n=\lambda_n/2$: $\sum\mu_n(1-\mu_n)=\frac14\sum\lambda_n(2-\lambda_n)$.

Which sequence converges to a minimizer of $f+g$: $x_n$, $y_n$ or $z_n$? What does $x_n$ converge to?

$y_n=\prox_{\gamma g}x_n$ and $z_n$ (same limit since $z_n-y_n\to0$) converge to $\hat y\in\Argmin(f+g)$; $x_n$ converges weakly to a fixed point $\hat x$ of $T$, with $\hat y=\prox_{\gamma g}\hat x$.

Give an example where Peaceman–Rachford ($\lambda_n=2$) does not converge.

$f=\ind_A$, $g=\ind_B$ with $A,B$ two distinct lines through $0$ in $\R^2$: $T=R_AR_B$ is a rotation of angle twice the angle between the lines; $x_{n+1}=Tx_n$ stays on a circle and does not converge, although $\Fix T=\{0\}=A\cap B\neq\emptyset$.

Why is a bounded linear operator weakly continuous, and why does this matter in the parallel DR proof?

$\ps{Lx_n}{y}=\ps{x_n}{L^*y}\to\ps{x}{L^*y}=\ps{Lx}{y}$. The proof needs to pass the weak limit $y_n\wto\hat y$ through $P_E$ and $(L^*L)^{-1}L^*$ to get $c_n\wto\hat v$ and $v_n\wto\hat v$; this is legitimate for linear bounded maps, not for a general nonexpansive map.

What does the hypothesis "$L^*L$ is an isomorphism" buy in the parallel DR?

$E=\ran L$ closed (so $\ind_E\in\Gz{\H}$), the least-squares formula $P_E=L(L^*L)^{-1}L^*$ for the prox of $\ind_E$, and the injectivity of $L$ used to read $Lv_{n+1}=L(v_n+\lambda_n(c_n-v_n))$ as an update of $v_n$.

Write PPXA+ for $m$ functions with $L_i=\Id$ and say which step is the synchronization.

$y_{n,i}=\prox_{\gamma g_i}x_{n,i}$ (parallel); $c_n=\frac1m\sum_iy_{n,i}$ (synchronization: the average, since $\sum L_i^*L_i=m\Id$); $x_{n+1,i}=x_{n,i}+\lambda_n(2c_n-v_n-y_{n,i})$ (parallel); $v_{n+1}=v_n+\lambda_n(c_n-v_n)$. $v_n\wto\hat v\in\Argmin\sum g_i$.

What is $\prox_{\gamma\sigma_C}$, and why does the exercise assume the $C_j$ bounded?

Moreau decomposition with $f=\ind_C$: $\prox_{\gamma\sigma_C}x=x-\gamma P_C(x/\gamma)=x-P_{\gamma C}x$ ($=\Id-P_C$ for $\gamma=1$). Boundedness makes $\sigma_{C_j}$ finite everywhere, so $\inte(\dom\sigma_{C_j})=\G$ and the qualification condition $\dom g_1\cap\bigcap_{i\ge2}\inte\dom g_i\neq\emptyset$ reduces to $\bigcap_{i\le p}\inte C_i\neq\emptyset$; the sum rule then converts "a minimizer exists" into $\zer(\sum\partial g_i)\neq\emptyset$.
