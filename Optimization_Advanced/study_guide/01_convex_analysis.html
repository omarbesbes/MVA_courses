Convexity, lower semicontinuity and existence

The vocabulary that every later theorem assumes: Hilbert spaces and adjoints, functions with values in $]-\infty,+\infty]$, epigraphs, lower semicontinuity, coercivity, convex sets and convex functions. The goal of the page is one object, the class $\Gz{\H}$, and one reflex: when does a minimizer exist, and when is it unique?

Overview

Where we are

The whole course follows one line of thought. This page builds the ground it stands on.

Every algorithm of the course minimizes a sum of functions, typically a smooth data-fit term plus a nonsmooth regularizer plus a constraint, and every convergence proof follows the same chain:

Fermat's rule $0\in\partial f(\hat x)$ (page 2) turns "being a minimizer" into "being a zero of an operator"; writing $f=g+h$ turns it into a zero of a sum of operators; a short algebraic rewrite turns that into a fixed point of a nonexpansive map built from gradient steps and proximity operators (pages 3–5); the Fejér / Krasnosel'skii–Mann machinery then proves that the iteration converges (pages 4–6). When no prox is available, page 7 replaces the exact step by a majorize–minimize step.

Each link of this chain needs the functions to belong to one class, $\Gz{\H}$: proper (there is something to minimize), lower semicontinuous (the epigraph is closed, so limits of good points are good points), convex (local minimizers are global, the subdifferential is well behaved, the prox is nonexpansive). This page defines these three words, proves what each of them buys, and answers the first of the four questions of the course:

Part I · slides 3–16

Optimization problems and the four questions

What we minimize, over what, and the one convention that makes constrained and unconstrained problems the same object: a function allowed to take the value $+\infty$.

Definition

Minimization problem, Argmin, and the $+\infty$ convention

Part I · slides 3–5SlidesKey#
Definition

Let $f:D\to[-\infty,+\infty]$ be a cost function on a set $D\subset\R^N$. A minimization problem is $$\text{Find }\hat x\in D\ \text{ such that }\ (\forall x\in D)\ f(\hat x)\le f(x)\ \iff\ f(\hat x)=\inf_{x\in D}f(x)\ \iff\ \hat x\in\Argmin_{x\in D} f(x).$$ A maximization problem ($f$ a reward) is the same thing for $-f$: $\ (\forall x\in D)\ f(\hat x)\ge f(x)\iff \hat x\in\Argmin_{x\in D}\big(-f(x)\big)$. Without loss of generality we study minimization problems with $f:D\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$.

Types. $D=\R^N$: unconstrained. $D$ countable: discrete ($D$ finite: combinatorial; $D\subset\Z^N$: integer). $D$ uncountable: continuous. With $P$ equality and $Q$ inequality constraints, $$D=\{x\in\R^N\mid (\forall i\in\{1,\dots,P\})\ \varphi_i(x)=\delta_i\ \text{ and }\ (\forall j\in\{1,\dots,Q\})\ \psi_j(x)\le\eta_j\},$$ with $\delta_i,\eta_j\in\R$ and $\varphi_i,\psi_j:\R^N\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$. If $\varphi_i=\ps{\cdot}{u_i}$ (resp. $\psi_j=\ps{\cdot}{u_j}$) the constraint is linear (affine); if in addition $f$ is linear, the problem is a linear program.

Remark ($+\infty$ convention). $\displaystyle \hat x\in\Argmin_{x\in D}f(x)\iff \hat x\in\Argmin_{x\in\R^N}\tilde f(x)$, where $\tilde f(x)=f(x)$ if $x\in D$ and $\tilde f(x)=+\infty$ otherwise. Allowing non-finite values gives a unifying view of constrained and unconstrained problems.

In one sentence

A constraint is a wall of height $+\infty$: minimizing $f$ over $D$ is minimizing $f+\ind_D$ over everything.

Why the definition is set up this way

$\inf$ versus $\min$. The infimum $\inf_D f$ always exists in $[-\infty,+\infty]$; a minimizer is a point where it is attained. "$\Argmin$" (capital A, a set in the slides' notation) may be empty ($f=e^x$ on $\R$), a single point, or a whole set (a segment for $f=\abs{\cdot}$ with a flat bottom, a subspace for least squares). Whether it is nonempty is the first of the four questions.

Why values in $]-\infty,+\infty]$ and not $[-\infty,+\infty]$. After the switch from max to min we only need $+\infty$ (to encode "forbidden"). Excluding $-\infty$ keeps every difference $f(x)-f(y)$ meaningful and avoids $\infty-\infty$; it is also what makes "$f(\hat x)=\inf f$" a real number once a minimizer exists.

Why the $+\infty$ trick is more than notation. Once a constraint set is a function, it can be added to other functions, it has an epigraph, a subdifferential (the normal cone, page 2), a prox (the projection, page 3), a conjugate (the support function, page 2). The splitting algorithms of pages 4–6 treat $\ind_D$ exactly like any other nonsmooth term. The reader should be able to say: a constrained problem is an unconstrained problem whose objective is $+\infty$ outside the feasible set.

Trick

T01 indicator trick Whenever a statement is proved for unconstrained problems with $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$, you get the constrained version for free by replacing $f$ with $f+\ind_C$. The price is that the hypotheses must now be checked for $f+\ind_C$ (proper: $\dom f\cap C\neq\emptyset$; lsc: $C$ closed; convex: $C$ convex). This is how existence over a convex set is proved below.

Worked example

Denoise $y\in\R^N$ under box constraints: $\min_{x\in[0,1]^N}\tfrac12\norm{x-y}^2$. In the unified form, $\tilde f=\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-y}^2+\ind_{[0,1]^N}$. The solution is the clipping $\hat x=\min(\max(y,0),1)$: on page 3 you will read it as $\hat x=\prox_{\ind_{[0,1]^N}}y=P_{[0,1]^N}y$.

Pitfall

$D$ finite (segmentation with a finite label set below) is a combinatorial problem: the convex theory of this course does not apply to it directly, even if the formula for $f$ looks convex. Convexity is a property of the function and of its domain.

To remember

Argmin = where the inf is attained (a set, possibly empty). Constraint = $+\infty$ wall: $\Argmin_D f=\Argmin_{\R^N}(f+\ind_D)$.

The four questions: existence, characterization, algorithm, performance

Part I · slide 6Slides#
Main questions to be addressed
  1. Existence / uniqueness of a solution $\hat x$?
  2. Characterization of solutions: necessary / sufficient conditions for $\hat x$ to be a solution.
  3. Designing an algorithm to approximate a solution in the frequent case when no closed form is available, i.e. building a sequence $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ of $\R^N$ such that $\lim_{n\to+\infty}x_n=\hat x$.
  4. Evaluation of the performance of the algorithm: convergence speed (rates), robustness to numerical errors, amenability to parallel / distributed implementations.
In one sentence

Exist, recognize, compute, measure: every page of the course answers one of these four verbs for a wider class of problems.

Keep the order in mind, because the logic of the proofs follows it. You cannot characterize a solution that does not exist (question 1 before 2); an algorithm is designed by rewriting the characterization as a fixed-point equation (2 before 3: Fermat's rule becomes $\hat x=\prox_{\gamma f}(\hat x-\gamma\nabla g(\hat x))$ on page 4); and the performance analysis reads the contraction or averagedness constants off that rewriting (3 before 4).

Question 3 is where this course differs from a generic optimization course: "large scale and distributed" means that the sequence must be computable with cheap operations (gradients, proximity operators of simple pieces, projections) that can run in parallel on the pieces of $f$. That is why the examples below are all written as sums of simple terms over a graph.

Definition

Q-linear and Q-superlinear convergence

Part I · slide 6Slides#
Definition

Let $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ converge to $\hat x$. If there exist $\rho\in\,]0,1[$ and $n^*\in\N$ such that $$(\forall n\ge n^*)\qquad \norm{x_{n+1}-\hat x}\le\rho\,\norm{x_n-\hat x},$$ the convergence rate is Q-linear. If $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\norm{x_{n+1}-\hat x}}{\norm{x_n-\hat x}}=0$, the rate is Q-superlinear.

In one sentence

"Q" is for quotient: compare each error with the previous one; a fixed fraction is linear, a vanishing fraction is superlinear.

Q-linear convergence means that, after $n^*$, every iteration gains at least a fixed number of digits: $\norm{x_n-\hat x}\le\rho^{\,n-n^*}\norm{x_{n^*}-\hat x}$, a straight line on a logarithmic plot. Contractions give it directly (Picard's theorem, page 4, with $\rho$ the Lipschitz constant). Superlinear means the line keeps bending downwards: the number of correct digits grows faster than linearly in $n$ (Newton-type methods double it at each step).

Most algorithms of this course are only proved to converge, with no rate, or with a sublinear rate such as $o(1/\sqrt n)$ on the residual $\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}$: that is the honest price of handling nonsmooth terms with weak hypotheses. Rates reappear when strong convexity or an error bound is added.

Interactive · what linear, superlinear and sublinear look like on a log scale

Three error sequences $e_n=\norm{x_n-\hat x}$: Q-linear $e_n=\rho^n$ (a straight line), Q-superlinear $e_n=\rho^{\,n(n+1)/2}$ (the quotient $e_{n+1}/e_n=\rho^{\,n+1}\to0$), and sublinear $e_n=1/(n+1)$ (the quotient tends to $1$: not Q-linear for any $\rho\lt1$). Move $\rho$.

Pitfall

"Linear" sounds fast but $\rho=0.99$ is linear too: $460$ iterations per digit. And a rate is a statement about the quotient of consecutive errors, not about the error itself: $e_n=1/(n+1)$ goes to zero but with quotient $\frac{n+1}{n+2}\to1$, so it is not Q-linear.

To remember

Q-linear: $e_{n+1}\le\rho\,e_n$, $\rho\lt1$ (straight line on a log plot). Q-superlinear: $e_{n+1}/e_n\to0$ (the line bends down).

Running examples: mesh denoising, segmentation, consensus, logistic regression

Valued graphs and four problems

Valued graph. $\mathcal V=\{v^{(i)}\mid i\in\{1,\dots,M\}\}$ is the set of vertices (objects), $\mathcal E=\{e^{(i,j)}\mid (i,j)\in\mathbb E\}$ the set of edges (object relationships). A directed reflexive graph has $|\mathbb E|\le M^2$ edges, a directed nonreflexive one $|\mathbb E|\le M(M-1)$, an undirected nonreflexive one $|\mathbb E|\le M(M-1)/2$. Weights $(x^{(i)})_{1\le i\le M}$ live on vertices and $(x^{(i,j)})_{(i,j)\in\mathbb E}$ on edges (scalars or vectors).

Mesh denoising (undirected nonreflexive graph: vertices and edges of the mesh; $x^{(i)}\in\R^3$ the true coordinates of vertex $i$, $y^{(i)}$ the measured ones; $N=3M$, $D=\R^N$): $$f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^M\Big(\norm{x^{(i)}-y^{(i)}}^2+\Big(\sum_{j\in\mathbb N_i}\omega_{i,j}\norm{x^{(i)}-x^{(j)}}^2\Big)^{1/2}\Big),$$ $\mathbb N_i$ the neighbourhood of vertex $i$, $(\omega_{i,j})_{j\in\mathbb N_i}$ nonnegative regularization constants.

Segmentation (undirected nonreflexive regular graph of pixels; $x^{(i)}\in\mathbb L$ the label of pixel $i$, $\mathbb L\subset\R$ finite; $y^{(i)}$ its intensity; $N=M$, $D=\mathbb L^M$): $$f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^M\alpha_i^p\,\abs{x^{(i)}-y^{(i)}}^p+\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathbb E}\omega_{i,j}^q\,\abs{x^{(i)}-x^{(j)}}^q,\qquad (p,q)\in[1,+\infty[^2,$$ $(\alpha_i)_i$, $(\omega_{i,j})_{(i,j)}$ nonnegative constants.

Multiagent consensus (directed nonreflexive graph of agents; $x^{(i)}\in\R^P$ the estimate at node $i$, $y^{(i)}\in\R^Q$ its observations; $N=MP$): $$D=\big\{(x^{(i)})_{1\le i\le M}\in(\R^P)^M\ \big|\ x^{(1)}=\dots=x^{(M)}\big\},\qquad f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^M g_i(x^{(i)},y^{(i)}),$$ where $-g_i:\R^P\times\R^Q\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ is the utility function at node $i$.

Linear classification / logistic regression. Learn linear functions $\ell_k(z)=x_{k,0}+x_k^\top z$ dividing the input space into $K$ regions, with $\Pr(G=k\mid Z=z)=\dfrac{\exp(x_{k,0}+x_k^\top z)}{1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{K-1}\exp(x_{\ell,0}+x_\ell^\top z)}$ for $k\le K-1$ and $\Pr(G=K\mid Z=z)=\dfrac{1}{1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{K-1}\exp(x_{\ell,0}+x_\ell^\top z)}$. Binary case ($y_i=-1$ if $g_i=1$, $y_i=+1$ if $g_i=2$): $$L(x)=\sum_{i=1}^n\log\big(1+\exp(-y_i\,x^\top z_i)\big),\qquad\text{minimize } f(x)=L(x)+\lambda\norm{x}_1$$ (the $\ell_1$ term regularizes against over-fitting for large $n$).

In one sentence

Every example is a sum of simple terms indexed by vertices and edges: a smooth part, a nonsmooth part, a constraint. The algorithms of pages 4–6 are built to split exactly along these lines.

Hilbert spaces and bounded operators

The course is written in a real Hilbert space $\H$, not only in $\R^N$. Nothing is lost for the practitioner (think $\R^N$ throughout), and two things are gained: the proofs never use coordinates, and the difference between strong and weak convergence, which is what the algorithms really deliver, becomes visible.

Definition

Hilbert space, bounded linear operators, adjoint

Part II · slides 2–4SlidesKey#
Definition

A (real) Hilbert space $\H$ is a complete real vector space endowed with an inner product $\ps{\cdot}{\cdot}$. The associated norm is $(\forall x\in\H)\ \norm{x}=\sqrt{\ps{x}{x}}$. Particular case: $\H=\R^N$ (Euclidean space of dimension $N$). $2^{\H}$ denotes the power set of $\H$ (the family of all subsets of $\H$).

Let $\H$ and $\G$ be two Hilbert spaces. A linear operator $L:\H\to\G$ is bounded (or continuous) if $$\norm{L}=\sup_{\norm{x}\le1}\norm{Lx}\lt+\infty.$$ In finite dimension, every linear operator is bounded. $\B(\H,\G)$ is the Banach space of bounded linear operators from $\H$ to $\G$.

Let $L\in\B(\H,\G)$. Its adjoint $L^*$ is the operator in $\B(\G,\H)$ defined by $$(\forall(x,y)\in\H\times\G)\qquad \ps{Lx}{y}=\ps{x}{L^*y}.$$ We have $\norm{L^*}=\norm{L}$. If $L$ is bijective (an isomorphism), then $L^{-1}\in\B(\G,\H)$ and $(L^{-1})^*=(L^*)^{-1}$. If $\H=\R^N$ and $\G=\R^M$, then $L^*=L^\top$.

In one sentence

A Hilbert space is "$\R^N$ with the right to be infinite-dimensional": angles and lengths from the inner product, limits guaranteed by completeness; the adjoint is the transpose written without coordinates.

What each word buys. The inner product gives orthogonality, projections, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $\abs{\ps{x}{y}}\le\norm x\norm y$ and the identity $\norm{x+y}^2=\norm x^2+2\ps{x}{y}+\norm y^2$ that every proof of pages 2–6 expands. Completeness (every Cauchy sequence converges) is what makes "the iterates converge to something in $\H$" possible at all: the Fejér/Krasnosel'skii–Mann theorems produce Cauchy-like behaviour and need a space in which to land.

Bounded = continuous. For a linear map, $\norm{Lx-Ly}=\norm{L(x-y)}\le\norm{L}\norm{x-y}$, so boundedness is Lipschitz continuity with constant $\norm{L}$; conversely continuity at $0$ gives a bound on the unit ball. The number $\norm{L}$ is the constant that appears in every step-size condition involving a linear operator ($\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm{L}^2\gt\beta/2$ on page 6, $\gamma\lt2/\norm{L}^2$ for a least-squares gradient step).

The adjoint is the operator that lets you move $L$ from one side of an inner product to the other. It exists and is unique by the Riesz representation theorem (for fixed $y$, $x\mapsto\ps{Lx}{y}$ is a bounded linear form, hence equals $\ps{x}{\cdot}$ for a unique vector, called $L^*y$). In coordinates it is the transpose. You will use it every time you differentiate a composition: the gradient of $x\mapsto\tfrac12\norm{Lx-z}^2$ is $L^*(Lx-z)$ (see Gâteaux differentiability), the subdifferential of $f\circ L$ involves $L^*\partial f(L\cdot)$ (page 2), and the dual variable of page 6 lives in $\G$ and comes back through $L^*$.

Examples

$\R^N$ with $\ps{x}{y}=x^\top y$; $\ell^2(\N)$, the square-summable sequences with $\ps{x}{y}=\sum_k x_ky_k$ (the infinite-dimensional example used below for weak convergence); $L^2(\Omega)$ with $\ps{u}{v}=\int uv$ (images as functions); the product space $\H^n$ with $\ps{x}{y}=\sum_{i=1}^n\ps{x_i}{y_i}$ (used in the next card). A matrix $A\in\R^{M\times N}$ is an element of $\B(\R^N,\R^M)$ with $\norm{A}$ its largest singular value and $A^*=A^\top$.

Pitfall

$\norm{L}$ is the operator norm (largest singular value), not the Frobenius norm. For a convolution with a kernel $h$, $\norm{L}=\max_\nu\abs{\hat h(\nu)}$ (the largest modulus of the Fourier transform of $h$); for a wavelet or tight frame $L^*L=\mu\Id$ and $\norm{L}=\sqrt\mu$. You will need this number in the labs.

To remember

$\ps{Lx}{y}=\ps{x}{L^*y}$; $\norm{L}=\sup_{\norm x\le1}\norm{Lx}=\norm{L^*}$; in $\R^N$, $L^*=L^\top$.

Proposition

The adjoint of $x\mapsto(x,\dots,x)$ is the sum

Part II · slide 4SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $n\ge1$ and let $\H^n$ carry the inner product $\ps{x}{y}=\sum_{i=1}^n\ps{x_i}{y_i}$. If $$L:\H\to\H^n:\ x\mapsto(x,\dots,x),\qquad\text{then}\qquad L^*:\H^n\to\H:\ y=(y_1,\dots,y_n)\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^n y_i.$$

In one sentence

Copying a vector $n$ times is adjoint to adding $n$ vectors together.

Why it is true

The inner product on the product space is a sum over the components; pairing the $n$ identical copies of $x$ with the $n$ components of $y$ gives $n$ inner products with the same left factor $x$, and bilinearity pulls the sum inside the right factor. The sentence to say out loud: the adjoint of "duplicate" is "sum", because the product inner product is itself a sum.

Hint — try it first

Compute $\ps{Lx}{y}$ with the definition of the inner product of $\H^n$ and read off what multiplies $x$.

Proof T16 product-space lifting

1. Expand. For $x\in\H$ and $y=(y_1,\dots,y_n)\in\H^n$, $$\ps{Lx}{y}=\ps{(x,\dots,x)}{(y_1,\dots,y_n)}=\sum_{i=1}^n\ps{x}{y_i}=\Big\langle x\ \Big|\ \sum_{i=1}^n y_i\Big\rangle.$$

2. Identify. By definition of the adjoint, $\ps{Lx}{y}=\ps{x}{L^*y}$ for all $x$; since the vector $u=\sum_i y_i$ satisfies $\ps{x}{u}=\ps{Lx}{y}$ for every $x$, and a vector is determined by its inner products with all $x$ (take $x=u-L^*y$), $L^*y=\sum_{i=1}^ny_i$.

3. Two by-products. $\norm{Lx}^2=\sum_{i=1}^n\norm{x}^2=n\norm x^2$, so $\norm{L}=\sqrt n$; and $L^*Lx=\sum_{i=1}^nx=nx$, i.e. $L^*L=n\,\Id$.

□
Why this tiny example matters

It is the T16 product-space trick, the engine of every parallel algorithm of pages 5–6. A sum $\sum_{i=1}^nf_i(x)$ of $n$ functions of the same variable is hard to handle with one prox; but on $\H^n$ the separable function $F(y)=\sum_if_i(y_i)$ has a prox computed component by component, in parallel ($\prox_F y=(\prox_{f_1}y_1,\dots,\prox_{f_n}y_n)$, page 3), and $$\sum_{i=1}^nf_i(x)=F(Lx)=\min_{y\in D}\,F(y)\quad\text{with}\quad D=\ran L=\{(x,\dots,x)\mid x\in\H\}$$ the consensus subspace. Minimizing $\sum_if_i$ becomes minimizing $F+\ind_D$ on $\H^n$: a separable term plus one linear constraint. The projection onto $D$ is averaging: since $L^*L=n\Id$, $$P_D=L(L^*L)^{-1}L^*=\tfrac1n LL^*:\ (y_1,\dots,y_n)\mapsto(\bar y,\dots,\bar y),\qquad \bar y=\tfrac1n\sum_{i=1}^ny_i .$$ So an iteration alternates "each agent solves its own prox" and "everybody averages": this is exactly parallel Douglas–Rachford / PPXA+ (page 5) and consensus ADMM (page 6).

Pitfall

The adjoint depends on the inner product chosen on $\H^n$. With weights $\ps{x}{y}=\sum_i\omega_i\ps{x_i}{y_i}$ the adjoint becomes $y\mapsto\sum_i\omega_iy_i$ and the projection onto $D$ a weighted average. Always say which inner product you use.

To remember

$L=$ duplicate, $L^*=$ sum, $L^*L=n\Id$, $P_{\ran L}=$ average. "Lift to the product space, split, then average."

Functions with values in $]-\infty,+\infty]$: domain, indicator, epigraph, lower semicontinuity

Four definitions that turn a function into a geometric object. The epigraph is the dictionary: "proper" means it is nonempty, "lower semicontinuous" means it is closed, "convex" means it is convex.

Definition

Domain and proper functions

Part II · slide 5SlidesKey#
Definition

Let $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ where $\H$ is a Hilbert space.

  • The domain of $f$ is $\dom f=\{x\in\H\mid f(x)\lt+\infty\}$.
  • The function $f$ is proper if $\dom f\neq\emptyset$.
In one sentence

The domain is where the function is "really defined"; proper means there is at least one admissible point, so that minimizing $f$ is not a vacuous question.

With the $+\infty$ convention, "$f(x)=+\infty$" reads "$x$ is forbidden". The domain collects the allowed points. A function that is $+\infty$ everywhere has $\inf f=+\infty$ and every point is trivially a minimizer: the theory would say nothing. "Proper" rules this out, and together with "$-\infty$ is not a value" it guarantees that $\inf f\lt+\infty$ and that $f(\hat x)$ is a real number whenever a minimizer exists.

Indicator function of a set

Definition

Let $C\subset\H$. The indicator function of $C$ is $$(\forall x\in\H)\qquad \ind_C(x)=\begin{cases}0 & \text{if } x\in C\\ +\infty & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$ Example: $C=[\delta_1,\delta_2]$.

In one sentence

$\ind_C$ is the constraint "$x\in C$" written as a cost: free inside, infinitely expensive outside.

Lower semicontinuity: the sequential definition

Part II · slide 10SlidesKey#
Definition

Let $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$. $f$ is lower semi-continuous (l.s.c.) at $x\in\H$ if, for every sequence $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ of $\H$, $$x_n\to x\quad\Rightarrow\quad \liminf_{n\to+\infty} f(x_n)\ \ge\ f(x).$$ $f$ is lower semi-continuous on $\H$ if it is l.s.c. at every $x\in\H$. Equivalently (next card): $f$ is l.s.c. on $\H$ if and only if $\epi f$ is closed.

In one sentence

Along any approach to $x$, the value at $x$ is never higher than what the approaching values promise: the function may jump up when you leave a point, never drop below the limit at the point itself.

Continuity says $f(x_n)\to f(x)$; lower semicontinuity keeps only one half of it, the half that minimization needs. If $x_n$ is a minimizing sequence converging to $x$, we want $f(x)\le\lim f(x_n)=\inf f$, i.e. we want the limit point to be at least as good as the sequence: that is exactly $f(x)\le\liminf f(x_n)$. The other half ($f(x)\ge\limsup f(x_n)$, "upper semicontinuity") would prevent nothing useful and would exclude indicator functions of closed sets, which jump from $0$ to $+\infty$.

The $\liminf$ is used (not $\lim$) because $f(x_n)$ need not converge; $\liminf$ always exists in $[-\infty,+\infty]$ (definition below). Equivalent formulations, worth knowing: $f$ is lsc on $\H$ iff $\epi f$ is closed, iff every lower level set $\lev_{\le\zeta}f=\{x\mid f(x)\le\zeta\}$ is closed (take a sequence $x_n\to x$ with $f(x_n)\le\zeta$: then $f(x)\le\liminf f(x_n)\le\zeta$).

The four pictures of the slides, with the verdicts:

Top: at a jump, lsc holds exactly when the function takes the lower value at the jump point (filled dot). Bottom: $\ind_{]a,b[}$ is not lsc at $a$ (approach $a$ from inside: $f(x_n)=0$ but $f(a)=+\infty$), while $\ind_{[a,b]}$ is lsc (approach from outside: $f(x_n)=+\infty\ge f(a)=0$, fine; from inside: $0\ge0$).

Pitfall

Lsc is a condition at the limit point, with the inequality in one direction only. A function can be lsc and wildly discontinuous ($\ind_C$ for $C$ closed, or $\abs{\cdot}$ with the value at $0$ lowered to $-1$). Conversely $x\mapsto\ind_{\{0\}}(x)$ with the value at $0$ raised to $1$ and $0$ elsewhere, i.e. $f=\mathbf 1_{\{0\}}$, is not lsc: $x_n=1/n\to0$, $f(x_n)=0$, $f(0)=1$.

To remember

lsc: $f(x)\le\liminf f(x_n)$ whenever $x_n\to x$. "The limit is at least as good as the sequence." Jumps are allowed if the point takes the low value.

Proposition

$f$ is l.s.c. on $\H$ $\iff$ $\epi f$ is closed

Part II · slide 10SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$. Then $f$ is lower semi-continuous on $\H$ if and only if $\epi f$ is a closed subset of $\H\times\R$.

In one sentence

"Limits of points above the graph stay above the graph" is the same sentence as "the value at a limit point is below the limit of the values".

Why it is true

A point of the epigraph is a pair (point, height) with height $\ge$ value. A converging sequence of such pairs has converging heights; lsc pushes the value at the limit point below the limit of the values, hence below the limit height, so the limit pair is in the epigraph. Conversely, if the epigraph is closed, run the argument on the pairs $(x_n,f(x_n))$ (after extracting a subsequence that realizes the $\liminf$): their limit $(x,\liminf f(x_n))$ must be in the epigraph, which is precisely the lsc inequality.

Note

The slides state the equivalence without proof; here is a complete one. The topology on $\H\times\R$ is the product one: $(x_n,\zeta_n)\to(x,\zeta)$ iff $x_n\to x$ and $\zeta_n\to\zeta$.

Hint — try it first

($\Rightarrow$) take $(x_n,\zeta_n)\in\epi f$ converging and compare $f(x)$ with $\liminf f(x_n)$ and with $\liminf\zeta_n$. ($\Leftarrow$) use a subsequence along which $f(x_{k_n})\to\liminf f(x_n)$ and feed the pairs $(x_{k_n},\ell+\varepsilon)$ to the closed epigraph.

Proof

1. lsc ⇒ closed. Let $(x_n,\zeta_n)_{n\in\N}$ be a sequence of $\epi f$ converging to $(x,\zeta)\in\H\times\R$. Then $x_n\to x$, $\zeta_n\to\zeta$ and $f(x_n)\le\zeta_n$ for every $n$. Lower semicontinuity at $x$ and monotonicity of $\liminf$ give $$f(x)\le\liminf_{n}f(x_n)\le\liminf_n\zeta_n=\zeta .$$ In particular $f(x)\le\zeta\lt+\infty$, so $x\in\dom f$ and $(x,\zeta)\in\epi f$. Hence $\epi f$ is closed.

2. closed ⇒ lsc. Assume $\epi f$ closed, let $x_n\to x$ and set $\ell=\liminf_nf(x_n)\in[-\infty,+\infty]$. If $\ell=+\infty$ there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, by the definition of $\liminf$ there is a subsequence $(x_{k_n})$ with $f(x_{k_n})\to\ell$.

3. The case $\ell=-\infty$ is impossible. If $\ell=-\infty$, fix any $\zeta\in\R$: for $n$ large, $f(x_{k_n})\le\zeta$, so $(x_{k_n},\zeta)\in\epi f$; these pairs converge to $(x,\zeta)$, which belongs to the closed set $\epi f$, so $f(x)\le\zeta$. Since $\zeta\in\R$ was arbitrary, $f(x)=-\infty$, contradicting $f(x)\in\,]-\infty,+\infty]$. So $\ell\in\R$.

4. The case $\ell\in\R$. Fix $\varepsilon\gt0$. For $n$ large, $f(x_{k_n})\le\ell+\varepsilon$, hence $(x_{k_n},\ell+\varepsilon)\in\epi f$, and $(x_{k_n},\ell+\varepsilon)\to(x,\ell+\varepsilon)\in\epi f$ by closedness: $f(x)\le\ell+\varepsilon$. Letting $\varepsilon\to0$: $f(x)\le\ell=\liminf_nf(x_n)$, which is lsc at $x$.

Proposition

Continuous ⇒ l.s.c.; finite sums and suprema of l.s.c. functions are l.s.c.

Part II · slide 11Slides#
Statement
  • Every continuous function on $\H$ is l.s.c.
  • Every finite sum of l.s.c. functions is l.s.c.
  • Let $(f_i)_{i\in I}$ be a family of l.s.c. functions. Then $\sup_{i\in I}f_i$ is l.s.c.
In one sentence

Lsc survives the two operations that build cost functions, adding finitely many terms and taking a supremum of arbitrarily many, because the $\liminf$ is superadditive and closed sets survive arbitrary intersections.

Why it is true

Continuity gives $\lim f(x_n)=f(x)$, which is more than the one-sided inequality. For sums, the inequality $\liminf(a_n+b_n)\ge\liminf a_n+\liminf b_n$ does the work (the worst case of a sum is at least the sum of the worst cases). For suprema, the cleanest view is geometric: $\epi(\sup_if_i)=\bigcap_i\epi f_i$ (being above the sup means being above each $f_i$), and an intersection of closed sets is closed, whatever the number of sets. Say it out loud: sup $=$ intersection of epigraphs, and intersections preserve closedness.

Hint — try it first

For the sum, write $a_n+b_n\ge\inf_{k\ge m}a_k+\inf_{k\ge m}b_k$ for $n\ge m$. For the sup, either use $f_i(x)\le\liminf f_i(x_n)\le\liminf\sup_jf_j(x_n)$ for each $i$, or intersect the epigraphs.

Proof

1. Continuous ⇒ lsc. If $x_n\to x$ and $f$ is continuous at $x$, then $f(x_n)\to f(x)$, so $\liminf f(x_n)=\lim f(x_n)=f(x)\ge f(x)$.

2. Sums. Let $f,g$ be lsc with values in $]-\infty,+\infty]$ and $x_n\to x$. For $n\ge m$, $f(x_n)+g(x_n)\ge\inf_{k\ge m}f(x_k)+\inf_{k\ge m}g(x_k)$ (no $\infty-\infty$ can occur since neither function takes the value $-\infty$). Taking $\inf_{n\ge m}$ on the left and letting $m\to+\infty$: $$\liminf_n\big(f(x_n)+g(x_n)\big)\ \ge\ \liminf_nf(x_n)+\liminf_ng(x_n)\ \ge\ f(x)+g(x).$$ Induction gives any finite sum.

3. Suprema, sequential route. Let $g=\sup_{i\in I}f_i$ and $x_n\to x$. For each $i$, $f_i(x_n)\le g(x_n)$, so $f_i(x)\le\liminf_nf_i(x_n)\le\liminf_ng(x_n)$. Taking the supremum over $i$: $g(x)\le\liminf_ng(x_n)$.

3'. Suprema, epigraph route. $(x,\zeta)\in\epi g\iff\sup_if_i(x)\le\zeta\iff(\forall i)\ f_i(x)\le\zeta\iff(x,\zeta)\in\bigcap_i\epi f_i$. Each $\epi f_i$ is closed (previous card), an arbitrary intersection of closed sets is closed, so $\epi g$ is closed and $g$ is lsc.

□
Pitfall

An infimum of lsc functions is not lsc in general: $f_n(x)=1-\min(1,n\abs x)$ are continuous, but $\inf_nf_n=\mathbf 1_{\{0\}}$ ($1$ at $0$, $0$ elsewhere) is not lsc at $0$. This matters: the Moreau envelope $\inf_y f(y)+\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{y-x}^2$ (page 3) is an infimum, and its continuity has to be proved by other means.

To remember

lsc is stable under $+$ (finite) and $\sup$ (arbitrary), not under $\inf$. Continuous $\Rightarrow$ lsc. Norms, affine maps, $\ind_C$ with $C$ closed: all lsc, hence so are the sums you will build from them.

Three tools of analysis, stated exactly as the slides state them. They are the gears of every existence proof: a minimizing sequence, a convergent subsequence, and an inequality that survives the limit.

Definition

Strong and weak convergence

Part II · slide 7SlidesKey#
Definition

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space, $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ a sequence in $\H$ and $\hat x\in\H$.

  • $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ converges strongly to $\hat x$ if $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to+\infty}\norm{x_n-\hat x}=0$. Notation: $x_n\to\hat x$.
  • $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ converges weakly to $\hat x$ if $\displaystyle(\forall y\in\H)\ \lim_{n\to+\infty}\ps{y}{x_n-\hat x}=0$. Notation: $x_n\wto\hat x$.

Remark: $x_n\to x\ \Rightarrow\ x_n\wto x$. In a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, strong and weak convergence are equivalent.

In one sentence

Strong: the distance to the limit vanishes. Weak: every "measurement" $\ps{y}{\cdot}$ of the sequence converges to the measurement of the limit, coordinate by coordinate, without control of the norm.

Strong ⇒ weak by Cauchy–Schwarz: $\abs{\ps{y}{x_n-\hat x}}\le\norm y\,\norm{x_n-\hat x}\to0$.

Finite dimension: weak ⇒ strong. Take $y=e_k$, the $k$-th basis vector: the $k$-th coordinate of $x_n-\hat x$ tends to $0$; there are finitely many coordinates, so $\norm{x_n-\hat x}^2=\sum_{k=1}^N\ps{e_k}{x_n-\hat x}^2\to0$. In infinite dimension the sum has infinitely many terms and the argument breaks, as the example below shows.

Why weak convergence is the natural output of the algorithms. The Fejér / Krasnosel'skii–Mann theorems of page 4 produce bounded sequences. In infinite dimension a bounded sequence need not have a strongly convergent subsequence, but it always has a weakly convergent one (the closed ball is weakly sequentially compact). The convergence theorems of pages 4–6 therefore conclude "$x_n\wto\hat x$"; in $\R^N$ that is plain convergence, so nothing is lost in practice, and the proofs gain in honesty: they identify exactly what is true in general.

Extra · the standard infinite-dimensional exampleExtra

In $\ell^2(\N)$ let $e_n$ be the $n$-th basis sequence ($1$ in position $n$, $0$ elsewhere). For any $y\in\ell^2$, Bessel's inequality $\sum_n\ps{y}{e_n}^2\le\norm y^2\lt+\infty$ forces $\ps{y}{e_n}=y_n\to0$: so $e_n\wto0$. But $\norm{e_n-0}=1$ for all $n$ and $\norm{e_n-e_m}=\sqrt2$ for $n\neq m$: no subsequence converges strongly. The weak limit $0$ is not even on the unit sphere where the sequence lives: the norm is only weakly lower semicontinuous ($\norm{\hat x}\le\liminf\norm{x_n}$ when $x_n\wto\hat x$), a fact used in Opial's lemma on page 4.

Definition

Bounded, closed and compact sets

Part II · slide 7Slides#
Definition

Let $S$ be a subset of a Hilbert space $\H$.

  • $S$ is bounded if it is included in a ball.
  • $S$ is closed if the limit of every converging sequence of elements of $S$ belongs to $S$.
  • $S$ is compact if, from every sequence $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ of $S$, one can extract a subsequence $(x_{n_k})_{k\in\N}$ which converges to a point of $S$.

If $S$ is compact, then it is closed and bounded. The converse holds when $\H$ is finite-dimensional.

In one sentence

Compact = "every sequence has a convergent subsequence, and the limit stays inside": the one property that manufactures limit points out of nothing.

Existence proofs need to produce a candidate minimizer out of a minimizing sequence. Nothing forces that sequence to converge (it can oscillate between two minimizers, see the widget of Weierstrass' theorem); compactness supplies a subsequence that does, and closedness keeps the limit in the admissible set. This is why "compact" is defined here sequentially: it is exactly the form used in the proofs.

Compact ⇒ closed and bounded. Closed: a convergent sequence of $S$ has a subsequence converging in $S$, and a subsequence of a convergent sequence has the same limit, so the limit is in $S$. Bounded: if $S$ were unbounded, pick $x_n\in S$ with $\norm{x_n}\ge n$; no subsequence can converge (a convergent sequence is bounded), contradiction.

Finite dimension ⇒ converse (Bolzano–Weierstrass). In $\R^N$ a bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence (extract coordinate by coordinate); closedness puts the limit in $S$. In infinite dimension this fails: the closed unit ball of $\ell^2$ is closed and bounded, but $(e_n)$ has no convergent subsequence since $\norm{e_n-e_m}=\sqrt2$. This single fact is the reason the course states Weierstrass' theorem with "compact" and the coercivity theorem with "finite-dimensional".

Examples

$[a,b]$ and closed balls of $\R^N$: compact. $]a,b[$: bounded, not closed. $\R$, a closed half-space, a closed subspace: closed, not bounded. The consensus subspace $\{x^{(1)}=\dots=x^{(M)}\}$: closed (a kernel of a continuous linear map), unbounded. The segmentation domain $\mathbb L^M$ with $\mathbb L$ finite: compact (finite), not convex.

To remember

Compact $\Rightarrow$ closed $+$ bounded; $\Leftarrow$ in $\R^N$ only. Compact is the extraction property.

Limits inferior and superior

Part II · slide 8Slides#
Definition

Let $(\xi_n)_{n\in\N}$ be a sequence of elements of $[-\infty,+\infty]$. Its infimum limit is $$\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\xi_n=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\inf\{\xi_k\mid k\ge n\}\in[-\infty,+\infty]$$ and its supremum limit is $\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to+\infty}\xi_n=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\sup\{\xi_k\mid k\ge n\}\in[-\infty,+\infty]$.

  • $\limsup\xi_n=-\liminf(-\xi_n)$;
  • $\liminf\xi_n\le\limsup\xi_n$;
  • $\lim_{n\to+\infty}\xi_n=\xi\in[-\infty,+\infty]$ if and only if $\liminf\xi_n=\limsup\xi_n=\xi$.
In one sentence

The liminf is the lowest value the sequence keeps coming back to; it always exists, which is why lsc is phrased with it.

The tail infimum $m_n=\inf\{\xi_k\mid k\ge n\}$ can only increase with $n$ (we remove terms from the set), so it has a limit in $[-\infty,+\infty]$, with no assumption on $(\xi_n)$. That limit is the $\liminf$. Symmetrically the tail supremum decreases to the $\limsup$. The three listed properties follow: the first by $\inf(-A)=-\sup A$, the second from $m_n\le M_n$, the third because a sequence squeezed between two tails with the same limit converges, and conversely.

Facts used in the proofs of this page (all immediate from the definition): (i) there is a subsequence $(\xi_{k_n})$ with $\xi_{k_n}\to\liminf\xi_n$ (choose $k_n\ge n$ with $\xi_{k_n}$ close to $m_n$); (ii) the $\liminf$ along any subsequence is $\ge$ the $\liminf$ of the full sequence; (iii) $\liminf(a_n+b_n)\ge\liminf a_n+\liminf b_n$ when no $-\infty$ occurs; (iv) if $\xi_n\le\zeta_n$ for all $n$ then $\liminf\xi_n\le\liminf\zeta_n$.

$\xi_n=(-1)^n\big(1+\tfrac{1}{n+1}\big)$: the tail infima (lower staircase) rise to $\liminf\xi_n=-1$, the tail suprema fall to $\limsup\xi_n=1$; the sequence has no limit.

To remember

$\liminf=\lim_n\inf_{k\ge n}$ (nondecreasing tails, always exists). $\lim$ exists $\iff\liminf=\limsup$. Some subsequence attains the $\liminf$.

Existence of a minimizer

The first of the four questions. Two theorems, one mechanism: a minimizing sequence, a convergent subsequence (compactness), and lower semicontinuity to pass to the limit. Coercivity is the device that produces compactness when the domain is not bounded.

Definition

Local, global and strict minimizers

Part II · slide 12Slides#
Definition

Let $S$ be a nonempty set of a Hilbert space $\H$, let $f:S\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be a proper function and let $\hat x\in S$.

  • $\hat x$ is a local minimizer of $f$ if $\hat x\in\dom f$ and there exists an open neighbourhood $O$ of $\hat x$ such that $(\forall x\in O\cap S)\ f(\hat x)\le f(x)$.
  • $\hat x$ is a (global) minimizer of $f$ if $(\forall x\in S)\ f(\hat x)\le f(x)$.
  • $\hat x$ is a strict local minimizer of $f$ if there exists an open neighbourhood $O$ of $\hat x$ such that $(\forall x\in(O\cap S)\setminus\{\hat x\})\ f(\hat x)\lt f(x)$.
  • $\hat x$ is a strict (global) minimizer of $f$ if $(\forall x\in S\setminus\{\hat x\})\ f(\hat x)\lt f(x)$.
In one sentence

Global: nobody beats $\hat x$. Local: nobody nearby beats it. Strict: nobody even ties.

The requirement $\hat x\in\dom f$ in the local definition is not decoration: without it, any point in the interior of $\{f=+\infty\}$ would be a "local minimizer" (all its neighbours tie at $+\infty$). The global definition needs no such clause: if $f$ is proper, a global minimizer automatically has a finite value.

The gap between local and global is the whole difficulty of nonconvex optimization (think of the segmentation problem or any deep-learning loss). Convexity closes the gap: every local minimizer of a convex function is global. Strictness is what uniqueness statements are made of: a strict global minimizer is the unique minimizer, and strict convexity makes every minimizer strict.

A nonconvex $f$ on $S=[-3,3]$: a strict local minimizer (left), a flat stretch of local minimizers that are not strict (middle), and the strict global minimizer (right).

To remember

Local: $f(\hat x)\le f(x)$ on $O\cap S$, with $\hat x\in\dom f$. Global: on all of $S$. Strict: $\lt$ for $x\neq\hat x$. Convex $\Rightarrow$ local $=$ global.

Theorem

Weierstrass: l.s.c. on a compact set ⇒ a minimizer exists

Part II · slide 13SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Let $S$ be a nonempty compact set of a Hilbert space $\H$. Let $f:S\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be a proper l.s.c. function. Then there exists $\hat x\in S$ such that $$f(\hat x)=\inf_{x\in S}f(x).$$

In one sentence

Take a sequence that drives $f$ down to its infimum; compactness gives it a limit point; lsc says the limit point is at least as good as the sequence: it is a minimizer.

Why it is true

The infimum $\delta$ exists but may be approached without being attained. A minimizing sequence approaches it. Two things could go wrong: the sequence could escape (no limit point), or the limit point could be a bad point (the function could jump up there). Compactness forbids the first, lower semicontinuity forbids the second. There is nothing else in the proof. Say it: minimizing sequence + extraction + lsc at the limit.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $S$ compact: a convergent subsequence of the minimizing sequence, with limit in $S$. Drop it: $f=e^x$ on $S=\R$ (lsc, proper, $\inf f=0$ not attained; the minimizing sequence $x_n=-n$ escapes), or $f(x)=x$ on $S=\,]0,1]$ (not closed; $x_n=1/n$ converges to $0\notin S$).
  • $f$ lsc: passing to the limit in "$f(x_{k_n})\to\delta$". Drop it: $f(x)=x^2$ for $x\neq0$ and $f(0)=1$ on $S=[-1,1]$: compact, $\inf f=0$ not attained.
  • $f$ proper: $\delta\lt+\infty$, so there is a genuine minimizing sequence; otherwise the statement is empty.
  • values $\gt-\infty$: the conclusion $f(\hat x)=\delta$ with $\delta\in\R$ shows a posteriori that $f$ is bounded below on $S$.
Hint — try it first

Let $\delta=\inf_Sf$. Build $(x_n)$ in $S$ with $f(x_n)\to\delta$; extract; use the lsc inequality at the limit and the definition of $\inf$.

Proof T02 direct method

1. Minimizing sequence. Let $\delta=\inf_{x\in S}f(x)$. Since $f$ is proper, $\delta\in[-\infty,+\infty[$. By definition of the infimum there is a sequence $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ of elements of $S$ such that $\lim_{n\to+\infty}f(x_n)=\delta$ (if $\delta\in\R$ choose $x_n$ with $f(x_n)\le\delta+\tfrac1{n+1}$; if $\delta=-\infty$ choose $f(x_n)\le-n$).

2. Extraction. Since $S$ is compact, there exists a subsequence $(x_{k_n})_{n\in\N}$ of $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ such that $x_{k_n}\to\hat x\in S$.

3. Lower semicontinuity. Then $f(\hat x)\le\liminf_{n}f(x_{k_n})=\delta$, because a subsequence of a convergent sequence has the same limit $\delta$.

4. Conclusion. On the other hand $f(\hat x)\ge\delta$ since $\hat x\in S$ and $\delta$ is the infimum. Hence $f(\hat x)=\delta$; and since $f(\hat x)\gt-\infty$, $\delta\in\R$: the infimum is finite and attained at $\hat x$.

Trick

T02 direct method: whenever you must prove that something is attained, take a sequence that approaches it, make it converge (compactness, or boundedness in finite dimension), and use a semicontinuity property to pass to the limit. The same three moves prove the coercive existence theorem, the existence of the prox (page 3) and the convergence of the MM schemes (page 7).

Definition

Coercive function

Part II · slide 14SlidesKey#
Definition

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space and $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$. $f$ is coercive if $$\lim_{\norm x\to+\infty}f(x)=+\infty,$$ i.e. for every $M\in\R$ there exists $R\gt0$ such that $\norm x\gt R\Rightarrow f(x)\gt M$.

In one sentence

Far away, the function is huge: whatever happens, the minimum has to be looked for inside some ball.

Coercivity is the substitute for a bounded domain. If $f$ is $+\infty$ at infinity, no minimizing sequence can escape: once $f(x_n)\le f(x_0)$ the points $x_n$ are trapped in a ball (T03). In finite dimension that ball is compact and Weierstrass finishes the job. An equivalent formulation worth knowing: $f$ is coercive iff all its lower level sets $\lev_{\le\zeta}f$ are bounded.

The three pictures of the slides:

Left: $f(x)=e^{-x}$ tends to $0$ as $x\to+\infty$: not coercive. Middle: $f\to+\infty$ at both ends: coercive. Right: $\ind_{[a,b]}$ equals $+\infty$ outside a bounded set: coercive.

Examples to classify at once

Coercive: $\norm{\cdot}^2$, $\norm{\cdot}$, $\norm{\cdot}_1$, $\ind_C$ with $C$ bounded, $f+g$ when $f$ is coercive and $g$ bounded below, $x\mapsto\norm{Ax-z}^2$ iff $\Ker A=\{0\}$ (exercise 5). Not coercive: constants, affine functions, $e^x$, $\log(1+e^{-yx})$ (bounded below by $0$, never blows up), $\ind_C$ with $C$ unbounded.

With $a_1,a_2\gt0$ the level sets are ellipses (bounded: coercive, unique minimizer $0$). With one coefficient equal to $0$ they are unbounded strips: not coercive, yet minimizers exist (a whole line): coercivity is sufficient, not necessary. With a negative coefficient they are unbounded hyperbolic regions and $\inf f=-\infty$.

Theorem

Existence in finite dimension: proper + l.s.c. + coercive ⇒ Argmin nonempty compact

Part II · slide 15SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Let $\H$ be a finite-dimensional Hilbert space. Let $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be a proper l.s.c. coercive function. Then the set of minimizers of $f$ is a nonempty compact set.

In one sentence

Coercivity shrinks the problem to a closed ball, finite dimension makes the ball compact, Weierstrass gives a minimizer; the same two facts make Argmin closed and bounded.

Why it is true

Pick any admissible point $x_0$. Far from $x_0$ the function exceeds $f(x_0)$, so no point out there can be a minimizer: everything relevant happens in a ball around $x_0$. A closed ball in finite dimension is compact, and $f$ restricted to it is still proper and lsc, so Weierstrass produces a minimizer of $f$ on the ball; it is a minimizer on all of $\H$ because outside the ball $f$ is larger than $f(x_0)$. Argmin sits inside that ball (bounded) and is closed because lsc passes "being a minimizer" to limits. Say it: coercivity confines, compactness extracts, lsc passes to the limit.

What each hypothesis buys
  • coercive: the ball $S$ on which to apply Weierstrass. Without it: $f=e^x$ (proper, lsc, bounded below, no minimizer).
  • lsc: Weierstrass on $S$, and closedness of $\Argmin f$. Without it: $x^2$ with $f(0)=1$ (coercive, no minimizer).
  • proper: the starting point $x_0$ with $f(x_0)\in\R$.
  • finite dimension: closed bounded $\Rightarrow$ compact. In infinite dimension the theorem fails as stated; it is rescued by convexity (next section), because convex lsc functions are weakly lsc and closed balls are weakly compact.
Hint — try it first

Use coercivity with $M=f(x_0)$ to find a radius $\eta$ such that $f\gt f(x_0)$ outside the ball $S=\{\norm{x-x_0}\le\eta\}$. Apply Weierstrass on $S$. For compactness of Argmin, show it is contained in $S$ and closed.

Proof T03 coercivity confines T02 direct method

1. An admissible point. Since $f$ is proper, there exists $x_0\in\H$ such that $f(x_0)\in\R$.

2. Confinement. The coercivity of $f$ implies that there exists $\eta\in\,]0,+\infty[$ such that, for every $x\in\H$ satisfying $\norm{x-x_0}\gt\eta$, $f(x)\gt f(x_0)$. (Indeed, with $M=f(x_0)$ there is $R$ with $\norm x\gt R\Rightarrow f(x)\gt f(x_0)$; take $\eta=R+\norm{x_0}$: $\norm{x-x_0}\gt\eta$ implies $\norm x\ge\norm{x-x_0}-\norm{x_0}\gt R$.)

3. A compact set. Let $S=\{x\in\H\mid\norm{x-x_0}\le\eta\}$. Then $S\cap\dom f\neq\emptyset$ (it contains $x_0$) and $S$ is compact, being closed and bounded in a finite-dimensional space.

4. Weierstrass on $S$. The restriction of $f$ to $S$ is proper and lsc, so there exists $\hat x\in S$ such that $f(\hat x)=\inf_{x\in S}f(x)\le f(x_0)$. For $x\notin S$, $f(x)\gt f(x_0)\ge f(\hat x)$. Thus $f(\hat x)=\inf_{x\in\H}f(x)$: $\Argmin f\neq\emptyset$.

5. Argmin is bounded. Every minimizer $x$ satisfies $f(x)=f(\hat x)\le f(x_0)$, hence $x\in S$ by step 2: $\Argmin f\subset S$ is bounded.

6. Argmin is closed. Let $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ be a sequence of minimizers converging to some $\bar x\in\H$. By lsc, $f(\bar x)\le\liminf_nf(x_n)=\inf_{x\in\H}f(x)$, and consequently $\bar x\in\Argmin f$. Therefore $\Argmin f$ is closed, and, being closed and bounded in finite dimension, compact.

Convexity

The third word of $\Gz{\H}$ and the one that makes optimization tractable: chords lie above the graph, hence level sets and Argmin are convex, local minimizers are global, and a first-order condition becomes a full characterization.

Definition

Convex set

Part II · slide 16SlidesKey#
Definition

$C\subset\H$ is a convex set if $$(\forall(x,y)\in C^2)(\forall\alpha\in\,]0,1[)\qquad \alpha x+(1-\alpha)y\in C .$$

In one sentence

A set is convex when it contains the whole segment between any two of its points: no bites, no holes, no corners pointing inward.

The point $\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y$ runs along the segment $[x,y]$ as $\alpha$ goes from $1$ ($=x$) to $0$ ($=y$). Convexity is exactly what the T04 chord argument needs: from a point $\hat x\in C$ you can move a little towards any other point $y\in C$ and stay in $C$. That is the only property of $C$ used in the first-order optimality condition.

Left: a chord between two points of $C$ leaves $C$: not convex. Middle and right: convex.

Examples

Convex: $\emptyset$, $\H$, affine subspaces (in particular the consensus subspace $\{x^{(1)}=\dots=x^{(M)}\}$ and $\Ker L$, $\ran L$), balls, half-spaces $\{\ps{u}{x}\le\eta\}$, boxes $[a,b]^N$, the simplex, cones $\{x\ge0\}$, any intersection of convex sets (so the feasible set of linear constraints $\varphi_i(x)=\delta_i$, $\psi_j(x)\le\eta_j$ with affine $\varphi_i,\psi_j$). Not convex: a finite set with at least two points (the segmentation domain $\mathbb L^M$), the unit sphere, the set of $k$-sparse vectors, the union of two disjoint balls.

To remember

$C$ convex $\iff$ $[x,y]\subset C$ for all $x,y\in C$. Intersections of convex sets are convex.

Definition

Gâteaux differentiability

Part II · slide 17SlidesKey#
Definition

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space and let $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be a proper function. $f$ is Gâteaux differentiable at $x\in\dom f$ if there exists $\nabla f(x)\in\H$ such that $$(\forall y\in\H)\qquad \ps{\nabla f(x)}{y}=\lim_{\substack{\alpha\to0\\ \alpha\neq0}}\frac{f(x+\alpha y)-f(x)}{\alpha}.$$

In one sentence

All directional derivatives exist and they are given by one vector through the inner product: $\nabla f(x)$ is the direction of steepest ascent and $\ps{\nabla f(x)}{y}$ the slope in direction $y$.

This is the weakest notion of gradient that still gives the first-order condition: the proof only needs the one-sided limit along a direction $y-\hat x$. Fréchet differentiability (the limit is uniform in $y$) implies Gâteaux differentiability; in finite dimension with continuous partial derivatives they coincide, and $\nabla f(x)$ is the usual gradient. Note that $\alpha$ may be negative: the slopes in directions $y$ and $-y$ must be opposite, which rules out kinks.

Two computations you will redo all year. For $f(x)=\tfrac12\norm{Lx-z}^2$ with $L\in\B(\H,\G)$: $f(x+\alpha y)-f(x)=\alpha\ps{Lx-z}{Ly}+\tfrac{\alpha^2}2\norm{Ly}^2$, so the quotient tends to $\ps{Lx-z}{Ly}=\ps{L^*(Lx-z)}{y}$ and $\nabla f(x)=L^*(Lx-z)$. For $f(x)=\tfrac12\ps{x}{Ax}+\ps{b}{x}$ with $A=A^*$: $\nabla f(x)=Ax+b$.

Pitfall

$\abs{\cdot}$ is not Gâteaux differentiable at $0$: the quotient $\abs{\alpha y}/\alpha=\abs y\,\sgn(\alpha)$ has different limits for $\alpha\to0^+$ and $\alpha\to0^-$. Page 2 repairs this with the subdifferential ($\partial\abs{\cdot}(0)=[-1,1]$). Also, $x$ must be in $\dom f$: a gradient at a forbidden point makes no sense, and the quotient must be finite for every $y$, so in practice $x$ is interior to $\dom f$.

To remember

Gâteaux: $\ps{\nabla f(x)}{y}=\lim_{\alpha\to0}\frac{f(x+\alpha y)-f(x)}\alpha$ for every direction $y$. $\nabla\tfrac12\norm{L\cdot-z}^2=L^*(L\cdot-z)$.

Theorem

First-order optimality over a convex set (variational inequality)

Part II · slide 17SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Let $C$ be a nonempty convex subset of a Hilbert space $\H$. Let $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be Gâteaux differentiable at $\hat x\in C$. If $\hat x$ is a local minimizer of $f$ over $C$, then $$(\forall y\in C)\qquad \ps{\nabla f(\hat x)}{y-\hat x}\ \ge\ 0 .$$ If $\hat x\in\inte(C)$, the condition reduces to $\nabla f(\hat x)=0$.

In one sentence

At a minimizer, no feasible direction goes downhill: the gradient makes an angle of at most $90°$ with every $y-\hat x$, i.e. $-\nabla f(\hat x)$ points out of $C$, into the normal cone.

Why it is true

Convexity of $C$ lets you walk from $\hat x$ straight towards any $y\in C$ while staying in $C$; local minimality says $f$ cannot decrease at the start of that walk; the Gâteaux derivative in the direction $y-\hat x$ is precisely the initial slope of that walk, hence $\ge0$. When $\hat x$ is interior you can walk in every direction and its opposite, so every slope is both $\ge0$ and $\le0$: the gradient vanishes. Out loud: feasible segment, nonnegative difference quotient, pass to the limit.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $C$ convex: the segment $[\hat x,y]$ is feasible, so the difference quotient is computed inside $C$. Drop it: $C=\{0,1\}\subset\R$ and $f(x)=(x-0.4)^2$. The minimizer of $f$ over $C$ is $\hat x=0$, but with $y=1$, $\ps{f'(0)}{y-\hat x}=-0.8\lt0$: the inequality fails. The segment $[0,1]$ is not in $C$, so moving slightly from $\hat x$ towards $y$ leaves $C$ and the proof cannot even start.
  • Gâteaux differentiable at $\hat x$: the one-sided quotient converges. For nonsmooth $f$, page 2 replaces $\nabla f(\hat x)$ by a subgradient: $0\in\partial f(\hat x)+N_C(\hat x)$.
  • local minimizer suffices: only small $\alpha$ are used. The condition is necessary; it becomes sufficient when $f$ is convex (gradient inequality, page 2).
Hint — try it first

For $y\in C$ and small $\alpha\gt0$, $\hat x+\alpha(y-\hat x)\in O\cap C$. Compare $f$ there with $f(\hat x)$, divide by $\alpha$, let $\alpha\to0^+$.

Proof T04 chord argument

1. A feasible segment. Let $O$ be an open neighbourhood of $\hat x$ on which $f(\hat x)\le f(x)$ for all $x\in O\cap C$, and fix $y\in C$. For $\alpha\in[0,1]$, $(1-\alpha)\hat x+\alpha y=\hat x+\alpha(y-\hat x)\in C$ by convexity of $C$. Since $O$ is open and $\hat x+\alpha(y-\hat x)\to\hat x$ as $\alpha\to0$, there exists $\delta\in\,]0,1]$ such that, for every $\alpha\in\,]0,\delta]$, $\hat x+\alpha(y-\hat x)\in O\cap C$, hence $$f\big(\hat x+\alpha(y-\hat x)\big)\ \ge\ f(\hat x).$$

2. Nonnegative difference quotients. For $\alpha\in\,]0,\delta]$, dividing by $\alpha\gt0$: $$\frac{f\big(\hat x+\alpha(y-\hat x)\big)-f(\hat x)}{\alpha}\ \ge\ 0 .$$

3. Pass to the limit. By Gâteaux differentiability at $\hat x$ in the direction $y-\hat x$, the quotient converges to $\ps{\nabla f(\hat x)}{y-\hat x}$ as $\alpha\to0$ (in particular as $\alpha\to0^+$). Limits preserve weak inequalities, so $\ps{\nabla f(\hat x)}{y-\hat x}\ge0$.

4. Interior point. If $\hat x\in\inte(C)$, there is $r\gt0$ with $B(\hat x,r)\subset C$. For any $z\in\H\setminus\{0\}$, the points $y_\pm=\hat x\pm\tfrac r{2\norm z}z$ belong to $C$; applying step 3 to $y_\pm$ gives $\pm\tfrac r{2\norm z}\ps{\nabla f(\hat x)}{z}\ge0$, so $\ps{\nabla f(\hat x)}{z}=0$ for every $z$, i.e. $\nabla f(\hat x)=0$ (take $z=\nabla f(\hat x)$).

Definition

Convex function, concave function

Part II · slides 18–20SlidesKey#
Definition

$f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ is a convex function if $$(\forall(x,y)\in\H^2)(\forall\alpha\in\,]0,1[)\qquad f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\ \le\ \alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y),$$ with the conventions $a+(+\infty)=+\infty$ and $\alpha\cdot(+\infty)=+\infty$ for $\alpha\gt0$.

$f:\H\to[-\infty,+\infty[$ is concave if $-f$ is convex.

In one sentence

Chords lie above the graph: between two points, the function never bulges above the straight line joining their values.

Read the inequality on the segment $[x,y]$: the left side is the value of $f$ at the point of parameter $\alpha$, the right side the value of the chord (the affine interpolation of $f(x)$ and $f(y)$) at the same point. Convexity says the graph is below every chord. Three consequences drive the whole course: (i) a convex function has no "valley you can be trapped in" (local $=$ global); (ii) it has a well-behaved set of supporting lines at every interior point (the subdifferential, page 2); (iii) adding $\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-x}^2$ to it gives a function with a unique minimizer (the prox, page 3).

The conventions with $+\infty$ mean: if $x$ or $y$ is forbidden, the right side is $+\infty$ and the inequality says nothing; so convexity only tests pairs in $\dom f$, and in particular forces $\dom f$ to be convex (next card).

$\abs x$: convex. $\sqrt{\abs x}$: not convex (the chord from $0$ to $1$ passes below the graph). $\ind_{[-\delta,\delta]}$: convex.

The portions of the chord drawn in red are the $\alpha$'s for which it fails; a convex function never shows red. The flat function $\max(\abs x-1,0)$ is convex but not strictly: the gap can be exactly $0$ with $x\neq y$.

Proposition

$f$ is convex $\iff$ $\epi f$ is convex; the domain of a convex function is convex

Part II · slides 19–20SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$. Then $f$ is convex if and only if its epigraph $\epi f$ is a convex subset of $\H\times\R$. Consequently, if $f$ is convex then $\dom f$ is convex.

In one sentence

"Graph below chords" and "the region above the graph contains its segments" are the same picture.

Why it is true

A segment between two points above the graph is a chord shifted upwards; it stays above the graph exactly when the chord does. For the converse, apply the convexity of the epigraph to the two points on the graph, $(x,f(x))$ and $(y,f(y))$: the segment between them is the chord, and "it lies in the epigraph" is the chord inequality. The domain is the projection of the epigraph on $\H$, and projections of convex sets are convex. Out loud: chords of the graph are segments of the epigraph.

($\Rightarrow$) take $(x,\zeta),(y,\xi)\in\epi f$ and bound $f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)$. ($\Leftarrow$) if $x,y\in\dom f$, use the points $(x,f(x))$ and $(y,f(y))$.

Proof

1. convex ⇒ convex epigraph. Let $(x,\zeta),(y,\xi)\in\epi f$ and $\alpha\in\,]0,1[$. Then $f(x)\le\zeta$, $f(y)\le\xi$ and, by convexity, $$f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\le\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y)\le\alpha\zeta+(1-\alpha)\xi\lt+\infty .$$ Hence $\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y\in\dom f$ and $\alpha(x,\zeta)+(1-\alpha)(y,\xi)=(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y,\ \alpha\zeta+(1-\alpha)\xi)\in\epi f$.

2. convex epigraph ⇒ convex. Let $x,y\in\H$ and $\alpha\in\,]0,1[$. If $f(x)=+\infty$ or $f(y)=+\infty$, the right-hand side of the chord inequality is $+\infty$ and there is nothing to prove. Otherwise $(x,f(x))$ and $(y,f(y))$ belong to $\epi f$, so by convexity of $\epi f$ the point $(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y,\ \alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y))$ belongs to $\epi f$, which reads $f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\le\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y)$.

3. The domain. If $x,y\in\dom f$ and $\alpha\in\,]0,1[$, the chord inequality gives $f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\le\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y)\lt+\infty$, so $\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y\in\dom f$. (Equivalently: $\dom f$ is the image of the convex set $\epi f$ by the linear projection $(x,\zeta)\mapsto x$, and linear images of convex sets are convex.)

Proposition

Sums, suprema, the class $\Gz{\H}$, indicator of a closed convex set

Part II · slide 20SlidesKey#
Statement
  • Every finite sum of convex functions is convex.
  • Let $(f_i)_{i\in I}$ be a family of convex functions. Then $\sup_{i\in I}f_i$ is convex.
  • $\Gz{\H}$ denotes the class of convex, l.s.c. and proper functions from $\H$ to $]-\infty,+\infty]$.
  • $\ind_C\in\Gz{\H}\iff C$ is a nonempty closed convex set. Proof on the slide: $\epi\ind_C=C\times[0,+\infty[$.
In one sentence

Convexity, like lsc, survives finite sums and arbitrary suprema; put the three words together and you get $\Gz{\H}$, the membership card every later theorem asks for, and $\ind_C$ holds it exactly when $C$ is nonempty, closed and convex.

Why it is true

Chord inequalities can be added (each one is a comparison of numbers in $]-\infty,+\infty]$), and the supremum of functions all lying below their chords lies below the supremum of the chords; geometrically $\epi\sup_if_i=\bigcap_i\epi f_i$ and intersections preserve convexity. For the indicator, the epigraph is a product $C\times[0,+\infty[$, and products preserve (and reflect) closedness and convexity, while nonemptiness is properness. Out loud: sums add inequalities, sups intersect epigraphs, and $\ind_C$ inherits every property from $C$.

Hint — try it first

Sum: add the two chord inequalities. Sup: bound $f_i(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)$ for each $i$ then take the sup over $i$. Indicator: write out $\epi\ind_C$ and use the dictionary proper / lsc / convex $\leftrightarrow$ nonempty / closed / convex epigraph.

Proof

1. Sums. Let $f,g$ be convex, $x,y\in\H$, $\alpha\in\,]0,1[$. Adding $f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\le\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y)$ and the same inequality for $g$ gives $(f+g)(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\le\alpha(f+g)(x)+(1-\alpha)(f+g)(y)$ (all terms are in $]-\infty,+\infty]$, so the addition is legitimate). Induction gives finite sums.

2. Suprema. Let $g=\sup_{i\in I}f_i$. For each $i$, $f_i(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\le\alpha f_i(x)+(1-\alpha)f_i(y)\le\alpha g(x)+(1-\alpha)g(y)$; taking the supremum over $i$ on the left yields the chord inequality for $g$. (Geometric version: $(x,\zeta)\in\epi g\iff(\forall i)\ f_i(x)\le\zeta\iff(x,\zeta)\in\bigcap_i\epi f_i$, and an intersection of convex sets is convex; conclude with convex $\iff$ convex epigraph.)

3. The epigraph of an indicator. $(x,\zeta)\in\epi\ind_C\iff x\in\dom\ind_C=C$ and $\ind_C(x)=0\le\zeta\iff(x,\zeta)\in C\times[0,+\infty[$. So $\epi\ind_C=C\times[0,+\infty[$.

4. Proper. $\dom\ind_C=C$, so $\ind_C$ is proper iff $C\neq\emptyset$.

5. Lsc. By lsc $\iff$ closed epigraph, $\ind_C$ is lsc iff $C\times[0,+\infty[$ is closed in $\H\times\R$. If $C$ is closed, a product of two closed sets is closed (limits are taken componentwise). Conversely, if $C\times[0,+\infty[$ is closed and $x_n\in C$, $x_n\to x$, then $(x_n,0)\to(x,0)$ stays in the closed set, so $x\in C$: $C$ is closed.

6. Convex. By convex $\iff$ convex epigraph, $\ind_C$ is convex iff $C\times[0,+\infty[$ is convex. A product of convex sets is convex (combine componentwise). Conversely, if $x,y\in C$ then $(x,0),(y,0)$ are in the convex set $C\times[0,+\infty[$, hence so is $(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y,0)$, i.e. $\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y\in C$.

7. Conclusion. $\ind_C\in\Gz{\H}$ iff $C$ is nonempty (4), closed (5) and convex (6).

□
$\Gz{\H}$: the membership card of the course

From page 2 on, almost every statement begins with "Let $f\in\Gz{\H}$". Here is what each letter buys, so that you can check membership in three reflexes:

  • proper ($\dom f\neq\emptyset$, values $\gt-\infty$): there is something to minimize, $\inf f\lt+\infty$, and the chord/Fenchel inequalities never meet $\infty-\infty$.
  • lsc ($\epi f$ closed): the direct method works, so minimizers exist under coercivity or compactness; the prox $\argmin f+\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-x}^2$ exists (page 3); $f=f^{**}$ (Moreau–Fenchel, page 2); convex closed epigraphs are weakly closed, which is what makes the algorithms converge in infinite dimension.
  • convex ($\epi f$ convex): local $=$ global, Argmin convex, $\partial f(x)\neq\emptyset$ on the interior of the domain, Fermat's rule is an equivalence, the prox is firmly nonexpansive.

Membership is checked by calculus, not by hand: $\Gz{\H}$ is stable under finite sums provided the domains intersect (a sum of proper functions may be improper: $\ind_{[0,1]}+\ind_{[2,3]}\equiv+\infty$), under suprema that are finite somewhere, under composition with a bounded affine map when the domain stays nonempty, and under multiplication by positive constants. Norms, affine functions, $\ind_C$ for $C$ nonempty closed convex, $\tfrac12\norm{L\cdot-z}^2$, $\log(1+e^{-y\,\cdot})$, $x\log x$: all in $\Gz{}$ of their space.

Pitfall

Always check $\dom f\cap C\neq\emptyset$ before saying "$f+\ind_C\in\Gz{\H}$". That is the hypothesis of the existence theorem over $C$ and it is the one students forget. Also, the infimum of convex functions is not convex in general ($\min(x^2,(x-2)^2)$ has two valleys).

Definition

Strictly convex function

Part II · slide 21SlidesKey#
Definition

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space and $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$. $f$ is strictly convex if $$(\forall x\in\dom f)(\forall y\in\dom f)(\forall\alpha\in\,]0,1[)\qquad x\neq y\ \Rightarrow\ f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\lt\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y).$$

In one sentence

Chords lie strictly above the graph: no flat piece anywhere, so the function cannot tie at two different points of a valley floor.

The quantifiers are over $\dom f$ because outside it both sides are $+\infty$ and a strict inequality would be false; so strict convexity, like convexity, is a property of $f$ on its domain. The role of strictness is uniqueness: if two distinct minimizers existed, the midpoint would do strictly better (theorem below). The three pictures of the slides:

Left and middle: convex but not strictly (a flat piece: the chord touches the graph on a whole segment). Right: strictly convex.

Examples, with a trick

$\norm{\cdot}^2$ is strictly convex because of the identity T12 $$\norm{\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y}^2=\alpha\norm x^2+(1-\alpha)\norm y^2-\alpha(1-\alpha)\norm{x-y}^2,$$ and the last term is $\lt0$ when $x\neq y$ (expand both sides). The same identity drives the Krasnosel'skii–Mann proof on page 4. In contrast a norm $\norm{\cdot}$ is never strictly convex ($\norm{\alpha x+(1-\alpha)2x}=\alpha\norm x+(1-\alpha)\norm{2x}$ for $x\neq0$), nor is an affine function, nor $\ind_C$ unless $C$ is a singleton ($0\lt0$ fails for $x\neq y$ in $C$). $x\mapsto x^4$ is strictly convex although its second derivative vanishes at $0$: strictness of the Hessian is sufficient, not necessary.

To remember

Strictly convex: strict chord inequality for $x\neq y$ in $\dom f$. Buys uniqueness, never existence. $\norm{\cdot}^2$ yes, $\norm{\cdot}$ no.

Theorem

Hessian characterization of (strict) convexity

Part II · slide 22SlidesExam#
Statement

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space and let $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be twice Fréchet differentiable on $\dom f$, which is a nonempty open convex set.

  • $f$ is convex if and only if, for every $x\in\dom f$, $\ (\forall z\in\H)\ \ps{z}{\nabla^2f(x)z}\ge0$.
  • If, for every $x\in\dom f$, $\ (\forall z\in\H\setminus\{0\})\ \ps{z}{\nabla^2f(x)z}\gt0$, then $f$ is strictly convex.
In one sentence

Convex means "curving upwards in every direction": the second derivative along any line is nonnegative; strictly positive curvature everywhere forces strict convexity.

Why it is true

Convexity is a one-dimensional property tested on segments: $f$ is convex iff every restriction $\varphi(t)=f(x+t(y-x))$ is convex, and $\varphi''(t)=\ps{y-x}{\nabla^2f(x+t(y-x))(y-x)}$. So the theorem is the classical "a $C^2$ function of one variable is convex iff $\varphi''\ge0$", applied along every segment of the open convex domain. The strict version follows because $\varphi''\gt0$ makes $\varphi'$ strictly increasing, hence the chord inequality strict. Out loud: restrict to lines, where convexity is a sign of the second derivative.

Note

The slides state this theorem without proof. Here is one, reduced to the one-dimensional case. "Twice Fréchet differentiable" gives the Taylor expansion $f(x+h)=f(x)+\ps{\nabla f(x)}{h}+\tfrac12\ps{h}{\nabla^2f(x)h}+o(\norm h^2)$ and the chain rule for $\varphi$.

Hint — try it first

Set $\varphi(t)=f(x+t(y-x))$ for $x,y\in\dom f$. Compute $\varphi''$. For "$\Leftarrow$" use the mean value theorem on $\varphi'$ between the three points $0$, $1-\alpha$, $1$. For "$\Rightarrow$" use the midpoint inequality $f(x+tz)+f(x-tz)\ge2f(x)$ and a second-order Taylor expansion.

Proof T05 restrict to the segment

1. Reduction to a line. Let $x,y\in\dom f$ with $x\neq y$. Since $\dom f$ is convex and open, there is an open interval $J\supset[0,1]$ such that $x+t(y-x)\in\dom f$ for $t\in J$. Define $\varphi:J\to\R$, $\varphi(t)=f(x+t(y-x))$. By the chain rule, $\varphi'(t)=\ps{\nabla f(x_t)}{y-x}$ and $\varphi''(t)=\ps{y-x}{\nabla^2f(x_t)(y-x)}$, where $x_t=x+t(y-x)$. Note that $\varphi(1-\alpha)=f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)$ and $\alpha\varphi(0)+(1-\alpha)\varphi(1)=\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y)$.

2. Nonnegative Hessian ⇒ convex. Assume $\ps{z}{\nabla^2f(u)z}\ge0$ for all $u\in\dom f$, $z\in\H$. Then $\varphi''\ge0$ on $J$, so $\varphi'$ is nondecreasing. Let $\alpha\in\,]0,1[$ and $c=1-\alpha\in\,]0,1[$. By the mean value theorem there are $\xi_1\in\,]0,c[$ and $\xi_2\in\,]c,1[$ with $$\frac{\varphi(c)-\varphi(0)}{c}=\varphi'(\xi_1)\le\varphi'(\xi_2)=\frac{\varphi(1)-\varphi(c)}{1-c}.$$ With $c=1-\alpha$ and $1-c=\alpha$ this reads $\alpha(\varphi(c)-\varphi(0))\le(1-\alpha)(\varphi(1)-\varphi(c))$, i.e. $\varphi(c)\le\alpha\varphi(0)+(1-\alpha)\varphi(1)$, which is $f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\le\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y)$. Pairs with $x=y$, or with $x$ or $y$ outside $\dom f$, satisfy the chord inequality trivially. So $f$ is convex.

3. Positive definite Hessian ⇒ strictly convex. If $\ps{z}{\nabla^2f(u)z}\gt0$ for $z\neq0$, then $\varphi''\gt0$ (as $y-x\neq0$), $\varphi'$ is strictly increasing, the inequality $\varphi'(\xi_1)\lt\varphi'(\xi_2)$ in step 2 is strict, and so is the chord inequality for $x\neq y$ in $\dom f$.

4. Convex ⇒ nonnegative Hessian. Assume $f$ convex and suppose $\ps{z}{\nabla^2f(x)z}\lt0$ for some $x\in\dom f$, $z\in\H$. For $t\gt0$ small, $x\pm tz\in\dom f$ (open), and convexity at the midpoint gives $f(x+tz)+f(x-tz)\ge2f(x)$. Taylor's formula to second order (the first-order terms cancel): $$f(x+tz)+f(x-tz)-2f(x)=t^2\ps{z}{\nabla^2f(x)z}+o(t^2)\ \ge\ 0 .$$ Dividing by $t^2$ and letting $t\to0^+$ gives $\ps{z}{\nabla^2f(x)z}\ge0$, a contradiction.

Theorem

Minimizers of a convex function: Argmin convex, local = global, strict ⇒ at most one

Part II · slide 23SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space and let $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be a proper convex function such that $\mu=\inf f\gt-\infty$.

  • $\{x\in\H\mid f(x)=\mu\}$ is convex.
  • Every local minimizer of $f$ is a global minimizer.
  • If $f$ is strictly convex, then there exists at most one minimizer.
In one sentence

Below a chord there is no room for a hidden valley: between two minimizers the function stays minimal, from a local minimizer you can see every other point along a chord, and a strict chord cannot touch the floor twice.

Why it is true

(i) On the segment between two minimizers, the chord is flat at height $\mu$ and the graph is below it but cannot go below $\mu$: it is flat too. (ii) From a local minimizer $\hat x$, walk a little towards any $y$: locally $f$ does not decrease, and the chord inequality says the value along the walk is below the chord from $f(\hat x)$ to $f(y)$; the only way both can hold is $f(\hat x)\le f(y)$. This is the T04 chord argument without derivatives. (iii) Two distinct minimizers would make the midpoint strictly better than $\mu$, impossible. Out loud: convexity propagates "locally optimal" along chords to the whole space.

What each hypothesis buys
  • convex: the chord inequality, used in all three items. Without it: $x^4/8-x^2+1$ has a local minimizer at $\pm2$ that is global, but $\cos$ has local minimizers that are not global, and $\Argmin(\abs{\abs x-1})=\{-1,1\}$ is not convex.
  • proper and $\mu\gt-\infty$: $\mu\in\R$, so "$f(x)=\mu$" and the subtraction of $(1-\alpha)f(\hat x)$ in the proof make sense. If $\mu=-\infty$ there are no minimizers and the first item would be about the empty set.
  • strictly convex: uniqueness only. Existence is a separate question ($e^x$ is strictly convex with no minimizer).
Hint — try it first

(i) Apply the chord inequality to two points of the level set $\{f=\mu\}$. (ii) For $y\neq\hat x$, choose $\alpha\in\,]0,1[$ small enough that $\hat x+\alpha(y-\hat x)$ lies in the neighbourhood $O$; compare. (iii) Midpoint of two minimizers.

Proof T04 chord argument

1. The level set at $\mu$ is convex. Let $\Omega=\{x\in\H\mid f(x)=\mu\}$, $(x,y)\in\Omega^2$ and $\alpha\in[0,1]$. We have $$f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\le\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y)=\alpha\mu+(1-\alpha)\mu=\mu,$$ and $f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\ge\mu$ since $\mu=\inf f$. Hence $f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)=\mu$, which shows that $\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y\in\Omega$.

2. Local ⇒ global. Let $\hat x$ be a local minimizer of $f$, with open neighbourhood $O$ such that $f(\hat x)\le f(x)$ for all $x\in O$; recall $\hat x\in\dom f$, so $f(\hat x)\in\R$. Let $y\in\H\setminus\{\hat x\}$. Since $O$ is open and $\hat x+\alpha(y-\hat x)\to\hat x$ as $\alpha\to0$, there exists $\alpha\in\,]0,1[$ such that $\hat x+\alpha(y-\hat x)\in O$. Then, by local minimality and convexity, $$f(\hat x)\ \le\ f\big(\hat x+\alpha(y-\hat x)\big)=f\big((1-\alpha)\hat x+\alpha y\big)\ \le\ (1-\alpha)f(\hat x)+\alpha f(y).$$ Subtracting the real number $(1-\alpha)f(\hat x)$ from both ends gives $\alpha f(\hat x)\le\alpha f(y)$, hence $f(\hat x)\le f(y)$ (this is trivially true if $f(y)=+\infty$). As $y$ is arbitrary, $\hat x$ is a global minimizer.

3. Strictly convex ⇒ at most one minimizer. If $f$ is strictly convex and $\hat x$ is a (local, hence global) minimizer, the second inequality of step 2 is strict for $y\neq\hat x$ with $y\in\dom f$, which yields $f(\hat x)\lt f(y)$: $\hat x$ is a strict global minimizer, so no other point is a minimizer. (Directly: two distinct minimizers $x\neq y$ would give $f(\tfrac12x+\tfrac12y)\lt\tfrac12\mu+\tfrac12\mu=\mu=\inf f$, absurd.)

Theorem

Existence and uniqueness of a minimizer over a closed convex set

Part II · slide 24SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space and $C$ a closed convex subset of $\H$. Let $f\in\Gz{\H}$ such that $\dom f\cap C\neq\emptyset$. If $f$ is coercive or $C$ is bounded, then there exists $\hat x\in C$ such that $$f(\hat x)=\inf_{x\in C}f(x).$$ If, moreover, $f$ is strictly convex, this minimizer $\hat x$ is unique.

In one sentence

Put the constraint into the function: $f+\ind_C$ is again in $\Gz{\H}$ and is coercive in both cases, so the existence theorem applies; strict convexity of $f$ makes $f+\ind_C$ strictly convex, so the minimizer is unique.

Why it is true

Everything has been prepared. $\ind_C\in\Gz{\H}$ because $C$ is nonempty (it meets $\dom f$), closed and convex; sums preserve lsc and convexity, and $\dom(f+\ind_C)=\dom f\cap C\neq\emptyset$ gives properness: so $f+\ind_C\in\Gz{\H}$. If $f$ is coercive, $f+\ind_C\ge f$ is coercive; if $C$ is bounded, $f+\ind_C=+\infty$ outside a ball, which is coercivity for free. The coercive existence theorem then gives a minimizer of $f+\ind_C$, which is exactly a minimizer of $f$ over $C$ (the indicator trick). Uniqueness is the previous theorem applied to $f+\ind_C$, strictly convex as the sum of a strictly convex and a convex function. Out loud: indicator trick, then existence by coercivity, then uniqueness by strict convexity.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $C$ closed convex: $\ind_C$ is lsc and convex. Drop closedness: $f(x)=x$ on $C=\,]0,1]$, no minimizer.
  • $f\in\Gz{\H}$: lsc for the direct method, convexity for the infinite-dimensional case and for uniqueness, proper to start.
  • $\dom f\cap C\neq\emptyset$: $f+\ind_C$ is proper. Forget it and the "minimizer" is any point with value $+\infty$.
  • $f$ coercive or $C$ bounded: coercivity of $f+\ind_C$. Drop both: $f=e^x$, $C=\R$.
  • strictly convex: uniqueness. Drop it: $f=\norm{\cdot}_1$ over the segment $C=\{(t,1-t):t\in[0,1]\}$ has a whole segment of minimizers.
Hint — try it first

Show $f+\ind_C\in\Gz{\H}$, then that it is coercive in both cases, then apply the two previous theorems.

Proof T01 indicator trick T03 coercivity (finite dimension)

1. $g=f+\ind_C$ is in $\Gz{\H}$. $C\neq\emptyset$ since $\dom f\cap C\neq\emptyset$; with $C$ closed and convex, $\ind_C\in\Gz{\H}$ (indicator card). A finite sum of lsc functions is lsc, a finite sum of convex functions is convex, and $\dom g=\dom f\cap C\neq\emptyset$, so $g$ is proper. Hence $g\in\Gz{\H}$.

2. $g$ is coercive. If $f$ is coercive: $g\ge f$ everywhere (as $\ind_C\ge0$), so $g(x)\to+\infty$ as $\norm x\to+\infty$. If $C$ is bounded, $C\subset B(0,R)$ for some $R$, and $g(x)=+\infty$ for $\norm x\gt R$: again $g(x)\to+\infty$.

3. Existence (finite dimension). By the coercive existence theorem, $\Argmin g\neq\emptyset$. Let $\hat x\in\Argmin g$. Then $g(\hat x)\lt+\infty$ (since $g$ is proper), so $\hat x\in C$, and for every $x\in C$, $f(x)=g(x)\ge g(\hat x)=f(\hat x)$: $f(\hat x)=\inf_{x\in C}f(x)$.

4. Uniqueness. If $f$ is strictly convex, so is $g$: for $x\neq y$ in $\dom g\subset\dom f$ and $\alpha\in\,]0,1[$, $g(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)=f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\lt\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y)=\alpha g(x)+(1-\alpha)g(y)$ (the indicator vanishes on $C$, which is convex). The minimizers of $f$ over $C$ are the minimizers of $g$; by the previous theorem there is at most one, and by step 3 at least one: exactly one.

□
Deeper — why the theorem holds in any Hilbert space Extra

The slides state the theorem for an arbitrary Hilbert space, where closed balls are not compact. Convexity rescues the direct method: (a) a bounded sequence has a weakly convergent subsequence (closed balls are weakly sequentially compact); (b) a closed convex set is weakly sequentially closed, so the weak limit stays in $C$ and, applied to $\epi g$, a function in $\Gz{\H}$ is weakly lsc: $g(\hat x)\le\liminf g(x_{k_n})$ when $x_{k_n}\wto\hat x$. Run the proof of Weierstrass with "weak" everywhere: minimizing sequence, confined to a ball by coercivity, weakly convergent subsequence, weak lsc at the limit. Fact (b) is the lemma closed convex ⇒ weakly closed of page 4. This is also why the course can prove existence of the prox and convergence of the algorithms in infinite dimension.

Where you will meet this theorem again

Page 3, with $f$ replaced by $f+\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-x}^2$ (strictly convex, coercive, in $\Gz{\H}$): existence and uniqueness of $\prox_fx$. With $f=\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-x}^2$ and $C$ closed convex: existence and uniqueness of the projection $P_Cx$. Every "well-defined operator" of the course is this theorem in disguise.

Reflex: $f+\ind_C$ is $+\infty$ outside $C$, so its chord inequality only tests pairs inside $C$. Pick $C$ where $f$ happens to be convex.

Hint

Take a function whose second derivative changes sign, and $C$ an interval on which it is nonnegative. Or make $C$ so small that nothing can go wrong.

Solution

1. An example. $f(x)=x^3$ and $C=[0,+\infty[$ (or $C=[0,1]$). $f$ is not convex on $\R$: $f''(x)=6x\lt0$ for $x\lt0$, or directly $f(\tfrac12(-2)+\tfrac12 0)=-1\gt\tfrac12f(-2)+\tfrac12f(0)=-4$. $C$ is a nonempty interval, hence convex.

2. $f+\ind_C$ is convex. Let $g=f+\ind_C$, $x,y\in\R$, $\alpha\in\,]0,1[$. If $x\notin C$ or $y\notin C$, the right-hand side $\alpha g(x)+(1-\alpha)g(y)$ is $+\infty$ and the chord inequality holds. If $x,y\in C$, then $\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y\in C$ (convexity of $C$) and $g$ coincides with $f$ at the three points; on $C=[0,+\infty[$, $f''=6x\ge0$, so the restriction of $f$ to the open convex set $]0,+\infty[$ is convex by the Hessian criterion, and the chord inequality extends to the endpoint $0$ by continuity. Hence $g$ is convex.

3. Other examples. $f(x)=-x^2$ with $C=\{0\}$: $f+\ind_C=\ind_{\{0\}}$ is convex (its epigraph is the vertical half-line $\{0\}\times[0,+\infty[$). $f=\cos$ with $C=[\pi/2,3\pi/2]$, where $\cos''=-\cos\ge0$. $f(x)=\sqrt{\abs x}$ with $C=[0,1]$ does not work: $\sqrt{\cdot}$ is concave on $[0,1]$.

4. Lesson. Convexity of $f+\ind_C$ is convexity of $f$ on $C$. This is why the course keeps constraints inside the function: the object that matters is $f+\ind_C$, and its membership in $\Gz{\H}$ is what the theorems test.

Exercise 2

Lower level sets of convex functions

Part II · slide 26★☆☆#
  1. Let $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be a convex function. Prove that for every $\zeta\in\R$, the lower level set $\lev_{\le\zeta}f=\{x\in\H\mid f(x)\le\zeta\}$ is convex.
  2. Show that the converse is false by providing an example of a nonconvex function the lower level sets of which are all convex.

Reflex: a level set is a set of points, so test two of its points with the chord inequality. For the converse, think of a function whose graph is not convex but whose "cross-sections" are intervals: anything monotone or bowl-shaped-but-concave.

Hint

1. If $f(x)\le\zeta$ and $f(y)\le\zeta$, bound $f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)$. 2. Look at $\sqrt{\abs x}$ in the epigraph explorer, or at any nondecreasing function on $\R$.

Solution

1. Level sets are convex. Let $\zeta\in\R$, $x,y\in\lev_{\le\zeta}f$ and $\alpha\in\,]0,1[$. By convexity, $$f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\le\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y)\le\alpha\zeta+(1-\alpha)\zeta=\zeta,$$ so $\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y\in\lev_{\le\zeta}f$. (If the level set is empty or a singleton it is trivially convex.) The same proof shows that $\lev_{\lt\zeta}f$ is convex, and that $\Argmin f=\lev_{\le\inf f}f$ is convex, which is the first item of the minimizers theorem.

2. The converse fails. Take $f:\R\to\R$, $f(x)=\sqrt{\abs x}$. It is not convex: $f(\tfrac12\cdot0+\tfrac12\cdot1)=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\gt\tfrac12f(0)+\tfrac12f(1)=\tfrac12$. Yet for $\zeta\lt0$, $\lev_{\le\zeta}f=\emptyset$, and for $\zeta\ge0$, $\lev_{\le\zeta}f=\{x:\abs x\le\zeta^2\}=[-\zeta^2,\zeta^2]$: every level set is an interval, hence convex.

3. Other examples. Any nondecreasing $f:\R\to\R$ ($x^3$, $\arctan$, $\log$ extended by $+\infty$ on $]-\infty,0]$): its level sets are half-lines or $\emptyset$. Caution: concavity is not the mechanism. $-\norm{\cdot}$ on $\R^N$ is concave, hence not convex, but its level sets $\{x:\norm x\ge-\zeta\}$ ($\zeta\lt0$) are complements of balls, not convex. What makes the examples work is that every cross-section $\{f\le\zeta\}$ is an interval.

4. Name. A function whose lower level sets are all convex is called quasiconvex. Quasiconvexity still gives "the set of minimizers is convex", but not "local $=$ global" nor any of the subdifferential calculus; the course stays with convexity.

Exercise 3

Convexity of the logistic loss

Part II · slide 27★★☆Lab#

Let $y\in\R$. Show that $f:\R\to\R:x\mapsto\log(1+\exp(-yx))$ is convex. When is it strictly convex?

Reflex: $f$ is $C^\infty$ on the open convex set $\R$, so compute $f''$ and look at its sign; strict positivity gives strict convexity.

Hint

$f'(x)=\dfrac{-y\,e^{-yx}}{1+e^{-yx}}$. Differentiate once more and simplify to a square over a square.

Solution

1. Derivatives. Write $u(x)=e^{-yx}$, $u'=-yu$. Then $f=\log(1+u)$, $f'=\dfrac{u'}{1+u}=\dfrac{-yu}{1+u}=\dfrac{-y}{1+e^{yx}}$ and $$f''(x)=\frac{-y\,u'(1+u)-(-yu)\,u'}{(1+u)^2}=\frac{-y\,u'}{(1+u)^2}=\frac{y^2e^{-yx}}{(1+e^{-yx})^2}\ \ge0 .$$

2. Convexity. $\dom f=\R$ is a nonempty open convex set and $f''\ge0$ everywhere, so $f$ is convex by the Hessian criterion (in dimension one, $\ps{z}{f''(x)z}=f''(x)z^2$).

3. Strict convexity. If $y\neq0$, $f''(x)\gt0$ for every $x$ (a positive constant times a positive exponential over a positive square), so $f$ is strictly convex. If $y=0$, $f\equiv\log2$ is constant: convex, not strictly convex (the chord inequality is an equality). Hence $f$ is strictly convex if and only if $y\neq0$.

4. Alternative without derivatives. $t\mapsto\log(1+e^t)$ is the convex "softplus" (it is $\log(e^0+e^t)$, a log-sum-exp), and $x\mapsto-yx$ is affine; a convex function of an affine map is convex (exercise 5, step 1).

5. Back to the running example. The logistic loss of Part I is $L(x)=\sum_{i=1}^n\log(1+\exp(-y_i\ps{z_i}{x}))$, a sum of compositions of the convex $t\mapsto\log(1+e^{t})$ with the affine maps $x\mapsto-y_i\ps{z_i}{x}$: convex, smooth, with $\nabla^2L(x)=\sum_i\frac{e^{-y_i\ps{z_i}{x}}}{(1+e^{-y_i\ps{z_i}{x}})^2}\,z_iz_i^\top$ (using $y_i^2=1$). It is strictly convex iff the $z_i$ span $\R^N$. $L$ is bounded below by $0$ but not coercive in general; $L+\lambda\norm{\cdot}_1$ with $\lambda\gt0$ is coercive, lsc and proper, so the regularized problem has a minimizer by the coercive existence theorem. See the widget of the Hessian criterion for the picture.

Exercise 4

Perspective function and Kullback–Leibler divergence

Part II · slide 28★★★#

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space and let $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ be a convex function. Let $g$ be the perspective function of $f$ defined as $$(\forall(x,t)\in\H\times\R)\qquad g(x,t)=\begin{cases}t\,f(x/t)&\text{if }t\gt0\\ +\infty&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

  1. How is the epigraph of $g$ related to the epigraph of $f$?
  2. Deduce that $g$ is a convex function.
  3. As a consequence of this result, show that the Kullback–Leibler divergence defined as $$h(x,y)=\begin{cases}\sum_{i=1}^Nx^{(i)}\ln\big(x^{(i)}/y^{(i)}\big)&\text{if }(x,y)\in(]0,+\infty[^N)^2\\ +\infty&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$ for $x=(x^{(i)})_{1\le i\le N}\in\R^N$, $y=(y^{(i)})_{1\le i\le N}\in\R^N$, is convex.

Reflex: $t f(x/t)$ is a rescaling; rescalings act on the epigraph as dilations, and a union of dilations of a convex set is a convex cone. Then recognize $x\ln(x/y)$ as the perspective of $x\ln x$.

Hint

1. $(x,t,\zeta)\in\epi g\iff t\gt0$ and $f(x/t)\le\zeta/t\iff(x/t,\zeta/t)\in\epi f$: so $(x,t,\zeta)=t\,(u,1,\eta)$ with $(u,\eta)\in\epi f$. 2. A cone $\{t e:t\gt0,e\in E\}$ generated by a convex set $E$ is convex. 3. Take $f(u)=u\ln u$ on $]0,+\infty[$.

Solution

1. The epigraph of $g$ is the cone generated by $\epi f\times\{1\}$. Order the variables of $g$ as $(x,t)$ and the epigraph as triples $(x,t,\zeta)\in\H\times\R\times\R$. Then $$(x,t,\zeta)\in\epi g\iff t\gt0\ \text{ and }\ t\,f(x/t)\le\zeta\iff t\gt0\ \text{ and }\ f(x/t)\le\zeta/t\iff t\gt0\ \text{ and }\ (x/t,\zeta/t)\in\epi f .$$ Writing $u=x/t$ and $\eta=\zeta/t$, i.e. $(x,t,\zeta)=t\,(u,1,\eta)$, this says $$\epi g=\{t\,(u,1,\eta)\mid t\gt0,\ (u,\eta)\in\epi f\}=\bigcup_{t\gt0}t\,E,\qquad E=\{(u,1,\eta)\mid(u,\eta)\in\epi f\}.$$ $\epi g$ is the (open) convex cone generated by the copy $E$ of $\epi f$ placed at height $t=1$: every point of $\epi g$ is a positive multiple of a point of $E$, and $E$ is the slice $\{t=1\}$ of $\epi g$.

2. $g$ is convex. $E$ is convex: it is the image of the convex set $\epi f$ ($f$ convex $\Rightarrow\epi f$ convex) by the affine injective map $(u,\eta)\mapsto(u,1,\eta)$, and affine images of convex sets are convex. Now let $p=te$ and $p'=t'e'$ be two points of $\epi g$ ($t,t'\gt0$, $e,e'\in E$) and $\alpha\in\,]0,1[$. Set $s=\alpha t+(1-\alpha)t'\gt0$ and $\beta=\alpha t/s\in\,]0,1[$, so that $1-\beta=(1-\alpha)t'/s$. Then $$\alpha p+(1-\alpha)p'=\alpha te+(1-\alpha)t'e'=s\big(\beta e+(1-\beta)e'\big)\in s\,E\subset\epi g,$$ since $\beta e+(1-\beta)e'\in E$ by convexity of $E$. Hence $\epi g$ is convex, and $g$ is convex by convex epigraph $\Rightarrow$ convex function. (The values of $g$ are in $]-\infty,+\infty]$: for $t\gt0$, $t f(x/t)\gt-\infty$.)

3. KL is a sum of perspectives. Let $\varphi:\R\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$, $\varphi(u)=u\ln u$ if $u\gt0$ and $+\infty$ otherwise. $\varphi$ is convex: $\dom\varphi=\,]0,+\infty[$ is open and convex and $\varphi''(u)=1/u\gt0$ there (Hessian criterion), the chord inequality being trivial when an endpoint is outside the domain. Its perspective is, for $t\gt0$, $$g(u,t)=t\,\varphi(u/t)=\begin{cases}t\cdot\frac ut\ln\frac ut=u\ln(u/t)&\text{if }u\gt0\\ +\infty&\text{if }u\le0,\end{cases}$$ and $g(u,t)=+\infty$ if $t\le0$. So $g(u,t)=u\ln(u/t)$ exactly on $]0,+\infty[^2$ and $+\infty$ elsewhere, and by step 2 $g$ is convex on $\R^2$. Finally $$h(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^Ng\big(x^{(i)},y^{(i)}\big)\qquad\text{for all }(x,y)\in\R^N\times\R^N,$$ including the value $+\infty$ as soon as one pair $(x^{(i)},y^{(i)})$ leaves $]0,+\infty[^2$. Each term is the composition of the convex $g$ with the linear map $(x,y)\mapsto(x^{(i)},y^{(i)})$, hence convex, and a finite sum of convex functions is convex (stability). Therefore $h$ is convex (jointly in $(x,y)$).

4. Remarks. Joint convexity is stronger than convexity in $x$ for fixed $y$; it is what makes KL usable as a data-fidelity term in the Poisson/NMR labs (page 8) with respect to both arguments. The perspective construction also produces $\norm{x}^2/t$ (perspective of $\norm{\cdot}^2$), convex on $\H\times\,]0,+\infty[$, a classic exam question.

Exercise 5

Least squares: $\norm{Ax-z}$ and $\norm{Ax-z}^2$

Part II · slide 29★★☆ExamLab#

Let $A\in\R^{M\times N}$ and $z\in\R^M$. Let $f:\R^N\to\R:x\mapsto\norm{Ax-z}$ and $g:\R^N\to\R:x\mapsto\norm{Ax-z}^2$.

  1. Prove that $f$ and $g$ are convex.
  2. Give a necessary and sufficient condition on $A$ for $g$ to be strictly convex.
  3. Show that $g$ admits a minimizer.
  4. Find the minimizers of $g$.
  5. What are the minimizers of $f$?
  6. Can $f$ be strictly convex?

Reflex: a convex function of an affine map is convex; for a quadratic, strict convexity is the Hessian $2A^\top A\succ0$, i.e. $\Ker A=\{0\}$; existence must be argued separately because $g$ is not coercive when $A$ has a kernel.

Hint

1. Triangle inequality on $\norm{\alpha(Ax-z)+(1-\alpha)(Ay-z)}$. 2. $\nabla^2g=2A^\top A$ and $\Ker A^\top A=\Ker A$. 3. $g(x)=\mathrm{dist}(z,Ax)^2$: project $z$ onto $\ran A$. 4. Normal equations. 5. $\sqrt{\cdot}$ is increasing. 6. Equality case of the triangle inequality; try $N=1$, $M=2$.

Solution (after the TD correction, C. Cosserat)

1. Convexity. The norm is convex: for $u,v\in\R^M$ and $\alpha\in\,]0,1[$, $\norm{\alpha u+(1-\alpha)v}\le\alpha\norm u+(1-\alpha)\norm v$ (triangle inequality and positive homogeneity). Since $x\mapsto Ax-z$ is affine, $A(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)-z=\alpha(Ax-z)+(1-\alpha)(Ay-z)$, hence $$f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)=\norm{\alpha(Ax-z)+(1-\alpha)(Ay-z)}\le\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y):$$ $f$ is convex. For $g=f^2$: $t\mapsto t^2$ is convex and nondecreasing on $[0,+\infty[$, so $g(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)=f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)^2\le(\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y))^2\le\alpha f(x)^2+(1-\alpha)f(y)^2$. (Alternatively: $g$ is $C^2$ with $\nabla^2g=2A^\top A\succeq0$.) In words: a convex function composed with an affine map is convex.

2. Strict convexity of $g$ $\iff$ $A$ injective. $g$ is twice differentiable with $\nabla g(x)=2A^\top(Ax-z)$ and $\nabla^2g(x)=2A^\top A$. The matrix $2A^\top A$ is symmetric positive semidefinite; it is positive definite iff it is invertible iff $\Ker(A^\top A)=\{0\}$, and $\Ker(A^\top A)=\Ker A$ (if $A^\top Ax=0$ then $\norm{Ax}^2=x^\top A^\top Ax=0$). So if $\Ker A=\{0\}$, i.e. $A$ injective (rank $N$), the Hessian criterion gives strict convexity. Conversely, if $A$ is not injective, pick $x_0\in\Ker A\setminus\{0\}$: $g(\tfrac12x_0)=\norm{\tfrac12Ax_0-z}^2=\norm z^2=\tfrac12\big(\norm{A0-z}^2+\norm{Ax_0-z}^2\big)=\tfrac12\big(g(0)+g(x_0)\big)$, an equality in the chord inequality for two distinct points: $g$ is not strictly convex. Hence $g$ strictly convex $\iff\Ker A=\{0\}$. (TD remark: expanding $g(x)=x^\top A^\top Ax-2z^\top Ax+\norm z^2$ and discarding the affine part, strict convexity of $g$ is that of $x\mapsto x^\top A^\top Ax$.)

3. Existence of a minimizer of $g$. The theorems of the course do not apply directly: $\R^N$ is not compact and $g$ is not coercive in general (if $x_0\in\Ker A\setminus\{0\}$, $g(tx_0)=\norm z^2$ stays bounded as $t\to\infty$). Geometry does the job: $g(x)=\norm{Ax-z}^2$ is the squared distance from $z$ to the point $Ax$, and $Ax$ ranges over the subspace $\ran A$ as $x$ ranges over $\R^N$, so $\inf g=\mathrm{dist}(z,\ran A)^2$. The subspace $\ran A$ is finite-dimensional, hence closed and convex, and the distance from a point to a closed convex set of a Hilbert space is attained, at the orthogonal projection $w=P_{\ran A}z$ (this is the existence theorem applied to the coercive strictly convex $\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-z}^2$ over the closed convex set $\ran A$). Writing $w=Ax_0$ for some $x_0\in\R^N$, we get $g(x_0)=\norm{w-z}^2\le\norm{Ax-z}^2=g(x)$ for all $x$: $x_0$ is a minimizer. (Alternative: restrict $g$ to $(\Ker A)^\perp$, where it is coercive, and note that $g$ is constant along $\Ker A$.)

4. The minimizers of $g$. $g$ is convex and differentiable on $\R^N$, so $x$ is a minimizer iff $\nabla g(x)=0$. Necessity is the interior case of the first-order condition with $C=\R^N$. Sufficiency holds because $g$ is a quadratic: for any $\hat x$ and $x$, $$g(x)=\norm{A(x-\hat x)+(A\hat x-z)}^2=\norm{A(x-\hat x)}^2+\ps{x-\hat x}{\nabla g(\hat x)}+g(\hat x),$$ so $\nabla g(\hat x)=0$ implies $g(x)\ge g(\hat x)$ (page 2 will give this for every convex differentiable function: Fermat's rule). Hence $$\Argmin g=\{x\in\R^N\mid A^\top Ax=A^\top z\}\qquad\text{(normal equations).}$$ By step 3 this set is nonempty; by the minimizers theorem it is convex (here an affine subspace, $x_0+\Ker A$). If $A^\top A$ is invertible ($\Ker A=\{0\}$), the minimizer is unique: $\hat x=(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top z$. In general one solution is $A^\dagger z$ with $A^\dagger$ the Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse.

5. The minimizers of $f$. $f=\sqrt g$ and $\sqrt{\cdot}$ is increasing on $[0,+\infty[$, so $f(x)\le f(y)\iff g(x)\le g(y)$: $f$ and $g$ have the same minimizers, $\Argmin f=\{A^\top Ax=A^\top z\}$.

6. Can $f$ be strictly convex? For $x,y\in\R^N$ and $\alpha\in\,]0,1[$, $f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)=\norm{\alpha(Ax-z)+(1-\alpha)(Ay-z)}$ equals $\alpha\norm{Ax-z}+(1-\alpha)\norm{Ay-z}$ exactly in the equality case of the triangle inequality, i.e. when $Ax-z$ and $Ay-z$ are positively collinear (there is $\lambda\ge0$ with $\lambda(Ax-z)=Ay-z$, or the symmetric relation). So $f$ fails to be strictly convex iff there exist $x\neq y$ and $\lambda\ge0$ with $A(\lambda x-y)=(\lambda-1)z$. This can be avoided: take $N=1$, $M=2$, $A=(1,0)^\top$, $z=(0,1)^\top$. Then $f(x)=\sqrt{x^2+1}$, $f'(x)=x/\sqrt{1+x^2}$, $f''(x)=(1+x^2)^{-3/2}\gt0$ for all $x$: $f$ is strictly convex, so yes, it can be. It cannot when $z\in\ran A$, e.g. $z=0$ and $A\neq0$: with $Au\neq0$, $f(tu)=t\norm{Au}$ is affine in $t\ge0$, an equality in the chord inequality. The TD remark: the graph of a norm is a cone, full of segments; composing with an affine map takes a slice of that cone (a hyperbola here), which may contain no segment.

7. Lesson for the labs. Every data term $\tfrac12\norm{Ax-z}^2$ you will meet (denoising, NMR, tomography) is convex, differentiable with $\nabla g=A^\top(Ax-z)$, and $\nu$-Lipschitz gradient with $\nu=\norm{A}^2$; it is strictly convex and coercive iff $A$ is injective, which is why a regularizer is added when it is not.

Self-check

Ten questions

Why does the course allow $+\infty$ as a value but not $-\infty$?

$+\infty$ encodes constraints ($f+\ind_C$), which makes constrained and unconstrained problems one object. $-\infty$ would make differences $f(x)-f(y)$ and sums meaningless ($\infty-\infty$), and would ruin the chord inequality; a minimization problem with value $-\infty$ has no minimizer anyway.

State lower semicontinuity in three ways.

(1) $x_n\to x\Rightarrow f(x)\le\liminf f(x_n)$; (2) $\epi f$ is closed; (3) every lower level set $\lev_{\le\zeta}f$ is closed. The indicator of an open interval fails all three; the indicator of a closed interval satisfies all three.

Give the three steps of Weierstrass' proof and the hypothesis each step uses.

Minimizing sequence (proper: $\inf f\lt+\infty$); convergent subsequence with limit in $S$ (compact); $f(\hat x)\le\liminf f(x_{k_n})=\inf f$ (lsc). Then $f(\hat x)\ge\inf f$ trivially, so equality.

Where does the coercive existence theorem use finite dimension, and what replaces it in a general Hilbert space?

To say that the closed ball $\{\norm{x-x_0}\le\eta\}$ is compact. In infinite dimension the ball is only weakly compact, and one needs convexity: a function in $\Gz{\H}$ is weakly lsc because its epigraph is closed and convex, hence weakly closed. That is the content of the "existence over a closed convex set" theorem.

$f=e^x$ on $\R$: proper? lsc? convex? strictly convex? coercive? minimizer?

Proper, continuous hence lsc, strictly convex ($f''=e^x\gt0$), not coercive ($e^x\to0$ as $x\to-\infty$), and $\inf f=0$ is not attained: strict convexity gives at most one minimizer, not existence.

Write the first-order optimality condition over a convex set and explain the sign geometrically.

$\ps{\nabla f(\hat x)}{y-\hat x}\ge0$ for all $y\in C$: no feasible direction $y-\hat x$ is a descent direction. Equivalently $-\nabla f(\hat x)\in N_C(\hat x)$: the steepest-descent direction points out of $C$. At an interior point $N_C(\hat x)=\{0\}$ and $\nabla f(\hat x)=0$.

Prove in two lines that every local minimizer of a convex function is global.

For $y\neq\hat x$ and small $\alpha\in\,]0,1[$, $\hat x+\alpha(y-\hat x)$ is in the neighbourhood where $\hat x$ is minimal, so $f(\hat x)\le f((1-\alpha)\hat x+\alpha y)\le(1-\alpha)f(\hat x)+\alpha f(y)$; subtract $(1-\alpha)f(\hat x)$ and divide by $\alpha$: $f(\hat x)\le f(y)$.

Why is $\ind_C\in\Gz{\H}$ exactly when $C$ is nonempty, closed and convex?

$\epi\ind_C=C\times[0,+\infty[$. Proper $\iff C\neq\emptyset$; lsc $\iff$ epigraph closed $\iff C$ closed; convex $\iff$ epigraph convex $\iff C$ convex (products preserve and reflect both properties).

Is the Hessian condition $\nabla^2f\succ0$ necessary for strict convexity? Is $\nabla^2f\succeq0$ sufficient for convexity on any domain?

No: $x^4$ is strictly convex with $f''(0)=0$. And the domain must be open and convex: the test is run along segments inside the domain; on a nonconvex domain chords may leave it (exercise 1: $x^3$ is convex on $[0,+\infty[$ but not on $\R$).

In exercise 5, why can't you invoke the coercive existence theorem for $g=\norm{A\cdot-z}^2$, and how do you prove existence instead?

$g$ is coercive only when $\Ker A=\{0\}$ (it is constant along $\Ker A$). Instead, $\inf g=\mathrm{dist}(z,\ran A)^2$ and the distance to the closed subspace $\ran A$ is attained at the orthogonal projection $P_{\ran A}z=Ax_0$; or restrict to $(\Ker A)^\perp$ where $g$ is coercive. The minimizers are the solutions of $A^\top Ax=A^\top z$.
