Duality, ADMM and primal-dual algorithms

One template, $\min_x f(x)+g(Lx)$, and its mirror image $\min_v f^*(-L^*v)+g^*(v)$. Weak and strong duality, Kuhn–Tucker points as certificates, the Lagrangian and its saddle points, ADMM as Douglas–Rachford in disguise, and the primal-dual algorithm as Forward–Backward in a clever metric.

Overview

Where we are

Pages 3–5 gave us algorithms that need the prox of each piece. This page is about the piece that has no cheap prox, $g\circ L$, and about the trick that makes it cheap again: go to the dual.

The template. Every problem of this page has the shape $$\textstyle\underset{x\in\H}{\text{minimize}}\ f(x)+g(Lx),\qquad f\in\Gz{\H},\ g\in\Gz{\G},\ L\in\B(\H,\G).$$ It is not a special case, it is the case:

  • TV denoising: $f=\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-z}^2$, $g=\lambda\norm{\cdot}_1$, $L=$ discrete gradient. The prox of $\lambda\norm{\cdot}_1$ is soft-thresholding; the prox of $\lambda\norm{L\,\cdot}_1$ has no closed form.
  • Constraints $Lx\in C$: $g=\ind_C$. The prox of $\ind_C$ is $P_C$; the prox of $\ind_C\circ L$ is a projection onto $L^{-1}(C)$, usually unavailable.
  • Consensus $x_1=\dots=x_m$ between $m$ agents, each with its own cost $g_i$: $f=\ind_{\Lambda_m}$ (the consensus subspace), $g=\sum_i g_i$, $L=\Id$.
  • Linear programs: $f=\ps{c}{\cdot}+\ind_{[0,+\infty[^N}$, $g=\ind_{[0,+\infty[^K}(\cdot-b)$.

Why dualize. $\prox_{g\circ L}$ is hard, but $\prox_{g^*}$ is free: by the Moreau decomposition, $\prox_{\gamma g^*}x=x-\gamma\prox_{g/\gamma}(x/\gamma)$. And in the dual problem the operator $L$ only appears inside $f^*(-L^*v)$, where it can be handled by a gradient step (when $f^*$ is smooth) or absorbed by a change of variables. The engine of the page is one identity (T17 dualize by conjugation): $$g(Lx)=\sup_{v\in\G}\ \ps{v}{Lx}-g^*(v)\qquad(g\in\Gz{\G},\ \text{Fenchel–Moreau}).$$ Replace $g(Lx)$ by this supremum, and the primal becomes an $\inf\sup$ of a function that is linear in the coupling: that is the Lagrangian, and everything else (dual problem, saddle points, ADMM, primal-dual algorithms) is what you do with it.

The three products of the page. (1) Duality theory: the dual problem, weak and strong duality, and the Kuhn–Tucker characterization of solutions, with two examples (linear programming; consensus ↔ sharing). (2) ADMM: alternating minimization of the augmented Lagrangian, which turns out to be Douglas–Rachford applied to the dual problem, hence converges. (3) The primal-dual algorithm (Condat–Vũ / Chambolle–Pock): explicit steps on $h$, prox steps on $f$ and $g^*$, no inversion of $L$, condition $\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm{L}^2\gt\beta/2$.

The one principle, dual version

Pages 2–5 ran on "Fermat's rule → zeros of a sum of operators → fixed points of a nonexpansive map → Fejér convergence". Here the sum is $\partial f+L^*\partial g L$, whose zeros are hard to reach because of $L$. Duality replaces the single unknown $x$ by a pair $(x,v)$ and the inclusion $0\in\partial f(x)+L^*\partial g(Lx)$ by the two decoupled inclusions $-L^*v\in\partial f(x)$, $Lx\in\partial g^*(v)$: each one involves one function only, so each one is one prox.

Fenchel–Rockafellar duality

A second problem, built from the conjugates and the adjoint, whose value is never above the primal value (weak duality), equals it under a qualification condition (strong duality), and whose solutions come in pairs with the primal ones (Kuhn–Tucker points).

Definition

Primal and dual problems

Part II.5 · slide 3SlidesKey#
Definition

Let $\H$ and $\G$ be two real Hilbert spaces, $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$, $g:\G\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ and $L\in\B(\H,\G)$. The primal problem is $$\underset{x\in\H}{\text{minimize}}\ f(x)+g(Lx),$$ and the dual problem is $$\underset{v\in\G}{\text{minimize}}\ f^*(-L^*v)+g^*(v).$$

In one sentence

Conjugate both functions, replace $L$ by its adjoint, flip one sign: the dual lives in $\G$ (the space of the constraint), not in $\H$.

Where the dual comes from

Write $g(Lx)$ through its conjugate (T17 dualize by conjugation; for $g\in\Gz{\G}$ this is Fenchel–Moreau $g^{**}=g$, on page 2): $$\inf_{x\in\H} f(x)+g(Lx)=\inf_{x\in\H}\sup_{v\in\G}\ f(x)+\ps{v}{Lx}-g^*(v).$$ Now do the formal (illegal in general!) swap of $\inf$ and $\sup$, and use $\ps{v}{Lx}=\ps{L^*v}{x}$: $$\sup_{v\in\G}\Big(\inf_{x\in\H}\ f(x)-\ps{-L^*v}{x}\Big)-g^*(v)=\sup_{v\in\G}\ -f^*(-L^*v)-g^*(v)=-\inf_{v\in\G}\ f^*(-L^*v)+g^*(v),$$ because $\inf_x f(x)-\ps{u}{x}=-\sup_x\ps{u}{x}-f(x)=-f^*(u)$ with $u=-L^*v$. So the dual problem is "the primal with $\inf$ and $\sup$ swapped", its value is $\mu^*$ and one expects $\mu=-\mu^*$. The swap is where everything can go wrong: $\sup\inf\le\inf\sup$ always (weak duality), with equality only under a condition (strong duality).

Reading the sign. The minus sign in $-L^*v$ is not cosmetic: it is what makes the two Fenchel–Young inequalities cancel exactly in the proof of weak duality ($\ps{x}{-L^*v}+\ps{Lx}{v}=0$). With $+L^*v$ you would get a problem whose value has nothing to do with $\mu$.

Examples. TV denoising $f=\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-z}^2$, $g=\lambda\norm{\cdot}_1$: $f^*(u)=\tfrac12\norm{u}^2+\ps{u}{z}$ and $g^*=\ind_{[-\lambda,\lambda]^K}$, so the dual is $\min_v\tfrac12\norm{L^*v}^2-\ps{L^*v}{z}+\ind_{[-\lambda,\lambda]^K}(v)$: a smooth function plus a box constraint, solvable by projected gradient (page 4). Linear programming and consensus are the two examples of the deck, below.

Pitfall

The dual is written as a minimization of $f^*(-L^*v)+g^*(v)$ (value $\mu^*$), so the relation with the primal value is $\mu=-\mu^*$, not $\mu=\mu^*$. Many textbooks write the dual as $\max_v -f^*(-L^*v)-g^*(v)$; same object, opposite sign.

To remember

Dual = conjugate both, adjoint $L$, minus sign: $\min_{v\in\G} f^*(-L^*v)+g^*(v)$. It comes from $g(Lx)=\sup_v\ps{v}{Lx}-g^*(v)$ and the swap $\inf\sup\to\sup\inf$.

Theorem

Weak duality: $\mu\ge-\mu^*$, and the duality gap

Part II.5 · slide 4SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Let $\H$, $\G$ be real Hilbert spaces, $f$ a proper function from $\H$ to $]-\infty,+\infty]$, $g$ a proper function from $\G$ to $]-\infty,+\infty]$, and $L\in\B(\H,\G)$. Let $$\mu=\inf_{x\in\H} f(x)+g(Lx)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mu^*=\inf_{v\in\G} f^*(-L^*v)+g^*(v).$$ Then $\mu\ge-\mu^*$. If $\mu\in\R$, the number $\mu+\mu^*\ (\ge0)$ is called the duality gap.

In one sentence

Any primal candidate $x$ and any dual candidate $v$ satisfy $f(x)+g(Lx)\ge-\big(f^*(-L^*v)+g^*(v)\big)$: every dual value is a lower bound on every primal value.

Why it is true

Fenchel–Young says $\varphi(a)+\varphi^*(b)\ge\ps{a}{b}$ for any pair of points. Apply it to $f$ at the pair $(x,-L^*v)$ and to $g$ at the pair $(Lx,v)$, and add: the two inner products are $\ps{x}{-L^*v}$ and $\ps{Lx}{v}$, which are opposite by definition of the adjoint. So the sum of the four terms is $\ge0$, for every $x$ and every $v$. No convexity, no lower semicontinuity, no qualification: weak duality is free, it only needs the functions to be proper so that the conjugates never take the value $-\infty$ and the sums make sense.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $f$, $g$ proper: then $f^*\gt-\infty$ and $g^*\gt-\infty$ everywhere (a conjugate of a proper function is a supremum of affine functions over a nonempty set), so $f^*(-L^*v)+g^*(v)$ is a well-defined element of $]-\infty,+\infty]$ and $\mu^*\gt-\infty$.
  • $L$ bounded: the adjoint exists, which is all the identity $\ps{x}{-L^*v}+\ps{Lx}{v}=0$ needs.
  • Nothing else. Convexity and closedness only enter for strong duality.
Hint — try it first

Write Fenchel–Young for $f$ at $(x,-L^*v)$ and for $g$ at $(Lx,v)$, add, and watch the inner products cancel.

Proof T10 Fenchel–Young as a leverT06 write it twice and add

1. Fenchel–Young twice. For every $x\in\H$ and $v\in\G$, by the Fenchel–Young inequality, $$f(x)+f^*(-L^*v)\ge\ps{x}{-L^*v}\qquad\text{and}\qquad g(Lx)+g^*(v)\ge\ps{Lx}{v}.$$

2. Add. $f(x)+g(Lx)+f^*(-L^*v)+g^*(v)\ge\ps{x}{-L^*v}+\ps{Lx}{v}=-\ps{Lx}{v}+\ps{Lx}{v}=0$, using $\ps{x}{L^*v}=\ps{Lx}{v}$.

3. Take infima. The left-hand side is $\ge0$ for all $(x,v)$. Taking the infimum over $x$ then over $v$: $\mu+\mu^*\ge0$, i.e. $\mu\ge-\mu^*$ (with the convention that the inequality is trivial when $\mu^*=+\infty$; and if $\mu=-\infty$ it forces $\mu^*=+\infty$).

Theorem

Strong duality under a qualification condition

Part II.5 · slide 5SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $\H$, $\G$ be real Hilbert spaces, $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $g\in\Gz{\G}$ and $L\in\B(\H,\G)$. If $$\inte(\dom g)\cap L(\dom f)\ne\emptyset\qquad\text{or}\qquad\dom g\cap\inte\big(L(\dom f)\big)\ne\emptyset,$$ then $$\mu=\inf_{x\in\H} f(x)+g(Lx)=-\min_{v\in\G} f^*(-L^*v)+g^*(v)=-\mu^*.$$ In particular the dual problem has a solution and the duality gap is $0$.

In one sentence

If the constraint "$Lx$ must land in $\dom g$" can be met with room to spare (an interior point), then swapping $\inf$ and $\sup$ is legitimate: no gap, and the dual infimum is attained.

Why it is true

The qualification condition is exactly the condition that makes the sum rule an equality, $\partial(f+g\circ L)=\partial f+L^*\partial g L$ (Moreau–Rockafellar, slide 9). That is no coincidence: at a primal solution $\hat x$, Fermat gives $0\in\partial(f+g\circ L)(\hat x)$; with the sum rule you can split this zero into a piece $-L^*\hat v\in\partial f(\hat x)$ and a piece $\hat v\in\partial g(L\hat x)$, and the splitting vector $\hat v$ is precisely a dual solution with zero gap (that is the Kuhn–Tucker mechanism of duality theorem (2)). So "strong duality" is "the sum rule applied to Fermat's rule and read through Fenchel–Young's equality case". When no primal solution exists the statement remains true, but the proof then needs a separation argument (Bauschke–Combettes, Thm 15.23); the slides state the theorem without proof.

Why an interior point? Because the sum rule can fail when the domains only touch: the classical counterexample on page 2 is two closed convex sets of $\R^2$ tangent at one point, whose indicators have $\partial(\ind_A+\ind_B)\ne\partial\ind_A+\partial\ind_B$ at the contact point; the corresponding duality has a gap or a non-attained dual.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $f,g\in\Gz{}$: lower semicontinuity and convexity make $f^{**}=f$, $g^{**}=g$ (Fenchel–Moreau), so the dual of the dual is the primal and the conjugates carry all the information.
  • Qualification: it is what permits the sum rule with equality, hence the splitting of a primal optimality condition into a Kuhn–Tucker pair. Drop it: weak duality survives, equality and attainment may fail.
  • "min" in the conclusion: the dual is attained even if the primal is not; the asymmetry comes from the condition being imposed on the primal domains.
Proof (when the primal has a solution; the slides state the theorem without proof) T08 Fermat + sum ruleT10 Fenchel–Young equality

0. Scope. Assume in addition that $\hat x\in\Argmin(f+g\circ L)$ exists (this is the case in every application of this page; the general case is Thm 15.23 in Bauschke–Combettes). Then $\mu=f(\hat x)+g(L\hat x)\in\R$.

1. Fermat + sum rule. $0\in\partial(f+g\circ L)(\hat x)=\partial f(\hat x)+L^*\partial g(L\hat x)$, the equality by the sum rule under the qualification condition. So there is $\hat v\in\partial g(L\hat x)$ with $-L^*\hat v\in\partial f(\hat x)$.

2. Fenchel–Young with equality. The equality case of Fenchel–Young ($u\in\partial\varphi(a)\iff\varphi(a)+\varphi^*(u)=\ps{a}{u}$) gives $f(\hat x)+f^*(-L^*\hat v)=\ps{\hat x}{-L^*\hat v}$ and $g(L\hat x)+g^*(\hat v)=\ps{L\hat x}{\hat v}$. Adding: $f(\hat x)+g(L\hat x)+f^*(-L^*\hat v)+g^*(\hat v)=0$, i.e. $\mu+\big(f^*(-L^*\hat v)+g^*(\hat v)\big)=0$.

3. Conclude. By weak duality, $f^*(-L^*v)+g^*(v)\ge-\mu$ for every $v$, and step 2 shows equality at $\hat v$: so $\hat v$ minimizes the dual, $\mu^*=f^*(-L^*\hat v)+g^*(\hat v)=-\mu$, the minimum is attained.

□
Pitfall

Strong duality does not say the primal is attained: $\mu$ is an $\inf$, $\mu^*$ a $\min$. And the qualification is on $\dom g$ versus $L(\dom f)$, in $\G$; checking $\dom f\cap\dom g\ne\emptyset$ "in $\H$" makes no sense since $g$ lives on $\G$.

To remember

Same condition as the sum rule, same mechanism: $\inte(\dom g)\cap L(\dom f)\ne\emptyset$ ⇒ $\mu=-\min(\text{dual})$. Strong duality = Fermat + sum rule + Fenchel–Young equality.

Proposition

Example 1: linear programming duality

Part II.5 · slide 6SlidesExam#
Statement

Let $L\in\R^{K\times N}$, $b\in\R^K$ and $c\in\R^N$. The primal problem $$\text{Primal-LP}:\qquad\underset{x\in[0,+\infty[^N}{\text{minimize}}\ \ps{c}{x}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad Lx\ge b$$ is associated with the dual problem $$\text{Dual-LP}:\qquad\underset{y\in[0,+\infty[^K}{\text{maximize}}\ \ps{b}{y}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad L^{\top}y\le c.$$ In addition, if the primal problem has a solution, then strong duality holds.

In one sentence

Put the constraints into indicators, conjugate them, and the Fenchel–Rockafellar dual of an LP is the textbook dual LP: "$\min c^\top x$, $Lx\ge b$, $x\ge0$" ↔ "$\max b^\top y$, $L^\top y\le c$, $y\ge0$".

Why it is true

The two conjugates are elementary: the conjugate of a linear function plus the indicator of the positive orthant is the indicator of "slope below $c$" (a sup of $\ps{u-c}{x}$ over $x\ge0$ is $0$ or $+\infty$), and the conjugate of the indicator of the shifted orthant $\{z\ge b\}$ is $\ps{v}{b}$ plus the indicator of $v\le0$. Plug them into $f^*(-L^{\top}v)+g^*(v)$ and the dual constraints appear by themselves; the substitution $y=-v$ turns "$v\le0$" into "$y\ge0$" and the minimization of $\ps{v}{b}$ into the maximization of $\ps{b}{y}$. Both conjugates were computed in the exercise of page 2.

Hint — try it first

Proof T01 indicator trickT07 freeze all coordinates but one

1. Fit the template. With $\H=\R^N$, $\G=\R^K$, set $f(x)=\ps{c}{x}+\ind_{[0,+\infty[^N}(x)$ and $g(z)=\ind_{[0,+\infty[^K}(z-b)$. Then $f(x)+g(Lx)$ equals $\ps{c}{x}$ when $x\ge0$ and $Lx\ge b$, and $+\infty$ otherwise: the primal problem is Primal-LP.

2. Conjugate of $f$. $f^*(u)=\sup_{x\ge0}\ps{u-c}{x}=\sum_{i=1}^N\sup_{x_i\ge0}(u_i-c_i)x_i$. Each one-dimensional sup is $0$ if $u_i-c_i\le0$ and $+\infty$ otherwise. Hence $f^*(u)=\ind_{]-\infty,0]^N}(u-c)$: $0$ if $u\le c$, $+\infty$ otherwise.

3. Conjugate of $g$. $g^*(v)=\sup_{z\ge b}\ps{v}{z}=\ps{v}{b}+\sup_{w\ge0}\ps{v}{w}$ (write $z=b+w$). The last sup is $\ind_{]-\infty,0]^K}(v)$ by the same coordinate argument. Hence $g^*(v)=\ps{v}{b}+\ind_{]-\infty,0]^K}(v)$.

4. Write the dual. $f^*(-L^{\top}v)+g^*(v)=\ind_{]-\infty,0]^N}(-L^{\top}v-c)+\ps{v}{b}+\ind_{]-\infty,0]^K}(v)$, i.e. minimize $\ps{b}{v}$ subject to $-L^{\top}v\le c$ and $v\le0$.

5. Change of sign. Put $y=-v$: minimize $-\ps{b}{y}$ subject to $L^{\top}y\le c$ and $y\ge0$, which is "maximize $\ps{b}{y}$ s.t. $L^{\top}y\le c$, $y\in[0,+\infty[^K$": Dual-LP. The primal value is $\mu$, the dual value is $\mu^*=-\max\ps{b}{y}$, and weak duality $\mu\ge-\mu^*$ reads $\min\ps{c}{x}\ge\max\ps{b}{y}$.

6. Strong duality. The slides state without proof that it holds as soon as the primal has a solution. If moreover the Slater-type condition of strong duality holds (some $x\ge0$ with $Lx\gt b$, i.e. $\inte(\dom g)\cap L(\dom f)\ne\emptyset$), this is the theorem above. Without an interior point the claim is the classical LP duality theorem: for polyhedral functions the interiority condition can be dropped (Bauschke–Combettes, Prop. 27.21 and Rockafellar's polyhedral refinement) Extra.

Example 2: sharing is the dual of consensus

Part II.5 · slides 7–8SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Let $\H$ be a real Hilbert space. For every $i\in\{1,\dots,m\}$, let $g_i:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ and $h_i:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$. The consensus problem is $$\underset{\substack{(x_1,\dots,x_m)\in\H^m\\ x_1=\dots=x_m}}{\text{minimize}}\ \sum_{i=1}^m g_i(x_i).$$ The sharing problem is $$\underset{\substack{(u_1,\dots,u_m)\in\H^m\\ u_1+\dots+u_m=\overline u}}{\text{maximize}}\ \sum_{i=1}^m h_i(u_i),\qquad\overline u\in\H.$$ If, for every $i$, $h_i=-g_i^*(\cdot-\overline u/m)$, then sharing is the dual problem of consensus.

In one sentence

"Everybody agrees on one $x$" has as dual "the prices $v_i$ sum to zero": the conjugate of the indicator of the diagonal $\Lambda_m$ is the indicator of the orthogonal $\{v:\sum_i v_i=0\}$, and shifting $v_i$ by $\overline u/m$ turns "sum $=0$" into "sum $=\overline u$".

Why it is true

Consensus is the template with $f=\ind_{\Lambda_m}$ (T01: the constraint is an indicator), $g(x)=\sum_i g_i(x_i)$ and $L=\Id$ on $\H^m$ (T16 product space). The dual is $\min_v f^*(-v)+g^*(v)$. Now $f^*$ is the support function of the diagonal subspace $\Lambda_m$: a supremum of the linear form $\ps{v}{(x,\dots,x)}=\ps{\sum_iv_i}{x}$ over $x\in\H$, which is $0$ if $\sum_iv_i=0$ and $+\infty$ otherwise. And $g^*$ is separable: $g^*(v)=\sum_ig_i^*(v_i)$. So the dual is "minimize $\sum_ig_i^*(v_i)$ subject to $\sum_iv_i=0$". The sharing problem is the same thing after the translation $u_i=v_i+\overline u/m$ (which moves the sum from $0$ to $\overline u$) and a change of sign (min of $g_i^*$ ↔ max of $h_i=-g_i^*$).

The distributed reading. Agent $i$ owns $g_i$ (its private data) and the variable $x_i$; the only coupling is the consensus constraint. In the dual, agent $i$ owns the "price" $v_i$ and the coupling is the single linear constraint $\sum_iv_i=0$: a resource $\overline u$ (zero, or $\overline u$ after the shift) is shared. At the optimum, by the Kuhn–Tucker conditions of duality theorem (2): $\hat v_i\in\partial g_i(\hat x)$ for every $i$ (from $L\hat x\in\partial g^*(\hat v)$, inverted coordinatewise) and $\sum_i\hat v_i=0$ (from $-\hat v\in\partial\ind_{\Lambda_m}(\hat x)=\Lambda_m^\perp$). Algorithmically (ADMM on consensus, widget below) each agent only computes $\prox_{g_i/\gamma}$ on its own data and exchanges one vector with a coordinator that averages: nothing else is communicated. The exercise below asks you to prove the result; the full solution is there.

Proof (as on slide 8; detailed version in the exercise) T01 indicator trickT16 product spaceT17 conjugation

1. Template. For $x=(x_1,\dots,x_m)\in\H^m$ set $g(x)=\sum_{i=1}^mg_i(x_i)$ and $\Lambda_m=\{(x_1,\dots,x_m)\in\H^m:\ x_1=\dots=x_m\}$. The consensus problem reads $\min_{x\in\H^m}\ \underbrace{\ind_{\Lambda_m}(x)}_{f(x)}+g(x)$, so $L=\Id$ and the dual problem is $\min_{v\in\H^m}f^*(-v)+g^*(v)$.

2. $f^*$. For $v=(v_i)_{1\le i\le m}\in\H^m$, $$f^*(v)=\sup_{x\in\Lambda_m}\ps{v}{x}=\sup_{x_1\in\H}\sum_{i=1}^m\ps{v_i}{x_1}=\sup_{x_1\in\H}\Big\langle\sum_{i=1}^mv_i\,\Big|\,x_1\Big\rangle=\ind_{\{0\}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^mv_i\Big).$$

Theorem

Duality theorem (1): primal zeros exist ⇔ dual zeros exist

Part II.5 · slide 10SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $\H$, $\G$ be real Hilbert spaces, $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $g\in\Gz{\G}$ and $L\in\B(\H,\G)$. Then $$\zer\big(\partial f+L^*\partial gL\big)\ne\emptyset\iff\zer\big((-L)\partial f^*(-L^*\cdot)+\partial g^*\big)\ne\emptyset.$$

In one sentence

The primal inclusion $0\in\partial f(x)+L^*\partial g(Lx)$ and the dual inclusion $0\in-L\partial f^*(-L^*v)+\partial g^*(v)$ are the same system of two coupled inclusions, read once for $x$ and once for $v$; the dictionary between them is $\partial f^*=(\partial f)^{-1}$.

Why it is true

Unpack the primal zero: $0\in\partial f(x)+L^*\partial g(Lx)$ means that some $v\in\partial g(Lx)$ satisfies $-L^*v\in\partial f(x)$. Now invert both inclusions with the Fenchel–Young dictionary ($u\in\partial\varphi(a)\iff a\in\partial\varphi^*(u)$ for $\varphi\in\Gz{}$): $x\in\partial f^*(-L^*v)$ and $Lx\in\partial g^*(v)$. Apply $-L$ to the first and add to the second: $0=-Lx+Lx\in-L\partial f^*(-L^*v)+\partial g^*(v)$. Every step is reversible, so the two existence statements are equivalent. The operator $(-L)\partial f^*(-L^*\cdot)$ is the chain-rule candidate for $\partial(f^*\circ(-L^*))$, hence dual zeros are dual solutions (next theorem).

What each hypothesis buys
  • $f,g\in\Gz{}$: needed for $\partial f^*=(\partial f)^{-1}$ and $\partial g^*=(\partial g)^{-1}$ (the equality case of Fenchel–Young uses $\varphi^{**}=\varphi$).
  • No qualification condition: this theorem compares two operator inclusions, not the subdifferentials of the sums.

Proof T10 Fenchel–Young ($\partial f^*=(\partial f)^{-1}$)T15 zeros dictionary

1. Unpack the sum. $(\exists x\in\H)\ 0\in\partial f(x)+L^*\partial g(Lx)$ $\iff$ $(\exists x\in\H)(\exists v\in\G)$ $-L^*v\in\partial f(x)$ and $v\in\partial g(Lx)$. (A zero of a sum of two sets is a pair of opposite elements, one in each set; here the element of $L^*\partial g(Lx)$ is $L^*v$ with $v\in\partial g(Lx)$.)

2. Invert. By the equality case of Fenchel–Young, for $\varphi\in\Gz{}$: $u\in\partial\varphi(a)\iff a\in\partial\varphi^*(u)$. So the system is equivalent to $(\exists x)(\exists v)$ $x\in\partial f^*(-L^*v)$ and $Lx\in\partial g^*(v)$.

3. Repack. If $x\in\partial f^*(-L^*v)$ and $Lx\in\partial g^*(v)$ then $0=-Lx+Lx\in-L\partial f^*(-L^*v)+\partial g^*(v)$. Conversely, if $0\in-L\partial f^*(-L^*v)+\partial g^*(v)$, there is $x\in\partial f^*(-L^*v)$ with $Lx\in\partial g^*(v)$. Hence the system is equivalent to $(\exists v\in\G)\ 0\in-L\partial f^*(-L^*v)+\partial g^*(v)$.

Theorem

Duality theorem (2): Kuhn–Tucker points give primal and dual solutions

Part II.5 · slides 11–12SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Let $\H$, $\G$ be real Hilbert spaces, $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $g\in\Gz{\G}$ and $L\in\B(\H,\G)$.

  • If there exists $\hat x\in\H$ such that $0\in\partial f(\hat x)+L^*\partial g(L\hat x)$, then $\hat x$ is a solution to the primal problem. Moreover, there exists a solution $\hat v$ to the dual problem such that $-L^*\hat v\in\partial f(\hat x)$ and $L\hat x\in\partial g^*(\hat v)$.
  • If there exists $(\hat x,\hat v)\in\H\times\G$ such that $-L^*\hat v\in\partial f(\hat x)$ and $L\hat x\in\partial g^*(\hat v)$, then $\hat x$ (resp. $\hat v$) is a solution to the primal (resp. dual) problem.

A pair $(\hat x,\hat v)\in\H\times\G$ such that $-L^*\hat v\in\partial f(\hat x)$ and $L\hat x\in\partial g^*(\hat v)$ is called a Kuhn–Tucker point.

In one sentence

A Kuhn–Tucker point is a pair in which each inclusion involves one function only; it certifies both solutions at once, and it is what every algorithm of this page actually computes.

Why it is true

Two inclusions, two Fermat rules. The pair $(-L^*\hat v,\hat v)$ with $\hat v\in\partial g(L\hat x)$ assembles into $0\in\partial f(\hat x)+L^*\partial g(L\hat x)\subset\partial(f+g\circ L)(\hat x)$ (the free inclusion of the sum rule), and Fermat says $\hat x$ is a primal solution. Symmetrically, inverting with $\partial f^*=(\partial f)^{-1}$, the pair $(\hat x,L\hat x)$ assembles into $0\in-L\partial f^*(-L^*\hat v)+\partial g^*(\hat v)\subset\partial\big(f^*\circ(-L^*)+g^*\big)(\hat v)$, and Fermat says $\hat v$ is a dual solution. The only non-trivial direction is "primal zero ⇒ dual solution exists", which is duality theorem (1).

Certificate. Given a candidate pair $(\hat x,\hat v)$ you can check optimality without solving anything: verify the two inclusions (or, equivalently, that the gap $f(\hat x)+g(L\hat x)+f^*(-L^*\hat v)+g^*(\hat v)$ is zero, by the equality case of Fenchel–Young). This is how one monitors an algorithm: the gap of the current pair is a computable upper bound on the suboptimality of $x_n$.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $f,g\in\Gz{}$: the inversion $\partial\varphi^*=(\partial\varphi)^{-1}$ and Fermat's rule for the dual function.
  • No qualification condition: the inclusions $\partial f+L^*\partial gL\subset\partial(f+g\circ L)$ and $-L\partial f^*(-L^*\cdot)\subset\partial(f^*\circ(-L^*))$ are free (the domains meet automatically: $\hat x\in\dom f$, $L\hat x\in\dom g$ because the subdifferentials are nonempty).
  • The converse "primal solution ⇒ KT point exists" is not claimed: it needs the sum rule with equality, hence the qualification condition (used in ADMM).

Link with Lagrange duality

Introduce the constraint $Lx=y$ explicitly, price it with a multiplier $v$, and the primal is an $\inf\sup$, the dual a $\sup\inf$, of one function of three variables. Solutions are its saddle points.

Proposition

Minimax problem: the primal as $\inf\sup$ of the Lagrangian

Part II.5 · slide 13SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $\H$, $\G$ be real Hilbert spaces, $f:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$, $g:\G\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ and $L\in\B(\H,\G)$. The primal problem is equivalent to finding $$\mu=\inf_{(x,y)\in\H\times\G}\ \sup_{v\in\G}\ \Lag(x,y,v),$$ where $\Lag$ is the Lagrange function defined by $$\big(\forall(x,y,v)\in\H\times\G^2\big)\qquad\Lag(x,y,v)=f(x)+g(y)+\ps{v}{Lx-y}.$$

In one sentence

Decouple $f$ from $g$ by giving $g$ its own variable $y$, then enforce $y=Lx$ with a multiplier: the sup over $v$ of $\ps{v}{Lx-y}$ is $0$ if the constraint holds and $+\infty$ otherwise.

Why it is true

$\ind_{\{0\}}$ is the support function of the whole space: $\sup_{v\in\G}\ps{v}{w}$ is $0$ when $w=0$ and $+\infty$ when $w\ne0$ (take $v=tw$, $t\to+\infty$). So the constraint $Lx-y=0$, written as an indicator (T01), is a sup of linear functions (T17, the simplest conjugation: $\ind_{\{0\}}^*=0$, so $\ind_{\{0\}}=\sup_v\ps{v}{\cdot}-0$). Substituting this sup inside the inf gives the minimax form. Nothing is swapped here: the sup is simply inside.

Proof T01 indicator trickT17 $\ind_{\{0\}}=\sup_v\ps{v}{\cdot}$

1. Introduce $y$. $\mu=\inf_{x\in\H}f(x)+g(Lx)=\inf_{(x,y)\in\H\times\G}f(x)+g(y)+\ind_{\{0\}}(Lx-y)$: the only $y$ that does not give $+\infty$ is $y=Lx$.

2. Indicator as a sup. $\ind_{\{0\}}(Lx-y)=\sup_{v\in\G}\ps{v}{Lx-y}$ (value $0$ if $Lx=y$; if $Lx\ne y$, $v=t(Lx-y)$ gives $t\norm{Lx-y}^2\to+\infty$).

3. Put the sup inside. $\mu=\inf_{(x,y)}\ f(x)+g(y)+\sup_{v}\ps{v}{Lx-y}=\inf_{(x,y)}\sup_{v}\ f(x)+g(y)+\ps{v}{Lx-y}$, since $f(x)+g(y)$ does not depend on $v$.

Proposition

Maximin problem: the dual as $\sup\inf$ of the Lagrangian; Lagrange multipliers

Part II.5 · slide 13SlidesKey#
Statement

With the same notation, the dual problem is equivalent to finding $$-\mu^*=\sup_{v\in\G}\ \inf_{(x,y)\in\H\times\G}\ \Lag(x,y,v),\qquad\Lag(x,y,v)=f(x)+g(y)+\ps{v}{Lx-y}.$$ Remark. $v$ is called the Lagrange multiplier associated with the constraint $Lx=y$.

In one sentence

For fixed $v$ the Lagrangian separates into a function of $x$ and a function of $y$; minimizing each is computing a conjugate, and $-\big(f^*(-L^*v)+g^*(v)\big)$ comes out. Weak duality is just "$\sup\inf\le\inf\sup$".

Why it is true

$\inf_{x,y}\Lag(x,y,v)=\inf_x\big(f(x)+\ps{L^*v}{x}\big)+\inf_y\big(g(y)-\ps{v}{y}\big)$ because the two groups of terms share no variable. Each infimum is minus a conjugate: $\inf_x f(x)-\ps{-L^*v}{x}=-f^*(-L^*v)$, $\inf_y g(y)-\ps{v}{y}=-g^*(v)$. So the inner inf is $-\big(f^*(-L^*v)+g^*(v)\big)$, and taking the sup over $v$ gives $-\mu^*$. Compare with the minimax: same function, the order of $\inf$ and $\sup$ reversed. Since $\sup_v\inf_{x,y}\Lag\le\inf_{x,y}\sup_v\Lag$ always holds (for any function of two groups of variables), this is a second proof of weak duality $-\mu^*\le\mu$; strong duality is the statement that the order can be exchanged.

Proof T17 dualize by conjugation

1. Expand the conjugates. $$\mu^*=\inf_{v\in\G}\ f^*(-L^*v)+g^*(v)=\inf_{v\in\G}\ \Big(\sup_{x\in\H}\ps{x}{-L^*v}-f(x)\Big)+\Big(\sup_{y\in\G}\ps{y}{v}-g(y)\Big).$$

2. Merge the two sups. A sum of two suprema over independent variables is the supremum over the pair, and $\ps{x}{-L^*v}+\ps{y}{v}=-\ps{v}{Lx-y}$: $$\mu^*=\inf_{v\in\G}\ \sup_{(x,y)\in\H\times\G}\ -\big(f(x)+g(y)+\ps{v}{Lx-y}\big)=\inf_{v\in\G}\ -\inf_{(x,y)\in\H\times\G}\Lag(x,y,v).$$

3. Flip the sign. $\inf_v(-\phi(v))=-\sup_v\phi(v)$, so $\mu^*=-\sup_{v}\inf_{(x,y)}\Lag(x,y,v)$, i.e. $-\mu^*=\sup_v\inf_{(x,y)}\Lag(x,y,v)$.

□
Insight

Why "multiplier"? If $g=\ind_{\{b\}}$ (constraint $Lx=b$), then $y=b$ is forced and $\Lag(x,b,v)=f(x)+\ps{v}{Lx-b}$ is the classical Lagrangian of $\min f(x)$ s.t. $Lx=b$. The Kuhn–Tucker inclusion $-L^*\hat v\in\partial f(\hat x)$ is the classical "$\nabla f(\hat x)+L^*\hat v=0$": the gradient of the cost is a combination of the constraint normals, with coefficients $\hat v$.

To remember

Dual $=\sup_v\inf_{x,y}\Lag$, primal $=\inf_{x,y}\sup_v\Lag$. $\inf_{x,y}\Lag(\cdot,\cdot,v)=-f^*(-L^*v)-g^*(v)$. Weak duality $=$ "$\sup\inf\le\inf\sup$".

Theorem

Saddle points of the Lagrangian ⇔ Kuhn–Tucker points with $\hat y=L\hat x$

Part II.5 · slides 14–15SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Definition. $(\hat x,\hat y,\hat v)\in\H\times\G^2$ is a saddle point of the Lagrange function $\Lag$ if $$\big(\forall(x,y,v)\in\H\times\G^2\big)\qquad\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,v)\le\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,\hat v)\le\Lag(x,y,\hat v).$$ Theorem. Let $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $g\in\Gz{\G}$, $L\in\B(\H,\G)$ and $(\hat x,\hat y,\hat v)\in\H\times\G^2$. Assume that $\inte(\dom g)\cap L(\dom f)\ne\emptyset$ or $\dom g\cap\inte(L(\dom f))\ne\emptyset$. Then $$(\hat x,\hat y,\hat v)\ \text{is a saddle point of }\Lag\iff(\hat x,\hat v)\ \text{is a Kuhn–Tucker point and}\ \hat y=L\hat x.$$

In one sentence

A saddle point is a point that minimizes in $(x,y)$ and maximizes in $v$; the three first-order conditions are exactly the two KT inclusions plus the constraint.

Why it is true

(⇒) The right inequality says $(\hat x,\hat y)$ minimizes $\Lag(\cdot,\cdot,\hat v)=\big[f(x)+\ps{\hat v}{Lx}\big]+\big[g(y)-\ps{\hat v}{y}\big]$, a sum of a function of $x$ and a function of $y$: Fermat on each gives $-L^*\hat v\in\partial f(\hat x)$ and $\hat v\in\partial g(\hat y)$. The left inequality says $\hat v$ maximizes the affine function $v\mapsto\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,v)=\text{const}+\ps{v}{L\hat x-\hat y}$, which is bounded above only if its slope vanishes: $L\hat x=\hat y$. Then $\hat v\in\partial g(L\hat x)\iff L\hat x\in\partial g^*(\hat v)$. (⇐) The slides' route: a KT point gives a primal solution $\hat x$ and a dual solution $\hat v$; the minimax and maximin formulas identify $\sup_v\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,v)=\mu$ and $\inf_{x,y}\Lag(x,y,\hat v)=-\mu^*$; strong duality $\mu=-\mu^*$ then squeezes $\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,\hat v)$ between the two.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $f,g\in\Gz{}$: Fermat's rule and the $\partial g\leftrightarrow\partial g^*$ inversion.
  • Qualification condition: used in the slides' proof of (⇐) through strong duality. The insight below shows it is not needed for this equivalence; it is needed to know that saddle points exist whenever the primal has a solution.

(⇒) 2. Rewrite. This is $-L^*\hat v\in\partial f(\hat x)$, $\hat v\in\partial g(\hat y)$, $\hat y=L\hat x$; and, by the Fenchel–Young dictionary, $\hat v\in\partial g(L\hat x)\iff L\hat x\in\partial g^*(\hat v)$. So $(\hat x,\hat v)$ is a Kuhn–Tucker point and $\hat y=L\hat x$.

(⇐) 3. Solutions. Conversely, assume $(\hat x,\hat v)$ is a Kuhn–Tucker point and $\hat y=L\hat x$. By duality theorem (2), $\hat x$ solves the primal and $\hat v$ solves the dual problem. By the minimax and maximin formulas, $$\mu=\inf_{(x,y)}\sup_v\Lag(x,y,v)=\sup_v\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,v),\qquad-\mu^*=\sup_v\inf_{(x,y)}\Lag(x,y,v)=\inf_{(x,y)}\Lag(x,y,\hat v).$$ (The first because $\sup_v\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,v)=f(\hat x)+g(L\hat x)=\mu$ as $\hat y=L\hat x$ and $\hat x$ is optimal; the second because $\inf_{x,y}\Lag(x,y,\hat v)=-f^*(-L^*\hat v)-g^*(\hat v)=-\mu^*$ as $\hat v$ is optimal.)

(⇐) 4. Strong duality. Under the qualification condition, $\mu=-\mu^*$, hence $\sup_v\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,v)=\inf_{(x,y)}\Lag(x,y,\hat v)$, which can be rewritten as $$\big(\forall(x,y,v)\in\H\times\G^2\big)\qquad\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,v)\le\Lag(x,y,\hat v).$$

(⇐) 5. Squeeze. Take $v=\hat v$: $\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,\hat v)\le\Lag(x,y,\hat v)$ for all $(x,y)$. Take $(x,y)=(\hat x,\hat y)$: $\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,v)\le\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,\hat v)$ for all $v$. Together: $\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,v)\le\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,\hat v)\le\Lag(x,y,\hat v)$, the saddle-point property.

Insight Extra

Steps 1–2 are reversible: $(\hat x,\hat y)$ minimizes the separable convex function $\Lag(\cdot,\cdot,\hat v)$ iff the two Fermat conditions hold, and $\hat v$ maximizes the affine $\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,\cdot)$ iff $L\hat x=\hat y$. So the equivalence "saddle ⇔ KT + $\hat y=L\hat x$" holds without any qualification condition; the slides' (⇐) route through strong duality is a different, instructive road to the same place, and it shows what the saddle value is: $\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,\hat v)=\mu=-\mu^*$.

Alternating-direction method of multipliers (ADMM)

Look for a saddle point by alternating minimizations in $x$ and $y$ and an ascent step in the multiplier. On the plain Lagrangian this fails; on the augmented Lagrangian it is ADMM, and ADMM is Douglas–Rachford applied to the dual problem, hence it converges.

Remark

Alternating minimization of $\Lag$ fails in general; the augmented Lagrangian

Part II.5 · slide 16SlidesKey#
Idea

Iterations for finding a saddle point $(\hat x,\hat y,\hat v)$: $$(\forall n\in\N)\qquad\begin{cases}x_n\in\Argmin\Lag(\cdot,y_n,v_n)\\ y_{n+1}\in\Argmin\Lag(x_n,\cdot,v_n)\\ v_{n+1}\ \text{such that}\ \Lag(x_n,y_{n+1},v_{n+1})\ge\Lag(x_n,y_{n+1},v_n).\end{cases}$$ But the convergence is not guaranteed in general. Solution: introduce an augmented Lagrange function. Let $\gamma\in\,]0,+\infty[$ and define $$\big(\forall(x,y,z)\in\H\times\G^2\big)\qquad\widetilde{\Lag}(x,y,z)=f(x)+g(y)+\gamma\ps{z}{Lx-y}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\norm{Lx-y}^2.$$ The Lagrange multiplier is $v=\gamma z$.

The plain Lagrangian is linear in the coupling, so its partial minimizations are often $-\infty$ or non-unique; adding $\tfrac\gamma2\norm{Lx-y}^2$ costs nothing at a saddle point (the constraint holds there) and makes every subproblem well posed.

Why plain alternation fails

For fixed $(y_n,v_n)$, $\Lag(\cdot,y_n,v_n)=f(x)+\ps{L^*v_n}{x}+\text{const}$. If $f$ is linear, or an indicator of an unbounded set, or simply has a linear growth direction, this is unbounded below unless $-L^*v_n$ happens to be a subgradient of $f$ somewhere, and then the Argmin is typically a whole face. Take the LP of Example 1: $f=\ps{c}{\cdot}+\ind_{\R^N_+}$, so $\inf_x\Lag(x,y_n,v_n)=-\infty$ as soon as one coordinate of $c+L^*v_n$ is negative, and the $x$-step is meaningless except at the exact dual solution. Even when the minimizations are well defined, the multiplier step only "improves" an affine function, with no control on the size of the move: the three steps do not decrease any merit function. The augmented term fixes both defects at once: the $x$-subproblem becomes $f+\tfrac\gamma2\norm{L\cdot-y_n+z_n}^2$, which is strongly convex as soon as $L^*L$ is invertible, and the multiplier step becomes a gradient step on a strongly concave-in-the-residual function.

Why it changes nothing. On the constraint set $\{Lx=y\}$ the added term vanishes, and $\widetilde{\Lag}(x,y,z)=\Lag(x,y,\gamma z)+\tfrac\gamma2\norm{Lx-y}^2$ differs from the Lagrangian by a term that is $\ge0$ and $=0$ at every point satisfying the constraint. A saddle point of $\Lag$ satisfies the constraint, so the two functions have the same saddle points (next theorem). Completing the square (T09) will turn $\gamma\ps{z}{Lx-y}+\tfrac\gamma2\norm{Lx-y}^2$ into $\tfrac\gamma2\norm{Lx-y+z}^2$ up to a constant, which is why the variable $z=v/\gamma$ (the "scaled multiplier") is more convenient than $v$.

To remember

$\widetilde{\Lag}=\Lag(x,y,\gamma z)+\tfrac\gamma2\norm{Lx-y}^2$: same saddle points, well-posed subproblems. Multiplier $v=\gamma z$.

Saddle points of the augmented Lagrangian

Statement

Let $\H$, $\G$ be real Hilbert spaces, $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $g\in\Gz{\G}$, $L\in\B(\H,\G)$ and $(\hat x,\hat y,\hat z)\in\H\times\G^2$. Assume that $\inte(\dom g)\cap L(\dom f)\ne\emptyset$ or $\dom g\cap\inte(L(\dom f))\ne\emptyset$. Then $$(\hat x,\hat y,\hat z)\ \text{is a saddle point of }\widetilde{\Lag}\iff(\hat x,\gamma\hat z)\ \text{is a Kuhn–Tucker point and}\ \hat y=L\hat x.$$

In one sentence

The penalty term has zero value and zero gradient on the constraint set, so it is invisible in the first-order conditions once $L\hat x=\hat y$; and it is $\ge0$ elsewhere, so it can only help the minimizing side of the saddle inequality.

Why it is true

(⇒) Write the three first-order conditions of $\widetilde{\Lag}$. The $z$-condition is $\gamma(L\hat x-\hat y)=0$: the constraint holds. Substituting it into the $x$- and $y$-conditions erases the terms $\gamma L^*(L\hat x-\hat y)$ and $\gamma(\hat y-L\hat x)$, and what remains is exactly the first-order conditions of $\Lag$ at $(\hat x,\hat y,\gamma\hat z)$, hence (previous theorem) a KT point $(\hat x,\gamma\hat z)$ with $\hat y=L\hat x$. (⇐) A KT point with $\hat y=L\hat x$ is a saddle of $\Lag$ at the multiplier $\gamma\hat z$; the chain of inequalities for $\widetilde{\Lag}$ follows from the one for $\Lag$ because $\widetilde{\Lag}=\Lag(\cdot,\cdot,\gamma\,\cdot)+\tfrac\gamma2\norm{L\cdot-\cdot}^2$ with a penalty that vanishes at $(\hat x,\hat y)$ and is nonnegative at $(x,y)$.

Proof T08 Fermat + sum ruleT09 the penalty is a square

(⇒) 1. First-order conditions. If $(\hat x,\hat y,\hat z)$ is a saddle point of $\widetilde{\Lag}$, then (Fermat in $(x,y)$ on a separable convex function plus differentiable quadratic terms, Fermat in $z$ on a concave... in fact affine function): $$\begin{cases}0\in\partial_x\widetilde{\Lag}(\hat x,\hat y,\hat z)=\partial f(\hat x)+\gamma L^*\hat z+\gamma L^*(L\hat x-\hat y)\\ 0\in\partial_y\widetilde{\Lag}(\hat x,\hat y,\hat z)=\partial g(\hat y)-\gamma\hat z+\gamma(\hat y-L\hat x)\\ 0=\nabla_z\widetilde{\Lag}(\hat x,\hat y,\hat z)=\gamma(L\hat x-\hat y).\end{cases}$$

(⇒) 2. Erase the penalty terms. The third line gives $L\hat x=\hat y$; inserting it in the first two, $$\begin{cases}0\in\partial_x\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,\gamma\hat z)=\partial f(\hat x)+L^*(\gamma\hat z)\\ 0\in\partial_y\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,\gamma\hat z)=\partial g(\hat y)-\gamma\hat z\\ 0=\nabla_v\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,\gamma\hat z)=L\hat x-\hat y,\end{cases}$$ which, as in the proof of the saddle-point theorem (steps 1–2), says that $(\hat x,\gamma\hat z)$ is a Kuhn–Tucker point and $\hat y=L\hat x$.

(⇐) 3. A saddle of $\Lag$. Conversely, if $(\hat x,\gamma\hat z)$ is a Kuhn–Tucker point and $\hat y=L\hat x$, then $(\hat x,\hat y,\gamma\hat z)$ is a saddle point of $\Lag$ by the saddle-point theorem (this is where the qualification condition is used in the slides' route). In addition, for all $(x,y,z)$, $$\widetilde{\Lag}(x,y,z)=\Lag(x,y,\gamma z)+\frac\gamma2\norm{Lx-y}^2.$$

(⇐) 4. Chain. Since $L\hat x=\hat y$, the penalty vanishes at $(\hat x,\hat y)$, and it is $\ge0$ at any $(x,y)$: $$\widetilde{\Lag}(\hat x,\hat y,z)=\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,\gamma z)\le\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,\gamma\hat z)=\widetilde{\Lag}(\hat x,\hat y,\hat z)\le\Lag(x,y,\gamma\hat z)\le\widetilde{\Lag}(x,y,\hat z).$$ This is the saddle-point property of $\widetilde{\Lag}$ at $(\hat x,\hat y,\hat z)$.

Derivation

Algorithm for finding a saddle point of $\widetilde{\Lag}$: $$(\forall n\in\N)\qquad\begin{cases}x_n=\argmin_{x\in\H}\widetilde{\Lag}(x,y_n,z_n)\\ y_{n+1}=\argmin_{y\in\G}\widetilde{\Lag}(x_n,y,z_n)\\ z_{n+1}\ \text{such that}\ \widetilde{\Lag}(x_n,y_{n+1},z_{n+1})\ge\widetilde{\Lag}(x_n,y_{n+1},z_n).\end{cases}$$ By performing a gradient ascent on the Lagrange multiplier (step $1/\gamma$), $$(\forall n\in\N)\qquad\begin{cases}x_n=\argmin_{x\in\H}\ f(x)+\gamma\ps{z_n}{Lx-y_n}+\frac\gamma2\norm{Lx-y_n}^2\\ y_{n+1}=\argmin_{y\in\G}\ g(y)+\gamma\ps{z_n}{Lx_n-y}+\frac\gamma2\norm{Lx_n-y}^2\\ z_{n+1}=z_n+\frac1\gamma\nabla_z\widetilde{\Lag}(x_n,y_{n+1},z_n)\end{cases}\iff\begin{cases}x_n=\argmin_{x\in\H}\ \frac12\norm{Lx-y_n+z_n}^2+\frac1\gamma f(x)\\ y_{n+1}=\prox_{g/\gamma}(z_n+Lx_n)\\ z_{n+1}=z_n+Lx_n-y_{n+1}.\end{cases}$$

ADMM (scaled form)

Let $\gamma\in\,]0,+\infty[$, $(y_0,z_0)\in\G^2$. For $n=0,1,\dots$

  1. $x_n=\argmin_{x\in\H}\ \tfrac12\norm{Lx-y_n+z_n}^2+\tfrac1\gamma f(x)$ (primal step: a prox-like problem with $L$ inside the square)
  2. $s_n=Lx_n$
  3. $y_{n+1}=\prox_{g/\gamma}(z_n+s_n)$ (prox of $g$: the piece whose prox is cheap)
  4. $z_{n+1}=z_n+s_n-y_{n+1}$ (multiplier update $=$ running sum of the residuals $Lx_n-y_{n+1}$)

Minimize in $x$, minimize in $y$, push the multiplier by the residual: ADMM is "alternating directions" on the penalized problem, with each minimization a prox-type computation.

Derivation line by line

$x$-step. In $\widetilde{\Lag}(x,y_n,z_n)$ only $f(x)+\gamma\ps{z_n}{Lx-y_n}+\tfrac\gamma2\norm{Lx-y_n}^2$ depends on $x$. Complete the square (T09) with $w=Lx-y_n$: $$\gamma\ps{z_n}{w}+\frac\gamma2\norm{w}^2=\frac\gamma2\big(\norm{w}^2+2\ps{z_n}{w}+\norm{z_n}^2\big)-\frac\gamma2\norm{z_n}^2=\frac\gamma2\norm{w+z_n}^2-\frac\gamma2\norm{z_n}^2.$$ The constant $-\tfrac\gamma2\norm{z_n}^2$ does not affect the argmin, and dividing the objective by $\gamma\gt0$ does not either: $x_n=\argmin\tfrac12\norm{Lx-y_n+z_n}^2+\tfrac1\gamma f(x)$. Existence and uniqueness: the quadratic is strongly convex when $L^*L$ is invertible (then $\norm{Lx}^2\ge c\norm{x}^2$ with $c\gt0$), so the sum with $f/\gamma\in\Gz{\H}$ has exactly one minimizer (page 1).

$y$-step. Same square with $w=Lx_n-y$: $\gamma\ps{z_n}{Lx_n-y}+\tfrac\gamma2\norm{Lx_n-y}^2=\tfrac\gamma2\norm{Lx_n-y+z_n}^2-\tfrac\gamma2\norm{z_n}^2=\tfrac\gamma2\norm{y-(z_n+Lx_n)}^2+\text{const}$. Hence $y_{n+1}=\argmin_y\ \tfrac1\gamma g(y)+\tfrac12\norm{y-(z_n+Lx_n)}^2=\prox_{g/\gamma}(z_n+Lx_n)$ by definition of the prox.

$z$-step. $\widetilde{\Lag}(x_n,y_{n+1},\cdot)$ is affine in $z$ with gradient $\nabla_z\widetilde{\Lag}=\gamma(Lx_n-y_{n+1})$. A gradient ascent step of size $1/\gamma$ gives $z_{n+1}=z_n+Lx_n-y_{n+1}$, and it does increase $\widetilde{\Lag}$: the increase is $\gamma\ps{Lx_n-y_{n+1}}{z_{n+1}-z_n}=\gamma\norm{Lx_n-y_{n+1}}^2\ge0$. In the unscaled multiplier $v=\gamma z$ this is $v_{n+1}=v_n+\gamma(Lx_n-y_{n+1})$: the classical "multiplier $+$ penalty parameter $\times$ residual".

Cost per iteration. One minimization of $\tfrac12\norm{L\cdot-w}^2+\tfrac1\gamma f$ (for $f=\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-c}^2$ or $f=0$: one linear system with matrix $L^*L+\gamma^{-1}\Id$ or $L^*L$; for $f=\ind_C$ it is a projection in the metric $L^*L$, rarely explicit), one $\prox_{g/\gamma}$, one product by $L$. Nothing is parallel across the three steps; the prox of $g$ is parallel when $g$ is separable.

In one sentence

Douglas–Rachford with relaxation $\lambda_n\equiv1$: the governing sequence $u_n$ converges to a fixed point of $\rprox_{\gamma f}\rprox_{\gamma g}$, and its shadow $\prox_{\gamma g}u_n$ converges to a minimizer.

Why it is true

With $T=\rprox_{\gamma f}\rprox_{\gamma g}$ (a composition of two nonexpansive reflections, T11), $u_{n+1}=\tfrac12(u_n+Tu_n)$: the Krasnosel'skii–Mann iteration with $\lambda_n=\tfrac12$ on a nonexpansive $T$, or equivalently the relaxed DR of page 5 with $\lambda_n\equiv1\in\,]0,2[$ and $\sum\lambda_n(2-\lambda_n)=+\infty$. Proof (on page 5) T14 Fejér + demiclosedness

1. Write $v_n=\prox_{\gamma g}u_n$, $w_n=\prox_{\gamma f}(2v_n-u_n)$; then $2w_n-(2v_n-u_n)=\rprox_{\gamma f}\rprox_{\gamma g}u_n=Tu_n$ and $u_{n+1}=u_n+w_n-v_n=u_n+\tfrac12(Tu_n-u_n)$.

2. Apply the Douglas–Rachford theorem with $\lambda_n\equiv1$: $u_n\wto\hat u\in\Fix T$ and $\prox_{\gamma g}u_n\wto\prox_{\gamma g}\hat u\in\Argmin(f+g)$. (The weak convergence of the shadow sequence is part of that theorem.)

Statement

Let $\H$, $\G$ be Hilbert spaces, $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $g\in\Gz{\G}$, $L\in\B(\H,\G)$ such that $L^*L$ is an isomorphism, and $\gamma\in\,]0,+\infty[$. The dual form of the unrelaxed Douglas–Rachford algorithm $$(\forall n\in\N)\qquad\begin{cases}v_n=\prox_{\gamma g^*}u_n\\ w_n=\prox_{\gamma f^*\circ(-L^*)}(2v_n-u_n)\\ u_{n+1}=u_n+w_n-v_n\end{cases}$$ can be re-expressed as $$(\forall n\in\N)\qquad\begin{cases}x_n=\argmin_{x\in\H}\ \tfrac12\norm{Lx-y_n+z_n}^2+\tfrac1\gamma f(x)\\ s_n=Lx_n\\ y_{n+1}=\prox_{g/\gamma}(z_n+s_n)\\ z_{n+1}=z_n+s_n-y_{n+1}\end{cases}$$ by setting $y_n=\gamma^{-1}(u_n-v_n)$ and $z_n=\gamma^{-1}v_n$.

In one sentence

ADMM's $z_n$ is the DR shadow $v_n/\gamma$, ADMM's $y_n$ is the DR "gap" $(u_n-v_n)/\gamma$, and the primal $x_n$ is the vector that realizes the subgradient inside the hard prox of $f^*\circ(-L^*)$.

Why it is true

Three dictionaries, one per step (T18 change variables to recognize an old algorithm). (i) The prox of $g^*$ is a prox of $g$ in disguise: Moreau's decomposition $\prox_{\gamma g^*}u=u-\gamma\prox_{g/\gamma}(u/\gamma)$ says precisely that $y_n=(u_n-v_n)/\gamma=\prox_{g/\gamma}(u_n/\gamma)$. (ii) The prox of $f^*\circ(-L^*)$ is characterized by a subgradient inclusion; the chain rule $\partial(f^*\circ(-L^*))=-L\,\partial f^*(-L^*\cdot)$ (valid because $\ran L^*=\H$, a consequence of $L^*L$ being onto) says that this subgradient is $-Lx_n$ for some $x_n\in\partial f^*(-L^*w_n)$, i.e. $-L^*w_n\in\partial f(x_n)$; unwinding $w_n$ in terms of $u_n,v_n,x_n$ turns this inclusion into the optimality condition of the ADMM $x$-step. (iii) The DR update $u_{n+1}=u_n+w_n-v_n$ then reads $u_{n+1}/\gamma=z_n+Lx_n$, which feeds the $y$- and $z$-updates.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $L^*L$ isomorphism: gives $\ran L^*=\H$ (since $\ran L^*\supset\ran(L^*L)=\H$), hence the qualification condition $\dom f^*\cap\inte(\ran L^*)=\dom f^*\ne\emptyset$ for the chain rule with equality, and it makes $x_n$ unique ($L$ injective) and the $x$-subproblem strongly convex.

Lemma

The graph of $\partial f$ is closed for strong × weak convergence

Part II.5 · slide 26SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space and $f\in\Gz{\H}$. If $(x_n,v_n)_{n\in\N}$ is a sequence of $\H^2$ such that $v_n\in\partial f(x_n)$, $x_n\to\hat x$ and $v_n\wto\hat v$, then $\hat v\in\partial f(\hat x)$.

In one sentence

Subgradient inequalities survive limits: strong convergence of the points and weak convergence of the slopes is enough to pass to the limit in $f(y)\ge f(x_n)+\ps{v_n}{y-x_n}$.

Why it is true

The inequality $f(y)\ge f(x_n)+\ps{v_n}{y-x_n}$ holds for every $y$. On the right, $f(x_n)$ can only drop in the limit (lsc gives $\liminf f(x_n)\ge f(\hat x)$), and $\ps{v_n}{y-x_n}\to\ps{\hat v}{y-\hat x}$ because a weakly convergent sequence paired with a strongly convergent one has convergent inner products (the weak one is bounded). So the limit inequality is $f(y)\ge f(\hat x)+\ps{\hat v}{y-\hat x}$, which is the definition of $\hat v\in\partial f(\hat x)$.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $f$ lsc: $\liminf f(x_n)\ge f(\hat x)$. Without it the limit inequality could fail.
  • $x_n\to\hat x$ strongly: needed to control $\ps{v_n}{x_n}$: weak × weak does not pass to the limit (e.g. $x_n=v_n=e_n$ an orthonormal sequence: $\ps{e_n}{e_n}=1$ but $e_n\wto0$).
  • $f$ convex: so that $\partial f$ is the set of global affine minorant slopes, i.e. the inequality holds for all $y$.

Proof (the slides recall the lemma without proof; here is the short one)

1. Subgradient inequality. For every $n$ and every $y\in\H$: $f(y)\ge f(x_n)+\ps{v_n}{y-x_n}$ (definition of $v_n\in\partial f(x_n)$; it forces $x_n\in\dom f$).

2. The inner product converges. $(v_n)$ is bounded (weakly convergent sequences are bounded, Banach–Steinhaus), say $\norm{v_n}\le M$. Then $$\abs{\ps{v_n}{y-x_n}-\ps{\hat v}{y-\hat x}}\le\abs{\ps{v_n-\hat v}{y-\hat x}}+\abs{\ps{v_n}{\hat x-x_n}}\le\abs{\ps{v_n-\hat v}{y-\hat x}}+M\norm{x_n-\hat x}\to0.$$

3. Pass to the limit. Taking $\liminf$ in step 1 and using the lower semicontinuity of $f$ ($\liminf f(x_n)\ge f(\hat x)$ since $x_n\to\hat x$): $$f(y)\ge\liminf_n f(x_n)+\lim_n\ps{v_n}{y-x_n}\ge f(\hat x)+\ps{\hat v}{y-\hat x}.$$ Choosing $y\in\dom f$ shows $f(\hat x)\lt+\infty$, so $\hat x\in\dom f$ and the inequality for all $y$ is exactly $\hat v\in\partial f(\hat x)$.

Theorem

Convergence of ADMM

Part II.5 · slides 25–28SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Let $\H$, $\G$ be Hilbert spaces, $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $g\in\Gz{\G}$, $L\in\B(\H,\G)$ such that $L^*L$ is an isomorphism, and $\gamma\in\,]0,+\infty[$. Assume that $\inte(\dom g)\cap L(\dom f)\ne\emptyset$ or $\dom g\cap\inte(L(\dom f))\ne\emptyset$, and that $\Argmin(f+g\circ L)\ne\emptyset$. Let $(y_0,z_0)\in\G^2$ and $$(\forall n\in\N)\qquad\begin{cases}x_n=\argmin_{x\in\H}\ \tfrac12\norm{Lx-y_n+z_n}^2+\tfrac1\gamma f(x)\\ s_n=Lx_n\\ y_{n+1}=\prox_{g/\gamma}(z_n+s_n)\\ z_{n+1}=z_n+s_n-y_{n+1}.\end{cases}$$ Then $x_n\wto\hat x$ where $\hat x\in\Argmin(f+g\circ L)$, and $\gamma z_n\wto\hat v$ where $\hat v\in\Argmin\big(f^*\circ(-L^*)+g^*\big)$.

Slide 25 initializes with $u_0\in\G$, $v_0=\prox_{\gamma g^*}u_0$, $y_0=\gamma^{-1}(u_0-v_0)$, $z_0=\gamma^{-1}v_0$ (a genuine DR start); slide 28 states it for any $(y_0,z_0)\in\G^2$, which is equivalent since after one ADMM step $(y_1,z_1)$ is of that form with $u_1=\gamma(y_1+z_1)$.

In one sentence

ADMM is DR on the dual, so the dual variables converge; $L^*L$ invertible lets you read the primal iterate off $Lx_n$, and the closed-graph lemma passes the Kuhn–Tucker inclusions to the limit.

Why it is true

Three ingredients. (1) The dual DR has fixed points: the qualification condition turns Fermat's rule into a primal zero of $\partial f+L^*\partial gL$, and duality theorem (1) converts it into a dual zero. (2) Dual DR converges: $z_n=v_n/\gamma$, $y_n=(u_n-v_n)/\gamma$ converge, and so does $s_n=z_{n+1}-z_n+y_{n+1}$. (3) Back to the primal: $x_n=(L^*L)^{-1}L^*s_n$ is a continuous linear image of $s_n$, so it converges to some $\hat x$ with $L\hat x=\hat y$; then the two KT inclusions hold at the limit: $L\hat x\in\partial g^*(\hat v)$ comes from $\hat v=\prox_{\gamma g^*}\hat u$, and $-L^*\hat v\in\partial f(\hat x)$ comes from the $x$-step optimality condition $\gamma L^*(y_n-z_n-s_n)\in\partial f(x_n)$ and the lemma. Duality theorem (2) finishes.

What each hypothesis buys
  • Qualification condition: sum rule with equality ⇒ $\Argmin(f+g\circ L)=\zer(\partial f+L^*\partial gL)$, so that a primal solution yields a KT pair and the dual DR has a fixed point. Without it the dual DR might have nothing to converge to.
  • $\Argmin(f+g\circ L)\ne\emptyset$: the starting point of the chain.
  • $L^*L$ isomorphism: the DR ↔ ADMM dictionary (chain rule with equality) and the recovery $x_n=(L^*L)^{-1}L^*s_n$. For $L=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\end{pmatrix}$ (so $L^*L$ has rank one) the $x$-step is not uniquely defined when $f=0$ and the argument breaks.
  • Finite dimension (in the proof): turns the weak convergence of $s_n$ into strong convergence, needed by the lemma. The theorem is stated with $\wto$ in Hilbert spaces; the slides prove the finite-dimensional case "for simplicity".

Definition

The primal-dual problem $f+g\circ L+h$ with $h$ smooth

Part II.5 · slide 29SlidesKey#
Problem

Let $\H$, $\G$ be real Hilbert spaces, $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $h\in\Gz{\H}$, $g\in\Gz{\G}$ and $L\in\B(\H,\G)$. It is assumed that $h$ is differentiable and has a $\beta$-Lipschitzian gradient with $\beta\in\,]0,+\infty[$. We want to $$\underset{x\in\H}{\text{minimize}}\ f(x)+g(Lx)+h(x).$$

Three pieces, three treatments: $f$ by its prox, $g\circ L$ by the prox of $g^*$ (the dual variable absorbs $L$), $h$ by its gradient. The algorithms of pages 4–5 handle two of the three; this one handles all three.

Examples

Selectivity estimation (lab 3): $f=\ind_C$ (simplex-type set, projection available), $g=q_b$ (prox available), $L=A$ binary matrix, $h=0$. Logistic regression with $\ell_1$ and a linear constraint: $h$ = logistic loss, $f=\lambda\norm{\cdot}_1$, $g=\ind_{\{b\}}$, $L=$ constraint matrix. The dual problem of this template is $\min_v(f+h)^*(-L^*v)+g^*(v)$, since the "primal function" is $f+h$.

Remark

ADMM for $f+g\circ L+h$ and its three limitations

Part II.5 · slide 30SlidesKey#
Statement

ADMM algorithm. Let $\gamma\in\,]0,+\infty[$. $$(\forall n\in\N)\qquad\begin{cases}x_n=\argmin_{x\in\H}\ \tfrac12\norm{Lx-y_n+z_n}^2+\tfrac1\gamma\big(f(x)+h(x)\big)\\ y_{n+1}=\prox_{g/\gamma}(z_n+Lx_n)\\ z_{n+1}=z_n+Lx_n-y_{n+1}.\end{cases}$$ Limitations. (i) The computation of $x_n$ at iteration $n$ may be complicated. (ii) Convergence requires $L^*L$ to be invertible. (iii) The smoothness of $h$ is not exploited.

ADMM treats $f+h$ as one block to be minimized jointly with a quadratic in $Lx$: an inner problem that is in general as hard as the original, and that needs $L^*L$ invertible to be well posed.

Why these are real limitations

(i) Even with $h=0$, the $x$-step is $\prox$-like only when $L^*L$ is a multiple of the identity (then it is $\prox_{f/(\gamma\nu)}$ of an explicit point, page 3); otherwise it is a linear system (quadratic $f$) or an inner iterative solver (nonsmooth $f$). With $h\ne0$ smooth but not quadratic (logistic loss), the $x$-step is a full nonlinear problem. (ii) In the DBSM lab, $A\in\{0,1\}^{6\times7}$, so $A^*A$ is a singular $7\times7$ matrix: the theorem does not apply. (iii) A $\beta$-Lipschitz gradient is exactly what makes an explicit step cheap and safe (Forward–Backward); ADMM ignores it.

To remember

ADMM's price: an inner argmin with $L$ in the square, $L^*L$ invertible, and no use of $\nabla h$. The primal-dual algorithm removes all three.

Idea 1: Arrow–Hurwicz, explicit primal-dual subgradient steps

Part II.5 · slide 31Slides#
Idea 1

The optimization problem is reformulated as finding $$\inf_{x\in\H}\ f(x)+h(x)+\underbrace{\sup_{v\in\G}\ \ps{v}{Lx}-g^*(v)}_{g(Lx)}=\inf_{x\in\H}\sup_{v\in\G}\ f(x)+h(x)+\ps{v}{Lx}-g^*(v).$$ Arrow–Hurwicz method. Let $(\tau_n)_{n\in\N}$ and $(\sigma_n)_{n\in\N}$ be sequences in $]0,+\infty[$. $$(\forall n\in\N)\qquad\begin{cases}t_n\in\partial f(x_n)\\ x_{n+1}=x_n-\tau_n\big(t_n+\nabla h(x_n)+L^*v_n\big)\\ s_n\in\partial g^*(v_n)\\ v_{n+1}=v_n-\sigma_n\big(s_n-Lx_{n+1}\big)\end{cases}$$ ⇝ requires stringent conditions on the choice of the step-size (e.g. decaying to zero).

Arrow–Hurwicz (subgradient descent-ascent)

Let $x_0\in\H$, $v_0\in\G$. For $n=0,1,\dots$

  1. pick $t_n\in\partial f(x_n)$; $x_{n+1}=x_n-\tau_n(t_n+\nabla h(x_n)+L^*v_n)$ (subgradient descent in $x$ on the saddle function)
  2. pick $s_n\in\partial g^*(v_n)$; $v_{n+1}=v_n+\sigma_n(Lx_{n+1}-s_n)$ (subgradient ascent in $v$: $\partial_v=Lx-\partial g^*(v)$)

Gradient descent on $x$ and gradient ascent on $v$ of the saddle function; on the bilinear coupling $\ps{v}{Lx}$ this is a rotation, and explicit rotations never settle unless the steps vanish.

Why it needs vanishing steps

The saddle function $K(x,v)=f(x)+h(x)+\ps{v}{Lx}-g^*(v)$ is convex in $x$, concave in $v$, and its coupling is bilinear. Take the pure coupling case $f=h=0$, $g=\ind_{\{b\}}$ (so $g^*=\ps{\cdot}{b}$), $\H=\G=\R$, $L=a$: $K(x,v)=v(ax-b)$, saddle point $(b/a,0)$. The simultaneous explicit scheme $x_{n+1}=x_n-\tau av_n$, $v_{n+1}=v_n+\sigma(ax_n-b)$ has iteration matrix $\begin{pmatrix}1&-\tau a\\ \sigma a&1\end{pmatrix}$ with eigenvalues $1\pm ia\sqrt{\tau\sigma}$ of modulus $\sqrt{1+a^2\tau\sigma}\gt1$: the iterates spiral out for every constant pair of steps, and only $\tau_n\sigma_n\to0$ (with $\sum\tau_n=+\infty$) can tame them. The slides' version uses $x_{n+1}$ in the $v$-step (Gauss–Seidel order); on the same example its matrix $\begin{pmatrix}1&-\tau a\\ \sigma a&1-\sigma\tau a^2\end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $1$, so the eigenvalues have modulus exactly $1$ when they are complex ($\sigma\tau a^2\lt4$): the iterates circle forever at constant distance from the saddle, Fejér but not convergent, like a rotation in the Krasnosel'skii–Mann picture. With nonsmooth $f$ or $g^*$, the subgradients $t_n,s_n$ add the usual subgradient-method chattering, which also forces $\tau_n,\sigma_n\to0$. Extra

Interactive · on a bilinear saddle, explicit steps spiral out or circle, implicit steps circle, extrapolated steps converge

Saddle function $K(x,v)=\tfrac c2x^2+v(ax-b)$ with $a=1$, $b=1$ ($f=0$, $h=\tfrac c2(\cdot)^2$ with $\beta=c$, $g=\ind_{\{b\}}$). Saddle point $\hat x=b/a$, $\hat v=-c\hat x/a$. Three schemes with constant steps $\tau,\sigma$: "simultaneous" (both steps from $(x_n,v_n)$), "sequential" (the slides' Arrow–Hurwicz; here $f=0$ so it coincides with the implicit idea 2) and "extrapolated" (idea 3, $v$-step at $2x_{n+1}-x_n$). The spectral radius $\rho$ of the exact $2\times2$ iteration matrix is computed: $\rho\lt1$ means convergence. With $c=0$ the coupling is purely bilinear: compare the three.

The primal-dual theorem's condition $\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm{L}^2\gt\beta/2$ reads $1/\tau-\sigma a^2\gt c/2$ here; it is sufficient for the extrapolated scheme ($\rho\lt1$ whenever it holds), not necessary. For $c=0$ the extrapolated scheme has $\rho=\sqrt{1-\sigma\tau a^2}$ when $\sigma\tau a^2\lt1$: the extrapolation turns a rotation into a contraction.

Implicit primal-dual steps (idea 2)

Let $x_0\in\H$, $v_0\in\G$. For $n=0,1,\dots$

  1. $x_{n+1}=\prox_{\tau_nf}\big(x_n-\tau_n(\nabla h(x_n)+L^*v_n)\big)$ (forward step on $h$ and on the coupling, backward step on $f$)

Taking the subgradient at the arrival point is exactly what a prox does ("$x-p\in\partial f(p)$"), so the nonsmooth chattering disappears; but the bilinear coupling is still handled explicitly and still rotates.

Derivation of the two prox updates

Move everything to one side: $0\in x_{n+1}-\big(x_n-\tau_n(\nabla h(x_n)+L^*v_n)\big)+\tau_n\partial f(x_{n+1})$, i.e. $p-w\in-\tau_n\partial f(p)$ with $p=x_{n+1}$ and $w=x_n-\tau_n(\nabla h(x_n)+L^*v_n)$; by the prox characterization $w-p\in\partial(\tau_nf)(p)\iff p=\prox_{\tau_nf}w$ (T08). Same for $v$: $0\in v_{n+1}-(v_n+\sigma_nLx_{n+1})+\sigma_n\partial g^*(v_{n+1})$ gives $v_{n+1}=\prox_{\sigma_ng^*}(v_n+\sigma_nLx_{n+1})$. Both proxes are available: $\prox_{\sigma g^*}=\Id-\sigma\prox_{g/\sigma}(\cdot/\sigma)$ by Moreau. Why no convergence with constant steps: in the bilinear example of the previous card ($f=0$, $g=\ind_{\{b\}}$), idea 2 is the same as the sequential Arrow–Hurwicz, whose eigenvalues lie on the unit circle (widget above, mode "sequential"): the iterates neither converge nor blow up.

Idea 3: extrapolate $2x_{n+1}-x_n$ — the primal-dual algorithm and its convergence

Part II.5 · slides 33–34SlidesKeyExamLab#
Statement

Idea 3. Use the approximation $x_{n+1}\simeq2x_{n+1}-x_n$ in the dual step.

Convergence of the PD algorithm. Let $\H$, $\G$ be Hilbert spaces, $L\in\B(\H,\G)$, $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $g\in\Gz{\G}$, and $h\in\Gz{\H}$ with a $\beta$-Lipschitzian gradient. Let $\tau\in\,]0,+\infty[$ and $\sigma\in\,]0,+\infty[$. Assume that $$\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm{L}^2\gt\beta/2\qquad\text{and}\qquad\zer\big(\partial f+\nabla h+L^*\partial gL\big)\ne\emptyset.$$ Let $x_0\in\H$, $v_0\in\G$, and $$(\forall n\in\N)\qquad\begin{cases}x_{n+1}=\prox_{\tau f}\big(x_n-\tau(\nabla h(x_n)+L^*v_n)\big)\\ v_{n+1}=\prox_{\sigma g^*}\big(v_n+\sigma L(2x_{n+1}-x_n)\big).\end{cases}$$ Then $x_n\wto\hat x\in\Argmin(f+h+g\circ L)$ and $v_n\wto\hat v\in\Argmin\big((f+h)^*\circ(-L^*)+g^*\big)$.

Remark. When $g=0$, we recover the Forward–Backward algorithm.

Primal-dual algorithm (Condat–Vũ; Chambolle–Pock when $h=0$)

Choose $\tau,\sigma\gt0$ with $\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm{L}^2\gt\beta/2$. Let $x_0\in\H$, $v_0\in\G$. For $n=0,1,\dots$

  1. $x_{n+1}=\prox_{\tau f}\big(x_n-\tau\nabla h(x_n)-\tau L^*v_n\big)$ (gradient step on $h$, dual correction $L^*v_n$, prox of $f$)
  2. $\bar x_{n+1}=2x_{n+1}-x_n$ (extrapolation)
  3. $v_{n+1}=\prox_{\sigma g^*}\big(v_n+\sigma L\bar x_{n+1}\big)=v_n+\sigma L\bar x_{n+1}-\sigma\prox_{g/\sigma}\big(v_n/\sigma+L\bar x_{n+1}\big)$ (Moreau: prox of $g$, no prox of $g^*$ needed)

Cost per iteration: one $\nabla h$, one $L$, one $L^*$, one $\prox_{\tau f}$, one $\prox_{g/\sigma}$. No linear system, no inner loop. The two prox steps are sequential within an iteration ($v_{n+1}$ needs $x_{n+1}$), but when $g=\sum_ig_i\circ L_i$ the $m$ dual updates are independent and run in parallel (exercise below).

In one sentence

The pair $(x_n,v_n)$ follows a Forward–Backward iteration in $\H\times\G$ for the metric $\begin{pmatrix}\tau^{-1}\Id&-L^*\\-L&\sigma^{-1}\Id\end{pmatrix}$: the extrapolation is what makes the off-diagonal $-L$ blocks appear, and the condition $\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm{L}^2\gt\beta/2$ is the FB step condition in that metric.

Why it is true

Fixed points are the right points. $(\hat x,\hat v)$ is a fixed point of the iteration iff $-\tau(\nabla h(\hat x)+L^*\hat v)\in\tau\partial f(\hat x)$ and $\sigma L\hat x\in\sigma\partial g^*(\hat v)$, i.e. $-L^*\hat v\in\partial f(\hat x)+\nabla h(\hat x)=\partial(f+h)(\hat x)$ and $L\hat x\in\partial g^*(\hat v)$: a Kuhn–Tucker point of the problem with "$f$" $=f+h$. By duality theorem (2) such a point gives $\hat x\in\Argmin(f+h+g\circ L)$ and $\hat v\in\Argmin((f+h)^*\circ(-L^*)+g^*)$; by duality theorem (1) the hypothesis $\zer(\partial f+\nabla h+L^*\partial gL)\ne\emptyset$ guarantees that one exists (T15).

The iteration is Forward–Backward in disguise (T18). Rewrite each prox as an inclusion. The $x$-step: $x_n-\tau(\nabla h(x_n)+L^*v_n)-x_{n+1}\in\tau\partial f(x_{n+1})$, i.e. $$\tau^{-1}(x_n-x_{n+1})-L^*(v_n-v_{n+1})\in\partial f(x_{n+1})+L^*v_{n+1}+\nabla h(x_n).$$ The $v$-step: $v_n+\sigma L(2x_{n+1}-x_n)-v_{n+1}\in\sigma\partial g^*(v_{n+1})$, i.e. $$\sigma^{-1}(v_n-v_{n+1})-L(x_n-x_{n+1})\in\partial g^*(v_{n+1})-Lx_{n+1}.$$ Stack $w=(x,v)\in\H\times\G$ and define the bounded self-adjoint operator $V$, the set-valued operator $A$ and the single-valued operator $B$ by $$V=\begin{pmatrix}\tau^{-1}\Id&-L^*\\-L&\sigma^{-1}\Id\end{pmatrix},\qquad A(x,v)=\big(\partial f(x)+L^*v,\ \partial g^*(v)-Lx\big),\qquad B(x,v)=\big(\nabla h(x),0\big).$$ The two inclusions are exactly $V(w_n-w_{n+1})\in Aw_{n+1}+Bw_n$, i.e. $w_{n+1}=(V+A)^{-1}(V-B)w_n=(\Id+V^{-1}A)^{-1}(\Id-V^{-1}B)w_n$: a Forward–Backward step of size $1$ for the inclusion $0\in V^{-1}Aw+V^{-1}Bw$ (whose zeros are the zeros of $A+B$, the KT points above). Why the extrapolation: without it (idea 2) the $v$-inclusion would read $\sigma^{-1}(v_n-v_{n+1})\in\partial g^*(v_{n+1})-Lx_{n+1}$, the operator on the left would be block-diagonal, and the skew part $(L^*v,-Lx)$ of $A$ would have to be treated forward, which is not co-coercive: that is the rotation. The term $-L(x_n-x_{n+1})$ created by $2x_{n+1}-x_n$ is exactly what completes the symmetric block $V$.

The metric. $V$ is positive definite iff $\tau\sigma\norm{L}^2\lt1$ (Schur complement: $\tau^{-1}\Id-\sigma L^*L\succ0$), which follows from the hypothesis since $\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm{L}^2\gt\beta/2\gt0$. Then $\ps{w}{w'}_V=\ps{Vw}{w'}$ is a Hilbert inner product on $\H\times\G$, equivalent to the usual one (so weak convergence is the same). In this metric, $V^{-1}A$ is maximally monotone (because $A$ is: the subdifferential of $f\oplus g^*$ plus a bounded skew-adjoint linear operator), so $(\Id+V^{-1}A)^{-1}$ is firmly nonexpansive, and $V^{-1}B$ is co-coercive: $\ps{V^{-1}Bw-V^{-1}Bw'}{w-w'}_V=\ps{\nabla h(x)-\nabla h(x')}{x-x'}\ge\beta^{-1}\norm{\nabla h(x)-\nabla h(x')}^2$ (Baillon–Haddad), while $\norm{V^{-1}(u,0)}_V^2=\ps{u}{(\tau^{-1}\Id-\sigma L^*L)^{-1}u}\le(\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm{L}^2)^{-1}\norm{u}^2$ (the $(1,1)$ block of $V^{-1}$ is the inverse Schur complement). Hence $V^{-1}B$ is $\nu$-Lipschitz-co-coercive in the $V$-metric with $\nu=\beta/(\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm{L}^2)$, and the FB condition "$\gamma\nu\lt2$" with step $\gamma=1$ is $\beta\lt2(\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm{L}^2)$: the hypothesis of the theorem, to the letter. The FB theorem (in its monotone-operator form) gives $w_n\wto$ a zero of $A+B$.

4. Forward–Backward. Relaxed form of the primal-dual algorithm

Statement

Let $\H$, $\G$ be Hilbert spaces, $L\in\B(\H,\G)$, $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $g\in\Gz{\G}$, and $h\in\Gz{\H}$ with a $\beta$-Lipschitzian gradient, $\beta\in\,]0,+\infty[$. Let $\tau\in\,]0,+\infty[$ and $\sigma\in\,]0,+\infty[$. Assume that $\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm{L}^2\ge\beta/2$ and $\zer(\partial f+\nabla h+L^*\partial gL)\ne\emptyset$. Let $$\delta=2-\frac\beta2\big(\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm{L}^2\big)^{-1}\in[1,2[,$$ and let $(\lambda_n)_{n\in\N}$ be a sequence in $[0,\delta]$ such that $\sum_{n\in\N}\lambda_n(\delta-\lambda_n)=+\infty$. Let $x_0\in\H$, $v_0\in\G$ and $$(\forall n\in\N)\qquad\begin{cases}p_n=\prox_{\tau f}\big(x_n-\tau(\nabla h(x_n)+L^*v_n)\big)\\ q_n=\prox_{\sigma g^*}\big(v_n+\sigma L(2p_n-x_n)\big)\\ (x_{n+1},v_{n+1})=(x_n,v_n)+\lambda_n\big(p_n-x_n,\ q_n-v_n\big).\end{cases}$$ Then $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ converges weakly to a minimizer of $f+h+g\circ L$.

In one sentence

The unrelaxed step is an averaged operator in the $V$-metric with averagedness $1/\delta$; relaxing with $\lambda_n\in[0,\delta]$ is the usual KM range, exactly as for Forward–Backward ($\delta=2-\gamma\nu/2$).

Why it is true

In the FB rewriting, the operator $T=J_{V^{-1}A}(\Id-V^{-1}B)$ is the composition of a $\tfrac12$-averaged map and of a $\nu/2$-averaged map with $\nu=\beta/(\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm{L}^2)$; the composition formula gives an $\alpha$-averaged operator with $1/\alpha=2-\nu/2=2-\frac\beta2(\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm{L}^2)^{-1}=\delta$ (T22). The relaxed iteration of an averaged operator converges weakly for $\lambda_n\in[0,1/\alpha]=[0,\delta]$ with $\sum\lambda_n(\delta-\lambda_n)=+\infty$. Compare with FB on page 4: $\delta=2-\gamma\nu/2$ with $\gamma=1$. The borderline case $\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm{L}^2=\beta/2$ gives $\delta=1$: $T$ is then merely nonexpansive and the relaxation becomes plain Krasnosel'skii–Mann averaging ($\lambda_n\in[0,1]$, $\sum\lambda_n(1-\lambda_n)=+\infty$), which excludes $\lambda_n\equiv1$: consistent with the strict inequality required by the unrelaxed theorem.

Proof (sketch, same route as the unrelaxed case) T22 count averagedness

1. $(p_n,q_n)=T(x_n,v_n)$ with $T=J_{V^{-1}A}(\Id-V^{-1}B)$ the primal-dual operator in the $V$-metric (previous card, steps 1–4), $\Fix T=\zer(A+B)\ne\emptyset$.

2. $J_{V^{-1}A}$ is $\tfrac12$-averaged (resolvent of a maximally monotone operator); $\Id-V^{-1}B$ is $\tfrac\nu2$-averaged with $\nu=\beta(\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm{L}^2)^{-1}\le2$ (Baillon–Haddad in the $V$-metric). By the composition formula, $T$ is $\alpha$-averaged with $\alpha=\frac{1}{2-\nu/2}=1/\delta$ when $\nu\lt2$, and nonexpansive when $\nu=2$ ($\delta=1$).

3. $w_{n+1}=w_n+\lambda_n(Tw_n-w_n)$ with $\lambda_n\in[0,\delta]=[0,1/\alpha]$ and $\sum\lambda_n(\delta-\lambda_n)=+\infty$: the relaxed averaged iteration theorem (or KM when $\delta=1$) gives $w_n\wto\hat w\in\Fix T$, a Kuhn–Tucker point; its first component is a minimizer of $f+h+g\circ L$ by duality theorem (2).

Exercise

Prove that sharing is the dual of consensus

Part II.5 · slide 7★★☆#

Let $\H$ be a real Hilbert space, $g_i,h_i:\H\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ for $i\in\{1,\dots,m\}$, $\overline u\in\H$. Prove that if $h_i=-g_i^*(\cdot-\overline u/m)$ for every $i$, then the sharing problem $\max\sum_ih_i(u_i)$ s.t. $u_1+\dots+u_m=\overline u$ is the dual of the consensus problem $\min\sum_ig_i(x_i)$ s.t. $x_1=\dots=x_m$. Make the distributed-computing reading explicit: what does each agent compute, what is exchanged?

Reflex: a constraint is an indicator (T01); a product-space problem $\min_{x\in\H^m}$ has $L=\Id$ (T16); the conjugate of the indicator of a subspace is the indicator of its orthogonal.

Hint

$f=\ind_{\Lambda_m}$, $g=\bigoplus g_i$, dual $=\min f^*(-v)+g^*(v)$. Compute $f^*(v)=\sup_{x\in\Lambda_m}\ps{v}{x}$ by parametrizing $\Lambda_m$ with one vector $x_1$. Then translate $v_i$.

Solution

1. The template. Work in the Hilbert space $\H^m$ with $\ps{x}{x'}=\sum_i\ps{x_i}{x'_i}$. Define $g:\H^m\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$, $g(x)=\sum_{i=1}^mg_i(x_i)$, and the closed subspace $\Lambda_m=\{(x_1,\dots,x_m)\in\H^m:\ x_1=\dots=x_m\}$. The consensus problem is $\min_{x\in\H^m}\ind_{\Lambda_m}(x)+g(x)$: it is the primal template with $f=\ind_{\Lambda_m}$, $L=\Id_{\H^m}$ (so $L^*=\Id$ and $\G=\H^m$). Its dual is therefore $$\underset{v\in\H^m}{\text{minimize}}\ f^*(-v)+g^*(v).$$

2. The conjugate of $f$. For $v=(v_i)_{1\le i\le m}$, since $\Lambda_m=\{(x_1,\dots,x_1):x_1\in\H\}$, $$f^*(v)=\sup_{x\in\Lambda_m}\ps{v}{x}=\sup_{x_1\in\H}\sum_{i=1}^m\ps{v_i}{x_1}=\sup_{x_1\in\H}\Big\langle\sum_{i=1}^mv_i\,\Big|\,x_1\Big\rangle=\ind_{\{0\}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^mv_i\Big),$$ because the supremum of a continuous linear form $\ps{s}{\cdot}$ over $\H$ is $0$ if $s=0$ and $+\infty$ otherwise (take $x_1=ts$, $t\to+\infty$). This is the general fact $\ind_E^*=\sigma_E=\ind_{E^\perp}$ for a closed subspace $E$: here $\Lambda_m^\perp=\{v:\sum_iv_i=0\}$ (the adjoint of $x\mapsto(x,\dots,x)$ is the sum, page 1). Note $f^*(-v)=f^*(v)$ since the condition $\sum_iv_i=0$ is symmetric.

3. The conjugate of $g$. $g$ is separable, so $g^*(v)=\sup_x\sum_i\big(\ps{v_i}{x_i}-g_i(x_i)\big)=\sum_i\sup_{x_i}\big(\ps{v_i}{x_i}-g_i(x_i)\big)=\sum_{i=1}^mg_i^*(v_i)$ (page 2).

4. The dual problem. Hence the dual reads $$\underset{\substack{(v_1,\dots,v_m)\in\H^m\\ v_1+\dots+v_m=0}}{\text{minimize}}\ \sum_{i=1}^mg_i^*(v_i).$$

5. Change of variables. Set $u_i=v_i+\overline u/m$. Then $\sum_iu_i=\sum_iv_i+\overline u$, so the constraint $\sum_iv_i=0$ becomes $\sum_iu_i=\overline u$, and $g_i^*(v_i)=g_i^*(u_i-\overline u/m)=-h_i(u_i)$. Minimizing $\sum_ig_i^*(v_i)=-\sum_ih_i(u_i)$ is maximizing $\sum_ih_i(u_i)$: the dual problem is the sharing problem, with value $\mu^*=-\max(\text{sharing})$, so that weak duality reads $\min(\text{consensus})\ge\max(\text{sharing})$, with equality under the qualification condition ($\inte(\dom g)\cap\Lambda_m\ne\emptyset$: a common point in the interior of all the $\dom g_i$).

6. Distributed reading. Primal side. Agent $i$ holds $g_i$ and a local copy $x_i$; the coupling is only "all copies equal". Dual side. Agent $i$ holds a price $v_i$ (or a share $u_i$ of the resource $\overline u$); the coupling is only "prices sum to zero" (shares sum to $\overline u$). At the optimum (Kuhn–Tucker, $L=\Id$): $L\hat x\in\partial g^*(\hat v)$ means $\hat x\in\partial g_i^*(\hat v_i)$, i.e. $\hat v_i\in\partial g_i(\hat x)$ for every $i$: each price is a slope of the local cost at the consensus; and $-\hat v\in\partial\ind_{\Lambda_m}(\hat x)=\Lambda_m^\perp$ means $\sum_i\hat v_i=0$: the slopes balance. Algorithm (ADMM with $f=\ind_{\Lambda_m}$, $L=\Id$, so $L^*L=\Id$ is invertible): the $x$-step is $x_n=P_{\Lambda_m}(y_n-z_n)$, i.e. the coordinator computes the average $\bar x_n=\frac1m\sum_i(y_{i,n}-z_{i,n})$ and broadcasts it; the $y$-step is $y_{i,n+1}=\prox_{g_i/\gamma}(z_{i,n}+\bar x_n)$, computed by agent $i$ alone; the $z$-step $z_{i,n+1}=z_{i,n}+\bar x_n-y_{i,n+1}$ is local too. Exchanged per iteration: each agent sends one vector ($y_{i,n}-z_{i,n}$) and receives one ($\bar x_n$); the functions $g_i$ never leave the agents, and $\gamma z_{i,n}\to\hat v_i$ are the prices (widget in the consensus card).

A primal-dual algorithm for $f+\sum_ig_i\circ L_i+h$

Part II.5 · slide 36★★☆Exam#

Let $\H$ and $(\G_i)_{1\le i\le m}$ be real Hilbert spaces. Let $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $h\in\Gz{\H}$, and, for every $i\in\{1,\dots,m\}$, $g_i\in\Gz{\G_i}$ and $L_i\in\B(\H,\G_i)$. It is assumed that $h$ is differentiable and has a $\beta$-Lipschitzian gradient with $\beta\in\,]0,+\infty[$. Propose a primal-dual algorithm to solve $$\underset{x\in\H}{\text{minimize}}\ f(x)+\sum_{i=1}^mg_i(L_ix)+h(x).$$

Reflex: several $g_i\circ L_i$ ⇒ product space (T16): one $g$ on $\G=\G_1\times\dots\times\G_m$ and one $L=(L_1,\dots,L_m)$; then apply the PD theorem and translate $\prox_{\sigma g^*}$, $L^*$ and $\norm{L}$.

Hint

$g(y_1,\dots,y_m)=\sum_ig_i(y_i)$, $Lx=(L_1x,\dots,L_mx)$. Compute $L^*$, $\norm{L}^2\le\sum_i\norm{L_i}^2$, $g^*$ and its prox (separable).

Solution

1. Lifting. Let $\G=\G_1\times\dots\times\G_m$ with $\ps{y}{y'}=\sum_i\ps{y_i}{y'_i}$, a Hilbert space. Define $g:\G\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$, $g(y)=\sum_{i=1}^mg_i(y_i)$, and $L:\H\to\G$, $Lx=(L_1x,\dots,L_mx)$. Then $g\in\Gz{\G}$ (a separable sum of $\Gz{}$ functions: proper, lsc, convex), $L\in\B(\H,\G)$, and $g(Lx)=\sum_ig_i(L_ix)$. The problem is $\min_x f(x)+g(Lx)+h(x)$: the primal-dual template.

2. Adjoint and norm. $\ps{Lx}{y}=\sum_i\ps{L_ix}{y_i}=\sum_i\ps{x}{L_i^*y_i}=\ps{x}{\sum_iL_i^*y_i}$, so $L^*y=\sum_{i=1}^mL_i^*y_i$. And $\norm{Lx}^2=\sum_i\norm{L_ix}^2\le\big(\sum_i\norm{L_i}^2\big)\norm{x}^2$, hence $\norm{L}^2\le\sum_{i=1}^m\norm{L_i}^2$ (an upper bound; the exact value is $\norm{\sum_iL_i^*L_i}$).

3. Conjugate and prox of $g^*$. $g^*(v)=\sum_ig_i^*(v_i)$ (separable conjugate) and the prox of a separable sum is the product of the proxes (page 3): $\prox_{\sigma g^*}(v)=\big(\prox_{\sigma g_i^*}(v_i)\big)_{1\le i\le m}$, each computed from $\prox_{g_i/\sigma}$ by Moreau: $\prox_{\sigma g_i^*}(v_i)=v_i-\sigma\prox_{g_i/\sigma}(v_i/\sigma)$.

4. The algorithm. Apply the PD theorem with $(f,g,L,h)$. With $v_n=(v_{1,n},\dots,v_{m,n})$:

Primal-dual algorithm for $f+\sum_ig_i\circ L_i+h$

Let $\tau,\sigma\gt0$ with $\tau^{-1}-\sigma\sum_{i=1}^m\norm{L_i}^2\gt\beta/2$; $x_0\in\H$, $v_{i,0}\in\G_i$. For $n=0,1,\dots$

  1. $x_{n+1}=\prox_{\tau f}\Big(x_n-\tau\Big(\nabla h(x_n)+\sum_{i=1}^mL_i^*v_{i,n}\Big)\Big)$ (coordinator: one gradient, one sum of the agents' contributions)
  2. $\bar x_{n+1}=2x_{n+1}-x_n$ broadcast to the $m$ agents
  3. for $i=1,\dots,m$ in parallel: $v_{i,n+1}=\prox_{\sigma g_i^*}\big(v_{i,n}+\sigma L_i\bar x_{n+1}\big)$, then send $L_i^*v_{i,n+1}$ back

5. Convergence. The condition $\tau^{-1}-\sigma\sum_i\norm{L_i}^2\gt\beta/2$ implies $\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm{L}^2\gt\beta/2$ by step 2. If moreover $\zer(\partial f+\nabla h+\sum_iL_i^*\partial g_iL_i)\ne\emptyset$ (the lifted condition $\zer(\partial f+\nabla h+L^*\partial gL)\ne\emptyset$, since $\partial g(y)=\prod_i\partial g_i(y_i)$ and $L^*\partial g(Lx)=\sum_iL_i^*\partial g_i(L_ix)$), then $x_n\wto\hat x\in\Argmin\big(f+h+\sum_ig_i\circ L_i\big)$ and $v_n\wto\hat v$ with $\hat v_i\in\partial g_i(L_i\hat x)$ for all $i$ and $-\sum_iL_i^*\hat v_i\in\partial f(\hat x)+\nabla h(\hat x)$ (the Kuhn–Tucker conditions of the lifted problem).

6. What is distributed. Agent $i$ stores $(g_i,L_i)$ and $v_{i,n}$, receives $\bar x_{n+1}$, computes one $L_i$, one $\prox_{g_i/\sigma}$ and one $L_i^*$, and sends back $L_i^*v_{i,n+1}$ (a vector of $\H$). The coordinator sums $m$ vectors, takes one gradient of $h$ and one $\prox_{\tau f}$. Per iteration: two communication rounds (broadcast, gather), no inner loop, no inversion. Compare with PPXA+, which handles the same sum but needs the inversion of $\Id+\sum_iL_i^*L_i$ (or a trick to avoid it) and no smooth $h$. A refinement (not asked): one may use a different $\sigma_i$ per block, with the condition $\tau^{-1}-\sum_i\sigma_i\norm{L_i}^2\gt\beta/2$ Extra.
