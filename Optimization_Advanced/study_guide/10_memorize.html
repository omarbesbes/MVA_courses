How to use this page

Memorizing works when you retrieve, not when you reread. Everything below asks you to produce an answer before you see it, and every answer points back to the card where it is proved.

The page follows the order in which memory is built. First the skeleton: the thirty statements that carry the course, in compressed form, plus the twelve formulas you must be able to write without thinking. Then the flashcards: one question per card, the answer on the back with a one-line reason and a chip to the card (hover the chip to see the full statement, click it to pin it). Then the two sections that sharpen the boundaries of each notion: minimal pairs (notions that look alike, with a test that tells them apart) and counterexamples (the examples that kill a false claim, each checked in one line). Finally three ways to test yourself: a graded quiz, six exam-style problems and an oral drill in which you explain one mechanism in 60 seconds.

A spaced-repetition plan (four weeks before the exam)

  • Day 1. Skeleton lines 1–13 (pages 1–3) read aloud; flashcards of pages 1–3; formulas 1–3.
  • Day 2. Skeleton lines 14–24 (pages 4–5); flashcards of pages 4–5; formulas 4–8. Redo yesterday's cards that you got wrong.
  • Day 4. Skeleton lines 25–30 (pages 6–7) and the labs; flashcards of pages 6–8; formulas 9–12; the minimal pairs.

  • Day 21 and day 28. All six problems; the full oral drill; the shuffled deck again. Untick "known" on any card you hesitated on, so that the filter brings it back.

Learn · the by-heart list

The course in 30 lines

One line per central result, in the compressed form you should be able to say without looking. The chip opens the card (hover for the full statement). If you can produce these thirty lines on a blank sheet, you know the skeleton of the exam.

The one line behind the thirty

Existence (lines 1–5) → Fermat's rule $0\in\partial F(\hat x)$ (6–10) → the resolvent $\prox_{\gamma f}=(\Id+\gamma\partial f)^{-1}$ turns the inclusion into a fixed-point equation (11–13) → Fejér monotonicity and averaging make the fixed-point iteration converge (14–21) → splitting and duality build the right averaged operator for each structure (22–28) → and when there is no prox, majorize instead (29–30).

Page 1 · Convexity and existence

  1. Weierstrass. $S$ nonempty compact, $f:S\to\,]-\infty,+\infty]$ proper lsc $\Rightarrow$ $\exists\,\hat x\in S$ with $f(\hat x)=\inf_S f$. Minimize, extract, lsc. Weierstrass
  2. Existence in finite dimension. $\H$ finite-dimensional, $f$ proper, lsc, coercive $\Rightarrow$ $\Argmin f$ is nonempty and compact. coercive existence
  3. Convex minimizers. $f$ proper convex with $\inf f\gt-\infty$: $\Argmin f$ is convex, every local minimizer is global, and strictly convex $\Rightarrow$ at most one minimizer. convex minimizers
  4. Existence over a closed convex set. $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $C$ closed convex, $\dom f\cap C\ne\emptyset$, $f$ coercive or $C$ bounded $\Rightarrow$ a minimizer over $C$ exists; unique if $f$ is strictly convex. existence over C
  5. Variational inequality. $\hat x$ local minimizer over a convex $C$ of $f$ Gâteaux differentiable at $\hat x$ $\Rightarrow$ $\ps{\nabla f(\hat x)}{y-\hat x}\ge0$ for all $y\in C$ (also sufficient when $f$ is convex). variational inequality

Page 2 · Subdifferential and conjugate

  1. Fermat. $f$ proper: $0\in\partial f(x)\iff x\in\Argmin f$, where $u\in\partial f(x)\iff f\ge f(x)+\ps{\cdot-x}{u}$ on all of $\H$. Fermat's rule
  2. Two subdifferentials to know. Convex and Gâteaux differentiable at $x$ $\Rightarrow$ $\partial f(x)=\{\nabla f(x)\}$; $\partial\ind_C=N_C$ with $N_C(x)=\{u:\ps{u}{y-x}\le0\ \forall y\in C\}$ for $x\in C$. smooth casenormal cone
  3. Sum rule. $\partial f+L^*\partial g\,L\subset\partial(f+g\circ L)$ whenever $\dom g\cap L(\dom f)\ne\emptyset$; equality for $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $g\in\Gz{\G}$ if $\inte(\dom g)\cap L(\dom f)\ne\emptyset$ or $\dom g\cap\inte L(\dom f)\ne\emptyset$. inclusionMoreau–Rockafellar
  4. Moreau–Fenchel. $f$ proper: $f$ lsc convex $\iff$ $f^{**}=f$; then $f^*\in\Gz{\H}$. Moreau–Fenchel
  5. Fenchel–Young. $f(x)+f^*(u)\ge\ps xu$, with equality $\iff u\in\partial f(x)$ $\iff$ (for $f\in\Gz{\H}$) $x\in\partial f^*(u)$: $\partial f^*=(\partial f)^{-1}$. Fenchel–Young

Page 3 · The proximity operator

  1. Prox is well defined. $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $\gamma\gt0$: $f+\frac1{2\gamma}\norm{\cdot-x}^2$ has exactly one minimizer, $\prox_{\gamma f}x$ (affine minorant + parabola: coercive and strictly convex). existence and uniqueness
  2. Characterization. $p=\prox_{\gamma f}x\iff\frac{x-p}{\gamma}\in\partial f(p)$, i.e. $\prox_{\gamma f}=(\Id+\gamma\partial f)^{-1}$. prox = resolvent
  3. Moreau decomposition. $\prox_{\gamma f^*}x=x-\gamma\prox_{\gamma^{-1}f}(\gamma^{-1}x)$; for $\gamma=1$, $\prox_f+\prox_{f^*}=\Id$. Moreau decomposition

Page 4 · Fixed points and Forward–Backward

  1. Picard. $T$ a strict contraction (constant $\rho\in[0,1[$) with fixed point $\hat x$ $\Rightarrow$ $x_{n+1}=Tx_n$ satisfies $\norm{x_n-\hat x}\le\rho^n\norm{x_0-\hat x}$. Picard
  2. Fejér convergence. Fejér-monotone with respect to $D$ and every weak sequential cluster point in $D$ $\Rightarrow$ $x_n\wto$ a point of $D$. Fejér convergence
  3. Demiclosedness. $T:C\to\H$ nonexpansive on a nonempty closed convex $C$, $x_n\in C$, $x_n\wto\hat x$ and $Tx_n-x_n\to0$ $\Rightarrow$ $\hat x\in\Fix T$. demiclosedness
  4. Krasnosel'skii–Mann. $T:C\to C$ nonexpansive ($C$ nonempty closed convex, $x_0\in C$), $\Fix T\ne\emptyset$, $\lambda_n\in[0,1]$ with $\sum\lambda_n(1-\lambda_n)=+\infty$ $\Rightarrow$ $x_{n+1}=x_n+\lambda_n(Tx_n-x_n)$ is Fejér, $Tx_n-x_n\to0$, $x_n\wto$ a point of $\Fix T$. KM
  5. Averaged operators. $A=(1-\alpha)\Id+\alpha R$ with $R$ nonexpansive; $A_1\circ A_2$ is $\frac{\alpha_1+\alpha_2-2\alpha_1\alpha_2}{1-\alpha_1\alpha_2}$-averaged; an $\alpha$-averaged $T$ with $\Fix T\ne\emptyset$ may be relaxed with $\lambda_n\in[0,1/\alpha]$, $\sum\lambda_n(1-\alpha\lambda_n)=+\infty$, and then $x_n\wto$ a point of $\Fix T$. compositionaveraged iteration
  6. The two bricks. $\prox_f$ is firmly nonexpansive ($\tfrac12$-averaged) for every $f\in\Gz{\H}$; $\Id-\gamma\nabla g$ is $\frac{\gamma\nu}{2}$-averaged for $\gamma\in\,]0,2/\nu[$ when $g\in\Gz{\H}$ has a $\nu$-Lipschitz gradient (Baillon–Haddad). prox firmly nonexpansivegradient step
  7. Forward–Backward. $\gamma\in\,]0,2/\nu[$, $\delta=2-\gamma\nu/2$, $\lambda_n\in[0,\delta]$ with $\sum\lambda_n(\delta-\lambda_n)=+\infty$, $\Argmin(f+g)\ne\emptyset$ $\Rightarrow$ $x_{n+1}=x_n+\lambda_n(\prox_{\gamma f}(x_n-\gamma\nabla g(x_n))-x_n)\wto$ a minimizer of $f+g$. Forward–Backward
  8. Proximal point. $\gamma_n\gt0$ with $\sum\gamma_n=+\infty$, $\Argmin f\ne\emptyset$ $\Rightarrow$ $x_{n+1}=\prox_{\gamma_nf}x_n\wto$ a minimizer of $f$. proximal point

Page 5 · Douglas–Rachford and PPXA+

  1. Reflect, then read the shadow. $\rprox_{\gamma f}=2\prox_{\gamma f}-\Id$ is nonexpansive, and $\zer(\partial f+\partial g)=\prox_{\gamma g}\big(\Fix(\rprox_{\gamma f}\circ\rprox_{\gamma g})\big)$. rproxDR dictionary
  2. Douglas–Rachford. Any $\gamma\gt0$, $\lambda_n\in[0,2]$ with $\sum\lambda_n(2-\lambda_n)=+\infty$, $\zer(\partial f+\partial g)\ne\emptyset$ $\Rightarrow$ $x_n\wto\hat x$, and the shadow $y_n=\prox_{\gamma g}x_n\wto\hat y=\prox_{\gamma g}\hat x\in\Argmin(f+g)$. Douglas–Rachford
  3. PPXA+. Parallel DR on $\H_1\times\dots\times\H_m$: $m$ proxes in parallel and one solve with $\sum_iL_i^*L_i$. $g_i\in\Gz{\H_i}$, $\sum_iL_i^*L_i$ an isomorphism, $\zer\big(\sum_iL_i^*\circ\partial g_i\circ L_i\big)\ne\emptyset$, any $\gamma\gt0$, $\lambda_n\in[0,2]$ with $\sum\lambda_n(2-\lambda_n)=+\infty$ $\Rightarrow$ $v_n\wto\hat v\in\Argmin\sum_ig_i\circ L_i$. PPXA+

Page 6 · Duality, ADMM and primal-dual

  1. Duality. For proper $f,g$: $\mu=\inf f+g\circ L\ \ge\ -\mu^*$ with $\mu^*=\inf_v f^*(-L^*v)+g^*(v)$; for $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $g\in\Gz{\G}$ and the sum-rule qualification condition, $\mu=-\mu^*$ and the dual infimum is attained. weak dualitystrong duality
  2. Kuhn–Tucker points. $-L^*\hat v\in\partial f(\hat x)$ and $L\hat x\in\partial g^*(\hat v)$ $\Rightarrow$ $\hat x$ solves the primal and $\hat v$ the dual; under the qualification condition, saddle points of $\Lag(x,y,v)=f(x)+g(y)+\ps{v}{Lx-y}$ are exactly the KT points with $\hat y=L\hat x$. KT pointssaddle points
  3. ADMM. $L^*L$ an isomorphism, the qualification condition, $\Argmin(f+g\circ L)\ne\emptyset$, any $\gamma\gt0$ $\Rightarrow$ $x_n\wto\hat x\in\Argmin(f+g\circ L)$ and $\gamma z_n\wto\hat v$, a dual solution: ADMM is Douglas–Rachford on the dual. ADMMDR ↔ ADMM
  4. Primal-dual. $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $g\in\Gz{\G}$, $h\in\Gz{\H}$ with a $\beta$-Lipschitz gradient, $\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm{L}^2\gt\beta/2$ and $\zer(\partial f+\nabla h+L^*\partial g\,L)\ne\emptyset$ $\Rightarrow$ $x_n\wto\hat x\in\Argmin(f+h+g\circ L)$ and $v_n\wto\hat v\in\Argmin\big((f+h)^*\circ(-L^*)+g^*\big)$. primal-dual

Page 7 · Majorization–Minimization

  1. MM and the half-quadratic majorant. $f(x_{n+1})\le h(x_{n+1},x_n)\le h(x_n,x_n)=f(x_n)$; if $\psi$ is differentiable on $]0,+\infty[$, $\psi(\sqrt\cdot)$ is concave, $\dot\psi\ge0$ and $\omega(x)=\dot\psi(x)/x$ has a finite limit at $0^+$, then $f=\psi(\abs\cdot)$ satisfies $f(x)\le f(y)+\dot f(y)(x-y)+\tfrac12\omega(\abs y)(x-y)^2$. MMhalf-quadratic
  2. MM quadratic. $x_{n+1}=x_n-\theta_nA(x_n)^{-1}\nabla f(x_n)$; $f$ coercive differentiable, $\nu\Id\preccurlyeq A(x_n)\preccurlyeq\bar\nu\Id$, $\theta_n\in[\underline\theta,2-\bar\theta]$ $\Rightarrow$ $f(x_n)-f(x_{n+1})\ge\mu_1\norm{x_{n+1}-x_n}^2\ge\mu_2\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2$, so (finite dimension) $\nabla f(x_n)\to0$, cluster points are stationary ($f\in C^1$), minimizers ($f$ convex). Answer

Proof idea · page 1

Why is "$f$ lsc" the same as "$\epi f$ closed"?

click or Space to flip

Answer

$\Rightarrow$: if $(x_n,\zeta_n)\in\epi f\to(x,\zeta)$, then $f(x)\le\liminf f(x_n)\le\lim\zeta_n=\zeta$. $\Leftarrow$: if $\liminf f(x_n)\lt\zeta\lt f(x)$, infinitely many $(x_n,\zeta)\in\epi f$ converge to $(x,\zeta)\notin\epi f$.

lsc = closed epigraph

Three facts about the minimizers of a convex function?

click or Space to flip

Answer

$f$ proper convex, $\inf f\gt-\infty$: $\Argmin f$ is convex; local minimizers are global; strictly convex $\Rightarrow$ at most one minimizer.

Why: the chord. Strict convexity buys uniqueness, never existence.

Statement · page 1

Existence and uniqueness of a minimizer over a closed convex set $C$?

Statement · page 1

First-order condition at a local minimizer over a convex set $C$ ($f$ Gâteaux differentiable)?

click or Space to flip

Answer

$\ps{\nabla f(\hat x)}{y-\hat x}\ge0$ for every $y\in C$; if $\hat x\in\inte C$, $\nabla f(\hat x)=0$. Sufficient when $f$ is convex.

Why: compare with $\hat x+\alpha(y-\hat x)\in C$, divide by $\alpha$, let $\alpha\to0^+$ (chord argument).

Formula · page 1

Hessian test for convexity and for strict convexity?

click or Space to flip

Answer

$f$ twice differentiable on an open convex $\dom f$: convex $\iff\nabla^2f(x)\succeq0$ for all $x$; $\nabla^2f\succ0$ everywhere $\Rightarrow$ strictly convex (not conversely: $x^4$).

Why: restrict to a line, $\varphi(t)=f(x+t(y-x))$, $\varphi''\ge0$.

Counterexample · page 1

A proper, lsc, strictly convex function on $\R$ with no minimizer?

click or Space to flip

Answer

$f(x)=e^x$: $\inf f=0$ is never attained. It is not coercive ($e^x\to0$ as $x\to-\infty$).

Why: the minimizing sequence $x_n=-n$ escapes to infinity; strict convexity buys uniqueness, not existence.

Statement · page 1

Strong versus weak convergence: definitions and the standard example?

click or Space to flip

Answer

$x_n\to\hat x$: $\norm{x_n-\hat x}\to0$. $x_n\wto\hat x$: $\ps{y}{x_n-\hat x}\to0$ for every $y$. Strong $\Rightarrow$ weak; equivalent in finite dimension; in $\ell^2$, $e_n\wto0$ while $\norm{e_n}=1$.

Trick · page 1

The indicator trick: what does a constraint become, and what do you get from $\ind_C$?

click or Space to flip

Answer

$\Argmin_{x\in C}f=\Argmin(f+\ind_C)$. Then $\partial\ind_C=N_C$, $\prox_{\gamma\ind_C}=P_C$ for every $\gamma$, $\ind_C^*=\sigma_C$; $\ind_C\in\Gz{\H}\iff C$ nonempty closed convex.

Formula · page 1

Adjoint of the duplication $L:x\mapsto(x,\dots,x)\in\H^m$? And $L^*L$?

Answer

$L^*(y_1,\dots,y_m)=\sum_{i=1}^my_i$ and $L^*L=m\Id$; the projection onto $\ran L$ (the diagonal) is the average.

Statement · page 2

Fermat's rule, exactly: which hypothesis, which conclusion?

click or Space to flip

Answer

$f$ proper (nothing else): $0\in\partial f(x)\iff x\in\Argmin f$.

Why: the horizontal line through $(x,f(x))$ supports the graph exactly when $f(x)$ is the lowest value. No convexity needed; it speaks of global minimizers only.

Fermat's rule
Formula · page 2

$\partial\abs{\cdot}(x)$ for every $x\in\R$?

click or Space to flip

Answer

$\{-1\}$ for $x\lt0$, $[-1,1]$ at $x=0$, $\{1\}$ for $x\gt0$.

Why: every line through the origin with slope in $[-1,1]$ stays below $\abs\cdot$; Fermat then says $0$ minimizes $\abs\cdot$ although it has no gradient there.

Proof idea · page 2

Why is $\partial f$ monotone?

click or Space to flip

Answer

Write $f(x_2)\ge f(x_1)+\ps{x_2-x_1}{u_1}$ and $f(x_1)\ge f(x_2)+\ps{x_1-x_2}{u_2}$, add: the values cancel and $\ps{u_1-u_2}{x_1-x_2}\ge0$.

Normal cone: definition and its value at an interior point, on a subspace, on the sphere of a ball?

Statement · page 2

Fenchel–Young: inequality, equality case, and the inversion?

click or Space to flip

Distinguish · notions that look alike

Minimal pairs

Most exam mistakes are swaps: lsc for continuous, weak for strong, $x_n$ for $y_n$, a bound for a majorant. Each block puts two (or more) look-alike notions side by side, says what separates them, and ends with a test you can run in ten seconds.

lower semicontinuous vs continuous

Lower semicontinuous

$x_n\to x\Rightarrow f(x)\le\liminf f(x_n)$; equivalently $\epi f$ is closed. Jumps are allowed when the point takes the lower value, and $+\infty$ values are allowed: $\ind_{[0,1]}$ is lsc.

Continuous

$x_n\to x\Rightarrow f(x_n)\to f(x)$. Continuous $\Rightarrow$ lsc, never conversely: $\ind_{[0,1]}$ jumps from $0$ to $+\infty$ at $1$.

Test. Look at each jump: lsc iff the value at the jump is the lower one (closed epigraph). $x^2$ with $f(0)=1$ is not lsc at $0$. lscclosed epigraph

proper vs finite-valued

Proper

$\dom f\ne\emptyset$ (and $f\gt-\infty$): at least one finite value. Indicators, the entropy $u\log u+\ind_{[0,+\infty[}$, $\theta$ of lab 3 are proper and take the value $+\infty$.

Finite-valued

$\dom f=\H$: $\norm\cdot$, $\tfrac12\norm{\cdot}^2$, Huber. A finite-valued $g\in\Gz{\H}$ makes the sum rule hold with equality for free.

Test. Is there a point where $f=+\infty$? Then $f$ is not finite-valued; it is proper as soon as one point is finite. domain, propersum rule

convex vs strictly convex vs strongly convex

Convex

$f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\le\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y)$: $\Argmin$ convex, local $=$ global. $\abs x$ is convex only.

Strictly convex

Strict inequality for $x\ne y$ in $\dom f$: at most one minimizer, no existence ($e^x$). $x^4$ is strictly convex.

Strongly convex Extra

$f-\frac\mu2\norm\cdot^2$ convex for some $\mu\gt0$: for $f\in\Gz{\H}$, existence and uniqueness at once; strong convexity of $g$ is the slide's example of the "additional assumption" under which Peaceman–Rachford converges.

In one dimension

$f''\ge0$ / $f''\gt0$ (sufficient, not necessary: $x^4$ at $0$) / $f''\ge\mu\gt0$.

Test. $e^x$ and $x^4$ are strictly but not strongly convex ($(e^x)''=e^x\to0$ as $x\to-\infty$, $(x^4)''(0)=0$); $\tfrac12x^2$ is strongly convex. strictly convexPeaceman–Rachford

coercive vs one bounded level set

Coercive

$f(x)\to+\infty$ as $\norm x\to+\infty$, equivalently every lower level set $\lev_{\le\zeta}f$ is bounded. It confines every minimizing sequence and every descent iterate to a ball.

One bounded level set

For existence in $\R^N$ (with lsc) one nonempty bounded $\lev_{\le\zeta}f$ is enough: $\frac{x^2}{1+x^2}$ has $\lev_{\le1/2}=[-1,1]$ but tends to $1$, so it is not coercive. Extra For $f\in\Gz{\R^N}$ one nonempty bounded level set forces all of them to be bounded.

Test. Follow $f$ along rays to infinity; exhibit one unbounded level set to refute coercivity: $\lev_{\le1}e^x=\,]-\infty,0]$. coerciveT03

weak vs strong convergence

Strong $x_n\to\hat x$

$\norm{x_n-\hat x}\to0$: the error itself vanishes.

Weak $x_n\wto\hat x$

$\ps{y}{x_n-\hat x}\to0$ for every $y$: every "measurement" converges, the norm need not. All the splitting theorems of pages 4–6 conclude weak convergence.

Test. In $\R^N$ they coincide (that is where you compute). In $\ell^2$, $e_n\wto0$ with $\norm{e_n}=1$. strong and weak

Fejér-monotone vs convergent

Fejér-monotone w.r.t. $D$

$\norm{x_{n+1}-x}\le\norm{x_n-x}$ for all $x\in D$: bounded, and every distance $\norm{x_n-x}$, $x\in D$, converges. Nothing more.

Convergent

Fejér w.r.t. $D$ plus every weak cluster point in $D$ gives $x_n\wto$ a point of $D$. A convergent sequence need not be Fejér: $1,\tfrac14,\tfrac13,\tfrac1{16},\tfrac15,\dots\to0$ is not monotone in distance to $0$.

Test. A rotation about $0$ is Fejér w.r.t. $D=\{0\}$ and never converges: check the second hypothesis (cluster points in $D$), usually through demiclosedness. FejérFejér convergence

strict contraction vs nonexpansive vs $\alpha$-averaged vs firmly nonexpansive

Strict contraction

$\norm{Tx-Ty}\le\rho\norm{x-y}$, $\rho\lt1$: one fixed point, Picard converges linearly.

Nonexpansive

$\rho=1$: $\Fix T$ may be empty or a whole set; Picard may circle (rotation); KM converges weakly when $\Fix T\ne\emptyset$.

$\alpha$-averaged

$T=(1-\alpha)\Id+\alpha R$, $R$ nonexpansive: when $\Fix T\ne\emptyset$, Picard ($\lambda_n=1$) converges weakly, relaxation up to $1/\alpha$; stable under composition.

Firmly nonexpansive

$\alpha=\frac12$: $\ps{Tx-Ty}{x-y}\ge\norm{Tx-Ty}^2$; every prox and projection; $2T-\Id$ is nonexpansive.

Test. Firmly $\subset$ averaged $\subset$ nonexpansive. A rotation is nonexpansive and not averaged; $P_C$ onto a line is firmly nonexpansive and not a contraction; $x\mapsto x/2$ is a contraction and $\frac14$-averaged ($R=-\Id$). nonexpansiveaveraged

prox vs projection vs resolvent

Prox

$\prox_{\gamma f}x=\argmin f+\frac1{2\gamma}\norm{\cdot-x}^2$ for $f\in\Gz{\H}$.

Projection

$P_C=\prox_{\gamma\ind_C}$ for every $\gamma\gt0$ ($C$ nonempty closed convex): the parameter disappears.

Resolvent

$(\Id+\gamma A)^{-1}$ of a (set-valued) operator $A$; $\prox_{\gamma f}$ is the resolvent of $A=\partial f$, single-valued because $\partial f$ comes from $f\in\Gz{\H}$.

One identity

$p=\prox_{\gamma f}x\iff x\in p+\gamma\partial f(p)$; for $f=\ind_C$: $x-p\in N_C(p)$.

Test. If $\gamma$ matters, it is not a projection; if you are writing a fixed-point dictionary (T15), think resolvent. prox = resolventprojection

Moreau envelope vs prox

Envelope $\env{\gamma}{f}$

A function: $\env{\gamma}{f}(x)=\min_yf(y)+\frac1{2\gamma}\norm{y-x}^2\le f(x)$, real-valued, and smooth even when $f$ is not ($\nabla\env{\gamma}{f}=\gamma^{-1}(\Id-\prox_{\gamma f})$ Extra); $\env{\gamma}{\ind_C}=d_C^2/(2\gamma)$.

Prox $\prox_{\gamma f}$

An operator $\H\to\H$: the point where the minimum is attained ($P_C$ for $\ind_C$).

Test. Value or point? They are tied by $\env{\gamma}{f}(x)=f(p)+\frac1{2\gamma}\norm{p-x}^2$ with $p=\prox_{\gamma f}x$. envelopeprox

gradient vs subgradient vs normal cone

Gradient

$\nabla f(x)$ from the directional derivatives (Gâteaux): a local, first-order object; exists for many nonconvex functions.

Subgradient

$u\in\partial f(x)$: slope of a global affine minorant touching at $x$. A set; may be empty, may contain many slopes ($\partial\abs\cdot(0)=[-1,1]$).

Normal cone

$N_C(x)=\partial\ind_C(x)$: the subgradients of a constraint, the outward directions at $x\in C$.

Bridge

$f$ convex and Gâteaux differentiable at $x$ $\Rightarrow$ $\partial f(x)=\{\nabla f(x)\}$.

Test. $f=-x^2$: $f'(x)=-2x$ exists everywhere, yet $\partial f(x)=\emptyset$ at every $x$ (no line stays below a downward parabola). smooth casenormal cone

Forward–Backward vs Douglas–Rachford vs ADMM vs primal-dual

Forward–Backward

$f+g$: $\prox_{\gamma f}$ and $\nabla g$ ($\nu$-Lipschitz). $\gamma\lt2/\nu$, $\lambda_n\le\delta=2-\gamma\nu/2$.

Douglas–Rachford

$f+g$: $\prox_{\gamma f}$ and $\prox_{\gamma g}$, nothing smooth. Any $\gamma\gt0$, $\lambda_n\le2$; output the shadow.

ADMM

$f+g\circ L$: $\prox_{g/\gamma}$ and an $x$-step with $L$ inside a square; needs $L^*L$ invertible and the qualification condition; any $\gamma\gt0$.

Primal-dual

$f+g\circ L+h$: $\prox_{\tau f}$, $\prox_{\sigma g^*}$ (Moreau), $\nabla h$, $L$, $L^*$; no inversion; $\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm L^2\gt\beta/2$.

Test. Three questions: is there a smooth term? Is a linear operator hidden in a nonsmooth term? Can you invert $L^*L$ cheaply? decision tree

Douglas–Rachford: governing sequence $x_n$ vs shadow $y_n$

Governing $x_n$

$x_n\wto\hat x\in\Fix(\rprox_{\gamma f}\rprox_{\gamma g})$, a point that depends on $\gamma$: $\hat x=\hat y+\gamma u$ with $u\in\partial g(\hat y)$.

Shadow $y_n=\prox_{\gamma g}x_n$

$y_n\wto\hat y=\prox_{\gamma g}\hat x\in\Argmin(f+g)$ (and $z_n$ too). This is the answer you report.

Test. $f=\abs{\cdot-1}$, $g=\frac12(\cdot-3)^2$: $x_n\to2-\gamma$ while $y_n\to2$, the minimizer. When there are several minimizers, $\gamma$ (and $x_0$) can change which one the shadow reaches. DR

Lagrangian vs augmented Lagrangian

Lagrangian

$\Lag(x,y,v)=f(x)+g(y)+\ps{v}{Lx-y}$, linear in the coupling: its partial minimizations can be $-\infty$ or non-unique, and alternating minimization is not guaranteed to converge.

Augmented Lagrangian

$\widetilde\Lag=f(x)+g(y)+\gamma\ps{z}{Lx-y}+\frac\gamma2\norm{Lx-y}^2$: same saddle points (multiplier $v=\gamma z$), well-posed subproblems; alternating on it is ADMM.

Test. On $Lx=y$ the two coincide; off it the augmented one adds a nonnegative penalty, invisible at a saddle point. augmented Lagrangiansame saddle points

Kuhn–Tucker point vs saddle point

Kuhn–Tucker point

A pair $(\hat x,\hat v)\in\H\times\G$: $-L^*\hat v\in\partial f(\hat x)$, $L\hat x\in\partial g^*(\hat v)$. Certifies both solutions with no qualification condition.

Saddle point

A triple $(\hat x,\hat y,\hat v)$: $\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,v)\le\Lag(\hat x,\hat y,\hat v)\le\Lag(x,y,\hat v)$ for all $(x,y,v)$.

Test. Count the variables. Under the qualification condition: saddle $\iff$ KT and $\hat y=L\hat x$. KTsaddle

weak duality vs strong duality

Weak

$\mu\ge-\mu^*$ for any proper $f$, $g$: Fenchel–Young twice. Every dual point is a lower-bound certificate.

Strong

$\mu=-\mu^*$ and the dual minimum is attained, for $f\in\Gz{\H}$, $g\in\Gz{\G}$ under the interior-point condition.

Test. The disc example ($f=x_1+\ind_A$, $g=\ind_{\{0\}}$, $Lx=x_2$) has $\mu=0=-\mu^*$ but no dual solution: "no gap" is not "strong duality as stated". strong duality

majorant vs upper bound

Upper bound

$f\le h$ everywhere. Minimizing $h$ tells you nothing about $f(x_{n+1})$ versus $f(x_n)$.

Majorant at $y$

$f\le h(\cdot,y)$ and $f(y)=h(y,y)$: the contact at the current point is what closes the sandwich $f(x_{n+1})\le h(x_{n+1},x_n)\le h(x_n,x_n)=f(x_n)$.

Test. $f=x^2$ at $x_n=0$, $h=x^2+(x-1)^2+1\ge f$: $\argmin h=\frac12$ and $f(\frac12)=\frac14\gt f(0)$. No contact, no decrease. majorant

MM quadratic vs Newton vs gradient

Gradient

$x-\gamma\nabla f(x)$: one scalar curvature for all directions; with $\gamma=1/\beta$ it is MM with $A=\beta\Id$.

Newton

$x-\nabla^2f(x)^{-1}\nabla f(x)$: the Taylor model, exact curvature, but not a majorant: no guaranteed decrease far from the solution.

MM quadratic

$x-\theta A(x)^{-1}\nabla f(x)$ with $A(x)$ chosen so that the quadratic lies above $f$ (e.g. $A\succcurlyeq\nabla^2f$, half-quadratic): local curvature and monotone decrease.

Block-coordinate

cyclic vs quasi-cyclic vs random block selection

Cyclic

$j_n-1=n\bmod J$: blocks $1,2,\dots,J,1,2,\dots$

Quasi-cyclic

Some $K\ge J$ with $\{1,\dots,J\}\subset\{j_n,\dots,j_{n+K-1}\}$ for every $n$: every block in every window of $K$ iterations.

a. Adjoint. $\ps{Lv}{y}=\sum_i\ps{L_iv}{y_i}=\ps{v}{\sum_iL_i^*y_i}$, so $L^*y=\sum_iL_i^*y_i$ and $L^*L=Q$. Because $v\mapsto Lv$ is a bijection from $\G$ onto $E$, $\min_vg(Lv)=\min_{x\in E}g(x)=\min_x\ind_E(x)+g(x)$, and minimizers correspond through $x=Lv$.

b. Proxes. $g$ is separable, so $\prox_{\gamma g}x=(\prox_{\gamma g_i}x_i)_i$ (prox of a separable sum). $P_Ex=Lc$ with $c=\argmin\norm{x-Lc}^2$, whose optimality condition is $L^*Lc=L^*x$: $P_E=LQ^{-1}L^*$, i.e. $P_Ex=\big(L_iQ^{-1}\sum_jL_j^*x_j\big)_i$. Why does Douglas–Rachford need no smoothness at all?

Because it only uses $\prox_{\gamma f}$ and $\prox_{\gamma g}$, and a prox of any $f\in\Gz{\H}$, for any $\gamma\gt0$, is firmly nonexpansive: monotonicity of $\partial f$ inverted through the resolvent. Firmly nonexpansive means "midpoint of $\Id$ and a nonexpansive map", so the reflections $\rprox$ are nonexpansive, their composition $T$ too, and DR is Krasnosel'skii–Mann on $T$ with $\mu_n=\lambda_n/2$. Smoothness was only needed to make an explicit gradient step averaged, and DR has no explicit step.

What does the extrapolation $2x_{n+1}-x_n$ do in the primal-dual algorithm?

It feeds the dual step with a look-ahead $x_{n+1}+(x_{n+1}-x_n)$ instead of $x_{n+1}$. Without it (idea 2), the bilinear coupling $\ps{v}{Lx}$ is handled explicitly and the iteration can rotate forever (the bilinear example of page 6). With it, the pair $(x_n,v_n)$ follows Forward–Backward in the metric $V=\begin{pmatrix}\tau^{-1}\Id&-L^*\\-L&\sigma^{-1}\Id\end{pmatrix}$: the extrapolation creates exactly the off-diagonal blocks, and it vanishes at a fixed point, so the fixed points are still the Kuhn–Tucker points. The condition $\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm L^2\gt\beta/2$ is FB's step condition in that metric.

Why is the prox the inverse of $\Id+\partial f$?

Write Fermat's rule for the function the prox minimizes, $f+\frac12\norm{\cdot-x}^2$. The quadratic is finite and differentiable, so the sum rule holds with equality: $0\in\partial f(p)+p-x$, i.e. $x\in p+\partial f(p)=(\Id+\partial f)(p)$. Existence and uniqueness of the minimizer say that every $x$ comes from exactly one $p$: $(\Id+\partial f)^{-1}$ is single-valued and defined everywhere, and it is $\prox_f$. The subgradient sits at the arrival point: an implicit step.

Why does Krasnosel'skii–Mann need $\sum\lambda_n(1-\lambda_n)=+\infty$?

The λ-identity gives the decrease $\norm{x_{n+1}-x}^2\le\norm{x_n-x}^2-\lambda_n(1-\lambda_n)\norm{Tx_n-x_n}^2$. Summing, $\sum\lambda_n(1-\lambda_n)\norm{Tx_n-x_n}^2\le\norm{x_0-x}^2$; only a divergent sum of weights forces the residual down ($\liminf=0$, then monotone). With $\lambda_n\equiv1$ on a rotation the weights are $0$ and nothing decreases; with $\lambda_n=1/(n+2)^2$ on $-\Id$ the weights are summable and $x_n$ stops at $0.217\,x_0$.

Where does the limit $\gamma\lt2/\nu$ of Forward–Backward come from?

From Baillon–Haddad: a convex $g$ with $\nu$-Lipschitz gradient makes $\Id-\gamma\nabla g$ a $\frac{\gamma\nu}2$-averaged operator, and an averagedness constant must be $\lt1$, so $\gamma\lt2/\nu$. Composed with the $\frac12$-averaged prox, the FB operator is $\frac1\delta$-averaged with $\delta=2-\gamma\nu/2$. At $\gamma=2/\nu$ the gradient step is only nonexpansive: on $\frac\nu2x^2$ it is $x\mapsto-x$, which oscillates.

Why must you output $y_n$, not $x_n$, in Douglas–Rachford?

Because $x_n$ converges to a fixed point of $T=\rprox_{\gamma f}\rprox_{\gamma g}$, and fixed points are not solutions: they sit "above" them, $\hat x=\hat y+\gamma u$ with $u\in\partial g(\hat y)$, at a place that depends on $\gamma$. The solution is the shadow $\hat y=\prox_{\gamma g}\hat x$, and $y_n=\prox_{\gamma g}x_n$ converges to it. Example: $\abs{\cdot-1}+\frac12(\cdot-3)^2$ has minimizer $2$, while $x_n\to2-\gamma$.

What does strong duality need, and why?

$f\in\Gz{\H}$, $g\in\Gz{\G}$, and the interior-point condition $\inte(\dom g)\cap L(\dom f)\ne\emptyset$ (or the symmetric one). It is the condition of the sum rule: when a primal solution $\hat x$ exists (the case proved on the card), $0\in\partial(f+g\circ L)(\hat x)$, and the sum rule splits it into $-L^*\hat v\in\partial f(\hat x)$, $\hat v\in\partial g(L\hat x)$, and Fenchel–Young equality makes $\hat v$ a dual solution with zero gap. When the domains only touch, the dual may have no solution: the disc example, where the dual objective $\sqrt{1+v^2}-v$ never reaches its infimum $0$.

Why does ADMM need $L^*L$ invertible, when the primal-dual algorithm does not?

ADMM's $x$-step minimizes $\frac12\norm{Lx-y_n+z_n}^2+\frac1\gamma f(x)$: $L$ sits inside a square, so the step must be well posed, and the convergence proof, which is DR on the dual, recovers the primal iterate as $x_n=(L^*L)^{-1}L^*s_n$. The primal-dual algorithm only applies $L$ and $L^*$ and pays for $L$ with the step condition $\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm L^2\gt\beta/2$ instead. Lab 3 ($A\in\{0,1\}^{6\times7}$, $A^\top A$ singular) is the case where this matters.

Why is MM monotone, and why is monotone not enough?

The sandwich: $f(x_{n+1})\le h(x_{n+1},x_n)$ because $h$ majorizes, $\le h(x_n,x_n)$ because $x_{n+1}$ minimizes $h(\cdot,x_n)$, $=f(x_n)$ because $h$ touches $f$ there. But a decreasing $f(x_n)$ can stall anywhere: convergence needs sufficient descent, $f(x_n)-f(x_{n+1})\ge\mu_2\norm{\nabla f(x_n)}^2$, which comes from uniform bounds $\nu\Id\preccurlyeq A(x_n)\preccurlyeq\bar\nu\Id$ and $\theta_n\le2-\bar\theta$; and even then, for nonconvex $f$, only stationary points (the tilted double well).

What is a Fejér-monotone sequence good for, and what is it not good for?

Fejér monotonicity with respect to the solution set $D$ keeps every solution in sight: the iterates stay bounded and every distance $\norm{x_n-x}$, $x\in D$, converges. That is half of Opial's lemma. It does not give convergence: a rotation about $0$ is Fejér with respect to $\{0\}$ and circles forever. The other half is "every weak cluster point is in $D$", which demiclosedness provides once the residual $Tx_n-x_n$ tends to $0$.

Why does averaging cure the rotation?

Replacing $Tx_n$ by $(1-\lambda)x_n+\lambda Tx_n$ makes the λ-identity bite: the squared distance to $0$ loses $\lambda(1-\lambda)\norm{Tx_n-x_n}^2$, which is positive as long as $x_n\ne0$. In the linear picture, the eigenvalues $e^{\pm i\theta}$ of modulus $1$ become $(1-\lambda)+\lambda e^{\pm i\theta}$, points of the chord between $1$ and $e^{\pm i\theta}$, strictly inside the unit circle. A nonexpansive map loses nothing; its average loses a quantity proportional to the residual.

Explain the Moreau decomposition.

Split $x=p+q$ with $q\in\partial f(p)$: that says $p=\prox_fx$. By Fenchel–Young, $q\in\partial f(p)\iff p\in\partial f^*(q)$, which says $q=\prox_{f^*}x$. So $x=\prox_fx+\prox_{f^*}x$, and with a parameter $\prox_{\gamma f^*}x=x-\gamma\prox_{\gamma^{-1}f}(\gamma^{-1}x)$. For $f=\ind_V$, $V$ a closed subspace, $f^*=\ind_{V^\perp}$ and it is $x=P_Vx+P_{V^\perp}x$. Its use: the prox of a conjugate (a support function, a dual term) costs nothing more than the prox of the function.

Why is the half-quadratic parabola a majorant?

Because $\psi$ is concave as a function of $x^2$: $\phi=\psi(\sqrt\cdot)$ lies below its tangent at $y^2$, $\phi(x^2)\le\phi(y^2)+\phi'(y^2)(x^2-y^2)$, with $\phi'(y^2)=\omega(\abs y)/2$. The identity $x^2-y^2=(x-y)^2+2y(x-y)$ turns this tangent in $x^2$ into a parabola in $x$, tangent to $f$ at $y$, of curvature $\omega(\abs y)=\dot\psi(\abs y)/\abs y$. Assumption (iv) keeps that curvature finite at $0$; without it ($\psi(u)=u$) you get IRLS and its infinite weights.

What does product-space lifting buy you?

It turns $m$ functions into two. Give each $g_i$ its own copy $x_i$ of the variable: $\sum_ig_i(x)$ becomes the separable $G(x_1,\dots,x_m)=\sum_ig_i(x_i)$ plus the constraint "all copies agree", $\ind_D$ with $D$ the diagonal. The prox of $G$ is $m$ independent proxes (parallel workers), the prox of $\ind_D$ is an average (one communication). Douglas–Rachford on $G+\ind_D$ is PPXA; with operators $L_i$ the average becomes one solve with $\sum_iL_i^*L_i$.

Why is the proximal point algorithm stable for every step size?

Because the step is implicit: $x_{n+1}=\prox_{\gamma_nf}x_n$ means $x_n-x_{n+1}\in\gamma_n\partial f(x_{n+1})$, the subgradient taken at the arrival point, and $\prox_{\gamma f}$ is firmly nonexpansive for every $\gamma\gt0$, with fixed points $\Argmin f$ whatever $\gamma$. A large step cannot overshoot. The only requirement is $\sum\gamma_n=+\infty$, an infinite total time: with $\gamma_n=2^{-(n+1)}$ on $\abs\cdot$ from $x_0=3$ the iterates stop at $2$.

How to revise

  • Retrieve, don't reread. Every session starts with a blank sheet: the thirty lines, then the twelve formulas, then check.
  • One page at a time, then mixed. Flashcards filtered by page until no hesitation; then all pages, shuffled, "only not known".
  • Keep the "known" marks honest. Tick a card on its page only when you can restate it and its proof idea; untick it the day you hesitate.
  • For every theorem, one counterexample per hypothesis. The rotation, $e^x$, $(-1)^n$, the disc, the double well, two lines: six examples cover most of the course.
  • Constants are computed, not memorized. $2/\nu$, $\delta=2-\gamma\nu/2$, $[0,2]$ for DR, $\tau^{-1}-\sigma\norm L^2\gt\beta/2$, $\theta_n\lt2$: each comes from one averagedness or descent computation (T22, T19).
  • Proofs are four moves. λ-identity, telescope, Fejér + demiclosedness, dictionary (T12–T15); MM proofs: evaluate the majorant built at $x_n$ at the new point $x_{n+1}$ (sufficient descent), telescope, compactness.
  • Distinguish before you compute. $x_n$ or $y_n$? lsc or continuous? bound or majorant? KT pair or saddle triple? The minimal pairs are the exam traps.
  • Time two exam problems a week and say one oral prompt aloud every day.
