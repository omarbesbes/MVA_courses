The proximity operator

The implicit gradient step that still makes sense when $f$ is not differentiable: definition, existence, the characterization $x-p\in\partial f(p)$, a cookbook of explicit formulas, and the Moreau decomposition.

Overview

Where we are

Page 2 ended with Fermat's rule $0\in\partial f(\hat x)$. This page builds the operator that inverts $\Id+\partial f$, and that operator is the brick of every algorithm of pages 4–6.

The one principle of the course reads: Fermat's rule → zeros of a sum of operators → fixed points of a nonexpansive composition → Fejér/Krasnosel'skii–Mann convergence. Page 3 sits exactly between the first two arrows. Fermat says a minimizer $\hat x$ of $f$ is a zero of $\partial f$. To find zeros by iteration you need a map whose fixed points are those zeros; the simplest such map is $$\hat x=\prox_{\gamma f}\hat x\iff 0\in\partial f(\hat x),$$ where $\prox_{\gamma f}=(\Id+\gamma\partial f)^{-1}$ is the object defined here. Everything on this page serves two purposes: (i) make sure $\prox$ is a well-defined single-valued map (existence, uniqueness, characterization), and (ii) give you a cookbook so that in a lab or an exam you can write $\prox_{\gamma f}$ explicitly for the functions that actually show up: indicators, powers $\chi\abs{\cdot}^q$, quadratics, separable sums, $\ell_1$ in a basis, support functions, conjugates, compositions with semi-orthogonal operators.

Remark

Explicit versus implicit gradient steps

Part IV · slide 2Slides#
The two schemes

Let $\H$ be a real Hilbert space and $f\in\Gz{\H}$ with a $\beta$-Lipschitz gradient. We look for $\hat x\in\Argmin f$.

  • Gradient descent (explicit): $\gamma\in\,]0,+\infty[$, $x_0\in\H$, $x_{n+1}=x_n-\gamma\nabla f(x_n)$. The sequence converges to a minimizer of $f$ provided one exists and $\gamma\in\,]0,2/\beta[$.
  • Alternative scheme (implicit): $x_{n+1}=x_n-\gamma\nabla f(x_{n+1})$ — the gradient is evaluated at the arrival point.

Three questions (slide 2): How to determine $x_{n+1}$ at each iteration of the implicit scheme? Which values of $\gamma$ guarantee convergence? What to do if $f$ is nonsmooth?

In one sentence

The implicit step $x_{n+1}=x_n-\gamma\nabla f(x_{n+1})$ is $x_{n+1}=(\Id+\gamma\nabla f)^{-1}x_n$: solving that equation is what the proximity operator does, and it keeps making sense when $\nabla f$ is replaced by $\partial f$.

How this page answers the three questions

Q1 — how to compute $x_{n+1}$? Rewrite the implicit step as $x_n=x_{n+1}+\gamma\nabla f(x_{n+1})$, i.e. $x_n\in(\Id+\gamma\partial f)(x_{n+1})$. The characterization theorem says exactly that $x_{n+1}=\prox_{\gamma f}(x_n)$. So the implicit equation has exactly one solution, the minimizer of the strictly convex coercive function $y\mapsto f(y)+\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{y-x_n}^2$ — that is how existence and uniqueness are proved. To compute it you do not minimize anything: you solve the inclusion $x_n-x_{n+1}\in\gamma\,\partial f(x_{n+1})$ by hand, and for the functions of the cookbook this gives a closed formula.

Q2 — which $\gamma$? All of them. Page 4 proves that $\prox_{\gamma f}$ is firmly nonexpansive for every $\gamma\gt0$ (prox is firmly nonexpansive), so the proximal point algorithm $x_{n+1}=\prox_{\gamma f}x_n$ converges weakly to a minimizer of $f$ for every $\gamma\gt0$ (indeed for any steps $(\gamma_n)$ with $\sum_n\gamma_n=+\infty$), provided $\Argmin f\neq\emptyset$. That is the same existence assumption as for gradient descent, but with no $2/\beta$ barrier and no Lipschitz constant needed. The widget below shows the mechanism on a quadratic: the explicit step multiplies the error by $1-\gamma\beta$, which explodes past $\gamma=2/\beta$; the implicit step multiplies it by $1/(1+\gamma\beta)\in\,]0,1[$, always.

Q3 — nonsmooth $f$? Replace $\nabla f$ by $\partial f$: the implicit step reads $x_n-x_{n+1}\in\gamma\partial f(x_{n+1})$, an inclusion that still has exactly one solution (existence–uniqueness theorem). The explicit scheme has no such luck: "$x_n-\gamma u_n$ with $u_n\in\partial f(x_n)$" is the subgradient method, which needs vanishing steps and is slow.

The explicit contraction factor is $\abs{1-\gamma\beta}$: it is $\lt1$ only for $\gamma\lt2/\beta$, and $1-\gamma\beta\lt0$ (the iterate jumps over the minimizer) as soon as $\gamma\gt1/\beta$. The implicit factor $1/(1+\gamma\beta)$ is $\lt1$ for every $\gamma\gt0$, and it goes to $0$ as $\gamma\to+\infty$: a huge implicit step jumps straight to the minimizer.

Insight

Explicit = evaluate the slope where you are; implicit = evaluate the slope where you land. For the gradient flow $\dot x=-\nabla f(x)$ the first is the forward Euler scheme, the second the backward (implicit) Euler scheme $\tfrac{x_{n+1}-x_n}{\gamma}=-\nabla f(x_{n+1})$: time still runs forward, but the slope is read at the end of the time step. Hence the names forward step (gradient) and backward step ($\prox$) of Forward–Backward on page 4.

To remember

Implicit step $x_{n+1}=x_n-\gamma\nabla f(x_{n+1})$ $\;\Longleftrightarrow\;$ $x_{n+1}=\prox_{\gamma f}x_n$. Any $\gamma\gt0$ works (weak convergence to a minimizer when $\Argmin f\neq\emptyset$); nonsmooth $f$ is fine.

The proximity operator

Two objects come out of one minimization: the value is the Moreau envelope, the argument is the proximal point. Existence and uniqueness make $\prox$ a map; Fermat's rule makes it the resolvent $(\Id+\partial f)^{-1}$.

Definition

Moreau envelope $\env{\gamma}{f}$

Part IV · slides 3–4Slides#
Definition

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space, $f\in\Gz{\H}$ and $\gamma\in\,]0,+\infty[$. The Moreau envelope of $f$ of parameter $\gamma$ is $$\env{\gamma}{f}:\H\to\R:\ x\mapsto\inf_{y\in\H}\ f(y)+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\norm{y-x}^2 .$$

In one sentence

$\env{\gamma}{f}(x)$ is the cheapest way to pay $f$ somewhere near $x$, the price of distance being a parabola of curvature $1/\gamma$: a smoothed, lowered copy of $f$.

Why the definition makes sense

Fix $x$ and look at the downward parabolas (caps) $y\mapsto c-\tfrac{1}{2\gamma}\norm{y-x}^2$ whose apex sits above $x$. Raise $c$ until the cap touches the graph of $f$ from below: the apex height at that moment is $\env{\gamma}{f}(x)$ and the contact point is $p=\prox_{\gamma f}x$. Indeed "$f(y)\ge c-\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{y-x}^2$ for all $y$" is exactly "$c\le f(y)+\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{y-x}^2$ for all $y$", and the largest such $c$ is the infimum. At the contact point the tangent of the cap is a supporting line of $f$, of slope $\tfrac{x-p}{\gamma}\in\partial f(p)$: the characterization theorem seen in a picture. Doing this for every $x$ sweeps out a new function that is everywhere below $f$ (take $y=x$: $\env{\gamma}{f}(x)\le f(x)$), everywhere finite (the existence theorem below says the infimum is attained, and Fenchel–Young gives a finite lower bound), and with no kinks: its slope at $x$ is the slope $\tfrac{x-p}{\gamma}$ of the cap at the contact point, a single number because $p$ is unique, even when $f$ has a corner at $p$ ($\nabla\env{\gamma}{f}(x)=\tfrac{x-\prox_{\gamma f}x}{\gamma}$, Extra below). Larger $\gamma$ means a flatter cap, so the envelope follows $f$ less closely: $\gamma$ is a smoothing radius. As $\gamma\to0^+$ the cap becomes a needle and $\env{\gamma}{f}\to f$ pointwise. Say it out loud: the envelope is the height of the highest cap of curvature $1/\gamma$ that fits under $f$; the prox is where it touches.

The classic picture (slide 4): $f=\abs{\cdot}$, whose envelope is $\tfrac1\gamma$ times the Huber function of page 2 with $\rho=\gamma$: $\env{\gamma}{\abs{\cdot}}(x)=\tfrac{x^2}{2\gamma}$ if $\abs{x}\le\gamma$, $\abs{x}-\tfrac\gamma2$ otherwise. The corner at $0$ is replaced by a parabola of width $2\gamma$.

Extra

Extra Three facts not proved on the slides but worth knowing: $\env{\gamma}{f}$ is convex, finite and Fréchet differentiable with $\nabla\env{\gamma}{f}=\gamma^{-1}(\Id-\prox_{\gamma f})$ (a $\gamma^{-1}$-Lipschitz gradient); $\inf\env{\gamma}{f}=\inf f$ and $\Argmin\env{\gamma}{f}=\Argmin f$; and $\env{\gamma}{f}\uparrow f$ pointwise as $\gamma\downarrow0$. So minimizing $f$ and minimizing its smooth envelope are the same problem — the proximal point algorithm of page 4 is gradient descent on $\env{\gamma}{f}$ with step $\gamma$.

To remember

$\env{\gamma}{f}(x)=\min_y f(y)+\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{y-x}^2\le f(x)$: a value, always smooth, with $\nabla\env{\gamma}{f}=\gamma^{-1}(\Id-\prox_{\gamma f})$ (Extra). "Envelope = value (smooth), prox = argument (a point)."

Definition

Proximity operator $\prox_f$

Part IV · slides 3–4SlidesKey#
Definition

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space and $f\in\Gz{\H}$. The proximity operator of $f$ is $$\prox_f:\H\to\H:\ x\mapsto\argmin_{y\in\H}\ f(y)+\tfrac12\norm{y-x}^2 .$$ For $\gamma\in\,]0,+\infty[$, $\prox_{\gamma f}x=\argmin_{y}\ f(y)+\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{y-x}^2$ is the point where the infimum defining $\env{\gamma}{f}(x)$ is attained: $$\env{\gamma}{f}(x)=f(\prox_{\gamma f}x)+\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{\prox_{\gamma f}x-x}^2 .$$

In one sentence

Stay close to $x$ (the quadratic) but pay $f$: the compromise point is $\prox_f x$, the compromise value is the envelope.

Why the definition makes sense

The word "argmin" hides a theorem: the next card shows that the minimizer exists and is unique, so $\prox_f$ really is a single-valued map $\H\to\H$ (Moreau, 1962). The weight $\tfrac12$ is there so that the gradient of the quadratic is simply $y-x$; dividing the objective by $\gamma$ shows that $\prox_{\gamma f}x$ minimizes $f(y)+\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{y-x}^2$, so the parameter $\gamma$ is the step size: large $\gamma$ means the quadratic leash is loose and the point moves far towards low values of $f$; small $\gamma$ keeps it close: $\prox_{\gamma f}x\to x$ as $\gamma\to0$ for every $x\in\overline{\dom f}$ (in general the limit is the projection $P_{\overline{\dom f}}\,x$ Extra; for $f=\ind_C$ the prox is $\PC$ for every $\gamma$).

Two examples to keep in mind, both derived below. $f=\abs{\cdot}$ on $\R$: $\prox_{\gamma f}$ is soft-thresholding, $x\mapsto\sgn(x)\max\{\abs{x}-\gamma,0\}$ (the hero picture). It kills everything in $[-\gamma,\gamma]$, and far from $0$ it is the identity shifted by $\gamma$ towards $0$ ($x\mapsto x-\gamma\sgn x$: every large coefficient pays the same bias $\gamma$). Do not confuse it with hard-thresholding, which keeps large values unchanged: hard-thresholding is the prox of no convex function, since it is discontinuous while every prox is nonexpansive (page 4). $f=\ind_C$: $\prox_f=\PC$, the projection, which is why the course writes algorithms with $\prox$ and gets projected methods for free.

Pitfall

$\prox_{\gamma f}$ is not $\gamma\prox_f$ nor $\prox_f$ applied to $\gamma x$: the parameter sits inside the function. Also $\prox$ is defined for $f\in\Gz{\H}$ only; for a nonconvex $f$ the argmin may be empty or a set (see the next card).

To remember

$\prox_{\gamma f}x=\argmin_y\big(f(y)+\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{y-x}^2\big)$; $\env{\gamma}{f}$ is the value, $\prox$ is the argument; $\gamma$ is the step.

Moreau envelope vs proximity operator

$\env{\gamma}{f}$: a value

$\env{\gamma}{f}:\H\to\R$, the minimum value of $y\mapsto f(y)+\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{y-x}^2$. Below $f$, finite, convex, and always differentiable, even where $f$ has corners or equals $+\infty$: $\nabla\env{\gamma}{f}(x)=\tfrac{x-\prox_{\gamma f}x}{\gamma}$, a $\gamma^{-1}$-Lipschitz gradient Extra. Examples: $\env{\gamma}{\abs\cdot}=\tfrac1\gamma\,$Huber (with $\rho=\gamma$); $\env{\gamma}{\ind_C}=d_C^2/(2\gamma)$.

$\prox_{\gamma f}$: an argument

$\prox_{\gamma f}:\H\to\H$, the point where that minimum is attained, characterized by $\tfrac{x-p}{\gamma}\in\partial f(p)$. Not a smoothed $f$ but a map (firmly nonexpansive, page 4), which may have flat pieces. Examples: $\prox_{\gamma\abs\cdot}$ = soft-thresholding at level $\gamma$; $\prox_{\ind_C}=\PC$.

The two meet in one formula: the slope of the envelope at $x$ is a subgradient of $f$ at the prox point, $\nabla\env{\gamma}{f}(x)=\tfrac{x-\prox_{\gamma f}x}{\gamma}\in\partial f(\prox_{\gamma f}x)$. For $f=\abs\cdot$ it is $P_{[-1,1]}(x/\gamma)$, i.e. $\gamma^{-1}$ times the orange clipping curve of the Moreau-decomposition widget; in the envelope widget it is the slope of the two gold lines.

Theorem

Existence and uniqueness of the proximal point

Part IV · slide 5SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $f\in\Gz{\H}$ and $\gamma\in\,]0,+\infty[$. For every $x\in\H$ there exists a unique $p\in\H$ such that $$f(p)+\frac1{2\gamma}\norm{p-x}^2=\inf_{y\in\H}\ f(y)+\frac1{2\gamma}\norm{y-x}^2 .$$ In other words $\prox_{\gamma f}x$ is well defined and $\env{\gamma}{f}(x)\in\R$.

In one sentence

A proper lsc convex function has an affine minorant; add a parabola and you get something strictly convex and coercive, which is all existence and uniqueness ever need.

Why it is true

The function $g=f+\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{\cdot-x}^2$ inherits "proper, lsc, convex" from $f$ (the quadratic is finite, continuous, convex). What the quadratic adds is two things. Coercivity: $f$ alone could go to $-\infty$ linearly or stay bounded, but it cannot do worse than an affine function (that is Fenchel–Young with any $u\in\dom f^*$), and a parabola beats every affine function at infinity. Strict convexity: a strictly convex term added to a convex one gives a strictly convex sum, and a strictly convex function has at most one minimizer. Existence comes from the existence theorem of page 1 (proper + lsc + convex + coercive on a Hilbert space), uniqueness from strict convexity. Say it out loud: the parabola buys coercivity and strict convexity; $\Gz{\H}$ supplies the rest.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $f$ proper: $g$ is not identically $+\infty$, so the infimum is $\lt+\infty$ and "minimizer" means something. Also needed for $f^*$ to be proper (Fenchel–Moreau).
  • $f$ lsc: the direct method (minimizing sequence, weak compactness, weak lsc) needs lsc; without it the infimum may not be attained. Counterexample: $f=\ind_{]0,1[}$ on $\R$ (convex, proper, not lsc), $x=2$: $\inf_{y\in]0,1[}\tfrac12(y-2)^2=\tfrac12$ is not attained.
  • $f$ convex: gives the affine minorant (through $f^*$) and the strict convexity of $g$. Counterexample for uniqueness: $f=\ind_{\{-1,1\}}$, $x=0$: both $\pm1$ minimize. Counterexample for existence: $f=-\norm{\cdot}^2$ (concave), $\gamma=1$: $g$ is unbounded below.
  • $\gamma\gt0$: the quadratic must be a bowl, not a cap.

You need an affine lower bound on $f$. Where does one come from when you only know $f\in\Gz{\H}$? Think of $f^*$ and of Fenchel–Young.

Proof T10 Fenchel–Young as a leverT03 coercivity

Fix $x\in\H$ and set $g=f+\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{\cdot-x}^2$.

1. An affine minorant of $f$. By Fenchel–Moreau, $f\in\Gz{\H}\iff f^*\in\Gz{\H}$; in particular $f^*$ is proper, so there exists $u\in\H$ with $f^*(u)\in\R$. The Fenchel–Young inequality then gives, for every $y\in\H$, $$f(y)\ \ge\ \ps{u}{y}-f^*(u).$$

2. Coercivity. Use $\ps{u}{y}\ge-\norm{u}\norm{y}$ and, for $\norm{y}\ge\norm{x}$, the triangle inequality $\norm{y-x}\ge\norm{y}-\norm{x}\ge0$, whose two sides are nonnegative and may therefore be squared: $$\big(\forall y\in\H,\ \norm y\ge\norm x\big)\qquad g(y)\ \ge\ \frac1{2\gamma}\big(\norm{y}-\norm{x}\big)^2-\norm{u}\,\norm{y}-f^*(u)\ \xrightarrow[\norm{y}\to+\infty]{}\ +\infty ,$$ because the quadratic term dominates the linear one. So $g$ is coercive.

3. $g\in\Gz{\H}$ and strictly convex. $g$ is proper (finite where $f$ is), lsc (sum of lsc functions; the quadratic is continuous), convex (sum of convex functions). Moreover $\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{\cdot-x}^2$ is strictly convex, and convex + strictly convex = strictly convex (write the convexity inequality for $f$ and the strict one for the quadratic, add).

4. Conclusion. Existence: a proper lsc convex coercive function on a Hilbert space has a minimizer (existence and uniqueness theorem, page 1: the minimizing sequence stays in a ball by coercivity, extract a weakly convergent subsequence, conclude by weak lsc of a convex lsc function). Uniqueness: a strictly convex function has at most one minimizer (minimizers of convex functions). Hence the minimizer $p$ exists and is unique, and $\env{\gamma}{f}(x)=g(p)\in\R$.

Theorem

Characterization: $p=\prox_f x \iff x-p\in\partial f(p)$

Part IV · slide 6SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space and $f\in\Gz{\H}$. For every $x\in\H$, $$p=\prox_f x\iff x-p\in\partial f(p)\iff x\in(\Id+\partial f)(p),$$ that is, $\prox_f=(\Id+\partial f)^{-1}$ is the resolvent of $\partial f$. For $\gamma\gt0$: $p=\prox_{\gamma f}x\iff x-p\in\gamma\,\partial f(p)\iff \dfrac{x-p}{\gamma}\in\partial f(p)$.

In one sentence

$\prox_f$ is the inverse of $\Id+\partial f$: it undoes "add a subgradient". Every $x$ is reached from exactly one $p$, and $x-p$ is a subgradient at the arrival point $p$.

Why it is true

Fermat's rule applied to the strictly convex function $f+\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-x}^2$: its subdifferential at $p$ is $\partial f(p)+(p-x)$ because the quadratic part is differentiable everywhere (sum rule: its qualification condition holds automatically because the quadratic is finite and continuous everywhere), so $0$ belongs to it exactly when $x-p$ is a subgradient of $f$ at $p$. The point is that $\Id+\partial f$ is a set-valued map which may send one $p$ to many $x$, but every $x$ has exactly one preimage, so its inverse is an honest single-valued map on the whole space — the previous theorem is what guarantees that. The sentence to say out loud: prox is the resolvent of the subdifferential.

Reading it as a step: $p=x-u$ with $u\in\partial f(p)$ is an implicit subgradient step of size $1$ (size $\gamma$ for $\prox_{\gamma f}$), the answer to question 1 of the motivation card.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $f$ proper, lsc, convex buys two things: the argmin exists and is unique (previous card), so "$=\prox_f x$" is a single point; and the sum rule $\partial\big(f+\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-x}^2\big)=\partial f+(\cdot-x)$. Fermat's rule itself needs no convexity ($0\in\partial g(p)\iff p$ minimizes $g$, for any proper $g$); what breaks without convexity is the sum rule. Example: $f(y)=-y^2/4$ on $\R$. Then $f+\tfrac12(\cdot-x)^2=\tfrac14y^2-xy+\tfrac12x^2$ is strictly convex with minimizer $p=2x$, but $\partial f(p)=\emptyset$ (a concave parabola has no affine minorant), so "$x-p\in\partial f(p)$" fails at the true minimizer.
  • The qualification condition of the sum rule holds automatically because $h=\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-x}^2$ is finite and continuous everywhere: $\partial(f+h)=\partial f+\nabla h$ whenever $f\in\Gz{\H}$ and $h$ is convex, finite and Gâteaux differentiable on $\H$ (Moreau–Rockafellar).
Hint — try it first

Fermat's rule, then the sum rule for $\partial\big(f+\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-x}^2\big)$. The gradient of $\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-x}^2$ at $p$ is $p-x$.

Proof T08 Fermat + sum rule + rewrite

1. Fermat. By Fermat's rule, $p=\prox_f x$ iff $p$ minimizes $g=f+\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-x}^2$ iff $0\in\partial g(p)$.

2. Sum rule. $h=\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-x}^2$ is convex, finite-valued and Gâteaux differentiable on $\H$ with $\nabla h(p)=p-x$ (expand $h(p+t d)=h(p)+t\ps{p-x}{d}+\tfrac{t^2}2\norm d^2$). Hence $\partial h(p)=\{p-x\}$ (convex + Gâteaux ⇒ singleton) and the sum rule applies (its qualification condition holds because $h$ is continuous everywhere): $\partial g(p)=\partial f(p)+p-x$.

3. Rewrite. $0\in\partial f(p)+p-x\iff x-p\in\partial f(p)\iff x\in p+\partial f(p)=(\Id+\partial f)(p)$.

4. The $\gamma$ version. Apply the above to $\gamma f\in\Gz{\H}$ and use $\partial(\gamma f)=\gamma\partial f$ (scaling).

Picture: inverting a graph

In 1-D, inverting a map means reflecting its graph across the diagonal. Take $f=\abs\cdot$. The graph of $\Id+\gamma\partial f$ is the half-line $p\mapsto p-\gamma$ for $p\lt0$, the vertical segment $\{0\}\times[-\gamma,\gamma]$ at $p=0$, and $p\mapsto p+\gamma$ for $p\gt0$. Reflect it: the vertical segment becomes the flat dead zone $[-\gamma,\gamma]\times\{0\}$ and the two half-lines become $x\mapsto x-\gamma\sgn x$ — that is soft-thresholding (the power widget draws both graphs). Read it as a step: the explicit subgradient step $x-\gamma\sgn x$ overshoots $0$ whenever $0\lt\abs x\lt\gamma$ (from $x=0.3$ with $\gamma=1$: $-0.7,\ 0.3,\ -0.7,\dots$ forever), whereas the implicit step may land on the kink, because the kink offers every slope in $[-1,1]$: every $\abs x\le\gamma$ lands exactly on $0$. Slogan: vertical pieces of $\partial f$ become flat pieces of $\prox_{\gamma f}$.

Trick

T08 To compute a prox, never minimize: solve the inclusion. The recipe: (1) check $f\in\Gz{\H}$: the answer exists and is unique, so any $p$ satisfying the inclusion is $\prox_{\gamma f}x$. (2) Write $\tfrac{x-p}{\gamma}\in\partial f(p)$. (3) Where $f$ is differentiable this is the equation $x-p=\gamma\nabla f(p)$: solve it (a linear system for a quadratic). (4) At a kink, $\partial f(p)$ is an interval, which sends a whole interval of $x$ to the same $p$ (a dead zone); treat each case, then check that the solution lies in the region the case assumed. (5) Before computing, simplify: symmetry (even $f$ ⇒ odd prox), the translation / scaling / quadratic-perturbation rules, and Moreau if $f^*$ is simpler (norms, support functions). Every formula of the cookbook below is obtained this way, and the same move gives the fixed-point equations of Forward–Backward, Douglas–Rachford and the primal-dual algorithm.

Pitfall

The inclusion is at $p$, not at $x$: $x-p\in\partial f(p)$, not $\partial f(x)$. An implicit (backward) step evaluates the subgradient at the arrival point. And $x-p$ is a subgradient — some element of $\partial f(p)$ — not a gradient: $f$ need not be differentiable anywhere (think $\ind_C$).

Insight

Monotonicity of $\partial f$ (page 2) is why $\prox$ behaves so well: if $x_1-p_1\in\partial f(p_1)$ and $x_2-p_2\in\partial f(p_2)$ then $\ps{(x_1-p_1)-(x_2-p_2)}{p_1-p_2}\ge0$, i.e. $\norm{p_1-p_2}^2\le\ps{x_1-x_2}{p_1-p_2}$: $\prox_f$ is firmly nonexpansive. Page 4 takes it from here.

Proposition

Prox of an indicator = projection; the obtuse-angle characterization; $\env{\gamma}{\ind_C}=\tfrac1{2\gamma}d_C^2$

Statement

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space and $C$ a nonempty closed convex subset of $\H$. Then for every $x\in\H$ $$\prox_{\ind_C}(x)=\argmin_{y\in C}\ \tfrac12\norm{y-x}^2=\PC(x),$$ the projection of $x$ onto $C$. Moreover:

In one sentence

With $f=\ind_C$ the "pay $f$" part only says "stay in $C$", so the prox is the nearest point of $C$; the subgradient $x-p$ is then a normal vector, which is the obtuse-angle condition.

Why it is true

$\ind_C$ is $0$ on $C$ and $+\infty$ outside (T01), so minimizing $\ind_C(y)+\tfrac12\norm{y-x}^2$ over $\H$ is minimizing $\tfrac12\norm{y-x}^2$ over $C$: the nearest point. The existence–uniqueness theorem for $\prox$ is, in this case, the projection theorem onto a closed convex set. The characterization $x-p\in\partial\ind_C(p)$ becomes geometric because $\partial\ind_C(p)$ is the normal cone: $x-p$ is normal to $C$ at $p$, i.e. makes an angle $\ge90^\circ$ with every direction $y-p$ pointing into $C$. For a vector space, $y-p$ runs over the whole space $C$ and its negatives, so "$\le0$ for all" becomes "$=0$ for all": orthogonality. Finally the envelope is the minimal value, $\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{\PC x-x}^2$. Say it out loud: projecting is proxing an indicator; the residual $x-p$ is a normal vector at $p$.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $C$ nonempty: $\ind_C$ proper. Closed: $\ind_C$ lsc (its epigraph is $C\times[0,+\infty[$), so the nearest point exists; for $C=\,]0,1[$ and $x=2$ there is none. Convex: $\ind_C$ convex, so the nearest point is unique; for the two-point set $\{-1,1\}$ and $x=0$ there are two.
  • Together: $\ind_C\in\Gz{\H}$ (stability card, page 1), so all prox results apply.

Why it is true

Three structural facts first. (i) $\varphi$ is even, so by the reflection rule $\prox_\varphi$ is odd: it suffices to treat $\xi\ge0$, and then $p\ge0$. (ii) $p$ and $\xi$ have the same sign and $\abs p\le\abs\xi$: the subgradient $\xi-p$ of an even convex function (hence minimal at $0$) has the sign of $p$. (iii) The map $p\mapsto p+q\chi\abs p^{q-1}\sgn(p)$ is continuous and strictly increasing from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$ (for $q\gt1$), so the equation has exactly one root — this is existence–uniqueness seen in 1-D. The shape matters more than the radicals: for small $q$ ($\lt2$) small inputs are strongly shrunk ($p\approx(\xi/q\chi)^{1/(q-1)}$ when $1\lt q\lt2$), with exact zeros (sparsity) only for $q=1$, while large inputs are almost untouched in relative terms ($p/\xi\to1$); $q=2$ shrinks everything by the same factor; large $q$ ($\gt2$) leaves small inputs alone and crushes large ones. The exercise makes this precise. Say it out loud: below $q=2$ the prox shrinks small values hard and large ones little, above $q=2$ the reverse; only $q=1$ sets small values exactly to $0$.

Statement

Let $\H$, $\G$ be Hilbert spaces, $L\in\B(\H,\G)$, $\gamma\in\,]0,+\infty[$ and $z\in\G$. If $f=\gamma\norm{L\cdot-z}^2/2$, then $$\prox_f=(\Id+\gamma L^*L)^{-1}(\cdot+\gamma L^*z),\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \prox_f x=(\Id+\gamma L^*L)^{-1}(x+\gamma L^*z).$$

In one sentence

For a differentiable $f$ the inclusion $x-p\in\partial f(p)$ is the equation $x-p=\nabla f(p)$; for a quadratic $f$ that equation is linear, so the prox is a linear solve.

Why it is true

$\nabla f(p)=\gamma L^*(Lp-z)$ (the gradient of $\tfrac12\norm{L\cdot-z}^2$ is the "normal equations" operator $L^*(L\cdot-z)$). So $x-p=\gamma L^*Lp-\gamma L^*z$, i.e. $(\Id+\gamma L^*L)p=x+\gamma L^*z$. The operator $\Id+\gamma L^*L$ is self-adjoint and bounded below by $\Id$ ($\ps{(\Id+\gamma L^*L)p}{p}=\norm p^2+\gamma\norm{Lp}^2\ge\norm p^2$), hence invertible; in finite dimension it is a symmetric positive definite matrix. The mechanism to say aloud: quadratic objective ⇒ linear optimality condition ⇒ prox = solve a SPD system. Note what the quadratic $\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-x}^2$ of the prox adds: the identity, which makes the system invertible even when $L^*L$ is not (no need for $L$ injective).

What each hypothesis buys
  • $L$ bounded linear: $f$ is a finite continuous convex function, so $f\in\Gz{\H}$ and $\partial f=\{\nabla f\}$. The adjoint $L^*$ exists because $L$ is bounded.
  • $\gamma\gt0$: $f$ is convex, hence in $\Gz{\H}$, so the prox theory of the course applies. For $\gamma\lt0$ (and $L\ne0$) $f$ is concave and leaves $\Gz{\H}$: the minimization defining the prox still has the same solution while $1+\gamma\norm L^2\gt0$ (the prox's own quadratic dominates), but it is unbounded below as soon as $1+\gamma\norm L^2\lt0$.
  • Nothing is assumed on $L^*L$: the $\Id$ term makes $\Id+\gamma L^*L$ invertible regardless.

In one sentence

Each rule is a change of variables in $x-p\in\partial g(p)$: shift, scale or flip $p$ until the inclusion reads "$x'-p'\in\partial f(p')$" for some new $x'$ and some multiple of $f$, then read off $p'=\prox_{cf}(x')$ for the appropriate multiple $cf$ of $f$.

Why it is true

The subdifferential of a transformed function is the transformed subdifferential: $\partial[f(\cdot-z)](p)=\partial f(p-z)$, $\partial[f(\rho\cdot)](p)=\rho\,\partial f(\rho p)$, $\partial[f(-\cdot)](p)=-\partial f(-p)$, $\partial[f+\tfrac\alpha2\norm\cdot^2+\ps z\cdot](p)=\partial f(p)+\alpha p+z$ (sum rule with a differentiable function). Plug each into $x-p\in\partial g(p)$ and rename. The quadratic perturbation is the most useful: a quadratic term added to $f$ can be absorbed into the prox's own quadratic, at the price of rescaling the point and the function by $1/(\alpha+1)$ (T09 complete the square). The envelope row is different in nature: it says that the prox of the smoothed function is a convex combination of $x$ and a prox of $f$ with a larger parameter — smoothing $f$ by $\gamma$ and then taking a step of size $1$ is the same as taking a step of size $1+\gamma$ on $f$ and moving back part of the way. Say it out loud: every rule is a change of variables in $x-p\in\partial g(p)$.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $f\in\Gz{\H}$: all transformed functions stay in $\Gz{\H}$ ($\alpha\gt0$ keeps the perturbation convex; $\rho\ne0$ keeps $f(\rho\cdot)$ proper), so every prox in the table exists.
  • $\gamma\in\R$ in the perturbation row: a constant does not change an argmin.

Throughout, $p=\prox_g x\iff x-p\in\partial g(p)$ (characterization), and the subdifferentials of the transformed functions are computed from the definition of $\partial$ or from the sum rule.

1. Translation. $g=f(\cdot-z)$ has $\partial g(p)=\partial f(p-z)$ (write the subgradient inequality at $p-z$). Set $u=p-z$: $x-p\in\partial f(p-z)\iff(x-z)-u\in\partial f(u)\iff u=\prox_f(x-z)$. Hence $p=z+\prox_f(x-z)$.

2. Quadratic perturbation. $h=\tfrac\alpha2\norm\cdot^2+\ps z\cdot+\gamma$ is convex, finite and differentiable with $\nabla h(p)=\alpha p+z$, so $\partial g(p)=\partial f(p)+\alpha p+z$ (sum rule). Then $$x-p\in\partial f(p)+\alpha p+z\iff x-z-(\alpha+1)p\in\partial f(p)\iff\frac{x-z}{\alpha+1}-p\in\frac1{\alpha+1}\partial f(p)=\partial\Big(\frac f{\alpha+1}\Big)(p),$$ using $\partial(\lambda f)=\lambda\partial f$ for $\lambda\gt0$. So $p=\prox_{f/(\alpha+1)}\big(\tfrac{x-z}{\alpha+1}\big)$.

3. Scaling. $g=f(\rho\,\cdot)$ has $\partial g(p)=\rho\,\partial f(\rho p)$ (if $u\in\partial f(\rho p)$ then $f(\rho y)\ge f(\rho p)+\ps u{\rho y-\rho p}=f(\rho p)+\ps{\rho u}{y-p}$, and conversely). Set $v=\rho p$: $x-p\in\rho\,\partial f(\rho p)\iff\rho x-v\in\rho^2\partial f(v)=\partial(\rho^2f)(v)\iff v=\prox_{\rho^2f}(\rho x)$. Hence $p=\rho^{-1}\prox_{\rho^2 f}(\rho x)$.

4. Reflection. Scaling with $\rho=-1$: $p=-\prox_{f}(-x)$.

5. Moreau envelope. Set $F(u,y)=f(u)+\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{u-y}^2+\tfrac12\norm{y-x}^2$ on $\H\times\H$. By definition, $\inf_u F(u,y)=\env{\gamma}{f}(y)+\tfrac12\norm{y-x}^2$, whose unique minimizer is $p=\prox_{\env{\gamma}{f}}x$, because the envelope is in $\Gz{\H}$. Indeed $(u,y)\mapsto f(u)+\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{u-y}^2$ is jointly convex, so its partial infimum $y\mapsto\env{\gamma}{f}(y)$ is convex; it is finite (the infimum is attained, existence theorem) and bounded above near every point by the continuous function $y\mapsto f(u_0)+\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{u_0-y}^2$ ($u_0\in\dom f$), hence continuous (a convex function bounded above on a neighbourhood of a point is continuous there). Swap the order of the infima. For fixed $u$, $y\mapsto\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{u-y}^2+\tfrac12\norm{y-x}^2$ is a strictly convex quadratic with gradient $\tfrac1\gamma(y-u)+(y-x)$, minimized at $y_u=\dfrac{u+\gamma x}{1+\gamma}$; there $u-y_u=\tfrac{\gamma}{1+\gamma}(u-x)$ and $y_u-x=\tfrac1{1+\gamma}(u-x)$, so (complete the square) the minimal value is $\tfrac{\gamma}{2(1+\gamma)^2}\norm{u-x}^2+\tfrac1{2(1+\gamma)^2}\norm{u-x}^2=\dfrac{\norm{u-x}^2}{2(1+\gamma)}$. Hence $\inf_y F(u,y)=f(u)+\tfrac1{2(1+\gamma)}\norm{u-x}^2$, minimized at the unique point $\hat u=\prox_{(1+\gamma)f}(x)$. The pair $(\hat u,y_{\hat u})$ minimizes $F$ over $\H\times\H$, so $y_{\hat u}$ minimizes $y\mapsto\inf_uF(u,y)$: indeed $\inf_uF(u,y_{\hat u})\le F(\hat u,y_{\hat u})=\min F\le\inf_uF(u,y)$ for every $y$. By uniqueness of that minimizer, $p=y_{\hat u}=\dfrac{\gamma x+\prox_{(1+\gamma)f}(x)}{1+\gamma}$.

Proposition

Prox of a separable sum

Part IV · slide 12SlidesKey#
Statement

For every $i\in\{1,\dots,n\}$ let $\H_i$ be a Hilbert space and $f_i\in\Gz{\H_i}$. If $$\big(\forall x=(x_1,\dots,x_n)\in\H_1\times\dots\times\H_n\big)\qquad f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^n f_i(x_i),$$ then $\big(\forall x\in\H_1\times\dots\times\H_n\big)\quad\prox_f(x)=\big(\prox_{f_i}(x_i)\big)_{1\le i\le n}$.

In one sentence

A sum of functions of separate variables is minimized by minimizing each term separately; the prox's quadratic is itself separable, so the prox is computed coordinate block by coordinate block.

Why it is true

In the product space the norm splits, $\norm{y-x}^2=\sum_i\norm{y_i-x_i}^2$, so $f(y)+\tfrac12\norm{y-x}^2=\sum_i\big(f_i(y_i)+\tfrac12\norm{y_i-x_i}^2\big)$: $n$ independent problems, each with a unique solution $\prox_{f_i}x_i$. The sum is bounded below by the sum of the minima, with equality exactly when every block is at its own minimizer. This is the rule that makes parallel algorithms possible (product-space lifting, T16): a prox in $\H^m$ is $m$ proxes computed at once. Say it out loud: separable function, separable prox.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $f_i\in\Gz{\H_i}$: each block problem has a unique solution, and $f\in\Gz{\H_1\times\dots\times\H_n}$ (proper: pick $x_i\in\dom f_i$ for each $i$; lsc and convex as a sum of lsc convex functions of the blocks).
  • Separability is the whole point: for $f(x_1,x_2)=\abs{x_1-x_2}$ the prox is not blockwise. It moves the two coordinates towards each other: both become $\tfrac{x_1+x_2}2$ if $\abs{x_1-x_2}\le2$, otherwise each moves by $1$ towards the other. It is computed by the $f\circ L$ rule with $L=[1\ \ {-1}]$, $LL^*=2$ (last card of this section).

Proof T07 freeze all blocks but one

1. Split the objective. The product space carries the inner product $\ps xy=\sum_i\ps{x_i}{y_i}$, so for $y=(y_1,\dots,y_n)$, $f(y)+\tfrac12\norm{y-x}^2=\sum_{i=1}^n g_i(y_i)$ with $g_i=f_i+\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-x_i}^2$.

2. Minimize term by term. Each $g_i$ has a unique minimizer $p_i=\prox_{f_i}x_i$ (existence–uniqueness). For every $y$, $\sum_ig_i(y_i)\ge\sum_ig_i(p_i)$, with equality iff $g_i(y_i)=g_i(p_i)$ for all $i$, i.e. iff $y_i=p_i$ for all $i$ (uniqueness). Hence $(p_1,\dots,p_n)$ is the unique minimizer: $\prox_f x=(\prox_{f_i}x_i)_i$.

2′. Same thing through the characterization. $\partial f(p)=\partial f_1(p_1)\times\dots\times\partial f_n(p_n)$ (page 2), so $x-p\in\partial f(p)\iff(\forall i)\ x_i-p_i\in\partial f_i(p_i)\iff(\forall i)\ p_i=\prox_{f_i}x_i$.

Prox in an orthonormal basis; soft-thresholding for $\lambda\norm\cdot_1$

Part IV · slide 13SlidesKeyLab#
Statement

Let $\H$ be a separable Hilbert space with an orthonormal basis $(b_i)_{i\in I}$. For every $i\in I$ let $\varphi_i\in\Gz\R$ with $\varphi_i\ge0$. For every $x\in\H$, if $$f(x)=\sum_{i\in I}\varphi_i\big(\ps x{b_i}\big)\qquad\text{then}\qquad\prox_f(x)=\sum_{i\in I}\prox_{\varphi_i}\big(\ps x{b_i}\big)\,b_i .$$ Remark (slide 13): the assumption $(\forall i)\ \varphi_i\ge0$ can be relaxed if $\H$ is finite dimensional.

Example (slide 13): $\H=\R^N$, $(b_i)$ the canonical basis, $f=\lambda\norm\cdot_1$ with $\lambda\in[0,+\infty[$: $$\big(\forall x=(x^{(i)})_{1\le i\le N}\in\R^N\big)\qquad\prox_{\lambda\norm\cdot_1}(x)=\big(\prox_{\lambda\abs\cdot}(x^{(i)})\big)_{1\le i\le N}=\big(\sgn(x^{(i)})\max\{\abs{x^{(i)}}-\lambda,0\}\big)_{1\le i\le N}.$$

In one sentence

In the coordinates of an orthonormal basis, $f$ is separable and the norm is Euclidean (Parseval), so the prox is the separable rule applied coefficient by coefficient: analyze, threshold each coefficient, synthesize.

Why it is true

The analysis map $U:x\mapsto(\ps x{b_i})_{i\in I}$ is an isometry of $\H$ onto $\ell^2(I)$ (Parseval: $\norm x^2=\sum_i\abs{\ps x{b_i}}^2$), and $f=F\circ U$ with $F(\xi)=\sum_i\varphi_i(\xi_i)$ separable. Minimizing $f(y)+\tfrac12\norm{y-x}^2$ over $y$ is the same as minimizing $F(\eta)+\tfrac12\norm{\eta-\xi}^2$ over $\eta=Uy$ with $\xi=Ux$, and that splits into independent scalar problems $\varphi_i(\eta_i)+\tfrac12(\eta_i-\xi_i)^2$. So $\eta_i=\prox_{\varphi_i}\xi_i$ and $y=U^{-1}\eta=\sum_i\eta_ib_i$. In finite dimension this is exactly the separable card after a unitary change of variables. In infinite dimension the sum $\sum_i\varphi_i(\cdot)$ is a series: $\varphi_i\ge0$ makes it a series of nonnegative terms, so $f$ is well defined with values in $[0,+\infty]$, lsc and convex, and the interchange of "inf" and "sum" is legitimate (see the Extra callout). Say it out loud: analyze, threshold, synthesize.

What each hypothesis buys
  • Orthonormal basis: Parseval turns $\norm{y-x}^2$ into $\sum_i(\eta_i-\xi_i)^2$. For a non-orthogonal basis the quadratic couples the coefficients and the rule fails (that is why the next cards need $LL^*=\mu\Id$).
  • $\varphi_i\in\Gz\R$: each scalar prox exists.
  • $\varphi_i\ge0$ (infinite dimension): makes $f$ a well-defined element of $\Gz{\H}$ when it is proper, and justifies the termwise minimization of an infinite sum. In $\R^N$ the sum is finite and nothing needs to be checked.

Extra · why "$\varphi_i\ge0$" in infinite dimension

Extra When $I$ is infinite, $f(x)=\sum_i\varphi_i(\xi_i)$ is a series. If the $\varphi_i$ could be negative the series might diverge or depend on the summation order, $f$ might fail to be lsc, and "minimize the sum termwise" would require exchanging an infimum with an infinite sum, which is not automatic. With $\varphi_i\ge0$: the partial sums increase, so $f$ is well defined in $[0,+\infty]$, lsc and convex as a supremum of finite sums of lsc convex functions, and $\inf\sum=\sum\inf$ holds for nonnegative terms by monotone convergence. One also needs the candidate $\sum_i\prox_{\varphi_i}(\xi_i)b_i$ to converge in $\H$; Bauschke–Combettes' version assumes $\varphi_i\ge\varphi_i(0)=0$, which gives $\abs{\prox_{\varphi_i}\xi_i}\le\abs{\xi_i}$ (prox is nonexpansive and fixes $0$), hence square-summability. The slide's remark says what matters for the exam: in $\R^N$ the sign condition is not needed at all.

Theorem

Moreau decomposition: $\prox_{\gamma f^*}x=x-\gamma\prox_{\gamma^{-1}f}(\gamma^{-1}x)$

Part IV · slide 14SlidesKeyExam#
Statement

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space, $f\in\Gz{\H}$ and $\gamma\in\,]0,+\infty[$. Then $$(\forall x\in\H)\qquad\prox_{\gamma f^*}x=x-\gamma\,\prox_{\gamma^{-1}f}(\gamma^{-1}x).$$ With $\gamma=1$: $\prox_{f^*}=\Id-\prox_f$, i.e. $x=\prox_fx+\prox_{f^*}x$ for every $x$.

Example (slide 14): if $\H=\R^N$ and $f=\tfrac1q\norm\cdot_q^q$ with $q\in\,]1,+\infty[$, then $f^*=\tfrac1{q^*}\norm\cdot_{q^*}^{q^*}$ with $\tfrac1q+\tfrac1{q^*}=1$, and $$(\forall x\in\R^N)\qquad\prox_{\frac\gamma{q^*}\norm\cdot_{q^*}^{q^*}}x=x-\gamma\,\prox_{\frac1{\gamma q}\norm\cdot_q^q}(\gamma^{-1}x).$$

In one sentence

Because $\partial f^*=(\partial f)^{-1}$, the inclusion defining the prox of $f^*$ is the inclusion defining the prox of $f$ read backwards: you get the prox of the conjugate for free from the prox of $f$.

Why it is true

Start from $p=\prox_{\gamma f^*}x$, i.e. $\tfrac{x-p}\gamma\in\partial f^*(p)$. Fenchel–Young's equality case says $u\in\partial f^*(p)\iff p\in\partial f(u)$: the graph of $\partial f^*$ is the graph of $\partial f$ with the two coordinates swapped. So $p\in\partial f\big(\tfrac{x-p}\gamma\big)$. Now call $r=\tfrac{x-p}\gamma$ the other piece of $x$ ($x=p+\gamma r$): the inclusion $p\in\partial f(r)$ reads $\tfrac x\gamma-r\in\tfrac1\gamma\partial f(r)$, which is exactly "$r=\prox_{\gamma^{-1}f}(x/\gamma)$". The point $x$ is thus split as $x=p+\gamma r$, where the pair $(r,p)$ lies on the graph of $\partial f$ ($p\in\partial f(r)$, equivalently $(p,r)$ lies on the graph of $\partial f^*$), like the orthogonal decomposition $x=P_Vx+P_{V^\perp}x$ — and indeed for $f=\ind_V$ (so $f^*=\ind_{V^\perp}$) it is that decomposition. The sentence to say out loud: Moreau decomposition = swap the roles of $p$ and $x-p$ through $\partial f^*=(\partial f)^{-1}$.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $f\in\Gz{\H}$: $f^*\in\Gz{\H}$ (Fenchel–Moreau), so both proxes exist, and $\partial f^*=(\partial f)^{-1}$ holds (it needs $f=f^{**}$).
  • $\gamma\gt0$: $\gamma f^*\in\Gz{\H}$ and $\gamma^{-1}f\in\Gz{\H}$; the formula is written so that the scalings cancel ($(\gamma f)^*=\gamma f^*(\cdot/\gamma)$ is hidden in it).

Proposition

$\prox_{\sigma_C}=\Id-\PC$ and soft-thresholding on an interval

Part IV · slide 15SlidesKey#
Statement

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space and $C\subset\H$ nonempty closed convex. Then $$(\forall x\in\H)\qquad\prox_{\sigma_C}x=x-\PC x .$$ Soft-thresholding. $\H=\R$, $\delta_1=\inf C$, $\delta_2=\sup C$. For every $x\in\R$, $$\sigma_C(x)=\begin{cases}\delta_1x&\text{if }x\lt0\\0&\text{if }x=0\\\delta_2x&\text{if }x\gt0\end{cases}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\prox_{\sigma_C}(x)=\operatorname{soft}_C(x)=\begin{cases}x-\delta_1&\text{if }x\lt\delta_1\\0&\text{if }x\in C\\x-\delta_2&\text{if }x\gt\delta_2 .\end{cases}$$

In one sentence

$\sigma_C$ is the conjugate of $\ind_C$, so Moreau with $\gamma=1$ gives its prox as "identity minus projection"; on the line, the projection onto an interval is clipping, and identity minus clipping is soft-thresholding with two thresholds.

Why it is true

Page 2 established $\sigma_C=\ind_C^*$ and $\ind_C\in\Gz{\H}$. The Moreau decomposition with $f=\ind_C$ and $\gamma=1$ reads $\prox_{\ind_C^*}=\Id-\prox_{\ind_C}=\Id-\PC$. Geometrically $x-\PC x$ is the normal component of $x$ at its projection — for a vector space $V$ it is $P_{V^\perp}x$, and $\sigma_V=\ind_{V^\perp}$ confirms it. In 1-D a nonempty closed convex set is an interval $C=[\delta_1,\delta_2]\cap\R$ (one or both ends possibly infinite), $\sigma_C(x)=\sup_{y\in C}xy$ equals $x\delta_2$ for $x\gt0$ and $x\delta_1$ for $x\lt0$ (possibly $+\infty$), and $\PC$ clips to $[\delta_1,\delta_2]$; subtracting the clip from $x$ leaves $0$ inside and the overshoot outside. With $C=[-\lambda,\lambda]$ you recover $\sigma_C=\lambda\abs\cdot$ and the soft-thresholding of the power card — a second proof of it, by duality. Say it out loud: the prox of a support function is the identity minus the projection onto the set.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $C$ nonempty closed convex: $\ind_C\in\Gz{\H}$, so $\sigma_C=\ind_C^*\in\Gz{\H}$, $\ind_C^{**}=\ind_C$ and both proxes exist. For a nonconvex or non-closed $C$, $\sigma_C=\sigma_{\overline{\operatorname{conv}}C}$ and the formula holds with the closed convex hull.
  • $\delta_1,\delta_2$ may be infinite: then $\sigma_C$ takes the value $+\infty$ on a half-line and the corresponding branch of $\operatorname{soft}_C$ never occurs (e.g. $C=[0,+\infty[$: $\sigma_C=\ind_{]-\infty,0]}$, $\operatorname{soft}_C(x)=\min\{x,0\}$).

Proposition

Prox of $f\circ L$ when $LL^*=\mu\Id$

Part IV · slide 16SlidesKeyLab#
Statement

Let $\H$ and $\G$ be Hilbert spaces, $f\in\Gz{\H}$ and $L\in\B(\G,\H)$ such that $LL^*=\mu\Id$ with $\mu\in\,]0,+\infty[$. Then $$\prox_{f\circ L}=\Id-\mu^{-1}L^*\circ(\Id-\prox_{\mu f})\circ L .$$

In one sentence

$f\circ L$ only sees the component of $x$ along $V=\ran L^*$; on $V$ the operator $L$ is $\sqrt\mu$ times an isometry, so the prox acts as $\prox_{\mu f}$ transported by $L$, and leaves the $V^\perp$ component untouched.

Why it is true

Write $\G=V\oplus V^\perp$ with $V=\ran L^*=(\Ker L)^\perp$ and $P_V=\mu^{-1}L^*L$ (check: it is self-adjoint, idempotent, and the identity on $V$). The subgradient $x-p$ lies in $L^*\partial f(Lp)\subset V$, so $p$ and $x$ have the same $V^\perp$ component: that is the "$\Id-\ldots$" part. On $V$, apply $L$ to the inclusion: $Lx-Lp\in LL^*\partial f(Lp)=\mu\partial f(Lp)$, which says $Lp=\prox_{\mu f}(Lx)$: the prox in $\H$ with parameter $\mu$ — the factor $\mu$ is the price of $L$ not being an isometry. Pull back with $\mu^{-1}L^*$ to get the $V$ component of $p$, add the two components, and the formula falls out. The sentence to say out loud: decompose along $\ran L^*$ and its orthogonal; $L$ carries one piece to a $\prox_{\mu f}$, the other piece does not move.

What each hypothesis buys
  • $LL^*=\mu\Id$ does three jobs: (i) $L$ is onto $\H$ ($y=L(\mu^{-1}L^*y)$), which is the qualification condition for the chain rule $\partial(f\circ L)=L^*\partial f(L\cdot)$ and makes $f\circ L$ proper; (ii) $\mu^{-1}L^*L$ is the projection onto $\ran L^*$ (closed, because $L^*$ is $\sqrt\mu$ times an isometry); (iii) applying $L$ to $L^*u$ gives $\mu u$, which turns the inclusion in $\G$ into a prox in $\H$. Without it, $\prox_{f\circ L}$ has no closed form in general: $\norm{L\cdot}_1$ with a generic $L$ (total variation) must be handled by a dual or primal-dual algorithm (page 6).
  • $f\in\Gz{\H}$: $\prox_{\mu f}$ exists and $f\circ L\in\Gz{\G}$.
Hint

Proof T08 rewrite the inclusion (the slide's proof, with the justifications filled in)

1. $L$ is onto and $V=\ran L^*$ is closed. For $y\in\H$, $L(\mu^{-1}L^*y)=\mu^{-1}LL^*y=y$, so $\ran L=\H$. Also $\norm{L^*y}^2=\ps{LL^*y}y=\mu\norm y^2$: $L^*$ is $\sqrt\mu$ times an isometry, hence injective with closed range. Therefore $V=\ran L^*=\overline{\ran L^*}=(\Ker L)^\perp$ (page 1: $(\ran L^*)^\perp=\Ker L$).

2. $P_V=L^*(LL^*)^{-1}L=\mu^{-1}L^*L$. Set $Q=\mu^{-1}L^*L$ and fix $x\in\G$. First, $Qx=L^*(\mu^{-1}Lx)\in\ran L^*=V$. Second, for every $v=L^*y\in V$, using $LL^*=\mu\Id$: $\ps{x-Qx}{L^*y}=\ps{Lx-\mu^{-1}LL^*Lx}{y}=\ps{Lx-Lx}{y}=0$. So $Qx\in V$ and $x-Qx\perp V$, and the vector-space characterization of the projection ($V$ is closed by step 1) gives $Qx=P_Vx$. Hence $P_V=\mu^{-1}L^*L$, which is the slide's $L^*(LL^*)^{-1}L$, and $P_{V^\perp}=\Id-\mu^{-1}L^*L$.

3. Characterization and chain rule. $f\circ L\in\Gz{\G}$ (convex and lsc as a composition with a bounded linear map; proper since $\ran L=\H$ meets $\dom f$). For $x\in\G$, $p=\prox_{f\circ L}x\iff x-p\in\partial(f\circ L)(p)=L^*\partial f(Lp)$. The chain rule holds with equality by Moreau–Rockafellar applied to $0+f\circ L$ (the zero function on $\G$, $f$ on $\H$): its second qualification condition reads $\dom f\cap\inte\big(L(\dom0)\big)=\dom f\cap\inte(\ran L)=\dom f\cap\H=\dom f\neq\emptyset$, true because $\ran L=\H$. (The inclusion $\supset$ always holds, page 2.) In particular $x-p\in\ran L^*=V$.

4. The $V^\perp$ component of $p$. $P_{V^\perp}(x-p)=0$, hence $P_{V^\perp}p=P_{V^\perp}x=x-P_Vx=x-\mu^{-1}L^*Lx$.

5. The $V$ component of $p$. Write $x-p=L^*u$ with $u\in\partial f(Lp)$. Apply $L$: $Lx-Lp=LL^*u=\mu u\in\mu\,\partial f(Lp)=\partial(\mu f)(Lp)$. By the characterization in $\H$, $Lp=\prox_{\mu f}(Lx)$. Therefore $P_Vp=\mu^{-1}L^*Lp=\mu^{-1}L^*\prox_{\mu f}(Lx)$.

6. Add. $p=P_Vp+P_{V^\perp}p=\mu^{-1}L^*\prox_{\mu f}(Lx)+x-\mu^{-1}L^*Lx=x-\mu^{-1}L^*\big(Lx-\prox_{\mu f}(Lx)\big)=x-\mu^{-1}L^*(\Id-\prox_{\mu f})(Lx)$. Since $p=\prox_{f\circ L}x$ exists and is unique, the necessary conditions we derived identify it.

Solution

3. At infinity. 4. Reading for the lab. In Lab 1 the coefficients to denoise are wavelet details: a few large ones (edges) and many small ones (noise). $q\le\tfrac32$ kills the small ones and keeps the large ones almost intact — the right shape; $q\ge3$ does the opposite. This is why small $q$ wins on the mean square error in the lab, and why $q=1$ (the only one with an exact dead zone) gives exactly sparse estimates.

  • Existence and uniqueness for $f\in\Gz{\H}$, any $\gamma\gt0$: Fenchel–Young gives an affine minorant, the parabola makes the objective coercive and strictly convex.
  • Characterization: $p=\prox_{\gamma f}x\iff\frac{x-p}\gamma\in\partial f(p)$. Prox is the resolvent $(\Id+\gamma\partial f)^{-1}$, the implicit subgradient step; the subgradient is evaluated at the arrival point.
  • Implicit step vs explicit step: $\prox_{\gamma f}$ works for every $\gamma\gt0$ and for nonsmooth $f$; gradient descent needs $\gamma\lt2/\beta$ and a Lipschitz gradient.
  • $\prox_{\ind_C}=\PC$; $p=\PC x\iff p\in C$ and $\ps{y-p}{x-p}\le0$ for all $y\in C$; vector space: $x-p\in C^\perp$; $\env{\gamma}{\ind_C}=d_C^2/(2\gamma)$.
  • $\prox_{\chi\abs\cdot}=$ soft-thresholding $\sgn(x)\max\{\abs x-\chi,0\}$; $\prox_{\chi\abs\cdot^2}=x/(1+2\chi)$; other powers: solve $p+q\chi\abs p^{q-1}\sgn p=\xi$.
  • $\prox_{\frac\gamma2\norm{L\cdot-z}^2}=(\Id+\gamma L^*L)^{-1}(\cdot+\gamma L^*z)$: a quadratic gives a linear system, always invertible.
  • Calculus: translation $z+\prox_f(\cdot-z)$; quadratic perturbation $\prox_{f/(\alpha+1)}\big(\frac{\cdot-z}{\alpha+1}\big)$; scaling $\rho^{-1}\prox_{\rho^2f}(\rho\,\cdot)$; reflection $-\prox_f(-\cdot)$; envelope $\frac{\gamma x+\prox_{(1+\gamma)f}x}{1+\gamma}$.
  • Separable sums: prox blockwise. Orthonormal basis: analyze, prox each coefficient, synthesize; $\lambda\norm\cdot_1$ ⇒ componentwise soft-thresholding.
  • Moreau decomposition: $\prox_{\gamma f^*}x=x-\gamma\prox_{\gamma^{-1}f}(\gamma^{-1}x)$; $\gamma=1$: $\prox_{f^*}=\Id-\prox_f$. Proof: $\partial f^*=(\partial f)^{-1}$.
  • $\prox_{\sigma_C}=\Id-\PC$; on an interval, two-sided soft-thresholding $\operatorname{soft}_C$. Norms are support functions of dual balls: $\prox_{\lambda\norm\cdot}=\Id-P_{\lambda B^*}$.
  • $LL^*=\mu\Id$ ⇒ $\prox_{f\circ L}=\Id-\mu^{-1}L^*(\Id-\prox_{\mu f})L$; unitary $L$: $L^*\prox_fL$ (Lab 1); $L=$ sum of $m$ copies: $\mu=m$, consensus projection.
  • Monotonicity of $\partial f$ + characterization ⇒ $\prox$ is firmly nonexpansive (page 4): this is what makes every proximal algorithm converge.
  • The one method: to compute any prox, solve $x-p\in\partial g(p)$ (T08); to get the prox of a conjugate, use Moreau (T17); to absorb a quadratic, complete the square (T09).

Self-check

Eleven questions

Why is $\prox_f x$ unique, and what exactly do "proper, lsc, convex" each contribute?

$f+\tfrac12\norm{\cdot-x}^2$ is strictly convex (convex + strictly convex) and coercive (Fenchel–Young gives an affine minorant $f\ge\ps u\cdot-f^*(u)$, which a parabola beats), proper and lsc as $f$ is. Proper: the inf is $\lt+\infty$ and $f^*$ is proper. Lsc: the minimizing sequence has a weak limit that is a minimizer. Convex: strict convexity of the sum (uniqueness) and existence of the affine minorant.

State the characterization of $\prox_{\gamma f}$ and say in one sentence why it is an implicit step.

$p=\prox_{\gamma f}x\iff\frac{x-p}\gamma\in\partial f(p)$, i.e. $p=x-\gamma u$ with $u\in\partial f(p)$: the subgradient is taken at the point you land on, not at the point you start from. Equivalently $\prox_{\gamma f}=(\Id+\gamma\partial f)^{-1}$.

What is $\prox_{\gamma\ind_C}$ and why does $\gamma$ not appear in the answer?

$\PC$, for every $\gamma\gt0$: $\gamma\ind_C=\ind_C$ because $\gamma\cdot0=0$ and $\gamma\cdot(+\infty)=+\infty$. Minimizing $\tfrac1{2\gamma}\norm{y-x}^2$ over $C$ does not depend on $\gamma$.

Derive soft-thresholding from the characterization.

$\xi-p\in\chi\,\partial\abs\cdot(p)$: if $p\gt0$ then $\xi-p=\chi$, $p=\xi-\chi$ (needs $\xi\gt\chi$); if $p\lt0$ then $p=\xi+\chi$ (needs $\xi\lt-\chi$); if $p=0$ then $\xi\in[-\chi,\chi]$. Hence $p=\sgn(\xi)\max\{\abs\xi-\chi,0\}$.

$f=\tfrac\gamma2\norm{L\cdot-z}^2$: write $\prox_f$ and explain why the operator you invert is invertible even if $L$ is not injective.

$\prox_fx=(\Id+\gamma L^*L)^{-1}(x+\gamma L^*z)$. $\ps{(\Id+\gamma L^*L)p}{p}=\norm p^2+\gamma\norm{Lp}^2\ge\norm p^2$: the identity coming from the prox's own quadratic bounds the operator below, whatever $L$.

Give $\prox_g$ for $g=f+\tfrac\alpha2\norm\cdot^2+\ps z\cdot$ and check it on $f=0$.

$\prox_gx=\prox_{f/(\alpha+1)}\big(\frac{x-z}{\alpha+1}\big)$. For $f=0$: $\prox_g x=\frac{x-z}{\alpha+1}$; directly, $x-p=\alpha p+z$ gives the same.

State the Moreau decomposition with its parameters and prove it in three lines.

$\prox_{\gamma f^*}x=x-\gamma\prox_{\gamma^{-1}f}(\gamma^{-1}x)$. Proof: $p=\prox_{\gamma f^*}x\iff\frac{x-p}\gamma\in\partial f^*(p)\iff p\in\partial f(\frac{x-p}\gamma)$ (Fenchel–Young) $\iff\frac x\gamma-\frac{x-p}\gamma\in\partial(\gamma^{-1}f)(\frac{x-p}\gamma)\iff\frac{x-p}\gamma=\prox_{\gamma^{-1}f}(\frac x\gamma)$.

Why is $\prox_{\sigma_C}=\Id-\PC$, and what does it give for $C=[-\lambda,\lambda]$?

$\sigma_C=\ind_C^*$ and Moreau with $\gamma=1$: $\prox_{\ind_C^*}=\Id-\prox_{\ind_C}=\Id-\PC$. For $C=[-\lambda,\lambda]$, $\sigma_C=\lambda\abs\cdot$ and $x-P_Cx$ is soft-thresholding at $\lambda$.

Under which hypothesis on $L$ is $\prox_{f\circ L}$ explicit, what is the formula, and what does it become for an orthonormal wavelet transform?

$LL^*=\mu\Id$: $\prox_{f\circ L}=\Id-\mu^{-1}L^*(\Id-\prox_{\mu f})L$. For $L$ unitary ($\mu=1$, $L^*L=\Id$): $L^*\prox_fL$ — transform, threshold, inverse-transform (Lab 1).

In the proof of the composition rule, where exactly is $LL^*=\mu\Id$ used?

Three places: $\ran L=\H$ (qualification for $\partial(f\circ L)=L^*\partial f(L\cdot)$ and properness); $P_V=\mu^{-1}L^*L$ for $V=\ran L^*$ (idempotence uses $LL^*=\mu\Id$); and $L(L^*u)=\mu u$, which turns $x-p=L^*u$, $u\in\partial f(Lp)$ into $Lx-Lp\in\mu\partial f(Lp)$, i.e. $Lp=\prox_{\mu f}(Lx)$.

Why does the implicit scheme $x_{n+1}=x_n-\gamma\nabla f(x_{n+1})$ have no restriction on $\gamma$, in one sentence (to be proved on page 4)?

Because $x_{n+1}=\prox_{\gamma f}x_n$ and $\prox_{\gamma f}$ is firmly nonexpansive for every $\gamma\gt0$ (monotonicity of $\partial f$ through the characterization), so the iteration is Fejér-monotone and converges; on $\tfrac\beta2x^2$ the factor is $1/(1+\gamma\beta)\lt1$ versus $\abs{1-\gamma\beta}$ for the explicit step.
