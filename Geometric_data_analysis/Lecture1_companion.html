Lecture 1 is the map of the whole course. A question that comes back

What should the model keep, and what should it throw away?

Lecture 1 asks this question four times. In §1.1, a regression keeps 5 or 10 columns, or ties columns into groups, and throws away the freedom to fit noise. In §1.2, a radiologist keeps bundles of neighbouring pixels, never 5 isolated pixels. In §2.2, Kendall keeps the shape of a triangle and throws away its position, size and orientation — and what is left is a sphere. In §2.3, a convolutional network keeps local patterns at several scales; its failures show what it threw away. Every map of §2.1 does the same: it hides most of the territory, and a good map says so.

Earlier lectures: none — the question is posed here. It returns in every later lecture: which columns a metric ignores (L2, feature scaling), intrinsic versus extrinsic dimension (L3), which symmetries a network ignores (L4, orientation and sampling), which paths a metric makes short (L5 §2.3), and whether a distance between distributions ignores geometry (L6: total variation and KL do; optimal transport does not).

§1 AI = model + data p. 10–45 · fr. 10–28

The first part of the lecture explains why data alone do not make a predictor. When each patient is described by many numbers, the data leave too much freedom, and a model must remove most of it. §1.1 shows the danger on a table; §1.2 shows it on medical images, where the danger is extreme.

§1.1 The curse of dimensionality p. 10–21 · fr. 10–20

The problem: what is a dataset?

Page 11 draws the object that the whole course is about: a table with N subjects (rows) and D variables (columns), plus one extra column ♥ that we care about and want to predict. In the slide the variables are what four people eat (apple, banana, steak, chicken, wine, beer) and ♥ is a cardiac outcome. The slide defines the task:

Supervised learning = Regression. We look for a formula F(x1, …, xD) of the D variables that best approximates an important quantity (♥).slide p. 11 (fr. 10)

The simplest model: linear regression

Page 12 fits the simplest formula: one weight per column, a, b, …, f, and the prediction is the weighted sum. “We choose the weights a, b, …, f by minimizing a least squares error.” In symbols (not written on the slide): with \(x_i \in \R^\text{D}\) the row of patient \(i\), \(y_i\) its value of ♥, a weight vector \(w \in \R^\text{D}\) and an intercept \(b\),

\[ \text{F}(x) = \langle w, x\rangle + b, \] \[ (\hat w, \hat b) = \arg\min_{w,\,b}\; \sum_{i=1}^{\text{N}} \big(y_i - \langle w, x_i\rangle - b\big)^2 . \]

After centring the columns and ♥, the minimiser solves the normal equations \(X^\intercal X\, \hat w = X^\intercal y\), where \(X\) is the N×D table. This is the whole method.

When least squares is safe: astronomy

Page 13 recalls where least squares comes from: “First applications to astronomy, with hundreds of observations on a handful of variables.” The figure shows the six numbers that describe an orbit (inclination, longitude of the ascending node, argument of periapsis, true anomaly, plus size and eccentricity). With N ≫ D, each weight is pinned down by many equations, and measurement errors average out: the variance of each estimated weight shrinks like 1/N.

When it breaks: medicine

Medicine has the opposite shape: “With lots of information about few patients, we quickly ‘discover’ spurious correlations. This is known as overfitting.” (p. 14). The mechanism is linear algebra, not bad luck.

  • The fit must satisfy N equations \(\langle w, x_i\rangle + b = y_i\) with D + 1 unknowns.
  • If D + 1 ≥ N and the rows are in general position, the N×(D+1) system has rank N, so it has an exact solution for every vector \(y\).
  • The training error is then zero whatever ♥ is — even if ♥ is pure noise. A perfect fit carries no information.

The figure of page 14 shows this on the slide’s own table. Look closely at the colours (step “Spurious fit” above): the steak column c has exactly the same four colours as ♥. With four patients, one of six columns matching the outcome is not surprising — and a model that puts all its weight on steak fits perfectly.

Watch out

Pages 14 and 20 use the same picture (one heavy weight on column c): first as an example of overfitting, then as an example of a sparse model. The picture cannot tell them apart. What makes a sparse model a regularisation is how the column was chosen: with a penalty that grows with the number of candidates (as in the Lasso below), not by keeping whichever column fits best. Lab 2 measures the difference.

Twenty jelly beans (XKCD 882)

Pages 15–18 tell the same story as a comic. Scientists test whether jelly beans cause acne: no link (p > 0.05). Then they test 20 colours separately, one test each. Nineteen show nothing; green gives p < 0.05, and the newspaper prints “Green jelly beans linked to acne! 95% confidence. Only 5% chance of coincidence!”

The error is in the last sentence. A test at level α = 0.05 has a 5% chance of a false alarm each time it is run. With m independent tests and no real effect, the chance of at least one false alarm is

\[ \mathbb{P}(\text{at least one } p \lt \alpha) \;=\; 1 - (1-\alpha)^m \;=\; 1 - 0.95^{20} \;\approx\; 64\% . \]

Each extra column of a table is one more jelly-bean colour: one more chance for noise to look like signal. The slide draws the conclusion (p. 19):

Having access to more patients is usually a good thing. But getting more information about each patient is very dangerous. […] Machine learning is about doing reliable statistics in this dangerous setting.slide p. 19 (fr. 18)
Lab 1

Twenty jelly beans

Question. If no colour has any effect, how often does at least one colour come out “significant” — and what do a correction and a shared control group change?

Each bar is the p-value of one colour in one simulated study, drawn as −log₁₀ p: the higher the bar, the smaller p. Bars above the horizontal line are “significant”. Focus the chart and use ← → to read values.

What the lab shows (default settings, 20,000 simulated studies, seed 882)
  • With 20 colours, 30 volunteers per group and α = 0.05, 64.5% ± 0.3% of the studies find at least one “significant” colour. The formula \(1-0.95^{20}\) gives 64.15%: the simulation agrees within its error bar.
  • The Bonferroni correction (test each colour at 0.05/20) brings the rate to 5.0% (formula: 4.88%).
  • Comparing every colour with one shared control group makes the 20 tests positively correlated. The rate drops to 42.2% — still more than eight times the nominal 5%. The formula \(1-(1-\alpha)^m\) assumes independent tests.
  • Under the null hypothesis the p-values are uniform: in 20,000 single tests, 4.89% fall below 0.05. The sample size n does not change the false-alarm rate; it only changes the power to detect a real effect.

The two remedies of the slides

“We must regularize our decision rules” (p. 20–21). Regularising means restricting the set of formulas F that the fit may choose, so that fewer of them can fit noise. The slides give two ways.

  • Sparsity (p. 20): “A sparse model will select 5 or 10 important columns. This is useful to handle tabular data (XGBoost…) or identify sources in signal processing (Lasso…).” The Lasso adds the sum of the absolute weights to the least-squares error; the penalty sets most weights exactly to zero:
    \[ \hat w = \arg\min_{w,\,b}\; \frac{1}{2\text{N}} \sum_{i=1}^{\text{N}} \big(y_i - \langle w, x_i\rangle - b\big)^2 + \alpha\, \|w\|_1, \]
    where \(\|w\|_1 = |w_1| + \dots + |w_\text{D}|\) and α ≥ 0 sets the strength of the penalty.
  • Domain structure (p. 21): “A structured model will leverage the geometry of the data. Think about the main food groups or the ATC classification for medical drugs.” In the figure (step “Structure” above), the weights inside each group have the same colour: fruits share one weight, meats another, drinks a third. That is the model
    \[ \text{F}(x) = \sum_{g=1}^{\text{G}} \beta_g\, \bar x_g + b, \qquad \bar x_g = \frac{1}{|g|}\sum_{j \in g} x_j , \]
    with G parameters instead of D, whatever the number of columns.

Both are priors: assumptions about the answer that the data cannot check by themselves. Sparsity bets that few columns matter. Structure bets that the groups are right. Neither is free: each helps exactly when its bet is true.

Lab 2

Noise that looks like signal

Question. With N patients and D random columns, how well does each model of the slides fit the training patients, and how well does it predict new ones — when ♥ is pure noise, when one column matters, and when one food group matters?

Curves: median over 30 simulated datasets, R² measured on 500 new patients (test) or on the N training patients (train). R² = 1 is a perfect prediction, 0 is no better than predicting the mean, negative is worse. Values below −1 are marked ▼ at the bottom. Focus the chart and use ← → to read values.

What the lab shows (N = 20, σ = 1, 30 datasets per point, seed 2024)
  • Least squares fits noise perfectly. With pure noise, the training R² of least squares reaches 1.000 at D = 19 = N − 1 and stays there. The test R² is −0.51 at D = 6, −73.9 at D = 19, then climbs back to −0.22 at D = 120. The slide setting (button “N = 4, D = 6”) gives a training R² of 1.000 and a median test R² of −1.01.
  • The jelly-bean scientist (keep the best single column, no penalty) shows a training R² of 0.13 at D = 6 and 0.32 at D = 120 on pure noise, with a negative test R² (−0.16 and −0.34). The reason is in the readout line on maximal correlations: the best of 120 noise columns correlates at 0.59 (median) with a noise ♥ on 20 patients, and with 4 patients the best of 6 columns already reaches 0.90.
  • The Lasso refuses to fit noise. With α = sd(♥)·√(2 ln D / N), it selects no column in 80% of the datasets at D = 6 and keeps its test R² near 0 (−0.02) at every D.
  • Each prior works when its bet is true. When ♥ depends on one column, the Lasso keeps a positive test R² (0.24 at D = 8, 0.23 at D = 15) while the group model fails (−0.08 at D = 15); the unpenalised best column does even better (0.44 at D = 15), because the Lasso also shrinks the true weight. When ♥ depends on one food group, the group model scores between 0.34 and 0.42 for every D ≥ 6 (the best possible is 0.50 at σ = 1), while the Lasso stays between −0.03 and 0.02 for D ≥ 10.
Key idea

When D is comparable to N or larger, the data admit many formulas that fit perfectly. A model is useful only if it forbids most of them. Which ones to forbid — few columns, tied columns, smooth functions — is a choice about the problem, not something the data can decide.

Beyond the slides

The recovery of least squares after D = N − 1 in Lab 2 is the “double descent” curve. When D > N − 1 there are infinitely many exact fits and the solver returns the one with the smallest norm; as D grows this solution spreads its weight over more columns and becomes smaller, so its predictions become more cautious. It still never beats predicting the mean when ♥ is noise. The Lasso penalty used in the lab is the classical “universal” threshold σ√(2 ln D / N): the largest correlation between D noise columns and a noise outcome is about that size, so the Lasso rarely lets noise in. Cross-validating α would shrink less and select more.

Watch out
  • “Supervised learning = Regression” is a simplification: classification is supervised too. Lecture 6 makes the two agree by predicting a probability for each class.
  • “95% confidence, only 5% chance of coincidence” is the classical misreading of a p-value. p < 0.05 means: if there were no effect, data this extreme would appear in less than 5% of studies. It is not the probability that the finding is a coincidence.
  • More columns are dangerous for an unconstrained model. With a suitable prior, extra informative columns help — the rest of the course is about such priors.

§1.2 A first example: medical imaging p. 22–45 · fr. 21–28

The problem: an image is a huge row

“A medical image is a massive lump of data” (p. 23). If each pixel is a column, a 3D scan is one row with millions of entries: “We observe millions to billions of variables on cohorts of a few thousand patients.” This is §1.1 with D ≫ N, pushed to the extreme.

Sampling the space of images is impossible

Page 24 counts. Allow 5 values per number. One number gives 5 possible samples, two numbers give 5² = 25, and a 128×128 image gives 5128·128 = 516,384, a number with 11,452 digits. “The set of all 2D/3D images is way too large to be sampled with a satisfying accuracy.”

The same count, read the other way: with N samples spread evenly in D dimensions, the grid step you can afford along each axis is \(\text{N}^{-1/\text{D}}\) of the range. For a cohort of N = 1,000 patients this is 0.001 in one dimension, 0.032 in two, 0.50 in ten and 0.9996 for a 128×128 image — no resolution at all. Averaging neighbouring samples, the basis of all local methods, needs neighbours; in high dimension there are none.

We cannot rely on sparsity

Page 25 shows a chest CT with five green pixels: “A good radiology exam does not rely exclusively on 5 or 10 pixels. We must learn how to group pixels in relevant bundles.” The reason is geometric. An organ is spread over thousands of pixels, and it moves from one patient to the next. No single pixel is reliably inside or outside it, so no small set of pixels carries its size or shape. Lab 3 tests this claim on synthetic images where the truth is known.

Lab 3

Five pixels are not enough

Question. To predict the area of a bright disc (an “organ”) from a 24×24 image, which works: 5 well-chosen pixels, least squares on all 576 pixels, or a structured model that adds all the pixels together? How does the answer depend on the number of training images and on whether the organ moves?

Six training images. Green squares: the k pixels chosen by the sparse model (orthogonal matching pursuit).

What the lab shows (σ = 0.3, k = 5, 300 test images; the learning curve is the median over 10 training sets per N)
  • Sparsity fails when the organ moves. In the default view (N = 20, moving organ) the 5-pixel model has a test R² of −0.125: worse than predicting the mean area. Its learning curve never passes 0.49, even with 300 images. When the organ is always centred, the same model reaches 0.81 at N = 20 and 0.86 at N = 300: a few pixels suffice only if they always sit on the same part of the organ.
  • Grouping pixels needs almost no data. The one-weight model “sum of all pixels” scores 0.965 in the default view and 0.943 with only 5 training images.
  • Honest surprise: least squares on all 576 pixels is not hopeless, even with far fewer images than pixels. Its minimum-norm solution spreads weight over many pixels — an implicit grouping. It scores 0.807 in the default view, and its learning curve reaches 0.962 at N = 150. But it needs many more images than the sum: 0.253 at N = 5, 0.765 at N = 20.

§2.1 The map is not the territory p. 46–51 · fr. 29–33

The problem

Every model is wrong in some way. Page 47 shows six maps of the world and of Paris — Google Maps, the RATP metro map, a Météo France chart, Anaximander’s disc, the Mercator and the Winkel projections — next to a man who asks “How can I trust these pictures?”. The title gives the answer: “A model is like a map: a warped and partial view of the world.” We can trust a map if we know what it keeps and how it warps the rest.

A map is not the territory it represents, but, if correct, it has a similar structure to the territory, which accounts for its usefulness.Alfred Korzybski, 1933 — slide p. 48

Page 49 quotes Borges’s one-paragraph story On exactitude in science: cartographers make “a map of the empire whose size was that of the empire, and which coincided point for point with it”, and the next generations find “that vast map was useless”. A model as detailed as the data is the data. It explains nothing, just as the least-squares fit with D ≥ N − 1 in Lab 2 reproduces every training patient and predicts nothing.

Why no map can be exact: a precise version of the slogan

The world maps of page 47 are the cleanest example of the four rules, because their distortions can be measured. A flat map of the sphere cannot preserve both angles and areas: Gauss proved in 1827 (Theorema Egregium) that curvature is intrinsic, so the curved sphere cannot be laid flat without stretching. Every projection chooses what to sacrifice. Tissot’s indicatrix makes the choice visible: draw a small circle on the globe, and look at its image on the map. The image is an ellipse; its semi-axes a ≥ b are the local stretching factors.

\[ \text{area distortion} = a\,b, \] \[ \text{maximal angular distortion } 2\omega = 2\arcsin\frac{a-b}{a+b} . \]

For the Mercator projection, with longitude λ and latitude φ, \(x = \lambda\) and \(y = \ln\tan(\pi/4 + \varphi/2)\). Both scale factors equal \(1/\cos\varphi\): circles stay circles (a = b, the map is conformal, which is why sailors used it) but their area is multiplied by \(1/\cos^2\varphi\): 4 at 60° and 33 at 80° of latitude.

Lab 4

What does each world map distort?

Question. The maps of page 47 all show the same territory. What does each projection keep, what does it sacrifice, and by how much?

What the lab shows (Natural Earth 1:110m coastlines; ellipses every 30°)
  • Mercator keeps angles and sacrifices areas. Every ellipse is a circle (2ω = 0) but areas grow like 1/cos²φ: ×1.333 at 30°, ×4.000 at 60°, ×33.16 at 80°. Greenland (2.19 million km² in these polygons) and DR Congo (2.33 million km²) have almost the same area, yet on the Mercator map Greenland covers 15.4 times the area of DR Congo.
  • Lambert keeps areas and sacrifices angles. Every ellipse has area 1, and the Greenland/Congo ratio is the true 0.94, but the angular distortion reaches 73.7° at 60° of latitude: shapes are squashed flat near the poles.
  • Winkel tripel compromises. Neither angles nor areas are exact (2ω = 11.5° on the equator, area ×1.24 at 60°), but no error is extreme: Greenland appears 2.1 times larger than DR Congo. This is why it is used for general-purpose world maps. The equirectangular map is worse on both counts (Greenland ×3.6, 2ω = 38.9° at 60°).
Key idea

A model is chosen for a task, not for being “right”. Mercator is the right map to steer a ship and the wrong map to compare countries. The honest move is to state which one you drew — the same holds for a sparse model, a kernel or a network.

§2.2 Example 1: the sphere of triangles p. 52–68 · fr. 34–49

The problem: menhirs and ley lines

The story starts with standing stones (p. 53). Page 54 maps “52 Menhir locations” in the Land’s End peninsula, in Cornwall: “Can you see alignments here? Some people do. Many authors have claimed that these ley lines demarcate ‘Earth energies’ and/or serve as guides for alien spacecraft.” To decide whether the alignments are real, one needs to know how many to expect by chance. In 1974 this led David Kendall to a precise question (p. 55):

Assuming that I draw 52 points at random in a square… How many flat triangles (say, with a 180° ± 1° angle) am I going to observe?slide p. 55 (fr. 36)

A triangle is “flat” when its largest angle is close to 180°, that is, when its three vertices are nearly aligned. Answering the question requires a probability distribution on the set of triangle shapes, and hence a clear idea of what that set is. The slide lists the papers that followed — The diffusion of shape (Kendall, 1977), Alignments in two-dimensional random sets of points (Kendall & Kendall, 1980), Simulating the ley hunter (Broadbent, 1980), Shape manifolds, Procrustean metrics, and complex projective spaces (Kendall, 1984) — “and the birth of modern shape analysis.”

Step 1: forget position, size and orientation

Page 56 (fly wings from [Kli15]) shows the standard preprocessing of shape analysis, Procrustes superimposition: (a) scale to the same size, (b) shift to the same position, (c) rotate to the same orientation. What is left after removing these three is the shape. Formally, two triangles have the same shape when a direct similarity — a map \(z \mapsto a z + b\) of the plane, identified with \(\CC\), with \(a \in \CC\setminus\{0\}\) and \(b \in \CC\) — sends one onto the other, vertex by vertex. Here \(|a|\) is the scaling, \(\arg a\) the rotation and \(b\) the translation. “Direct” means that reflections are not allowed.

Step 2: the space of shapes is two-dimensional

A triangle (A, B, C) has 6 real coordinates; direct similarities have 4 real parameters (2 for \(a\), 2 for \(b\)); so shapes form a space of dimension 6 − 4 = 2. Page 57 makes this concrete: if A ≠ B, there is exactly one direct similarity that sends A to (−1, 0) and B to (+1, 0). It sends C to the point

\[ z \;=\; \frac{2\,\text{C} - \text{A} - \text{B}}{\text{B} - \text{A}} \;\in\; \CC , \]

which records the shape: it is the position of C seen from the midpoint of AB, in units of half of AB. The triangles with A = B ≠ C correspond to the extra point \(z = \infty\). The slide: “Identify z ∈ ℂ ∪ {∞} with all non-degenerate triangle shapes.” In this picture the equilateral triangles are \(z = \pm i\sqrt3\) (the sign gives the orientation), the flat triangles are the real axis, the triangles that are isosceles with apex C (|CA| = |CB|) are the imaginary axis, and those isosceles with apex A or B are the circles \(|z+1| = 2\) and \(|z-1| = 2\).

Step 3: a clever change of coordinates gives a sphere

The plane of \(z\) distorts the geometry: equilateral triangles are two points, but the point at infinity plays a special role that no triangle deserves. Page 58 announces: “Up to a clever change of coordinates: this is actually a sphere!” The two poles are the direct and indirect equilateral triangles (p. 59) and the equator is the set of flat triangles (p. 60). The slides do not give the change of coordinates (“It has an OK-ish expression using cos and sin”, p. 62). Here is one, in algebraic form.

Beyond the slides — an explicit Kendall embedding

Centre the triangle with the orthonormal Helmert coordinates

\[ u = \frac{\text{B}-\text{A}}{\sqrt2}, \qquad v = \frac{2\text{C}-\text{A}-\text{B}}{\sqrt6}, \] \[ |u|^2 + |v|^2 = \sum_{\text{X}\in\{\text{A},\text{B},\text{C}\}} |\text{X}-\text{G}|^2 , \]

where G is the centroid. Then set

\[ \begin{aligned} \textbf{K}(\text{A},\text{B},\text{C}) &= (k_1, k_2, k_3) \in \R^3, \\ k_1 &= \frac{|u|^2-|v|^2}{2\,(|u|^2+|v|^2)}, \\ k_2 &= \frac{\operatorname{Re}(\bar u\, v)}{|u|^2+|v|^2}, \qquad k_3 = \frac{\operatorname{Im}(\bar u\, v)}{|u|^2+|v|^2} . \end{aligned} \]

A direct similarity multiplies both \(u\) and \(v\) by the same complex number \(a\), so K does not change: K depends only on the shape. One checks that \(\|\textbf{K}\| = 1/2\) always: the image is the sphere of centre 0 and diameter 1, as on page 62. In terms of the slide’s coordinate, \(v/u = z/\sqrt3\), and K is the inverse stereographic projection of \(z/\sqrt3\). The third coordinate has a simple meaning: it equals \(2\sqrt3\,\mathcal{A}/(a^2+b^2+c^2)\), where \(\mathcal{A}\) is the signed area of the triangle and a, b, c its side lengths. It is 0 exactly for flat triangles, and it reaches ±1/2 exactly for equilateral ones, because \(a^2+b^2+c^2 \ge 4\sqrt3\,|\mathcal{A}|\) with equality only for equilateral triangles (Weitzenböck’s inequality). This is the Hopf map from the unit sphere of \(\CC^2\) to the 2-sphere; the author’s own notes use the equivalent coordinates \(\xi = v/u\), \(|\xi| = \tan(\theta/2)\).

First properties: symmetries (p. 61)

“This representation respects the main symmetries of the set of triangles”:

  • “The sets of isoceles triangles with respect to A, B and C correspond to three great circles that are equally spaced with each other.”
  • “Axial symmetries correspond to a North-South inversion across the Equator.”
  • “The Equator of flat triangles + the meridians of isoceles triangles cut the sphere in 12 pieces. These exactly correspond to the 6 permutations of the vertices ABC × { the identity or an axial symmetry }.”

The deeper reason: relabelling the vertices or reflecting the plane changes the triangle but not its geometry, so it must act on the shape space by a symmetry of that space. In the sphere picture these actions are rotations and reflections of the sphere — the sphere is the representation in which the symmetries of triangles become symmetries of the space.

Metric properties: the sphere measures Procrustes distances (p. 62)

The key result of the lecture compares two triangles, (A, B, C) in red and (D, E, F) in blue. First move the red triangle by the similarity S that brings it as close as possible to the blue one, in the least-squares sense; the remaining error is the Procrustes distance. The slide states that this error is a chord of Kendall’s sphere:

\[ \min_{\SIM{\text{similarity S}}} \| \SIM{\text{S}(\ABC{\text{A}})} - \DEF{\text{D}} \|^2_{\mathbb{R}^\text{2}} + \| \SIM{\text{S}(\ABC{\text{B}})} - \DEF{\text{E}} \|^2_{\mathbb{R}^\text{2}} + \| \SIM{\text{S}(\ABC{\text{C}})} - \DEF{\text{F}} \|^2_{\mathbb{R}^\text{2}} \] \[ =~ \text{Var}(\DEF{\text{D},\text{E},\text{F}}) \,\cdot\, \| \textbf{K}(\ABC{\text{A},\text{B},\text{C}}) - \textbf{K}(\DEF{\text{D},\text{E},\text{F}})\|^2_{\mathbb{R}^\text{3}} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \min_{\SIM{\text{similarity S}}}\; & \| \SIM{\text{S}(\ABC{\text{A}})} - \DEF{\text{D}} \|^2_{\mathbb{R}^\text{2}} \\ {}+{} & \| \SIM{\text{S}(\ABC{\text{B}})} - \DEF{\text{E}} \|^2_{\mathbb{R}^\text{2}} \\ {}+{} & \| \SIM{\text{S}(\ABC{\text{C}})} - \DEF{\text{F}} \|^2_{\mathbb{R}^\text{2}} \end{aligned} \] \[ =~ \text{Var}(\DEF{\text{D},\text{E},\text{F}}) \,\cdot\, \| \textbf{K}(\ABC{\text{A},\text{B},\text{C}}) - \textbf{K}(\DEF{\text{D},\text{E},\text{F}})\|^2_{\mathbb{R}^\text{3}} \]

with K the Kendall embedding from “the set of non-degenerate triangles to the sphere of center (0, 0, 0) and diameter 1”, \(\textbf{K} : (\text{A},\text{B},\text{C}) \in \mathbb{R}^{\text{3}\times\text{2}} \backslash \{\text{A}=\text{B}=\text{C}\} \mapsto \textbf{K}(\text{A},\text{B},\text{C}) \in \mathbb{R}^\text{3}\). In words: “The chord distance on the sphere of Kendall corresponds to the Euclidean distance on triplets of points in the plane, up to similarities.”

Why it works: a three-line derivation

Use the Helmert coordinates of the box above: \(p = (u_\text{P}, v_\text{P})\) for (A, B, C) and \(q = (u_\text{Q}, v_\text{Q})\) for (D, E, F), two vectors of \(\CC^2\), with the Hermitian product \(\langle p, q\rangle = \bar u_\text{P} u_\text{Q} + \bar v_\text{P} v_\text{Q}\).

  1. The best translation matches the centroids, and the Helmert map is an isometry on centred triangles, so the error of S becomes \(\|a\,p - q\|^2\) for some \(a \in \CC\).
  2. This is a one-dimensional complex least-squares problem. Its minimum is \(\|q\|^2 - |\langle p,q\rangle|^2/\|p\|^2 = \|q\|^2 \sin^2\rho\), where \(\cos\rho = |\langle p,q\rangle|/(\|p\|\,\|q\|)\) and \(\rho \in [0, \pi/2]\).
  3. For the Hopf map, \(\textbf{K}(\text{P})\cdot\textbf{K}(\text{Q}) = \tfrac14\cos 2\rho\), so \(\|\textbf{K}(\text{P})-\textbf{K}(\text{Q})\|^2 = \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 - \tfrac12\cos 2\rho = \sin^2\rho\).

Hence the minimum equals \(\|q\|^2\,\|\textbf{K}(\text{P})-\textbf{K}(\text{Q})\|^2\), with \(\|q\|^2 = |\text{D}-\text{G}|^2 + |\text{E}-\text{G}|^2 + |\text{F}-\text{G}|^2\) and G the centroid of (D, E, F). Two by-products: the Procrustes angle ρ is the great-circle distance on the sphere of diameter 1 (an arc of angle 2ρ on a circle of radius 1/2), and the largest possible Procrustes angle, π/2, is reached between a shape and its mirror when both are equilateral — the two poles.

Watch out — two conventions hidden in the slide’s formula
  • “Var” is a sum, not a mean. The identity holds with Var(D, E, F) = |D − G|² + |E − G|² + |F − G|², the sum of squared distances to the centroid. With the usual variance (the mean of these three terms) the right-hand side is 3 times too small. Lab 6 checks this on 10,000 random pairs: the ratio of the two sides is exactly 3.000000000 with the mean, and 1 with the sum (largest relative error 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁴).
  • “Similarity” means direct similarity. If reflections are allowed, the minimum can only decrease, and the identity fails: in the same 10,000 pairs, a reflection gives a strictly smaller error in 49.8% of the cases. With reflections, the shape space is the sphere folded across the equator — a hemisphere — and the distance is the chord to the nearer of K(D, E, F) and its mirror image.
  • The slide spells “isoceles”; the usual spelling is “isosceles”.

Statistical properties: Kendall’s theorem (p. 63–65)

Pages 63–65 draw the three vertices at random from a Gaussian distribution in the plane and show the histogram of the resulting shapes on the sphere.

  • Isotropic Gaussian (p. 63): the histogram is flat — every shape is equally likely. This is Kendall’s theorem: if A, B, C are independent standard Gaussians in the plane, K(A, B, C) is uniformly distributed on the sphere. The proof is short: the Helmert coordinates \((u, v)\) are then independent standard complex Gaussians, so \((u, v)/\|(u,v)\|\) is uniform on the unit sphere of \(\CC^2 \simeq \R^4\), and the Hopf map sends the uniform measure on that sphere to the uniform measure on the 2-sphere.
  • Non-isotropic Gaussians (p. 64–65): stretching the cloud horizontally makes flat triangles more likely (the equator gets brighter) and equilateral ones rarer (the poles get darker). The density depends only on the latitude.
Beyond the slides — exact densities for stretched Gaussians

\[ \text{density on the equator} = \frac{r^2+1}{2r}, \] \[ \text{density at the poles} = \frac{4r^2}{(r^2+1)^2} . \]

Summary and use in software (p. 66–68)

Kendall showed that the space of triangles is best understood as a sphere for topological, geometric and statistical reasons. You cannot “unsee” this elegant result. Most importantly, his theorem showed that shapes naturally belong to a curved geometric space.slide p. 66 (fr. 47)

The three reasons: topological — the space of shapes, point at infinity included, is a closed surface without boundary, a sphere; geometric — the chord (or arc) of the sphere is the Procrustes distance, and the symmetries of triangles are rotations of the sphere; statistical — random Gaussian triangles are uniform on it.

Pages 67–68 show what the idea became. Kilian, Mitra and Pottmann (2007) compute geodesics — shortest paths — in a space of shapes, which gives natural interpolations between poses of an elephant or a skeleton. Von Radziewsky et al. (2016) compute barycentric interpolation in a space of hands: a weighted average of six hand poses, taken in the curved shape space rather than coordinate by coordinate.

When it fails, and how it is used

  • More points, same idea. For k landmarks in the plane the shape space is the complex projective space \(\CC P^{k-2}\), of real dimension 2k − 4 (Kendall, 1984); only for k = 3 is it a sphere. In 3D, a rotation can turn a planar triangle over, so triangles in space form a hemisphere (the sphere folded along the equator of flat triangles); for four or more points in 3D the shape space has singular points at the collinear configurations.
  • Landmarks must correspond. The construction needs labelled points (A, B, C). Surfaces and images have no natural labels; Lecture 6 compares shapes as distributions of points to get “an invariance to resamplings” (L6 §1.5; L6 Lab 7 measures this invariance).
  • Degenerate shapes. K is undefined when A = B = C, and nearly flat triangles are fragile: a tiny move of the middle vertex flips the orientation, i.e. jumps across the equator.
  • In practice, Procrustes superimposition is the first step of geometric morphometrics (the fly wings of p. 56); statistics are then done on the curved space, or in a tangent plane at the mean shape. The Python library GeomStats, used in the Lecture 5 lab session, implements Kendall shape spaces.
Key idea

Throwing away what should not matter (position, size, orientation) does not leave a flat space: it leaves a curved one, here a sphere. Working in that space makes distances, symmetries and probabilities simple. This model has no parameter to fit — its structure comes entirely from symmetry.

The problem: group neighbouring pixels, at every scale

Page 70 brings back the three levels of §1.2: “Let’s talk about the first way of grouping pixels with their neighbors” — the small square window around each pixel. §1.2 showed that a model of images must group pixels; the question is how to do it cheaply, everywhere in the image, and at several scales at once.

Filtering, also known as the convolution product (p. 71–78)

Convolution (i.e. weighted average of the neighboring pixels): Cheap generalization of the product “a · x”, parameterized by the coefficients of a small filter φ.slide p. 71 (fr. 51)

Lecture 4 writes the formula (L4 §1.2, slide p. 20): for an image \(\text{x}\) and a small filter \(\varphi\),

\[ (\varphi \star \text{x})\,[\,\text{i}, \text{j}\,] \;=\; \sum_{\text{k},\text{l}} \varphi\,[\,\text{k}, \text{l}\,]\cdot \text{x}\,[\,\text{i}-\text{k}, \text{j}-\text{l}\,] . \]

“Generalization of the product”: if φ has a single non-zero coefficient a at the centre, φ ⋆ x = a·x. Otherwise each output pixel mixes a few neighbours with fixed weights. Four properties make convolutions the workhorse of imaging:

  • Linear in x, like a product.
  • Translation-equivariant: shifting the image shifts the output by the same amount. The same weights are used at every position, so a pattern is detected wherever it appears.
  • Local: a 5×5 filter looks at 25 pixels. This is the grouping of neighbours that §1.2 asked for.
  • Cheap: a K×K filter costs K² multiply–adds per pixel, and the same weights serve everywhere, so a filter has K² parameters instead of one per pixel.

A multi-scale prior: wavelets (p. 79)

An iconic application: Deep Art (p. 82–98)

Take a painting (the “style”, a cubist picture) and a photo (the “content”, a cat). Encode both with the same CNN, then build a new image whose features are μ of the painting and m, M of the cat (overlay 7, p. 88), or μ, m of the painting and M of the cat (overlay 8, p. 89). The result is the cat painted in the style of the picture; ten other styles follow (p. 90–98). The more scales come from the painting, the stronger the stylisation: overlay 8 is much more cubist than overlay 7.

Why this works: the small-scale features μ describe textures — local patterns of colour and stroke — and are nearly independent of the layout, while the large-scale features M describe where things are. Style lives in the fine scales, content in the coarse ones.

Beyond the slides

The Deep Art images come from the algorithm of Gatys, Ecker and Bethge (2015), which the cited paper [NN16] explores. It does not decode features directly: it starts from noise and runs gradient descent on the pixels to match the Gram matrices (correlations between feature channels, averaged over positions) of the painting in several layers, and the raw features of the photo in one deep layer. Averaging over positions is what throws away the layout and keeps the texture — the statistics that Lab 10 measures. The slide’s μ/m/M is a simplification of this.

The dream application, and its limits (p. 99–100)

The images of page 100 are from Deep neural networks are easily fooled: High confidence predictions for unrecognizable images (Nguyen, Yosinski & Clune, 2015): regular patterns that a network classifies with high confidence as a king penguin, a starfish, a baseball, an electric guitar, a freight car, a remote control, a peacock or an African grey parrot. Each contains the right local patterns — the stripes of a penguin, the stitches of a baseball — with none of the global structure. The network trusts its texture detectors; the “object” features M are built from them and inherit their blind spots.

When it fails, and how it is used

  • It works when the task is decided by local patterns and plenty of labelled images exist: texture analysis, segmentation, detection (the uses listed on p. 42).
  • It fails when the decision needs global structure or geometry that the training set does not constrain: fooling images, shortcuts learned from scanner artefacts, sensitivity to small shifts once subsampling breaks equivariance (Lab 9).
  • It needs a grid. Convolution uses the ordered neighbourhoods of a pixel grid (p. 42: “Ordered memory structure. Explicit neighborhoods. Fast convolutions.”). Point clouds, meshes and graphs — levels 2 and 3 of §1.2 — have no such structure; Lecture 4 is about what replaces it.
Watch out
  • The page-81 claim “translation-equivariant” holds for convolutions, not for whole CNNs: subsampling (stride) keeps equivariance only for shifts that are multiples of the stride (Lab 9), and the final classifier is meant to be invariant, not equivariant.
  • In deep-learning libraries “convolution” usually computes a correlation (no flip of φ). For learned filters this does not matter; for the derivative filters of Lab 9 it flips the sign of the signed output.
  • The third blur filter of the slides is slightly asymmetric: one coefficient that should be 0.3 (by symmetry) is 0.2, visible in filter_3.png. It makes no visible difference to the output.
  • μ, m, M are the slide’s shorthand for “features from early, middle and late layers”; they are not specific quantities defined in the cited papers.

Key idea

A CNN is a multi-scale grouping of neighbouring pixels with learned weights: the structure (small filters, zoom-outs, the same weights everywhere) is the prior, the data only tune it. Its strength and its weakness are the same thing: it is an excellent detector of local patterns, and little more.

The triangles of §2.2 give a clean example. Comparing two triangles by the Euclidean distance between their raw coordinates says that a triangle and the same triangle moved one metre are far apart. import numpy as np

def r2(y, yhat):
    return 1 - np.sum((y - yhat) ** 2) / np.sum((y - y.mean()) ** 2)

def fit_least_squares(X, y):
    """Least squares with an intercept; minimum-norm solution when D >= N - 1."""
    Xc, yc = X - X.mean(0), y - y.mean()
    w = np.linalg.lstsq(Xc, yc, rcond=None)[0]
    return w, y.mean() - X.mean(0) @ w

3. Kendall embedding and the formula of p. 62 (§2.2, Labs 5–7)

import numpy as np

def kendall(T):
    """Kendall embedding of a triangle T = (A, B, C) given as 3 complex numbers."""
    A, B, C = T
    u, v = (B - A) / np.sqrt(2), (2 * C - A - B) / np.sqrt(6)      # Helmert coordinates
    s, w = abs(u) ** 2 + abs(v) ** 2, np.conj(u) * v
    return np.array([(abs(u) ** 2 - abs(v) ** 2) / 2, w.real, w.imag]) / s

def procrustes_error(P, Q):
    """min over direct similarities z -> a z + b of sum_k |a P_k + b - Q_k|^2."""
    Pc, Qc = P - P.mean(), Q - Q.mean()
    a = np.vdot(Pc, Qc) / np.vdot(Pc, Pc)
    return np.sum(abs(a * Pc - Qc) ** 2)

4. Kendall’s question (§2.2, Lab 8)

import numpy as np
from itertools import combinations

def max_angle_deg(P):
    """P: (..., 3, 2) triangles -> largest angle in degrees."""
    a = np.linalg.norm(P[..., 1, :] - P[..., 2, :], axis=-1)
    b = np.linalg.norm(P[..., 0, :] - P[..., 2, :], axis=-1)
    c = np.linalg.norm(P[..., 0, :] - P[..., 1, :], axis=-1)
    s = np.sort(np.stack([a, b, c], -1), -1)            # the largest side faces the largest angle
    cos = (s[..., 0] ** 2 + s[..., 1] ** 2 - s[..., 2] ** 2) / (2 * s[..., 0] * s[..., 1])
    return np.degrees(np.arccos(np.clip(cos, -1, 1)))

5. Tissot’s indicatrix (§2.1, Lab 4)

import numpy as np

def mercator(lam, phi):
    return np.array([lam, np.log(np.tan(np.pi / 4 + phi / 2))])

def tissot(proj, lam, phi, h=1e-6):
    """Semi-axes of Tissot's ellipse from the Jacobian (per unit of ground distance)."""
    east = (proj(lam + h, phi) - proj(lam - h, phi)) / (2 * h * np.cos(phi))
    north = (proj(lam, phi + h) - proj(lam, phi - h)) / (2 * h)
    a, b = np.linalg.svd(np.column_stack([east, north]), compute_uv=False)
    return a * b, np.degrees(2 * np.arcsin((a - b) / (a + b)))    # area factor, max angular distortion

for lat in [0, 30, 60, 80]:

    out = np.full(v.size, fill); out[idx] = v.ravel()[idx]
    return out.reshape(v.shape)

Self-test

Try to answer before opening each answer.

1. What is the “curse of dimensionality” in the sense of this lecture?

With many variables per subject and few subjects (D comparable to or larger than N), there are many formulas that fit the data perfectly, most of them by chance. More columns = more ways for noise to look like signal (p. 19: more information about each patient is “very dangerous”). §1.2 adds the sampling side: the space of images is far too large to be covered by examples.

2. Why does least squares reach a training R² of exactly 1 when D ≥ N − 1?

The fit must solve N equations ⟨w, xi⟩ + b = yi with D + 1 unknowns. If D + 1 ≥ N and the rows are in general position, the N×(D+1) system has rank N and a solution for every y — even pure noise. Lab 2: train R² = 1.000 from D = 19 with N = 20.

3. In XKCD 882, what is the chance of at least one “significant” colour if none has an effect?

1 − 0.9520 ≈ 64% for 20 independent tests at 5%. Lab 1 measures 64.45% ± 0.34%. With a shared control group the tests are correlated and the rate is 42.2%, still far above 5%.

4. What does the Bonferroni correction do, and what does it cost?

It tests each of the m hypotheses at α/m, so that the probability of any false alarm stays below α (Lab 1: 5.0%). The cost is power: a real effect must be stronger to be detected.

5. What are the two regularisation strategies of pages 20–21, and which prior does each encode?

Sparsity (few columns matter: Lasso, trees/XGBoost) and domain structure (columns come in meaningful groups that share a weight: food groups, the ATC drug classification). Each helps when its assumption is true (Lab 2).

6. In Lab 2, when does the Lasso win and when does the group model win?

If ♥ depends on one column, the Lasso keeps a positive test R² (0.23 at D = 15) while the group model fails (−0.08). If ♥ depends on a whole group, the group model scores about 0.4 at every D while the Lasso stays near 0. If ♥ is noise, the Lasso wisely selects nothing and stays near 0.

7. Why can’t we sample the space of 128×128 images? How big is 5128·128?

With 5 values per pixel there are 516,384 images, a number with 11,452 digits. No dataset covers a measurable part of that space; equivalently, the grid spacing N−1/D is about 1 for any realistic N.

8. Why does a 5-pixel model fail to predict the area of an organ that moves?

When the organ moves, no pixel is reliably inside or outside it, so no small set of pixels carries its size. Lab 3: test R² −0.125 with 20 images, never above 0.49. Summing all pixels (one tied weight, a “grouping” prior) reaches 0.965.

9. Name the three levels of analysis of pages 26–44 and one strength of each.

Pixel grid (explicit neighbourhoods, fast convolutions), point clouds and surfaces (compact, precise, easy to deform), biomechanical/physiological model (prior knowledge, robust to noise, infers what cannot be seen such as blood flow).

10. What does “the map is not the territory” require of a model?

Highlight what matters for the task, discard the rest, be accurate up to a sensible tolerance, and be transparent about omissions and distortions (p. 50–51). A model as detailed as the data (Borges’s 1:1 map) is useless.

11. What does Tissot’s indicatrix measure? What does Mercator keep and lose?

The image of a small circle of the globe on the map: an ellipse whose axes are the local stretch factors. Mercator keeps angles (all ellipses are circles) and loses areas (×1/cos²φ, ×4 at 60°; Greenland appears 15.4× larger than DR Congo, though it is smaller).

12. Why is the space of triangle shapes two-dimensional?

A triangle has 6 real coordinates; direct similarities z ↦ az + b have 4 real parameters. 6 − 4 = 2. Concretely, sending A to −1 and B to +1 leaves only the position z of C.

13. Where are the equilateral and flat triangles in the z-plane?

Equilateral: z = ±i√3 (the sign is the orientation). Flat: z real (or z = ∞, A = B). Isosceles with apex C: z imaginary; with apex A or B: the circles |z + 1| = 2 and |z − 1| = 2.

14. What do the poles and the equator of Kendall’s sphere represent?

The two poles are the direct and the indirect equilateral triangles; the equator is the set of flat (degenerate, aligned) triangles. The height on the sphere is 2√3·(signed area)/(a² + b² + c²).

15. How do relabelling and mirroring act on the sphere, and why are there 12 pieces?

A cyclic relabelling is a rotation by 120° about the polar axis; swapping two vertices is a half-turn about an equatorial axis; a mirror flips north and south. The equator and the three isosceles meridians cut the sphere into 12 pieces = 6 orderings of the side lengths × 2 orientations.

16. State the identity of page 62 with its conventions.

min over direct similarities S of Σk ‖S(Pk) − Qk‖² = Var(Q)·‖K(P) − K(Q)‖², where Var(Q) is the sum of squared distances of Q’s vertices to their centroid (with the mean, the right side is 3 times too small), and reflections are not allowed.

17. What is Kendall’s theorem about random triangles, and why is it true?

If A, B, C are independent isotropic Gaussians, the shape is uniform on the sphere. The Helmert coordinates are then independent standard complex Gaussians, so their direction is uniform on the unit sphere of ℂ²; the Hopf map K sends that uniform distribution to the uniform distribution on the 2-sphere.

18. What happens to the distribution of shapes when the Gaussian cloud is stretched?

Flat triangles become more likely, equilateral ones less: with σx/σy = r the density is (r² + 1)/(2r) at the equator and 4r²/(r² + 1)² at the poles (1.25 and 0.64 for r = 2), depending only on the latitude.

19. How many flat triangles (angle ≥ 179°) among 52 uniform points in a square? Why does the answer for the menhirs depend on the null model?

About 129 of the 22,100 (Lab 8: 128.6 ± 14.1). The real Land’s End sites give 156: significant against a square (3.8% of random configurations reach it), borderline against the peninsula (10.4%), ordinary against a model that keeps the clustering of the sites (65.2%). The conclusion is only as good as the null model — clustering alone creates alignments.

20. Give four properties of a convolution.

Linear; translation-equivariant (shift in → same shift out); local (small support); cheap (K² operations per pixel, the same K² weights everywhere). Lab 9 checks equivariance exactly — and shows that subsampling by 2 breaks it for odd shifts.

21. Why do wavelets compress images better than pixels?

Natural images are smooth with a few edges: in a multi-scale basis most coefficients are near zero and a few large ones carry the image. Lab 10: 5% of Haar coefficients give 26.9 dB, 5% of pixels 9.5 dB. Sparsity is a prior whose value depends on the coordinates.

22. What are μ(x), m(x), M(x), and how does Deep Art use them?

Features from early (micro), middle (meso) and late (macro) layers of a CNN. Deep Art builds an image with μ (and m) of a painting and M of a photo: the photo’s layout with the painting’s textures. The real algorithm matches statistics (Gram matrices) of the painting’s features, which is why the output has the painting’s fine-scale energy without copying its pixels (Lab 10).

23. What does “μ(x) is reliable; M(x) really isn’t” mean?

CNNs are good detectors of local patterns, but their “object” features are built from those patterns and can be fooled: images with the right textures and no object are classified with high confidence (Nguyen et al., 2015).

24. According to page 102, what is the course about?

Understanding similarity metrics: the implicit priors they reflect and how to compute with them efficiently. Observations are turned into an N×N similarity matrix, then generic methods (clustering, classification, regression, visualisation) are applied.

25. In Lab 11, why do raw coordinates fail to group triangles by shape?

Raw coordinates measure position, size, orientation and vertex labelling as much as shape; the random moves dominate the distances (ARI 0.002). Kendall’s chord is invariant to direct similarities, so only shape remains (ARI 1.000). The prior is in the metric.
