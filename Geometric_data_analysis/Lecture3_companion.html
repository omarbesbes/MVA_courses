§0 Where this lecture sits

Lecture 2 assumed the best case: a table with few, informative columns, built with domain experts. Lecture 3 starts from the worst case, which is also the usual one: “machine learning is about tables that have more columns than rows” (p. 3). It asks what data look like in a space of many dimensions, and answers with a picture: a Gaussian cloud in dimension 100 is a thin soap bubble, two samples are orthogonal, and all distances are nearly equal (p. 9–15). Learning is only possible because real data are not spread like that: they sit near a low-dimensional structure (“the manifold hypothesis”, p. 17).

The rest of the lecture looks for that structure through the neighbours of each point. A graph keeps only “who is close to whom” (§1). From a graph one can estimate a dimension (§2) and a curvature (§3), two local descriptors that also classify the archetypes of geometry: balls, planes and trees. The plan’s third part, “Global embeddings: lab session with UMAP” (p. 19), has no slides in this deck; §4 covers it, and Lecture 4 opens with it (L4 P.2).

A question that comes back

What can we learn from the neighbours alone?

Every method of this lecture forgets the coordinates and keeps, for each point, the list of its neighbours. From that list alone, one can measure distances along the data (shortest paths, Lab 5), a dimension (how fast neighbourhoods grow, §2), a curvature (how neighbourhoods overlap, §3) and even a map of the whole dataset (§4). Two failures frame the answer. In white noise, neighbours carry no information at all: every point is at the same distance from every other (P.3). And quantities that depend on how the data sit in space, like the mean curvature H, can never be recovered from the neighbours (the Theorema Egregium, §3.3).

Earlier lectures. Lecture 1 asked what a model should keep and what it should throw away; Lecture 2 asked what a model assumes where there is no data. Here, the model keeps only the neighbourhoods and throws away everything else. Later lectures build on the same list: graph neural networks pass messages along it (L4 §1.2), Riemannian geometry turns a field of local norms into geodesics (L5 §3.7), and Ollivier’s curvature already compares two neighbourhoods with optimal transport, the distance of L6.

Prologue — the curse of dimensionality, seen from geometry p. 1–19 · fr. 1–19

The first nineteen slides are not a numbered section, but they carry the lecture’s main statistical idea. They go from a comic about p-values to a picture of a Gaussian cloud in dimension 100, and end on the hypothesis that makes learning possible. This page splits them in four parts.

P.1 Recap and the statistical curse p. 2–8 · fr. 2–8

Where we are (p. 2–3). The recap repeats the two first lectures: a good model is “simple, accurate and honest”, domain experts come first, and trees, K-NNs, linear and kernel regression are “well-understood baselines”. Then the question of the day: “What about the curse of dimensionality?”, over Lecture 1’s table of N patients × D variables. “Remember: machine learning is about tables that have more columns than rows.”

The problem: more questions than answers (p. 4–7). XKCD 882 tests 20 colours of jelly beans against acne, each at the 5% level, and one colour (green) comes out “significant”; the newspaper prints “95% confidence, only 5% chance of coincidence”. With 20 independent tests of true null hypotheses, the chance of at least one false alarm is 1 − 0.9520 = 64%; L1 Lab 1 measured it. A table with many columns is the same situation: each column is a hypothesis, each row a piece of evidence, and with more columns than rows some column will fit the target by luck.

The slide’s example (p. 8). Tyler Vigen’s chart: “Number of people who drowned by falling into a pool correlates with Films Nicolas Cage appeared in”, 1999–2009. Read off the slide’s image, the two series are

Their Pearson correlation is 0.666, the “66.6%” printed on Vigen’s site, and a t-test with 11 points gives p = 0.025: “significant” at the 5% level. Nobody believes in a causal link. The number is not wrong; the procedure is. Vigen’s site compares thousands of series and prints the pairs that correlate. Lab 1 measures how easily such a pair appears.

Two workarounds (p. 8). “Sparsity: trees, lasso… Smoothness: polynomial regression, kernels…”. Both restrict the family of models before looking at the data. Sparsity assumes that only a few columns matter, so a model may not spread small weights over all of them (the Lasso of L1 Lab 2, the few splits of a tree in L2 §1). Smoothness assumes that the answer changes slowly when the input changes, so nearby patients get nearby predictions (L2 §6). Each is a prior: it helps when it is true of the problem, and hurts when it is not.

Watch out

The chart on p. 8 has two vertical axes, 80–140 drownings on the left and 0–6 films on the right, stretched so that the two curves overlap. Any two series can be made to look aligned by choosing the two ranges. The honest pictures are the ones in Lab 1: both series on one standardised scale, or one against the other in a scatter plot. (The chart also spells the actor’s name “Nicholas” on its right axis.)

  • The lesson for tables. Each column of a wide table is a candidate. With more columns than rows, the best-looking column tells almost nothing, which is why models need a prior such as sparsity or smoothness, and why a correlation must be tested on data that were not used to find it.
Beyond the slides

The random-walk effect has a name: nonsense correlation. G. Udny Yule described it in 1926 (“Why do we sometimes get nonsense-correlations between time-series?”). The usual t-test assumes independent observations; for trending series, the number of effectively independent years is much smaller than the number of years, so the test is far too optimistic. The remedy is to correlate year-to-year changes rather than levels, or to use a test that accounts for autocorrelation.

P.2 What does a Gaussian look like in dimension D? p. 9–13 · fr. 9–13

The question. Most intuitions about data come from pictures in dimension 1 or 2: a bell curve (p. 9), a round bright blob (p. 10). The slides ask what the same distribution, the standard Normal \(\mathcal{N}(0, \Id_\D)\), looks like when each point has D coordinates. All figures were made by the author’s script code/gaussian_bubble.py, rebuilt in Lab 2 with the same settings (100,000 points, the same bins and colours).

The norm (p. 11). The slide plots histograms, for D = 1, 2, 4, 9, 16 and 25, of the Euclidean norm

\[\|\text{x}\| ~=~ \sqrt{\text{x}[\text{1}]^2 + \cdots + \text{x}[\text{D}]^2}~.\]

“We recognize the sum of D independent, indentically distributed variables of mean 1. Taking the square root, we get a random variable of mean \(\sqrt{\text{D}}\).” Each coordinate x[i] is Normal with mean 0 and variance 1, so x[i]² has mean 1 and variance 2. The squared norm therefore has mean D and variance 2D: it grows like D while its spread only grows like \(\sqrt{2\text{D}}\). Taking the square root, \(\|\text{x}\| \approx \sqrt{\text{D}}\), with a spread that tends to \(1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.71\), whatever D. This is why the six histograms of p. 11 have the same width and simply move to the right.

Rescaled norms (p. 12). Dividing by \(\sqrt{\D}\),

\[\frac{\text{1}}{\sqrt{\text{D}}}\|\text{x}\| ~=~ \sqrt{\tfrac{\text{1}}{\text{D}}\big( \text{x}[\text{1}]^2 + \cdots + \text{x}[\text{D}]^2 \big) }\]

“concentrates around its mean value 1”: the histograms sharpen as D grows. The average of D independent squares tends to 1 (the law of large numbers), and its fluctuations are Gaussian of size \(1/\sqrt{2\text{D}}\) (the central limit theorem), so the rescaled norm is about \(1 \pm 1/\sqrt{2\text{D}}\): 1 ± 0.14 for D = 25, 1 ± 0.07 for D = 100.

The soap bubble (p. 13). To see a cloud in dimension D, the slide keeps the first two coordinates of each point and stretches them so that the point sits at its true (rescaled) distance from the centre:

\[\frac{\|\text{x}\|}{\sqrt{\text{D}} \cdot \sqrt{\text{x}[\text{1}]^\text{2} + \text{x}[\text{2}]^\text{2}} } \big( \text{x}[\text{1}], \text{x}[\text{2}] \big)~.\]

The direction of (x[1], x[2]) is uniformly random, and the radius is \(\|\text{x}\|/\sqrt{\D}\). For D = 2 the picture is the familiar blob; from D = 4 on a hole opens in the middle, and for D = 100 the cloud is a thin ring. “This provides a faithful visualization of a Normal Gaussian sample in dimension D.” The slide’s title credits Ferenc Huszár, whose blog post “Gaussian distributions are soap bubbles” (2017) made the picture popular.

Key idea

The density of \(\mathcal{N}(0, \Id_\D)\) is largest at the centre, yet almost no sample lies there. The density at radius r is proportional to \(e^{-r^2/2}\), which decreases, but the area of the sphere of radius r is proportional to \(r^{\D-1}\), which increases. The mass at radius r is their product, \(r^{\D-1} e^{-r^2/2}\), which peaks at \(r = \sqrt{\D - 1}\). In dimension 25, the probability of falling within distance 1 of the centre is 6×10⁻¹⁴. “Typical” and “most likely” are different things in high dimension.

Watch out
  • “Of mean √D” is an approximation. The mean of a square root is not the square root of the mean (Jensen’s inequality): \(\mathbb{E}\|\text{x}\| = \sqrt{2}\,\Gamma(\tfrac{\D+1}{2})/\Gamma(\tfrac{\D}{2}) \approx \sqrt{\D - 1/2}\), which is 0.798 for D = 1 and 4.950 for D = 25, against \(\sqrt{\D}\) = 1 and 5. The exact statement is \(\mathbb{E}\|\text{x}\|^2 = \D\). The peaks of p. 11 sit at \(\sqrt{\D-1}\), the mode of the distribution.
  • Concentration is the law of large numbers. The slide attributes it to the central limit theorem; the CLT adds the bell shape and the \(1/\sqrt{2\D}\) width.
  • “Faithful” holds for norms, not for distances. The picture preserves each point’s distance to the centre and a uniform direction. It does not preserve distances between points: two samples in dimension 100 are almost orthogonal (P.3), so they are about √2 times the radius apart, while two points of the drawn ring can be anywhere from 0 to twice the radius apart.
  • The 2D picture of p. 10 is \(e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\) in the script, a Normal with standard deviation \(1/\sqrt{2}\) rather than 1; the 1D curve of p. 9 uses \(e^{-t^2/2}\). The shapes are the same up to scale. The slide also writes “indentically” for “identically”.

P.3 White noise and the geometry of distances p. 14–16 · fr. 14–16

Samples are not like the mean (p. 14). The slide draws a vector of D = 100 independent Normal values as a 10 × 10 image, in the coolwarm colour map from −3 (blue) to +3 (red): the mean (a uniform grey), two samples x1 and x2, and their difference. “Samples do not look like the average value of the distribution, and are all orthogonal to each other.” The images come from the last, active part of the author’s script; Lab 3 reads the hundred values of each back from the colours of the slide’s own images.

Why orthogonal. The cosine of the angle between two independent samples is

\[\cos\theta = \frac{\langle \text{x}_1, \text{x}_2\rangle}{\|\text{x}_1\|\,\|\text{x}_2\|} = \frac{\sum_{i} \text{x}_1[i]\,\text{x}_2[i]}{\|\text{x}_1\|\,\|\text{x}_2\|}.\]

The numerator is a sum of D independent terms of mean 0 and variance 1, so it is of size \(\sqrt{\D}\); the denominator is about D. Hence \(\cos\theta \approx \mathcal{N}(0, 1/\D)\): the angle is 90° give or take \(57.3°/\sqrt{\D}\), that is ±5.7° for D = 100.

Geometric consequences (p. 15). “If we assume that our D > 10 features are independent and identically distributed:

  • We require \(10^\D\) samples to enable basic statistics: histograms, density estimation…
  • \(\|\text{x}_i - \text{x}_j\|\) is constant up to a minor deviation.
  • The distance matrix contains no useful information.
  • All of our intuitions break down.”

With 10 bins per axis, a histogram in dimension D has \(10^\D\) cells, and a million samples leave almost all of them empty from D = 7 on. The difference of two samples is Normal with variance 2 per coordinate, so \(\|\text{x}_i - \text{x}_j\| \approx \sqrt{2\D} \pm 1\): the relative spread of all pairwise distances shrinks like \(1/\sqrt{2\D}\). When every point is at the same distance from every other, “nearest neighbour” means nothing, and every method built on distances (K-NN, kernels, clustering) sees no structure.

Garbage in, garbage out (p. 16). XKCD 1838 (“you pour the data into this big pile of linear algebra, then collect the answers on the other side”), with the slide’s conclusion: “Doing machine learning is about understanding that your data is not white noise. We need to put a low-dimensional structure on our problem.”

Key idea

The premise of p. 15 is independence. Real data are high-dimensional but not independent: the 64 pixels of a handwritten digit are strongly tied to each other. Lab 3 takes 1,000 real digits and shuffles each pixel column separately. This keeps every pixel’s distribution but makes the pixels independent, exactly the slide’s assumption. The distances then collapse to those of white noise. The information was in the dependence between columns, not in the columns.

Beyond the slides

The loss of contrast between near and far neighbours was proved by Beyer, Goldstein, Ramakrishnan and Shaft (“When is ‘nearest neighbor’ meaningful?”, 1999): for independent coordinates, the ratio of the largest to the smallest distance from a query point tends to 1. The same paper notes the escape route used in Lab 3: when the data have fewer effective dimensions than coordinates, or come in well-separated clusters, nearest neighbours stay meaningful.

P.4 The manifold hypothesis, and the plan p. 17–19 · fr. 17–19

The hypothesis (p. 17). A “swiss roll”: points in 3D that lie on a rolled-up sheet, coloured by their position along the sheet. “What matters for statistics is the intrinsic dimension “d” of the dataset, not the extrinsic dimension “D” of the feature space.” Here D = 3 coordinates describe each point, but d = 2 numbers suffice: the position along the roll and the height. The manifold hypothesis states that real high-dimensional data behave like this: images, sounds or patient records occupy a thin, curved set of low dimension inside the space of all possible values.

Why it rescues statistics. A method that only uses neighbourhoods, such as K-NN, needs enough points to fill each neighbourhood, and the number of points needed grows like \(10^{\text{d}}\), not \(10^\D\). Its error then depends on d. Lab 6 measures d for the 64-pixel digits: between 5 and 10, depending on the scale.

The plan (p. 18–19). The overview gives the rest of the course: “Lecture 3: “Discrete” geometries = graphs. Lecture 4: Deep learning on graphs and point clouds. Lecture 5: “Continuous” geometries = manifolds. Lecture 6: Spaces of probability distributions. Lecture 7: Hardware bottlenecks.” Its aim: “bridge the gap between “discrete” and “continuous” descriptions of the underlying problem structures.” Today’s plan has three parts: “1. Why do we care about graphs? 2. Local descriptors and archetypes: Dimension. Curvature. 3. Global embeddings: Lab session with UMAP.”

Beyond the slides

The claim that statistics depends on d is a theorem for local methods. For K-nearest-neighbour regression, Kpotufe (“k-NN regression adapts to local intrinsic dimension”, 2011) showed that the error rate is governed by the dimension of the data near each query point, not by the number of coordinates. For local polynomial regression on data lying on an unknown manifold, Bickel and Li (2007) obtained the rate of dimension d. Neither method needs to know the manifold: the neighbourhoods adapt by themselves.

§1 Why do we care about graphs? p. 20–30 · fr. 20–29

§1.1 Similar patients, and what a graph is p. 21–22 · fr. 20–21

The problem (p. 21). Another coffee with the domain experts, and a sentence every data scientist hears: “We may not fully understand the columns of our table… But we can certainly tell you that Patient A is similar to Patient B!” Sometimes a similarity is easier to trust than the coordinates it would be computed from. Sometimes it is all there is: contacts between people, citations between papers, roads between towns.

Two views of the same object (p. 22). “Algorithmic definition: a collection of vertices and edges. Geometric perspective: a metric space that is defined locally… and that we would like to understand globally!” Formally, an undirected graph is a set of vertices V and a set E of unordered pairs {i, j}, the edges. Its graph distance d(i, j) is the number of edges on a shortest path from i to j (or the smallest total length, if edges have lengths). This is a true distance: it is zero only from a vertex to itself, symmetric, and satisfies the triangle inequality, because a path from i to k through j is one of the candidates for the shortest path from i to k.

Local definition, global object. Each vertex only knows its neighbours, yet the distances between all pairs follow. A breadth-first search from one vertex finds its neighbours (distance 1), then their new neighbours (distance 2), and so on; the figure below does it for the slide’s graph.

§1.2 Networks, curves and meshes: intrinsic and extrinsic distances p. 23–24 · fr. 22–23

Where graphs come from (p. 23–24). “Transportation networks” (the Paris metro map) and “Communication and power lines” are graphs by construction. In medical imaging, “Vascular networks” are curves in 3D and “Anatomical surfaces”, such as the cortex extracted by FreeSurfer [Fis12], are meshes: graphs of vertices and edges that approximate a surface.

The distinction. “Intrinsic graph distances ≠ Extrinsinc [sic] Euclidean distances.” The extrinsic distance is the straight line through space; the intrinsic distance is the shortest route along the object. On the folded cortex, two points on opposite banks of a sulcus are a few millimetres apart in space and several centimetres apart along the surface. Which one matters depends on the question: diffusion along the cortex follows the surface, an MRI coil sees the straight line.

§1.3 From high-dimensional samples to graphs p. 25–26 · fr. 24–25

The idea (p. 25). Even when the data are points in \(\R^\D\), we can build a graph from them and forget the coordinates. The slide shows the K-nearest-neighbour graph of a swiss roll, from Gabriel Peyré’s numerical tours [Pey11]: “If your data has a low-dimensional structure, this should be visible on its neighborhood structure.” If the points lie on a sheet, their neighbours lie on the same sheet, and the graph is a mesh of that sheet.

Three constructions. Let \(\text{x}_1, \dots, \text{x}_\text{N}\) be the points.

  • Ball graph: an edge i — j when \(\|\text{x}_i - \text{x}_j\| \lt \varepsilon\). Its adjacency matrix is the slide’s “Ball connectivity matrix”, the box kernel \(\mathbf{1}[\,\|\text{x}_i - \text{x}_j\| \lt \varepsilon\,]\).
  • K-nearest-neighbour graph: i → j when j is one of the K points closest to i. The relation is not symmetric (j may have K closer points than i), so it is made undirected by keeping an edge when either point chooses the other (“or”), or only when both do (“and”, mutual neighbours).
  • Gaussian weights: every pair gets a weight \(w_{ij} = \exp(-\|\text{x}_i - \text{x}_j\|^2 / 2\sigma^2)\), the slide’s “Gaussian kernel matrix” (p. 26). It is a complete graph whose weights fade with distance.

Why the choice matters. A radius ε that suits a dense region isolates the points of a sparse one, and a radius that connects the sparse region turns the dense one into a huge clique. K-NN adapts to the density, since every point has at least K neighbours, but it can link two distant groups when one of them is too small to provide K neighbours. Gaussian weights avoid thresholds but give a dense N × N matrix, which is why they are usually restricted to the K nearest neighbours (UMAP adapts σ to each point, §4). All three encode the same prior: what is close is similar.

Watch out

The two 8 × 8 matrices of p. 26 are hand-coloured in Inkscape, with seven shades of red. They are consistent with each other (the ball’s edges are exactly the pairs of the two darkest off-diagonal shades), but they are schematic: one diagonal cell of the “Gaussian” matrix, (4, 4), is not the darkest shade, whereas a Gaussian kernel always equals 1, its maximum, on the diagonal. §1.4 Discrete and continuous geometry p. 27–29 · fr. 26–28

Classical geometry (p. 27). The bridges of Königsberg, “Discrete graph”, ≠ Albert Chern’s Sudanese Möbius band, “Continuous surface”. Euler’s solution of the bridges problem (1736) is the founding example of graph theory: whether one can walk across each of the seven bridges exactly once depends only on which land masses the bridges connect, not on their shapes or lengths. A walk that uses every edge once must enter and leave each intermediate vertex, so it needs every vertex to have even degree, except possibly the two ends. In Königsberg all four land masses have odd degree, so no such walk exists (figure below). The Möbius band, by contrast, is continuous: its interesting properties, such as having a single side, live in the continuum.

Modern geometry (p. 28). “Tree” ≃ “Hyperbolic salad” [Red]. The left picture is the Cayley graph of the free group on two generators: a tree in which every vertex has four neighbours, drawn with edges that halve at each generation. The number of vertices at distance r from the centre is \(4 \cdot 3^{r-1}\): it grows exponentially. A curly lettuce leaf does the same thing with tissue: its edge grows faster than the edge of a flat disc could, so the leaf ruffles. Exponential growth of the area of discs is the signature of negative curvature, the geometry of the hyperbolic plane, where a disc of radius r has area \(2\pi(\cosh r - 1)\). Lab 7 measures it on graphs, and Lecture 5 builds the continuous version, the Poincaré disk (L5 §1.2, L5 Lab 2).

The object, not its encoding (p. 29). DiffusionNet [SACO22] learns on human bodies given as fine meshes, coarse meshes or point clouds, and gives the same answer on all of them: “Mesh triangulations, sampling densities or point cloud representations should not distract ourselves from the underlying objects.” A graph is one discretisation of a shape among many, and a good method should not depend on which one we happened to receive.

§1.5 From local connectivity to global structure p. 30 · fr. 29

The challenge (p. 30). A plate of spaghetti [Mat11]: “Going from local connectivity to global structure is the main open challenge in geometry.” Locally, each strand only touches its neighbours; globally, following one strand from end to end, or counting the strands, requires putting all the local information together.

The simplest global computation: shortest paths. On a K-NN graph whose edges have their Euclidean lengths, Dijkstra’s algorithm adds up short, trustworthy steps into long distances. If each step stays on the sheet, the sum approximates the distance along the sheet: the intrinsic distance of §1.2. This is how Isomap (Tenenbaum, de Silva and Langford, 2000) unrolls a swiss roll (§4).

When it fails. Too few neighbours and the graph falls apart into pieces with no path between them; too many and some edges jump between two layers of the roll. One such short circuit is enough to make every long distance wrong, because shortest paths will use it.

§2 Dimension p. 31–39 · fr. 30–37

§2.1 Dimension = number of degrees of freedom p. 32–33 · fr. 30–31

The intuition (p. 32–33). “Pong is 1D. Pac-Man is 2D. Minecraft is 3D.” The player’s paddle in Pong slides along a line, Pac-Man moves in a plane maze, and Minecraft’s player walks in space. Likewise for shapes: “A segment is 1D. A disk is 2D. A ball is 3D.” The dimension is the number of independent numbers needed to say where one is, locally: a sphere is 2D although it sits in 3D space, because near any point two coordinates (latitude and longitude) suffice.

The problem. Data do not come with such coordinates. We only see points (or a graph), with noise, at a finite sampling density. The next two subsections give the slides’ two estimators: one for point clouds (local PCA) and one for “pure” graphs (volume growth).

§2.2 Local PCA and a rule of thumb p. 34–36 · fr. 32–34

The method (p. 34). “Fitting an ellipsoid to a K-NN graph is easy using local PCA.” The slide shows a graph made of a chain (left) and of a two-dimensional patch (right), with an elongated ellipse on the chain and a round one on the patch. Take a point and its neighbours \(\text{x}_1, \dots, \text{x}_\text{K}\), with mean \(\bar{\text{x}}\), and compute their covariance matrix

\[C ~=~ \frac{1}{\text{K}} \sum_{k=1}^{\text{K}} (\text{x}_k - \bar{\text{x}})\,\t{(\text{x}_k - \bar{\text{x}})}.\]

Its eigenvectors are the axes of the best-fitting ellipsoid and its eigenvalues the squared lengths of these axes. The slide’s rule (p. 36), verbatim: “Let \(\lambda_\text{1}, \dots, \lambda_\text{D}\) denote the lengths of the principal axes. \(\lambda_\text{1}^\text{2} \gt \dots \gt \lambda_\text{D}^\text{2}\) are the diagonal coefficients of the PCA. We normalize them as \(\text{l}_\text{i} = \lambda_\text{i}^\text{2} / (\lambda_\text{1}^\text{2} + \dots + \lambda_\text{D}^\text{2})\). Then, we may define the local dimension d as the smallest index such that \(\text{l}_\text{1} + \cdots + \text{l}_\text{d} \gt \text{80\%}\). Other conventions exist!”

Why it works. A smooth d-dimensional surface looks flat when one zooms in: within a small radius r, the points spread along d directions with a variance of order r², and they deviate from the flat tangent space only by the curvature, a variance of order \(r^4\kappa^2\). For small r, the d largest eigenvalues dominate and the rule returns d.

When it fails. Three ways, all measured in Lab 6.

  • Scale. Real data have noise of some size σ in every direction. If the neighbourhood is smaller than a few σ, the noise looks like extra dimensions; if it is much larger than the radius of curvature, the curvature does. The estimate depends on K, and there may be no good K.
  • The threshold. 80% is a convention. For an isotropic neighbourhood of dimension d, every \(\text{l}_\text{i} = 1/\text{d}\), and the rule returns the smallest k with k/d > 0.8: exact up to d = 5, but 5 for d = 6, 9 for d = 10 and 10 for d = 12. It undercounts by design.
  • In between. “What is the dimension of an ellipsoid? d = 1, d = 1.5?, d = 2” (p. 35). There is no right answer, and the slide’s middle ellipse is a good test: in the SVG file its axes are 9.043 and 3.857 mm long, so \(\text{l}_\text{1} = 0.846\) and the rule says d = 1. Continuous indices exist: the participation ratio \((\sum_i \lambda_i^2)^2/\sum_i \lambda_i^4\) gives 1.35, and the effective rank \(\exp(-\sum_i \text{l}_\text{i} \log \text{l}_\text{i})\) gives 1.54, very close to the slide’s “1.5?”.
Beyond the slides

“Other conventions exist!” Two popular ones avoid choosing a neighbourhood size. The correlation dimension (Grassberger and Procaccia, 1983) counts the pairs of points closer than r and fits the growth of that count with r, the point-cloud version of §2.3. The TwoNN estimator (Facco, d’Errico, Rodriguez and Laio, 2017) only uses, for each point, the ratio μ = r₂/r₁ of the distances to its second and first neighbours. On a d-dimensional set of roughly uniform density, μ follows a Pareto law with exponent d, so \(\hat{\text{d}} = \text{N} / \sum_i \log \mu_i\). It is purely local, which makes it sensitive to noise, as the lab shows.

§2.3 Volume growth: a dimension for “pure” graphs p. 37–38 · fr. 35–36

The problem (p. 37). A graph given only by its edges has no coordinates, so no covariance matrix. It still has balls: \(B(\text{x}, \text{r})\), the set of vertices within r edges of x.

The idea. In a d-dimensional space, the volume of a ball grows like the radius to the power d: a segment of length 2r, a disk of area πr², a ball of volume 4πr³/3. The slide: “Hausdorff dimension: we pick d such that: \(\mathrm{Vol}(B(\text{x},\text{r})) \sim \text{r}^\text{d}\). We estimate:

\[\text{d} \simeq \frac{\log(\mathrm{Vol}(B(\text{x},\text{r})))}{\log(\text{r})}\]

by a linear regression on \(\text{r} = \text{1}, \text{2}, \text{3}, \text{4}, \text{5}, \ldots\)” On a graph, the volume is the number of vertices in the ball. The slide’s picture is a 9 × 9 grid coloured by distance to the centre: the balls are diamonds with 1, 5, 13 and 25 vertices for r = 0, 1, 2, 3, that is \(2\text{r}^2 + 2\text{r} + 1\), which grows like r².

Fractals (p. 38). “This definition works well for many objects, including fractals.” Halving the side of a square gives 4 copies, so \(\text{d} = \log 4 / \log 2 = 2\); a cube gives 8 copies, \(\log 8/\log 2 = 3\); the Sierpinski triangle gives 3 copies of half size, so \(\text{d} = \log 3 / \log 2 \approx 1.585\): more than a curve, less than a surface.

Watch out
  • The name. The quantity of p. 37 is the growth (or mass) dimension. The Hausdorff dimension is defined through coverings by small sets and is 0 for any finite or countable set, hence for any graph. The two agree on self-similar fractals such as the Sierpinski triangle, which is why the slide’s examples work.
  • Small radii are biased. The ratio \(\log \mathrm{Vol} / \log \text{r}\) ignores the constant in \(\mathrm{Vol} \approx c\,\text{r}^\text{d}\) and is undefined at r = 1. The regression slope removes the constant, but lower-order terms remain: on the slide’s own grid, \(2\text{r}^2 + 2\text{r} + 1\) gives a slope of 1.55 over r = 1…5, not 2. Counting the vertices at distance exactly r (4r on the grid) or using larger radii fixes it (Lab 7).

§2.4 Cliques, grids and trees p. 39 · fr. 37

When there is no power law (p. 39). “Problem: \(\mathrm{Vol}(B(\text{x},\text{r}))\) is not always a polynomial function of r [TDGC+21]. Cliques: we fill the graph and plateau very quickly. Grids: we retrieve a polynomial. Trees: the volume of a ball grows exponentially fast.” In a clique every vertex is a neighbour, so the ball of radius 1 is already the whole graph. In a tree where every vertex has three neighbours, the sphere of radius r has \(3 \cdot 2^{\text{r}-1}\) vertices: no power of r can keep up.

Three archetypes. “This reminds us of classical examples in continuous geometry: the sphere, the Euclidean plane and the Poincaré disk.” On a sphere of radius 1, a disc of radius r has area \(2\pi(1 - \cos \text{r})\): it grows like πr² at first, then slows down and plateaus at 4π, the whole sphere. In the plane the area is πr². In the hyperbolic plane (the Poincaré disk of Lecture 5) it is \(2\pi(\cosh \text{r} - 1)\), which grows like \(\pi e^\text{r}\). These three growth laws correspond to positive, zero and negative curvature: Lecture 4 summarises “Cliques are like balls with positive curvature. Grids are like planes with flat curvature. Trees are like saddles with negative curvature” (L4 P.1, slide p. 4).

§3.1 Curvature of a surface p. 41 · fr. 38

Curves first (p. 41). The slide is hand-drawn. For a curve, the curvature at a point is κ = 1/r, where r is the radius of the circle that best hugs the curve there (the osculating circle): a tight bend has a small circle and a large curvature (“most curved of the 3”), a gentle bend a large circle (“least curved of the 3”).

§3.2 Energies of curvature in physics and biology p. 42–46 · fr. 39–43

Soap films (p. 42). “Soap bubbles minimize: \(\mathrm{area(\mathcal{S})} = \int_{\mathcal{S}} 1 \, dA\) under constant volume, or with boundary conditions. They correspond to minimal surfaces with \(H = \kappa_1 + \kappa_2 = 0\) in cases 2 and 3.” Case 2 is a film between two rings (a catenoid), case 3 a film on a helical wire (a helicoid). Why H = 0: pushing a surface by a small distance φ along its normal changes its area by \(-\int_{\mathcal{S}} H\,\varphi\,dA\) (up to the sign convention of the normal). A surface of minimal area cannot be improved by any push, so H = 0 everywhere. A closed bubble (case 1) must also keep its volume, which only allows pushes with \(\int \varphi\,dA = 0\); the condition becomes H = constant, and the sphere with H = 2/R wins.

Red blood cells (p. 43). “Red blood cells minimize: \(\mathrm{Helfrich}(\mathcal{S}) = \int_{\mathcal{S}} (H-H_0)^2 \, dA\) or a variant of this energy, under constant volume.” A membrane resists bending, not stretching; \(H_0\) is its preferred (“spontaneous”) curvature. Minimising this energy reproduces the biconcave disc and, with other parameters, the cup-shaped and spiky cells of the slide’s pictures.

Curvatubes (p. 44). Anna Song’s model [Son22] generalises both: “Curvatubes minimize \(F(\mathcal{S}) = \int_{\mathcal{S}} p(\kappa_1,\kappa_2) \, dA\) under constant volume, where:

\[p(\kappa_1,\kappa_2) = a_{2,0} \, \kappa_1^2 + a_{1,1} \, \kappa_1 \kappa_2 + a_{0,2} \, \kappa_2^2 + a_{1,0} \,\kappa_1 + a_{0,1} \,\kappa_2 + a_{0,0}~.\]
\[\begin{aligned} p(\kappa_1,\kappa_2) = {}& a_{2,0} \, \kappa_1^2 + a_{1,1} \, \kappa_1 \kappa_2 + a_{0,2} \, \kappa_2^2 \\ & + a_{1,0} \,\kappa_1 + a_{0,1} \,\kappa_2 + a_{0,0}~.\end{aligned}\]

The slide shows four textures, obtained with four polynomials written in terms of H, κ1 and κ2: \(H^2 + \kappa_1^2 + \kappa_2^2\), \((H-20)^2 + 5\kappa_2^2\), \((H-25)^2 + (\kappa_1-12.5)^2 + (\kappa_2-12.5)^2\) and \((H-45)^2 + (\kappa_1-45)^2 + 10\kappa_2^2\). Each is a sum of squares, so it is smallest where every square vanishes, and that point says which shape the texture prefers. The second polynomial is zero only at κ1 = 20, κ2 = 0: tubes of radius 1/20, and the second panel is indeed made of tubes. Lab 8 does the four panels.

A descriptor and a method (p. 45–46). The simulated textures resemble real ones: open aluminium foam and closed polymer foam imaged by micro-CT, the lamina cribrosa behind the eye, trabecular bone, the endoplasmic reticulum: “Curvature is a powerful descriptor”. The computational trick (p. 46) turns the surface energy into a “convolutional volumetric loss function (phase-field)”: the surface is replaced by a smooth field u on a 3D grid, close to +1 inside and −1 outside, and the energy becomes \(\mathcal{E}_\epsilon(u) = \int_\Omega p(\kappa^\epsilon_{1,u}, \kappa^\epsilon_{2,u})\, \epsilon|\nabla u|^2\, dx\), where \(\epsilon|\nabla u|^2\) is concentrated on the thin transition layer and the curvatures are those of the level sets of u, computed with derivatives on the grid (convolutions). Then: “Start with white noise (texture generation) and minimize with gradient descent. Implemented on GPU with PyTorch. Combines maths + GPU computing + imaging data ⟹ Perfectly within your reach!”

Watch out

“Under constant volume” is not the whole story for red blood cells. The cell membrane can bend but hardly stretch, so the standard models keep both the area and the volume fixed. The profile on the slide’s own image is annotated “V/V₀ = 0.641”: the cell’s volume divided by the volume of a sphere with the same area, a ratio that is only meaningful when the area is fixed. With the volume alone fixed, a sphere would do.

§3.3 What a graph can see: the Theorema Egregium p. 47 · fr. 44

The picture (p. 47). Two shortest paths (geodesics) start side by side, on a sphere (“Sperical (>0)” [sic]), on a plane (“Euclidean (=0)”) and on a saddle-shaped surface (“Hyperbolic (<0)”) [TDGC+21]. On the sphere they come together, on the plane they stay parallel, on the saddle they move apart. How fast they separate is governed by the Gaussian curvature K: two geodesics that start parallel, a small distance ε apart, are about \(\varepsilon \cos(\sqrt{K}\, t)\), ε or \(\varepsilon \cosh(\sqrt{-K}\, t)\) apart after a length t. On a sphere of radius R, the gap closes after a quarter of a great circle, t = πR/2: two meridians that are parallel at the equator meet at the pole.

The theorem. “Theorem Egregium (Gauss, 1827): \(H = \kappa_1 + \kappa_2\) is extrinsic → depends on the embedding. \(K = \kappa_1 \cdot \kappa_2\) is intrinsic → can be defined on graphs.” Roll a sheet of paper into a cylinder: distances measured along the paper do not change, but H goes from 0 to 1/r. K stays 0. Gauss proved that K can always be computed from distances along the surface alone, so a creature living on the surface, or an algorithm that only sees a graph, can measure K but never H.

A discrete K: the angle deficit. On a mesh made of flat triangles, all the curvature sits at the vertices. At a vertex, add up the angles of the triangles that meet there: on a flat sheet they sum to 360°. The angle deficit 2π − Σ angles is positive at the tip of a cone, zero on a flat region and negative where the surface ruffles. It is the discrete integral of K over the vertex’s share of area. Descartes’ theorem says that the deficits of any convex polyhedron add up to exactly 4π, and the Gauss–Bonnet theorem generalises it: \(\int_{\mathcal{S}} K\, dA = 2\pi\chi\), where χ is the Euler characteristic (2 for a sphere, 0 for a torus). Only lengths and angles enter: the deficit is intrinsic.

Watch out

The Latin name is Theorema Egregium, “remarkable theorem”; the slide writes “Theorem Egregium”, and the figure it borrows says “Sperical”. More importantly, “can be defined on graphs” is a programme rather than a formula: K lives on surfaces, and a general graph has no triangles or angles. The next two subsections show three ways of defining curvature on a graph, and they do not agree with each other.

§3.4 Gromov’s hyperbolicity p. 48 · fr. 45

Thin triangles (p. 48). “A graph is δ-hyperbolic if all geodesic triangles are thin” [Gro87]: whatever three points a, b, c are chosen, each side of a shortest-path triangle stays within distance δ of the union of the two other sides. In a tree, triangles are tripods and δ = 0. In the Euclidean plane, large triangles are fat, and no δ works. In the hyperbolic plane every triangle is thin, whatever its size.

A computable version. Checking all triangles is awkward; the four-point condition is equivalent up to a constant factor. For any four vertices x, y, z, w, form the three sums d(x, y) + d(z, w), d(x, z) + d(y, w) and d(x, w) + d(y, z); the graph is δ-hyperbolic when the largest sum never exceeds the middle one by more than 2δ. In a tree the two largest sums are always equal.

“Global definition, suited to the study of groups such as SL2(ℤ).” The right picture is the ball of radius 8 of the Cayley graph of SL2(ℤ), the group of 2 × 2 integer matrices of determinant 1: one vertex per matrix, one edge per multiplication by a generator. From the SVG file we recovered the generators: a matrix u of order 6, a matrix s of order 4, and −Id, which give 212 vertices and 466 edges, exactly the file’s graph. The graph is made of small cycles glued along a tree, and its δ is 1 (Lab 10). Unlike the curvatures of §3.5, δ is a property of the whole graph at large scale.

§3.5 Forman and Ollivier–Ricci curvature p. 49–50 · fr. 46–47

Curvature of an edge. Both definitions attach a number to each edge and compare how the neighbourhoods of its two endpoints relate: spread apart (tree-like, negative) or overlapping (clique-like, positive).

Forman (p. 49) [SMJ+16, SSG+18, TDGC+21]: “Forman curvature of an edge i ↔ j:

\[4 - \text{degree}_\text{i} - \text{degree}_\text{j} + 3 \cdot \text{triangles}(\text{i}, \text{j},\,\cdot\,)~.\]

More precise but complex variations of this formula also exist.” Every extra edge at an endpoint opens a new direction away from the edge (negative); every triangle closes one (positive). On the slide’s figure both endpoints have degree 6 and the edge lies in 2 triangles: 4 − 6 − 6 + 6 = −2.

Ollivier–Ricci (p. 50) [WHY+22]: “Ollivier-Ricci curvature of an edge i ↔ j: is the optimal transport distance between \(\mathcal{N}(\text{x}_\text{i}, 1)\) and \(\mathcal{N}(\text{x}_\text{j}, 1)\) larger than the distance between xi and xj?” Here \(\mathcal{N}(\text{x}_\text{i}, 1)\) is the neighbourhood of radius 1 of xi, seen as a uniform distribution of mass, not a Normal distribution. The curvature is

\[\kappa(\text{i}, \text{j}) ~=~ 1 - \frac{\W\big(\mathcal{N}(\text{x}_\text{i}, 1),\, \mathcal{N}(\text{x}_\text{j}, 1)\big)}{\d(\text{x}_\text{i}, \text{x}_\text{j})},\]

where \(\W\) is the optimal transport cost of moving one distribution onto the other, with the graph distance as the cost of moving a unit of mass (Lecture 6). If the two neighbourhoods are closer to each other than their centres (W₁ < d, as on a sphere or in a clique), κ > 0; if they are farther (as in a tree, where the other neighbours lead away), κ < 0. On grids, the neighbourhoods simply slide along: κ = 0. This matches Ollivier’s idea: on a positively curved space, small balls are closer than their centres.

Watch out
  • Forman’s zero is not flatness. On a square grid every edge has Forman curvature 4 − 4 − 4 = −4, and on the triangular lattice 4 − 6 − 6 + 6 = −2, although both tile the flat plane. The formula ranks edges correctly (more triangles, larger value) but its values must be compared with those of a reference graph, not with 0. This is one reason for the “more precise but complex variations”.
  • Which neighbourhood? Conventions differ for Ollivier–Ricci: uniform on the neighbours only, uniform on the ball including the vertex (our reading of \(\mathcal{N}(\text{x}_\text{i}, 1)\)), or a fixed share α of the mass kept at the vertex. The values change; the signs on the archetypes do not (Lab 10 offers the three).

§3.6 Toy models p. 51 · fr. 48

The picture (p. 51). Plastic equilateral triangles clipped together: “5 triangles per vertex: positive curvature. 6 triangles per vertex: flat curvature. 7 triangles per vertex: negative curvature. ⟹ Basic intuition that guides current research in the field.” Five triangles leave a gap of 60° at the vertex, which closes into a cone; continuing everywhere closes the surface into an icosahedron, whose 12 vertices carry 12 × 60° = 720° = 4π of deficit, as Descartes requires. The three curvatures agree on the signs (Lab 10): Ollivier–Ricci gives +1/3, 0 and −1/4 on the three triangulations; Forman gives 0, −2 and −4, in the same order but shifted; the growth of balls (Lab 7) is quadratic on the flat lattice, exponential (×2.62 per step) on the {3,7} tiling, and finite on the icosahedron. This section fills the gap with material that is not on the slides; it is marked as such.

The problem. Given the neighbourhood graph of N points, find N points in the plane (or in \(\R^\text{d}\), d small) whose arrangement shows the structure of the data: unrolled sheets, clusters, branches. It is the “local → global” problem of p. 30 in its most practical form.

Three families, three priorities.

  • Keep all distances: Isomap (Tenenbaum, de Silva and Langford, 2000). Compute shortest-path distances in the K-NN graph (Lab 5), then find the points of the plane whose Euclidean distances best match them. The best match in the least-squares sense of classical multidimensional scaling is given by the two leading eigenvectors of the double-centred matrix \(B = -\tfrac{1}{2} J \Delta^{(2)} J\), where \(\Delta^{(2)}\) holds the squared distances and \(J = \Id - \tfrac{1}{\text{N}}\mathbf{1}\t{\mathbf{1}}\). Isomap preserves long distances, which makes it fragile: one short circuit ruins it.
  • Keep neighbours close: Laplacian eigenmaps (Belkin and Niyogi, 2003). Look for coordinates y that vary slowly along the edges, that is, that minimise \(\sum_{i \lt j} w_{ij}\,(y_i - y_j)^2 = \t{y} L\, y\) with \(L = D - W\) (D the diagonal of degrees), under a normalisation that forbids the constant solution. The minimisers are the eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian with the smallest non-zero eigenvalues: the graph’s slowest “vibration modes”, the discrete analogue of the harmonics of a drum. On a swiss roll the first one runs along the roll, the next ones oscillate faster, which is what the author’s unused figure shows.
  • Keep neighbourhoods, forget the rest: UMAP (McInnes, Healy and Melville, 2018), the method of the planned lab session. It weights the K-NN graph with a scale adapted to each point (the distance to its nearest neighbour sets the zero, and a width σi is chosen so that each point has the same total weight, log₂ K), symmetrises the weights, and then lays the points out in the plane by stochastic gradient descent: neighbours attract, random pairs repel. It starts from the Laplacian eigenmap. Its cousin t-SNE (van der Maaten and Hinton, 2008) uses the same principle with probabilities.

How to judge a picture. An embedding cannot keep everything: a sphere cannot be flattened without distortion, and a tree has too many vertices at distance r to fit in a plane (§2.4). A useful score is trustworthiness (Venna and Kaski, 2001): among each point’s 10 nearest neighbours in the picture, how many were far away in the data, and how far? It is 1 when the picture never places strangers side by side. On the swiss roll, where the true coordinates are known, we can also check whether a coordinate of the picture follows the position along the roll.

Watch out

UMAP and t-SNE keep neighbourhoods, not distances or densities. The size of a cluster in the picture, and the gap between two clusters, carry little information: a tight cluster can be drawn large and two distant groups can be drawn side by side. Read these pictures as “who is next to whom”, then check any claim on the original data. The page’s UMAP is a compact re-implementation of umap-learn’s algorithm (same weights, same force law, spectral start); on the two datasets below its trustworthiness (0.998 on the roll, 0.990 on the digits) is within 0.002 of umap-learn’s (0.998 and 0.989).

What the slides conclude · references

The deck has no conclusion slide; it ends on the toy models: “⟹ Basic intuition that guides current research in the field” (p. 51). Its main claims, in order, are: “Two simple workarounds: Sparsity… Smoothness…” (p. 8); samples of a high-dimensional Gaussian “do not look like the average value of the distribution, and are all orthogonal to each other” (p. 14); for independent features, “The distance matrix contains no useful information” (p. 15); “Doing machine learning is about understanding that your data is not white noise. We need to put a low-dimensional structure on our problem” (p. 16); “What matters for statistics is the intrinsic dimension” (p. 17); a graph is “a metric space that is defined locally… and that we would like to understand globally!” (p. 22); “Going from local connectivity to global structure is the main open challenge in geometry” (p. 30); balls grow like a plateau, a polynomial or an exponential on cliques, grids and trees (p. 39); K is intrinsic and “can be defined on graphs” (p. 47). The next lecture opens with the author’s own summary: “Curse of dimensionality ⟹ Statistics on white noise is hopeless. We must understand the intrinsic structure of our data, even when it is embedded in a high-dimensional space of features. We may need to unwrap the manifold of plausible data samples” (L4 P.1, slide p. 2).

What to keep from this page. High dimension is not the enemy; independence is. The labs show it three ways: independent coordinates make every distance alike (Lab 3), while real digits keep informative neighbourhoods and a local dimension between 5 and 10 (Lab 6). A neighbourhood graph captures that structure, but only if its scale is right: too few neighbours break it into pieces, too many short-circuit it (Lab 5). From the graph alone come a dimension (growth of balls) and a curvature (overlap of neighbourhoods), with one caveat: each definition has its own conventions, biases and zero (Lab 7, Lab 10). The three archetypes — cliques, grids and trees; spheres, planes and hyperbolic planes — are the reference points of the rest of the course.

0. The page’s random generator

"""The random generator of the companion pages (mulberry32 + Marsaglia's polar method), in Python.

The page's labs draw their random data with this generator; with the same seed, these functions return the
same numbers, so the snippets below reproduce the page's results instead of results "within sampling error".
"""
import math

M32 = 0xFFFFFFFF


def _imul(a, b):
    return (a * b) & M32


class Rng:
    def __init__(self, seed):
        self.a = (seed & M32) or 1
        self.spare = None

    def uniform(self):                       # uniform in [0, 1), bit-for-bit as the pages' G.rng(seed).uniform()
        self.a = (self.a + 0x6D2B79F5) & M32
        a = self.a
        t = _imul(a ^ (a >> 15), 1 | a)
        t = ((t + _imul(t ^ (t >> 7), 61 | t)) & M32) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & M32) / 4294967296

    def normal(self):                        # Marsaglia's polar method, keeping the spare value as the pages do
        if self.spare is not None:
            s0, self.spare = self.spare, None
            return s0
        while True:
            u, v = 2 * self.uniform() - 1, 2 * self.uniform() - 1
            s = u * u + v * v
            if 0 < s < 1:
                break
        f = math.sqrt(-2 * math.log(s) / s)
        self.spare = v * f
        return u * f

    def int(self, n):
        return math.floor(self.uniform() * n)

    def range(self, lo, hi):
        return lo + (hi - lo) * self.uniform()

    def normals(self, n):
        return [self.normal() for _ in range(n)]

    # ---- vectorised versions (same stream as repeated calls; used for millions of draws) ----
    def uniform_array(self, n):
        import numpy as np
        k = np.arange(1, n + 1, dtype=np.uint64)
        a = (np.uint64(self.a) + k * np.uint64(0x6D2B79F5)) & np.uint64(M32)
        self.a = int(a[-1]) if n else self.a
        m = np.uint64(M32)
        t = ((a ^ (a >> np.uint64(15))) * (a | np.uint64(1))) & m
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> np.uint64(7))) * (t | np.uint64(61))) & m)) & m) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> np.uint64(14))) & m).astype(np.float64) / 4294967296

    def normal_array(self, n):
        import numpy as np
        out = []
        if self.spare is not None and n > 0:
            out.append(self.spare); self.spare = None
        need = n - len(out)
        while need > 0:
            m = max(64, int(need * 0.66) + 64)             # pairs of uniforms to try
            u = self.uniform_array(2 * m)
            x, y = 2 * u[0::2] - 1, 2 * u[1::2] - 1
            s = x * x + y * y
            ok = (s < 1) & (s > 0)
            # keep the stream exact: stop at the pair that completes the request, rewind the rest
            idx = np.flatnonzero(ok)
            pairs_needed = (need + 1) // 2
            if len(idx) >= pairs_needed:
                last = int(idx[pairs_needed - 1])
                self.a = int((self.a - (2 * m - 2 * (last + 1)) * 0x6D2B79F5) & M32)
                idx = idx[:pairs_needed]
            f = np.sqrt(-2 * np.log(s[idx]) / s[idx])
            vals = np.empty(2 * len(idx)); vals[0::2] = x[idx] * f; vals[1::2] = y[idx] * f
            if len(vals) > need:
                self.spare = float(vals[-1]); vals = vals[:-1]
            out.extend(vals.tolist()); need -= len(vals)
        return np.array(out[:n])

def sierpinski(level):
    tris = [((0, 0), (2 ** level, 0), (0, 2 ** level))]              # integer barycentric-like coordinates
    for _ in range(level):
        new = []

            m = lambda p, q: ((p[0] + q[0]) // 2, (p[1] + q[1]) // 2)
            new += [(a, m(a, b), m(a, c)), (m(a, b), b, m(b, c)), (m(a, c), m(b, c), c)]
        tris = new

Cheat sheet

Gaussians in high dimension (P.2)

‖x‖ ≈ √D ± 0.71; ‖x‖/√D ≈ 1 ± 1/√(2D). Mass at radius r ∝ rD−1e−r²/2, peak at √(D−1): the centre is empty. “Faithful” soap bubble: true norms, uniform angles, wrong pairwise distances.

White noise (P.3)

Independent samples are orthogonal: θ = 90° ± 57.3°/√D. Pairwise distances ≈ √(2D) ± 1: all alike. 10D cells for a histogram. The culprit is independence: real data keep informative distances.

Spurious correlations (P.1)

One test at 5% is fooled 5% of the time; the best of M is fooled 1 − 0.95M of the time. Trending series correlate by chance (Yule). Priors: sparsity, smoothness.

Graphs from points (§1.3)

Ball: ‖xi − xj‖ < ε (density-sensitive). K-NN: not symmetric, make it “or” or “and”. Gaussian: w = exp(−d²/2σ²), dense. Scale is everything: too small disconnects, too large short-circuits.

Local dimension (§2.2)

Local PCA: li = λi²/Σλj²; d = smallest index with l1 + … + ld > 80%. Undercounts for d ≥ 6. Continuous: participation ratio (Σλ²)²/Σλ⁴, effective rank eH(l). TwoNN: N/Σ log(r₂/r₁).

Growth dimension (§2.3–2.4)

|B(r)| ∼ rd: slope of log |B| vs log r. Small r is biased (1.55 on the grid). Clique: plateau; grid: polynomial; tree: exponential (k(k−1)r−1). Sphere 2π(1 − cos r), plane πr², hyperbolic 2π(cosh r − 1).

Curvature of surfaces (§3.1–3.3)

κ = 1/r. Principal κ1, κ2; H = κ1 + κ2 (extrinsic), K = κ1κ2 (intrinsic, Theorema Egregium). Minimal surfaces: H = 0. Angle deficit 2π − Σ angles ≈ K · area; Σ deficits = 2πχ (4π for a sphere).

Curvature of graphs (§3.4–3.6)

Forman: 4 − degi − degj + 3·triangles (zero shifted). Ollivier–Ricci: 1 − W₁(mi, mj)/d(i, j): > 0 cliques, 0 grids, < 0 trees; bottlenecks very negative. Gromov δ: four-point condition, 0 on trees, a large-scale notion.

Global embeddings (§4)

Isomap = MDS on graph distances (keeps distances, fragile). Laplacian eigenmaps = smallest eigenvectors of L (smooth, robust, distorts the ends). UMAP = fuzzy K-NN graph + attraction/repulsion (keeps neighbourhoods, not distances or densities). Judge with trustworthiness.

Self-test

1. Why is a correlation of 0.666 between two 11-year series weak evidence on Vigen’s site?
Because it was selected among many pairs. Its p-value (0.025) is computed as if it were the only test; with 100 random candidates, one reaches |r| ≥ 0.666 in 91% of trials (Lab 1). Smooth, trending series make chance correlations even larger.
2. State the slides’ two workarounds for the statistical curse, and what each assumes.
Sparsity (trees, Lasso): only a few columns matter. Smoothness (polynomials, kernels): the answer changes slowly with the input, so neighbours get similar predictions. Both restrict the model family before looking at the data.
3. What is \(\mathbb{E}\|\text{x}\|^2\) for x ∼ 𝒩(0, IdD)? Is \(\mathbb{E}\|\text{x}\| = \sqrt{\D}\)?
\(\mathbb{E}\|\text{x}\|^2 = \D\) exactly. \(\mathbb{E}\|\text{x}\|\) is slightly smaller, \(\approx\sqrt{\D - 1/2}\) (Jensen): 4.950 for D = 25. The slide’s “mean √D” is an approximation.
4. Why do the histograms of p. 11 all have the same width?
‖x‖² has mean D and standard deviation √(2D); taking the square root divides the spread by about 2√D, so ‖x‖ has standard deviation close to 1/√2 ≈ 0.71 for every D (0.603 for D = 1, 0.703 for D = 25).
5. The density of a Gaussian is largest at its centre. Why are samples never there in high dimension?
The volume near the centre is tiny: the mass at radius r is proportional to \(r^{\D-1}e^{-r^2/2}\), which peaks at \(\sqrt{\D-1}\). For D = 25, P(‖x‖ < 1) = 6×10⁻¹⁴.
6. What does the soap-bubble picture preserve, and what does it distort?
It preserves each point’s distance to the centre (rescaled) and gives uniform directions. It distorts distances between points: in dimension 100 two samples are almost orthogonal, about √2 times the radius apart, but the drawing puts them anywhere on a ring.
7. Why are two independent high-dimensional samples almost orthogonal?
Their inner product is a sum of D independent terms of mean 0, so it is of size √D, while the product of their norms is about D: cos θ ≈ 𝒩(0, 1/D), so θ ≈ 90° ± 57.3°/√D (± 5.7° for D = 100; the slide’s pair: 92.8°).
8. Which assumption of p. 15 makes “the distance matrix contain no useful information”, and how does Lab 3 test it?
Independence of the features. Lab 3 shuffles each pixel column of the digits separately (same marginals, independent columns): the relative spread of distances drops from 0.170 to 0.100 and the nearest neighbour moves from 34% to 69% of the median distance, close to white noise in dimension 64.
9. What is the difference between the intrinsic and the extrinsic dimension?
The extrinsic dimension D is the number of coordinates of the feature space; the intrinsic dimension d is the number of parameters needed to move along the data locally (2 for a swiss roll in 3D, about 5–10 for the 64-pixel digits). Local methods need a number of samples that grows with d, not D.
10. Give the two definitions of a graph on p. 22 and explain “defined locally”.
Algorithmic: vertices and edges. Geometric: a metric space. Each vertex only knows its neighbours, yet shortest paths define distances between all pairs: the global metric follows from local information.
11. Why is a K-NN graph not symmetric, and how is it usually made undirected?
j can be among the K nearest points of i while i is not among those of j (j may have K closer points). One keeps an edge when either chooses the other (“or”, the union) or only when both do (“and”, mutual neighbours).
12. Why can a single ball radius fail on data of uneven density?
A radius that suits the dense region isolates points of the sparse region (24 of 50 halo points in Lab 4 with ε = 0.5), and a radius that reaches the sparse points makes the dense region a huge clique. K-NN adapts, since every point gets K neighbours.
13. What is a short circuit in a K-NN graph, and why is one enough to ruin Isomap?
An edge between two points that are close in space but far along the data (neighbouring layers of a roll). Shortest paths use it for every long distance, so the global distances collapse: on the roll, one such edge (K = 8) drops the correlation with the true distances from 0.9987 to 0.818.
14. How does the slide define the local dimension from local PCA? When does it fail?
d = smallest index with l1 + … + ld > 80%, where li are the normalised PCA eigenvalues. It fails at small scale (noise looks like extra dimensions), at large scale (curvature does), and by design for d ≥ 6 (it returns ⌊0.8d⌋ + 1).
15. What does the rule say about the middle ellipse of p. 35, and what continuous answers exist?
Axis ratio 2.345 gives l1 = 0.846, so d = 1. The participation ratio gives 1.35 and the effective rank exp(−Σ l log l) gives 1.54, close to the slide’s “1.5?”.
16. What is the “Hausdorff dimension” of p. 37 really, and why is its estimate on r = 1…5 biased?
It is the growth (mass) dimension, |B(r)| ∼ rd; the true Hausdorff dimension of a graph is 0. On the grid |B| = 2r² + 2r + 1, whose lower-order terms pull the slope over r = 1…5 down to 1.55.
17. How do balls grow on a clique, a grid and a tree, and which continuous surfaces do these recall?
Plateau after r = 1; polynomial (rd); exponential (k(k−1)r−1). They recall the sphere (disc area 2π(1 − cos r), bounded), the plane (πr²) and the hyperbolic plane (2π(cosh r − 1)).
18. Define H and K. Which is intrinsic, and what does that mean?
H = κ1 + κ2, K = κ1κ2. K is intrinsic (Theorema Egregium): it depends only on distances measured along the surface, so it is unchanged by bending (a rolled sheet keeps K = 0 while H becomes 1/r) and can be estimated from a mesh or a graph.
19. Why do soap films between two rings have H = 0, and what happens when the rings are pulled apart?
They minimise area, and the first variation of area is \(-\int H\varphi\,dA\), so H = 0 (a catenoid). Beyond h = 1.0554 R the two flat discs have less area, and beyond h = 1.3255 R no catenoid exists: the film snaps.
20. How does the angle deficit measure curvature on a mesh?
At a vertex, 2π minus the sum of the angles of the incident triangles: positive at a cone tip, zero on flat parts, negative where the surface ruffles. It approximates K times the vertex’s area, and all deficits add up to 2πχ (Gauss–Bonnet; 4π for a sphere, 0 for a torus).
21. What does it mean for a graph to be δ-hyperbolic, and what are δ for a tree, a grid and SL₂(ℤ)?
Every geodesic triangle is δ-thin (each side within δ of the other two); computably, the four-point condition. A tree has δ = 0; the n × n grid has δ = n − 1 (8 for 9 × 9), growing with size; the ball of radius 8 of the Cayley graph of SL₂(ℤ) has δ = 1.
22. Compute the Forman curvature of an interior edge of the square grid, the triangular lattice and the icosahedron. What is odd?
4 − 4 − 4 = −4; 4 − 6 − 6 + 3·2 = −2; 4 − 5 − 5 + 3·2 = 0. The order is right (more triangles, larger), but flat lattices get negative values: the zero is shifted.
23. What does the Ollivier–Ricci curvature compare, and what sign does it give on cliques, grids and trees?
The optimal transport distance between the neighbourhoods of the two endpoints and the distance between the endpoints: κ = 1 − W₁/d. Positive in cliques (neighbourhoods overlap), 0 on grids, negative in trees (e.g. −1/2 with ball neighbourhoods on the 3-regular tree).
24. Why are bottleneck edges important for graph neural networks?
Information between two dense regions must flow through them, so messages from many vertices are squeezed into one edge (“over-squashing”, Topping et al.). They are the most negatively curved edges: −1.333 (Ollivier–Ricci) and −12 (Forman) for the bridge of two 8-cliques in Lab 10.
25. Which embedding would you use to unroll a swiss roll, and which to look at clusters of digits? Why?
Isomap unrolls the roll when the K-NN graph has no short circuit (rank correlation 0.9997 with K = 6), because it preserves distances along the data. For digits, UMAP (98.5% same-label neighbours in 2D, trustworthiness 0.990) keeps neighbourhoods, which is what clusters are; but its cluster sizes and gaps should not be read as distances.
