§0 Where this lecture sits

Six lectures built a geometric vocabulary for data: distances and kernels (Lecture 2), graphs and curvature (Lecture 3), convolutions and message passing (Lecture 4), Riemannian metrics (Lecture 5) and distances between distributions (Lecture 6). Almost every formula in them is a loop over pairs of points: a kernel sum, a nearest neighbour, a transport cost. Lecture 7 asks what these loops cost on real hardware, and how to make them fast.

It has three parts and a conclusion. What a GPU is: thousands of cores in about a hundred blocks, a pyramid of memories, four rules, and a worked example, nearest neighbour search, written four ways (p. 7–27). Compilation: why turning formulas into fast code is a hard problem (graph colouring, LLVM, just-in-time compilers), and the author’s answer, the symbolic matrices of the KeOps library, with its benchmark and applications (p. 28–47). The hardware itself: the A100 chip, its number formats, other accelerators and the history of computing power (p. 48–57). The conclusion is a tour of the whole course and advice for a research career (p. 58–65).

The slides show code and timings rather than formulas. Most of the thirteen labs therefore measure the computer you are reading on: its processor in JavaScript, and its graphics card through WebGPU when the browser offers it. The page’s Python scripts repeat the measurements on an Apple M3 Pro, with PyTorch on the Apple GPU (MPS), with hand-written Metal kernels, and with PyKeOps on the processor.

A question that comes back

Where does the time go?

The slides ask it of every program, under the word “Bottleneck”: for the four versions of nearest neighbour search, “(M × N × D) CPU operations and memory transfers”, “(M × N × D) Device \(\leftrightarrow\) Thread memory transfers”, “(M × N) Device \(\leftrightarrow\) Thread memory transfers”, and finally “(M × N × D) GPU computations” (p. 23–26). The answer is rarely the arithmetic. It is the memory (Lab 3: one access to main memory costs the time of hundreds of operations), the intermediate arrays that a library writes (Lab 4, Lab 8), the translation of a formula into code (Lab 6, Lab 7), or the precision of the numbers (Lab 13). The question has a practical form: before optimising a program, measure which layer of the machine it waits for.

Earlier lectures. Lecture 1 asked what a model should keep and what it should throw away; Lecture 2, what a model assumes where there is no data; Lecture 3, what can be learned from the neighbours alone; Lecture 4, what a simple baseline would do; Lecture 5, what depends on the coordinates; Lecture 6, what a distance notices and what it ignores. §1 What is a GPU? p. 7–27 · fr. 7–26

§1.1 Nvidia sells economies of scale p. 8–9 · fr. 7–8

The slides. p. 8, “Nvidia focuses its marketing on economies of scale”, shows a photograph captioned “Mythbusters Demo GPU versus CPU – 2009”: a machine with a single paint gun beside a wall of tubes that fire a whole painting at once. p. 9 shows the chart “40 Years of Microprocessor Trend Data”: single-threaded performance grows “1.5X per year”, then “1.1X per year”; “GPU-Computing perf” grows “1.5X per year”, and a green arrow promises “x1000 by 2025”. Below: “Simple message: 10,000 cores \(\Longrightarrow\) x1,000 acceleration vs. a 10-core CPU. But how did we fill those tubes with the correct paintballs?”

The problem. For thirty years, programs became faster without being rewritten: each new processor ran the same code at a higher clock frequency. Around 2005 this stopped. Higher frequencies would have needed more power than a chip can dissipate, so frequencies stayed near 3–4 GHz while the number of transistors kept doubling. Manufacturers spent the new transistors on more cores. A GPU takes this idea to the extreme: thousands of simple cores and little else.

What “x1,000” hides. Many cores only help a program that has many independent tasks and can feed them with data. The question of p. 9, how to fill the tubes, is the subject of the whole lecture: the answer is memory management (§1.5–§1.6), and a naive program can waste most of the chip (§1.9, §2.7).

What the lab measures. The chart of p. 9 is Nvidia’s version of a public data set: the microprocessor trend data first collected by Horowitz, Labonte, Shacham, Olukotun, Hammond and Batten, and extended to 2021 by Karl Rupp. Rupp also publishes the peak arithmetic rates and memory bandwidths of high-end CPUs and GPUs since 2007. The lab fits the growth rates on these data.

Lab 1

Fifty years of processors, and CPUs against GPUs (p. 9, 56)

Question. Do the growth rates of p. 9 hold on the published data? How far apart are the peak arithmetic rates of CPUs and GPUs?

Single-thread performance, frequency, cores, transistors and power (Rupp’s data, log scale)
Peak arithmetic rate of high-end Intel Xeon CPUs and NVIDIA GPUs (Rupp’s data)
What the lab shows (default: split in 2005, single precision)
  • The slide’s rates are right. Single-thread performance grew by a factor 1.453 per year from 1985 to 2004 and 1.124 per year from 2005 to 2021: “1.5X” then “1.1X”. The clock frequency tells why: it grew by a factor 1.328 per year, then stopped (0.997 per year). Transistors kept growing (factors 1.434 and 1.405 per year), and so did the number of cores after 2005 (a factor 1.311 per year).
  • The gap in peak arithmetic is 2 to 10, not 1,000. In single precision, the NVIDIA GPU of each year had 1.75 to 10.02 times the peak rate of the best Intel Xeon of the same year (2007–2018): 6.12 in 2007, 8.69 in 2013, 1.75 in 2018, when 56-core Xeons with 512-bit vector units arrived. The memory bandwidth ratio went from 2.4 to 3.8. Over these years the Xeon peak grew faster (a factor 1.516 per year) than the GPU peak (1.358 per year).
  • Where “x1,000” comes from. The arrow of p. 9 compares GPU throughput with the performance of one CPU thread, extrapolated to 2025. Against a whole multicore CPU running well-written code, a GPU is typically several times faster on arithmetic and memory bandwidth, not a thousand times. Large measured speed-ups usually compare a tuned GPU code with a CPU code that uses one core and no vector instructions (§2.7).
Watch out

The chart of p. 9 is not cited. It is Nvidia’s marketing figure built on the public “40 Years of Microprocessor Trend Data” (the dots are single-thread SpecINT scores); the green “GPU-Computing perf” line and the “x1000 by 2025” arrow are Nvidia’s own additions and have no data behind them after 2015. The Mythbusters photograph of p. 8 is uncited too (Nvidia’s promotional video, 2009, reproduced in the author’s thesis, Figure 2.2).

§1.2 Scientific programs are memory bound p. 10 · fr. 9

The slide. Two photographs: a jammed highway, “The curse of parallelism: traffic jams.” [Fro12], and a server rack before and after cable management, “Structure is required. Design choices favor “bankable” program architectures.”

The idea. A core can only compute on numbers that are next to it. Every number of a large array has to travel from memory to a core, through a few shared roads. When thousands of cores ask for data at once, the roads saturate first: the program is memory bound. Its speed is set by the number of bytes it moves, not by the number of operations it performs.

A number that decides it. The arithmetic intensity of a program is the number of operations it performs per byte it reads or writes. A chip has a machine balance: its peak operation rate divided by its memory bandwidth. A program whose intensity is below the balance waits for memory; above it, it waits for arithmetic. On an A100, the balance is about 12.5 operations per byte, 50 per float32 (§3.1, Lab 12). Adding two arrays performs one operation per 12 bytes: it runs at 1/150 of the peak.

Why structure helps. The cable picture is the second half of the answer. Memory systems move data in blocks (cache lines of 64 or 128 bytes, memory transactions of a whole group of threads). A program that reads contiguous data uses every byte of each block; a program that jumps around pays for a full block to use one number. GPUs reward programs that are organised for them, the “bankable” architectures of the slide: dense arrays, regular loops, predictable accesses.

§1.3 Inside a GPU: redundant blocks p. 11–14 · fr. 10–13

The slides. p. 11, “Let’s open up a GPU”: a GeForce RTX 2080 Ti card. p. 12: a die photograph, “7,000 cores on a single GPU”, and a block diagram, “The Turing architecture.” p. 13, “GPUs and large administrations follow the same plan”: the ground plan of the Admiralty and War Offices (1884), with its “Blocks” and “Device Memory”, and a photograph of a nineteenth-century office annotated “CUDA block”, “Shared Memory”, “Thread Memories”, “Cores”: “GPU \(\simeq\) 100 redundant blocks. Inside a CUDA block: workers and buffers.” p. 14, “Redundancy is key to recover high yields in spite of defects” [Dor97, Pee11]: a silicon crystal, a 12-inch wafer, and “GeForce RTX 3090 \(\gt \) GeForce RTX 3080 \(\gt \) GeForce RTX 3070 \(\gt \) …”.

Blocks. A GPU is not one processor with thousands of cores but about a hundred identical processors, the streaming multiprocessors (SMs), each with its own small memories and a few dozen cores (§3.1). The office analogy comes from the author’s thesis: like the clerks of a large administration, cores work in teams, each team shares a shelf of documents (the shared memory), each clerk has a desk (the registers), and everything else is in the library downstairs (the device memory).

Why redundancy. A chip is etched on a disk of silicon, and a few specks of dust or crystal defects land at random on each wafer. A large die is likely to be hit somewhere. If the die is made of identical blocks, a defective block can be switched off and the rest of the chip sold. This is why the A100 uses 108 of the 128 SMs of its GA100 die, and why several products are cut from the same die: a GA102 with 82 working SMs (out of 84) becomes an RTX 3090, one with at least 68 an RTX 3080.

Lab 2

Why sell 108 blocks out of 128? (p. 13–14)

Question. With defects that land at random on the die, what share of dies are perfect, and what share can be sold once a few blocks may be switched off?

What the lab shows (default: GA100, 0.1 defect per cm², SMs on 60% of the die; illustrative values)
  • A perfect large die is the exception. With 0.1 defect per cm², a GA100 die (8.26 cm²) receives 0.826 defects on average, and only 43.8% of the dies have none.
  • Twenty spare blocks absorb almost every defect that lands on an SM. Each SM survives with probability 0.99614; on average 0.495 SMs of 128 are defective, and the chance that fewer than 108 work is negligible. The dies that are lost are those hit outside the SMs (memory controllers, caches, input–output), which have no spare in this model: 71.9% of the dies can be sold as A100, against 43.8% if all 128 SMs had to work.
  • Binning. On a GA102 with the same assumptions, 77.3% of the dies reach the 82 working SMs of an RTX 3090 and 0.51% more reach the 68 of an RTX 3080. The RTX 3070 of the slide is not a lesser GA102: it uses its own die, the GA104.
Watch out

Foundries do not publish their defect densities, and the share of the die taken by the SMs is not published either: the defaults of the lab are illustrative, and the lab lets you vary both. The qualitative conclusions do not depend on them. The line “RTX 3090 \(\gt \) 3080 \(\gt \) 3070” mixes two things: the 3090 and the 3080 are two grades of the same GA102 die (82 and 68 working SMs), while the 3070 is a GA104, a smaller die of 48 SMs of which 46 are used (NVIDIA’s GA102 whitepaper). The “7,000 cores” of p. 12 match the A100 (108 SMs × 64 = 6,912 float32 cores); the RTX 2080 Ti of p. 11 has 68 SMs and 4,352 cores.

§1.4 GPUs are built to render 3D meshes p. 15 · fr. 14

The slide. A diagram of ray tracing [Hen08]: rays leave the camera through the pixels of the image, hit a scene object, and a shadow ray is sent to the light source (“Simulating light rays”). Beside it, a render from Ray tracing in one weekend [Shi20]. Then: “Nvidia GeForce RTX (Ray Tracing Texel eXtreme) \(\Longleftrightarrow\) Geometric computations \(+\) textures, on independent patches of the screen.”

Why this matters for data science. A frame of a video game is millions of small, identical and independent computations: one per pixel or per ray, each reading a few triangles and a few texels (pixels of textures) near each other. GPUs are designed for exactly this pattern: the same instruction applied by many threads at once to nearby data. A scientific computation runs fast on a GPU when it can be written in the same shape. A kernel sum \(\sum_j k(x_i, y_j)\, b_j\) is such a computation: one independent task per point \(x_i\), reading the same list of points \(y_j\) as every other task (§1.9, §2.7).

§1.5 Five layers of memory p. 16 · fr. 15

The slide. “1 GPU \(\simeq\) 100 blocks of 100 cores. On the CPU host: HDD / SSD – 1 TB. Host RAM – 100 GB. On the GPU device: Device RAM – 10 GB. Shared block-wise memories – 1 Kb/core. Thread-wise registers – 1 Kb/core. Time(Device RAM \(\leftrightarrow\) Core) \(\simeq\) 100 arithmetic operations.” A diagram stacks the layers: HDD/SSD; Host RAM and CPU cores; Device RAM; shared memory; thread memory; GPU cores.

The idea. Memory is a trade-off between size and speed. The layers closest to the cores (registers, shared memory) are tiny but answer in a few cycles; the device memory is a thousand times larger but a hundred times slower to reach; the host memory is on the other side of a cable (the PCI Express bus of a desktop). A CPU has the same pyramid, with caches (L1, L2, L3) managed by the hardware. On a GPU, the shared memory is managed by the programmer: deciding what to keep close to the cores is part of the program.

What the lab measures. The pyramid can be measured from a web page. The lab follows a chain of pointers through an array of increasing size: each step reads the address of the next one, so the processor cannot guess ahead and must wait for the memory. As long as the array fits in a cache, a step is fast; beyond each cache size, the time per step jumps. It then reads the same arrays sequentially, and times an arithmetic operation, to compare the three. When the browser offers WebGPU, it measures the graphics card too.

Question. How long does one memory access take, depending on the size of the data and on the order of the accesses? How many arithmetic operations fit in the time of one access to main memory?

What the lab shows (in the run used to check this page: Chrome on an Apple M3 Pro; other machines give other plateaus)
  • The pyramid is visible. A random access costs 1.7 ns while the data fit in the first-level cache (up to 64 KB), 6.3 ns in the second level (256 KB to 8 MB) and 85 ns once the data (64 MB and more) live in main memory: a ratio of about 49.

On the graphics card of this computer (WebGPU), the device memory delivers 130 GB/s and the arithmetic units 4.0 TFLOP/s: about 120 operations per float read. A chain of dependent reads takes 68 ns per read in 16 KB of data, 130 ns in 1 MB and 290 ns in 64 MB: every level is slower than on the processor, and the graphics card makes up for it by keeping thousands of threads in flight, so that some are always ready to compute. The page’s script measures the same card with Metal kernels: 130 GB/s, 4.2 TFLOP/s, and 49, 107 and 276 ns for a dependent read.
Watch out

“1 Kb/core” is written with a lower-case b, which means kilobit; the recap of p. 22 says “1 KB \(\simeq\) 100 float numbers per core”, so kilobytes are meant (1 KB holds 256 float32 numbers). As orders of magnitude the figures are fair: an A100 SM has 256 KB of registers and up to 164 KB of shared memory for its 64 float32 cores, about 4 KB and 2.6 KB per core [KGJ+20]. The “Device RAM – 10 GB” is the 11 GB of the RTX 2080 Ti; an A100 has 40 or 80 GB.

§1.6 Four rules of GPU programming p. 17 · fr. 16

The slide. “1. Promote block-wise parallelism. 2. Reduce Host \(\leftrightarrow\) Device memory transfers. 3. Reduce Device \(\leftrightarrow\) Shared/Thread memory transfers. 4. Promote block-wise, contiguous memory accesses.”

What each rule means. The author’s thesis (§2.1) spells them out.

  1. Block-wise parallelism. “Threads can interact with each other during the execution of a program, but may only do so inside their own CUDA block.” Split the work into independent pieces of a few hundred threads.
  2. Host–device transfers. “Incessant back-and-forth copies between the ‘CPU’ and ‘GPU’ RAMs may quickly become the bottleneck of modern research codes.” Keep the data on the device.
  3. Device–shared/thread transfers. “Due to the (relatively) high latency of the Device memory, programmers should refrain from storing and handling intermediate buffers outside of the Shared memory of a CUDA block.” Do not write large intermediate arrays; consume values where they are computed.
  4. Contiguous accesses. “GPUs’ memory circuits are wired in a way that promotes contiguous, page-wise exchanges between the Device and the Shared memories […] somewhat ill-suited to the processing of sparse matrices which rely on rare but random memory accesses.”

Where the labs meet them. Rule 4 is the difference between the random and sequential curves of Lab 3 and the subject of Lecture 4’s Lab 5, which counted the memory transactions of a graph convolution. Rule 3 separates the implementations of nearest neighbour search in Lab 4 and of the benchmark of Lab 8. Rule 2 explains why a GPU loses on small problems: copying the data and starting a kernel cost tens of microseconds, the time of millions of operations.

§1.7 The CUDA toolkit, a C++ dialect p. 18–21 · fr. 17–20

The slides. Four slides of code, “The CUDA toolkit – a C++ dialect for GPU programming”. The kernel, executed by every thread:

__global__ void
My_CUDA_kernel(int param, float *device_data, float *device_output) {

    // We use the indices of the current thread and CUDA block
    // to assign each worker to its place in the computation plan:
    int i = blockIdx.x * blockDim.x + threadIdx.x;

    // We declare local variables as in standard C++.
    // They'll be stored in the Thread memory whenever possible:
    float some_value = 0;
    // We access the Shared memory through a raw C++ pointer:
    extern __shared__ float shared_mem[];

    // We handle transfers with a transparent interface:
    some_value    = device_data[i];  // Thread memory <- Device RAM
    shared_mem[i] = device_data[i];  // Shared memory <- Device RAM

    // Computations are written in standard C++ and executed in parallel
    // by all the threads of the CUDA block:
    for(int k = 0; k < param; k++) {
        some_value = some_value + k * shared_mem[i];
        ...
    }

    // We may create checkpoints for all threads in a CUDA block.
    // This may impact performances.
    __syncthreads();

    // We write results back to the Device RAM with:
    device_output[i] = some_value;  // Device RAM <- Thread memory
}

and the main program, executed by the CPU:

// The main C++ program, executed by the CPU:
int main(void) {
    int N = 1024; float *host_data, *host_out, *device_data, *device_out;

    // Allocate memory on the device - the API is a bit heavy:
    cudaMalloc((void**) &device_data,  N * sizeof(float));

    // Device RAM <- Host RAM:
    cudaMemcpy(device_data, host_data, N * sizeof(float),
               cudaMemcpyHostToDevice);

    // Set the parameters of the CUDA block:
    int block_size = 128; int grid_size  = N / block_size;
    int shared_mem_size = 2 * block_size * sizeof(float);
    // Run the GPU kernel:
    My_CUDA_kernel<<<grid_size, block_size, shared_mem_size>>>(...);

    // Wait for the GPU to finish its computations:
    cudaDeviceSynchronize();

    // Host RAM <- Device RAM:
    cudaMemcpy(host_out, device_out, N * sizeof(float),
               cudaMemcpyDeviceToHost);

    // Process and save the result "output array":
    ...

    // Don't forget to free the allocated memory:
    cudaFree(device_data);

    // And exit gracefully:
    return 0;
}

How to read it. The kernel is written for one thread. When the CPU launches it with <<<grid_size, block_size, shared_mem_size>>>, the GPU runs grid_size blocks of block_size threads, here 8 blocks of 128. Each thread finds its place from its block number and its number in the block: i = blockIdx.x * blockDim.x + threadIdx.x. Its local variables live in its registers; __shared__ memory is visible to the threads of its block only; __syncthreads() makes every thread of the block wait until all have reached it. Around the kernel, the CPU allocates device memory, copies the input over the bus, launches, waits, copies the result back and frees the memory: rule 2 of §1.6 is visible in these copies.

Watch out

The code is a sketch and would not run as printed. shared_mem[i] uses the global index i, which goes up to 1023, while each block only has 2 * block_size = 256 floats of shared memory: the shared array must be indexed by threadIdx.x. device_out is never allocated with cudaMalloc (nor freed), and host_data and host_out are never allocated. grid_size = N / block_size works because 1024 is a multiple of 128; the usual form is (N + block_size - 1) / block_size with a test if (i < N) in the kernel. As written, each thread reads back only its own element of the shared memory, so the shared memory brings nothing; it pays off when threads read each other’s values, after a __syncthreads(), as in the tiled scheme of §1.9.

§1.8 Recap on GPUs p. 22 · fr. 21

The slide. “1,000 € \(=\) 1 GPU \(=\) 100 \(\times\) 100 cores with 5 main layers of memory: Large arrays are slow: Memory read/write \(\gg\) Arithmetics. Fast buffers are small: 1 KB \(\simeq\) 100 float numbers per core. To optimize the Shared and Thread memories: C++ or Assembly. What it means in practice. PyTorch, TensorFlow or JAX give scientists arrays that live in the device memory, and a catalogue of kernels written by specialists: matrix products, convolutions, reductions. Each call reads its inputs from the device memory and writes its output back. A computation that chains ten such calls moves its data ten times, even when every call is individually optimal. Only code written for the formula at hand can keep intermediate values in the fast layers (rule 3). The next example shows the price of this rule, and §2 how to obey it without writing C++.

§1.9 A practical example: nearest neighbour search p. 23–26 · fr. 22–25

The slides. Four implementations of the same query, each with its “Bottleneck”. p. 23, on the CPU:

import torch

y = torch.rand(N, D)                  # (N, D)

“Bottleneck: \((\text{M}\times\text{N}\times\text{D})\) CPU operations and memory transfers.” p. 24 moves the arrays to the GPU (x = x_.cuda(), x = y_.cuda()), with the same three lines: “\((\text{M}\times\text{N}\times\text{D})\) Device\(\leftrightarrow\)Thread memory transfers.” p. 25 uses an identity:

import torch
x = torch.rand(M, D).cuda()            # (M, D)
y = torch.rand(N, D).cuda()            # (N, D)

# Use that |x-y|^2 = |x|^2 - 2 (x.y) + |y|^2:
dots = x @ y.T                         # (M, N)
sq_y = (y ** 2).sum(dim=1)             # (N,)

sqdists = - 2 * dots + sq_y.view(1,N)  # (M, N)
indices = sqdists.argmin(dim=1)        # (M,)

“\((\text{M}\times\text{N}\times\text{D})\) GPU computations if \(\text{D} \gt \text{100}\), \((\text{M}\times\text{N})\) Device\(\leftrightarrow\)Thread memory transfers otherwise.” p. 26 draws the last scheme: “On-the-fly, tiled reduction: optimal memory management. Bottleneck: \((\text{M}\times\text{N}\times\text{D})\) GPU computations.”

Four programs, four bottlenecks. The first program stores three arrays with M × N × D, M × N × D and M × N numbers: for M = N = 10,000 points in D = 3, 1.2 GB each. Moving it to the GPU makes the arithmetic parallel but not the traffic: every intermediate array is written to the device memory and read back. The matrix-product trick replaces the M × N × D array by an M × N one and hands the heavy part to a matrix product, the most optimised routine of any library; it still writes and reads M × N numbers. The tiled scheme of p. 26 writes nothing: each thread keeps its running minimum in a register, and the points \(y_j\) are brought to the block in tiles that all its threads share.

The tiled scheme, step by step. In the words of the thesis (§2.2): each block of K threads is given K consecutive points \(x_i\); the loop over \(j\) is cut into tiles of K points; for each tile, the K threads load K points \(y_j\) from the device memory into the shared memory, one each, in a single contiguous read; they wait for each other (__syncthreads()); each thread then compares its \(x_i\) with the K points of the tile, reading them from the shared memory; they wait again, and move to the next tile. “Instead of storing the M-by-N computed values […] in superfluous quadratic buffers […], generating at least 2MN high-latency transfers between the Thread and the Device memories, KeOps maximizes the use of the Shared memory and consumes the relevant values of F […] on-the-spot, in the registers of the CUDA cores.”

§1.10 Recap on nearest neighbour search p. 27 · fr. 26

The slide.

\[ \forall \, i \in [\!\,1, \mathrm{M}\,\!]\,,~~ \text{index}[\,i\,]~\gets~ \arg \min_{j=1}^{\mathrm{N}} ~ \sum_{k=1}^{\mathrm{D}} \big( x[\,i,\,k\,] - y[\,j,\,k\,] \big)^2 \]

“Each improvement provides a \(\times\text{10}\) to \(\times\text{100}\) speed-up. Going even further, for structured data: Clusterize the two point clouds. Sort them to ensure that the clusters are contiguous in memory. Skip whole blocks of the tiled distance matrix. Standard benchmarks (ann-benchmarks.com) and libraries: FAISS…”

Skipping blocks. The tiled scheme computes every entry of the M × N distance matrix. If the points are grouped into clusters stored next to each other, each tile of the matrix compares one cluster of queries with one cluster of points; the distance between two bounding boxes gives a lower bound for all the distances in the tile. Once each query has a candidate neighbour, every tile whose lower bound exceeds the current candidates can be skipped without computing it. The answer is still exact; only the order of the work changes. This is the block-sparse reduction of KeOps, and the same idea as the cell lists of the Vicsek model (Lab 11).

§2.1 Compilation is a major bottleneck p. 29 · fr. 27

The slide. The formula of nearest neighbour search again,

\[ \forall \, i \in [\,1, \mathrm{M}\,]\,,~~ \text{index}[\,i\,]~\gets~ \arg \min_{j=1}^{\mathrm{N}} ~ \sum_{k=1}^{\mathrm{D}} \big( x[\,i,\,k\,] - y[\,j,\,k\,] \big)^2 \]

“We have seen 4-5 different strategies, increasingly fast but complex. Optimal schemes for \(\mathrm{M} \lt \text{1,000}\) look completely different. Naive GPU implementations are often x100–x1,000 too slow. Reaching optimal run times is hard.”

The problem. The formula is one line; the program that runs it well is not. The best program depends on the sizes (M, N, D), on the chip (its number of blocks, the size of its shared memory), and on what is done with the result. Writing a program for each case by hand is not possible, which is why the translation from formulas to machine code, compilation, is where the speed is won or lost.

Why small problems are different. With M \(\lt\) 1,000 queries, the tiled scheme of §1.9 launches fewer than four blocks of 256 threads: an A100 with 108 SMs would sit mostly idle. A good program for this case splits the loop over \(j\) instead, so that many blocks share the work for the same queries and combine their partial minima at the end. Same formula, different plan.

§2.2 Compilation is a deep scientific problem p. 30–31 · fr. 28–29

The slides. p. 30: a map coloured with four colours, “The 4 color theorem”, and the same map as a graph, “4-coloring a planar graph.” p. 31, “Register allocation via k-coloring of the interference graph”:

def f(a):
    b = a ** 2
    c = 5 * b
    d = c + 6
    return d
function(R1):
    R2 = R1 ** 2
    R1 = 5 * R2
    R2 = R1 + 6
    return R2

The graph has four nodes a, b, c, d and three edges labelled “line 1” (a–b), “line 2” (b–c) and “line 3” (c–d); coloured with two colours, it gives “Register 1” to a and c and “Register 2” to b and d.

The problem. A processor computes only on registers, a few dozen named slots next to its arithmetic units. A program has many more variables. The compiler must decide which value lives in which register at each moment, and two values that are needed at the same time cannot share a register. When there are not enough registers, some values must be spilled: written to memory and read back later, the slow path of §1.5.

From programs to graphs. Build a graph with one node per value and an edge between two values that must be kept at the same time: the interference graph. A valid allocation with k registers is a colouring of this graph with k colours, adjacent nodes getting different colours. Map colouring and register allocation are the same problem. Chaitin and colleagues showed in 1981 that every graph is the interference graph of some program: register allocation is as hard as graph colouring, which is NP-complete for k ≥ 3. Compilers therefore use heuristics. The classic one (Chaitin, then Briggs) removes a node with fewer than k neighbours, colours the rest, puts it back with a free colour, and spills when no such node exists.

A subtlety of the slide. Which values “must be kept at the same time” depends on the rule. The graph of p. 31 joins the operand and the result of each line: a and b on line 1, b and c on line 2, c and d on line 3. With the usual rule of compilers (liveness), a value interferes only with the values that are still needed after the line where it is defined. In b = a ** 2, a is never used again, so b may take its register: the graph has no edges and one register suffices, R1 = R1 ** 2. The slide’s rule is the conservative one used when an instruction may not write into its own operand.

§2.3 LLVM, a welcome consolidation p. 32 · fr. 30




The slide. A diagram [Lat11]: “C frontend”, “Fortran frontend” and “Ada frontend” lead to a “Common optimizer”, which leads to an “x86 backend”, a “PowerPC backend” and an “ARM backend”.


The idea. Without a shared middle, every pair of a language and a processor needs its own compiler: 3 × 3 compilers in the drawing. LLVM splits the job in three. A front end translates a language into one intermediate representation (LLVM IR), a common optimizer improves that representation (removing dead code, unrolling loops, allocating registers), and a back end translates it for one processor. A new language needs one front end; a new chip needs one back end; every improvement of the optimizer helps all of them. Clang (C, C++), Rust, Swift and Julia use LLVM, and so do the compilers of GPU languages: NVIDIA’s CUDA compiler is built on it, and so are the shader compilers of several browsers and drivers.





§2.4 Just-in-time compilation p. 33 · fr. 31




The slide. “Dream: turn high-level Python code into an optimal GPU binary. Reality: very hard combinatorial problem, task-specific heuristics. Existing libraries focus on different targets: Shaders for 3D meshes. Convolutions on 2D and 3D grids – with varying filter sizes, channels… Fusion of matrix multiplications and non-linearities for MLPs, Transformers. \(\Longrightarrow\) A critical mass is required to attract investments. What about geometric ML?”


What “just in time” means. A classic compiler translates a program once, before it runs, without knowing the data. A just-in-time (JIT) compiler translates it while it runs, when the sizes and types are known: it can specialise the code for D = 3, unroll the loop over the coordinates, and keep the three coordinates of \(x_i\) in registers. The price is the compilation time, paid at the first call, and a cache to avoid paying it twice. The JavaScript engine of the browser reading this page is itself a JIT compiler: Lab 4 measured the effect of specialising a loop for D = 3 by hand (2.9 times faster than the generic loop), and Lab 7 generates such code automatically.


Why the investment question matters. Deep learning compilers (XLA, TorchInductor, TVM, Triton) are excellent at what deep learning does: dense matrix products, convolutions on grids, element-wise operations fused together. Computations on point clouds, graphs and distributions (distance matrices, kernel sums, nearest neighbours) are a smaller market, and the generic tools serve them less well. KeOps is the author’s answer for one important family.

Kernel k(\(x_i\), \(y_j\)). Numerous transforms.”


Three ways to store a matrix. A dense array stores all M × N entries: simple, but the size grows with the square of the number of points (10 GB of float32 for 50,000 points). A sparse matrix stores only the non-zero entries with their indices: compact for graphs, where each node has a few neighbours, but slow on GPUs because the indices send the cores to scattered memory places (rule 4). A symbolic matrix stores no entry at all: a formula F and the data arrays \(x_i\) and \(y_j\). An entry is computed when it is needed, in registers, and consumed at once by a reduction (a sum, a minimum, an argmin). The memory footprint is that of the data, linear in M + N; the cost is arithmetic, which GPUs have in abundance.


The slogan of p. 47. “Symbolic matrices are to geometric ML what sparse matrices are to graph processing”: the right data structure for distance and kernel matrices, which are dense in value but described by little data.

§2.9 Kriging, splines, Gaussian processes p. 41–42 · fr. 39–40




The slides. p. 41: “A standard tool for regression [Lec18]”, a figure with a target function, training data, a prediction and a 2σ credible region. “Under the hood, solve a kernel linear system:”




\[ (\lambda \,\text{Id} + K_{xx}) \,a ~=~b \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad a ~\gets~(\lambda \,\text{Id} + K_{xx})^{-1} b \]




“where \(\lambda \geqslant 0\) et [sic] \((K_{xx})_{i,j} = k(x_i,x_j)\) is a positive definite matrix.” p. 42: “KeOps symbolic tensors \((K_{xx})_{i,j} = k(x_i,x_j)\): Can be fed to standard solvers: SciPy, GPyTorch, etc. GPytorch on the 3DRoad dataset (N = 278k, D = 3): 7h with 8 GPUs \(\rightarrow\) 15mn with 1 GPU. Provide a fast backend for research codes: see e.g. Kernel methods through the roof: handling billions of points efficiently, by G. Meanti, L. Carratino, L. Rosasco, A. Rudi (2020).”


The problem. Kriging, spline smoothing, Gaussian process regression and kernel ridge regression (Lecture 2, §6) all predict with a weighted sum of kernels centred on the data, \(f(x) = \sum_j k(x, x_j)\, a_j\), whose weights solve the system above. For N = 278,000 points, the matrix \(K_{xx}\) has 7.7 × 10¹⁰ entries: 310 GB in float32. A direct solve (Cholesky) is out of the question.


Matrix-free solvers. The conjugate gradient method solves a symmetric positive definite system using only products \(v \mapsto (\lambda\,\text{Id} + K_{xx})\, v\): each iteration is one kernel sum, exactly the reduction of §2.7, and the matrix is never stored. The number of iterations depends on the condition number of \(\lambda\,\text{Id} + K_{xx}\): a larger \(\lambda\) (more noise, more smoothing) means fewer iterations. GPyTorch adds preconditioners and uses the same products to estimate the log-determinant needed to fit the hyperparameters. With a KeOps backend, each product runs at the speed of p. 38.

§2.10 Particles and samplers p. 43–44 · fr. 41–42




The slides. p. 43, “KeOps lets researchers focus on their models, results and theorems”: “Some applications to dynamical systems [DM08, DFMAT17] and statistics [CDF19] with A. Diez, G. Clarté et P. Packing problem in 2D with 10k repulsive balls. Collective Monte Carlo sampling on the hyperbolic Poincaré disk.”


The Vicsek model. N particles move at constant speed \(v_0\) on a square with periodic sides (a torus). At each step, each particle takes the average direction of the particles within distance r of it, adds a random angle of amplitude η, and moves. With little noise, the particles align and move together; with much noise, they stay disordered; in between, bands of aligned particles form. The polarisation \(\big|\sum_j e^{\mathrm{i}\theta_j}\big| / \text{N}\) measures the order: 1 when all move together, about \(1/\sqrt{\text{N}}\) when the directions are random. Each step is a reduction over pairs: for each i, a sum over the j within distance r.


Brute force or structure. KeOps runs this reduction by brute force: all N² pairs, with the distance test inside the formula. For 100,000 swimmers, that is 10¹⁰ pairs per step, about 15 ms at the speed of p. 38: real time. On a processor, the classic alternative is a cell list: cut the square into cells of side r, and compare each particle only with the particles of its own and the eight neighbouring cells. This is the block skipping of §1.10 in its simplest form. The lab runs both, on the processor and on the graphics card.

§3.1 The Ampere architecture in depth p. 49–51 · fr. 46–48

What an SM is. The streaming multiprocessor is the physical unit that runs CUDA blocks. It is divided into four sub-partitions (the “squads” of the slide), each with its own scheduler, register file and cores. Every clock cycle, a scheduler issues one instruction to a warp, a group of 32 threads that execute it together on 32 lanes; the 16 float32 cores of a sub-partition therefore take two cycles per warp instruction. The registers of all the threads resident on the SM are kept in the 64 KB register files, which is why a kernel that uses many registers per thread leaves room for fewer threads (lower occupancy).

§3.2 Integer and floating-point formats p. 52–54 · fr. 49–51




The slides. p. 52, “Integer cores: handle memory addresses – Float-32 cores: great for 3D geometry”: a 32-bit integer drawn as “1 sign bit, 31 significand bits”, with the range “\(1 - 2^{31}\) — \(+2^{31}\) \(\simeq\) ± 2,147,483,647”; a float32 as “1 sign, 8 exponent, 23 significand”, with “\(2^{-126}\) — \(2^{+127}\) \(\simeq 10^{-38}\) — \(10^{+38}\)” and “\(1 + 2^{-23} \simeq 1.000\,000\,1\)”. p. 53, “Float-64 cores: great for physics simulation”: “1 sign, 11 exponent, 52 significand”, “\(2^{-1022}\) — \(2^{+1023}\) \(\simeq 10^{-308}\) — \(10^{+308}\)”, “\(1 + 2^{-52} \simeq 1.000\,000\,000\,000\,000\,2\)”. p. 54, “Tensor cores: great for CNNs and transformers”: bfloat16, “1 sign, 8 exponent, 7 significand”, “\(1 + 2^{-7} \simeq 1.007\)”, “256 bits \(\simeq\) 4x4 bfloat-16”, and a product of two 4 × 4 tiles added to a third. Under each format, three rows of bits combined with ×, + and =: the fused multiply–add.


How a float works. A floating-point number stores a sign, an exponent e and a significand m: its value is \(\pm (1.m)_2 \times 2^{e - \text{bias}}\). The exponent sets the range, the significand the precision. float32 and bfloat16 share the 8-bit exponent, hence the same range (\(10^{-38}\) to \(10^{38}\)); bfloat16 keeps only 7 bits of significand, so the gap between 1 and the next number is \(2^{-7}\), almost 1%. float16 makes the other trade: 5 exponent bits (largest value 65,504) and 10 significand bits. Tensor Cores multiply bfloat16 or float16 tiles and add the products in float32, which keeps much of the accuracy at a fraction of the cost.


Why integers matter for GPUs. Every array access computes an address, base + 4 * (i * N + j): integer arithmetic. Since the Volta generation, NVIDIA GPUs have separate integer units (16 per quarter on the A100, beside the float32 ones), so that index computations run alongside the floating-point work instead of taking its cycles. A 32-bit index also sets a limit: \(2^{31}\) elements, 8 GB of float32. Beyond, programs need 64-bit indices, a common source of bugs in large-scale codes.

§3.3 Speed, power consumption, versatility and cost p. 55 · fr. 52




The slide. “Trading speed vs. power consumption vs. versatiliy [sic] vs. manufacturing costs”: a frame of the video “How do Smartphone CPUs Work?” by Branch Education, showing a phone chip with its CPU, GPU, DSP and dedicated blocks (“Design Principles: Hardware Accelerators. Faster, Less Power”), and a block diagram of Google’s “Tensor Processing Units”.


The trade-off. A general-purpose core can run anything, but spends most of its energy deciding what to do: fetching and decoding instructions, predicting branches, moving data through caches. A dedicated circuit does one thing with little control logic, so it is faster and uses less power, but it is useless for anything else and costs a new chip design. A phone chip therefore carries a dozen accelerators (video decoding, image processing, neural networks) around a few CPU cores. A GPU sits in the middle: programmable, but organised for one pattern of work. A TPU goes further: its core is a large systolic array of multiply–add units through which matrix tiles flow, very efficient for the matrix products of neural networks and far less flexible than a GPU. The more specialised the chip, the more it matters that the computation fits its mould, which brings back the question of §2.4.





§3.4 The CPU vs. GPU uncoupling occurred in the early 2000s p. 56 · fr. 53




The slide. “The CPU vs. GPU uncoupling occured [sic] in the early 2000’s”: a log scale of “Flops” from 100 M to 100 T against the years 1990 to 2020; a red line “GPUs” rising steeply from about 2000, a blue curve “CPUs” that flattens; along the GPU line, screenshots of Mario games from the 1990s to 2023; along the CPU curve, screenshots of Windows from 3.1 to 10.


What the data say. The two curves are drawn by hand and their levels are not meant literally: the chart puts GPUs near 1 TFLOP/s and CPUs below 10 GFLOP/s in 2020, while an RTX 3090 of 2020 reached 35.6 TFLOP/s in float32 (NVIDIA’s GA102 whitepaper) and, in Rupp’s data, the best Xeon of 2018 reached 9.3 TFLOP/s. The published data behind Lab 1 tell a more precise story: the single-thread performance of CPUs, which is what Windows needs, slowed from 1.45× to 1.12× per year around 2005, while the throughput of GPUs, which is what games (and neural networks) need, kept growing. The peak rates of the best server CPUs followed, several times below the GPUs of the same year, by adding cores and wide vector units. The uncoupling is between two kinds of work, latency-bound and throughput-bound, more than between two kinds of chips.





§3.5 Computing power available to ML researchers p. 57 · fr. 54




The slide. “Computing power available to ML researchers”: a green curve on the same axes, rising slowly through “Focus on math” in the 1990s, faster after “Theano, Caffe / TF, PyTorch…”, faster still after “AWS, GCE / Jean Zay…”, and flattening at “Covid”; four dashed futures after 2022: “Miracle”, “Sustained growth”, “Local supply”, “War in Taiwan”.

S.3 Seven threads




Curse of dimensionality. Lecture 1 showed that least squares fits pure noise exactly once there are as many columns as rows (L1 Lab 2). Lecture 2 added useless columns to its toy data: nearest neighbours collapsed (R² 0.94 → 0.08), forests resisted (L2 Lab 13). Lecture 3 explained why: a Gaussian in high dimension is a thin shell, where all points are at the same distance (L3 Lab 2). The remedy of Lectures 4 and 5 is structure, expert knowledge and neighbourhoods. Lecture 6 measured the curse on distances between samples (L6 Lab 13), and Lecture 7 on the algorithms themselves: skipping blocks of the distance matrix works in 2 or 3 dimensions and fails in 10 (Lab 5).


Similarity is the model. From the similarity matrix of Lecture 1 (L1 Lab 11: the right shape distance recovers the families perfectly, raw coordinates not at all) to the scalings of K-NN in Lecture 2 (L2 Lab 5), the graphs of Lecture 3, the attention kernel of Lecture 4 (L4 Lab 10), the Riemannian metrics of Lecture 5 and the distances between distributions of Lecture 6, the choice of similarity decided the answer. Lecture 7 makes the choice free at run time: a symbolic matrix takes any formula (Lab 7).


Local → global. Convolutions and zoom-outs (Lecture 1), trees and nearest neighbours (Lecture 2), graphs built from neighbourhoods, whose global shape is “the main open challenge” (L3 §1.5), message passing and its bottlenecks (L4 Lab 4), geodesics integrated from a local metric (L5 Lab 12), and transport plans that forget who was next to whom (L6 Lab 10). In Lecture 7, the Vicsek model turns a purely local alignment rule into global order (Lab 11).


Discrete ↔ continuous. Pixels, meshes and physical models of the same heart (Lecture 1); sums over data points that become integrals (Lecture 2); curvature on graphs and on surfaces (L3 Lab 9); a cotangent Laplacian that ignores the mesh where a plain average does not (L4 Lab 3); the Poincaré grid as a discrete half-plane (L5 Lab 4); histograms, samples and weighted clouds as one distribution (L6 Lab 4). Lecture 7 is the practical side: the continuous objects are handled through millions of points, and the loops over pairs must be fast.


Symmetry and curvature. Forgetting similarities puts triangles on a sphere (Lecture 1); models inherit the invariances of their metric (Lecture 2); bending a sheet does not change its Gaussian curvature (Lecture 3); a sphere cannot be combed (L4 Lab 2); the isometries of the half-plane give all its geodesics (Lecture 5); the invariances of the relative entropy make Gaussians hyperbolic, those of transport make them flat (Lecture 6). In Lecture 7, a hyperbolic distance is one line of formula (§2.6).


Honest models. Every lecture compared a method with a simple baseline, and the baseline often won: Mercator’s map inflates Greenland 15.4 times against Congo (L1 Lab 4); a plane beats every flexible model on the slides’ toy data (Lecture 2); a spurious correlation is found in 91% of searches through 100 noise series (L3 Lab 1); K-NN beats the graph networks (Lecture 4); a tree is not a grid of the disk (Lecture 5); least squares on one-hot vectors is as invariant as logistic regression (Lecture 6). Lecture 7 applies the rule to speed: the “x1,000” of p. 9 compares a GPU with one CPU thread (Lab 1), and on road altitudes the altitude of the nearest recorded point beats an exact Gaussian process fitted to all 278,319 points (1.27 m against 1.53 m) (Lab 10).


Scale. Cheap convolutions and wavelets (Lecture 1); K-NN “fast, parallel and GPU-friendly” and kernel systems at scale (Lecture 2); curvature energies on GPUs (Lecture 3); graphs that waste GPU memory bandwidth (L4 Lab 5); the cost of computing geodesics (Lecture 5); transport solvers from the Hungarian method to multiscale Sinkhorn (L6 Lab 12). Lecture 7 is the thread’s main station: memory layers, the four rules, nearest neighbours four ways, symbolic matrices and the benchmark of p. 38.

S.5 How the pieces fit









The arc. The course started from a table with more columns than rows and a warning: without a prior, learning is hopeless (Lecture 1). The priors it proposed are geometric. Choose a similarity that forgets what should not matter (Lectures 1 and 2). Trust neighbourhoods more than coordinates, and let local connections reveal the global shape (Lectures 3 and 4). When the space is curved, measure lengths in its own metric (Lecture 5). When the data are samples, compare distributions, with a distance that notices what matters (Lecture 6). Every one of these ideas ends up as the same computation, a reduction over pairs of points, and Lecture 7 showed that its cost is decided by memory, by compilers and by the choice of number format more than by the formula.


What to take away. Three habits, one per layer of the diagram. Before choosing a method, ask what it assumes where there is no data and what it should ignore. Before trusting a result, compare it with the simplest baseline that could explain it. Before optimising a program, measure where its time goes. The labs of these seven pages are there to practise the three.

What the slides conclude · references




The technical conclusion is the recap of p. 47: turning scientific code into fast programs is an open problem and a valuable skill; symbolic matrices are to geometric machine learning what sparse matrices are to graph processing; GPUs are “more versatile than you think”. The last section (p. 58–65) closes the whole course with the tour of p. 59 and advice for a research career.


What to keep from this page. A GPU is about a hundred blocks of cores with a pyramid of memories, and most scientific programs wait for the memory, not for the arithmetic (Lab 3). The four rules of §1.6 say how not to wait: work in blocks, keep data on the device, do not write intermediate arrays, read contiguous data. Nearest neighbour search shows each rule at work, and the same answer computed four ways (Lab 4). Compilers decide how much of the machine a formula can use; register allocation is graph colouring, hard in general and solved by heuristics (Lab 6). Symbolic matrices let a library generate the right loop for each formula: the benchmark of p. 38 is the proof, on the slide’s data and on other hardware (Labs 7 and 8). Kernel methods, clustering and particle models are all reductions over pairs (Labs 9–11), and speed is not accuracy: on the road data of p. 42, the exact Gaussian process on all 278,319 points, solved in 7 minutes on a laptop, loses to the altitude of the nearest recorded point (1.53 m against 1.27 m) (Lab 10). Peak rates follow from the block diagram, and the machine balance decides which programs can reach them (Lab 12). Smaller number formats trade accuracy for speed, which neural networks tolerate and physics does not (Lab 13).


Points of the slides that needed correcting or qualifying are listed in the section where they occur: the uncited marketing chart of p. 9 and the gap between “x1,000” and measured peak ratios; the RTX 3070 of p. 14, which is not a GA102 bin; “1 Kb/core” (p. 16); the CUDA code of p. 18–21 (shared memory indexed by the global index, device_out and the host arrays never allocated); x = y_.cuda() on p. 24; the values of sqdists on p. 25 and the float32 cancellation of the identity; the interference rule of p. 31; the four formulas of p. 37 (a search within one cloud; x_i[...,0] refused by PyKeOps 2.3; the cosine formula for unit vectors); the two sources of “7h with 8 GPUs → 15mn with 1 GPU” (p. 42); “192 KB of Shared memory” (p. 51); the 32-bit integer of p. 52 (two’s complement, range \(-2^{31}\) to \(2^{31} - 1\)); the levels of the hand-drawn curves of p. 56. """The random generator of the companion pages (mulberry32 + Marsaglia's polar method), in Python.

The page's labs draw their random data with this generator; with the same seed, these functions return the
same numbers, so the snippets below reproduce the page's results instead of results "within sampling error".
"""
import math

M32 = 0xFFFFFFFF


def _imul(a, b):
    return (a * b) & M32


class Rng:
    def __init__(self, seed):
        self.a = (seed & M32) or 1
        self.spare = None

    def uniform(self):                       # uniform in [0, 1), bit-for-bit as the pages' G.rng(seed).uniform()
        self.a = (self.a + 0x6D2B79F5) & M32
        a = self.a
        t = _imul(a ^ (a >> 15), 1 | a)
        t = ((t + _imul(t ^ (t >> 7), 61 | t)) & M32) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & M32) / 4294967296

    def normal(self):                        # Marsaglia's polar method, keeping the spare value as the pages do
        if self.spare is not None:
            s0, self.spare = self.spare, None
            return s0
        while True:
            u, v = 2 * self.uniform() - 1, 2 * self.uniform() - 1
            s = u * u + v * v
            if 0 < s < 1:
                break
        f = math.sqrt(-2 * math.log(s) / s)
        self.spare = v * f
        return u * f

    def int(self, n):
        return math.floor(self.uniform() * n)

    def range(self, lo, hi):
        return lo + (hi - lo) * self.uniform()

    def normals(self, n):
        return [self.normal() for _ in range(n)]

    # ---- vectorised versions (same stream as repeated calls; used for millions of draws) ----
    def uniform_array(self, n):
        import numpy as np
        k = np.arange(1, n + 1, dtype=np.uint64)
        a = (np.uint64(self.a) + k * np.uint64(0x6D2B79F5)) & np.uint64(M32)
        self.a = int(a[-1]) if n else self.a
        m = np.uint64(M32)
        t = ((a ^ (a >> np.uint64(15))) * (a | np.uint64(1))) & m
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> np.uint64(7))) * (t | np.uint64(61))) & m)) & m) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> np.uint64(14))) & m).astype(np.float64) / 4294967296

    def normal_array(self, n):
        import numpy as np
        out = []
        if self.spare is not None and n > 0:
            out.append(self.spare); self.spare = None
        need = n - len(out)
        while need > 0:
            m = max(64, int(need * 0.66) + 64)             # pairs of uniforms to try
            u = self.uniform_array(2 * m)
            x, y = 2 * u[0::2] - 1, 2 * u[1::2] - 1
            s = x * x + y * y
            ok = (s < 1) & (s > 0)
            # keep the stream exact: stop at the pair that completes the request, rewind the rest
            idx = np.flatnonzero(ok)
            pairs_needed = (need + 1) // 2
            if len(idx) >= pairs_needed:
                last = int(idx[pairs_needed - 1])
                self.a = int((self.a - (2 * m - 2 * (last + 1)) * 0x6D2B79F5) & M32)
                idx = idx[:pairs_needed]
            f = np.sqrt(-2 * np.log(s[idx]) / s[idx])
            vals = np.empty(2 * len(idx)); vals[0::2] = x[idx] * f; vals[1::2] = y[idx] * f
            if len(vals) > need:
                self.spare = float(vals[-1]); vals = vals[:-1]
            out.extend(vals.tolist()); need -= len(vals)
        return np.array(out[:n])

kernel void gauss_sum(device const float* x [[buffer(0)]], device const float* y [[buffer(1)]],
                      device const float* v [[buffer(2)]], device float* out [[buffer(3)]],
                      constant uint& n [[buffer(4)]], constant uint& m [[buffer(5)]], constant float& g [[buffer(6)]],
                      uint i [[thread_position_in_grid]], uint l [[thread_position_in_threadgroup]]) {

  float3 xi = float3(0.0);
  if (i < n) xi = float3(x[3*i], x[3*i+1], x[3*i+2]);

def _pad3(a):
    import torch
    a = torch.as_tensor(np.ascontiguousarray(a, dtype=np.float32))
    if a.shape[1] < 3:
        a = torch.cat([a, torch.zeros(a.shape[0], 3 - a.shape[1])], 1)
    return a.contiguous().to("mps")


class GaussSum:
    """v -> (sum_j exp(-g |x_i - y_j|^2) v_j)_i on the Apple GPU; x, y fixed (arrays of shape (n, d), d <= 3)."""
    def __init__(self, x, y, g):
        import torch
        self.x, self.y, self.g = _pad3(x), _pad3(y), float(g)
        self.n, self.m = self.x.shape[0], self.y.shape[0]
        self.out = torch.empty(self.n, device="mps")

    def __call__(self, v):
        import torch
        vv = torch.as_tensor(np.asarray(v, dtype=np.float32)).to("mps")
        metal().gauss_sum(self.x, self.y, vv, self.out, self.n, self.m, self.g, threads=((self.n + 255) // 256) * 256, group_size=256)
        return self.out.cpu().numpy().astype(np.float64)


def argmin_metal(x, y):
    import torch
    xt = torch.as_tensor(np.ascontiguousarray(x, dtype=np.float32)).to("mps"); yt = torch.as_tensor(np.ascontiguousarray(y, dtype=np.float32)).to("mps")
    n, D = xt.shape; m = yt.shape[0]; out = torch.empty(n, dtype=torch.int32, device="mps")
    run = lambda: metal().argmin_sq(xt, yt, out, n, m, D, threads=((n + 63) // 64) * 64, group_size=64)
    return run, out


def cg(matvec, b, lam, tol, maxit=5000):
    """Conjugate gradients for (lam Id + K) a = b with a matrix-free product (same algorithm as the page)."""
    a = np.zeros_like(b); r = b.copy(); p = b.copy(); rr = r @ r; nb = np.sqrt(b @ b); hist = [np.sqrt(rr) / nb]; it = 0
    while it < maxit and np.sqrt(rr) > tol * nb:
        Ap = matvec(p) + lam * p
        alpha = rr / (p @ Ap); a += alpha * p; r -= alpha * Ap
        rr2 = r @ r; beta = rr2 / rr; rr = rr2; p = r + beta * p
        hist.append(np.sqrt(rr) / nb); it += 1
    return a, it, hist

5. Skipping blocks (§1.10, Lab 5)


"""Lab 5 (p. 27): exact nearest neighbours with block skipping. Both clouds (the page's generator, float32) are sorted along a
Morton curve and cut into blocks of 64 consecutive points; for each block of queries, the blocks of points are visited in
increasing order of the distance between bounding boxes, until that lower bound exceeds every current candidate.
Exactness is checked against scipy's cKDTree."""

def morton_sort(A):
    n, D = A.shape; k3 = min(3, D)
    lo, hi = A[:, :k3].min(0).astype(np.float64), A[:, :k3].max(0).astype(np.float64)
    span = np.where((hi - lo) * (1 + 1e-9) > 0, (hi - lo) * (1 + 1e-9), 1.0)
    q = np.zeros((n, 3), dtype=np.int64)
    q[:, :k3] = np.clip(np.floor(1024 * (A[:, :k3].astype(np.float64) - lo) / span), 0, 1023)
    code = np.zeros(n, dtype=np.int64)
    for b in range(9, -1, -1):
        for k in range(3):
            code = code * 2 + ((q[:, k] >> b) & 1)
    perm = np.lexsort((np.arange(n), code))                      # by code, ties by index (as the page)
    return perm, A[perm]

def block_sparse(X, Y, B=64):
    px, SX = morton_sort(X); py, SY = morton_sort(Y); M, D = SX.shape; N = SY.shape[0]
    nbx, nby = -(-M // B), -(-N // B)
    box = lambda S, nb: (np.array([S[b * B:(b + 1) * B].min(0) for b in range(nb)], dtype=np.float64), np.array([S[b * B:(b + 1) * B].max(0) for b in range(nb)], dtype=np.float64))
    xlo, xhi = box(SX, nbx); ylo, yhi = box(SY, nby)
    SX64, SY64 = SX.astype(np.float64), SY.astype(np.float64)
    best = np.full(M, np.inf); idx = np.full(M, -1); visited = 0
    for a in range(nbx):
        gap = np.maximum(0, np.maximum(ylo - xhi[a], xlo[a] - yhi)); lb = (gap ** 2).sum(1)
        order = sorted(range(nby), key=lambda b: lb[b])          # stable, as the page's sort
        i0, i1 = a * B, min(M, (a + 1) * B)
        for b in order:
            if lb[b] > best[i0:i1].max():
                break
            visited += 1
            j0, j1 = b * B, min(N, (b + 1) * B)
            d = ((SX64[i0:i1, None, :] - SY64[None, j0:j1, :]) ** 2).sum(2)
            jm = d.argmin(1); dm = d[np.arange(i1 - i0), jm]
            upd = dm < best[i0:i1]
            best[i0:i1][upd] = dm[upd]; idx[i0:i1][upd] = j0 + jm[upd]
    out = np.empty(M, dtype=np.int64); out[px] = py[idx]
    return out, visited / (nbx * nby), nbx * nby

def parse(src):
    lines = [l.strip() for l in src.split("\n") if l.strip()]
    params = [p.strip() for p in re.match(r"def\s+\w+\s*\(([^)]*)\)\s*:", lines[0]).group(1).split(",") if p.strip()]
    ins = []

        ins.append((d, uses, e))

    names = list(params)
    for d, _, _ in ins:
        if d not in names: names.append(d)

def interference(prog, rule):
    params, ins, ret, names = prog
    live, after = {ret}, [None] * len(ins)
    for k in range(len(ins) - 1, -1, -1):
        after[k] = set(live); d, uses, _ = ins[k]; live.discard(d); live |= set(uses)
    G = nx.Graph(); G.add_nodes_from(names)
    G.add_edges_from(itertools.combinations(sorted(live), 2))          # values live on entry
    for k, (d, uses, _) in enumerate(ins):
        G.add_edges_from((d, v) for v in after[k] if v != d)
        if rule == "slide":
            G.add_edges_from((d, u) for u in uses if u != d)
    maxlive = max([len(live)] + [len(a) for a in after])
    return G, maxlive

def chaitin(G, k):
    deg = {v: G.degree(v) for v in G}; removed, stack = set(), []
    order = list(G.nodes)
    while len(stack) < len(order):
        pick = next((v for v in order if v not in removed and deg[v] < k), None)
        if pick is None:
            pick = max((v for v in order if v not in removed), key=lambda v: deg[v])
        removed.add(pick); stack.append(pick)
        for u in G[pick]:
            if u not in removed: deg[u] -= 1
    color = {}
    for v in reversed(stack):
        used = {color[u] for u in G[v] if color.get(u, -1) >= 0}
        c = next((c for c in range(k) if c not in used), -1); color[v] = c
    return color

            c = dict(zip(nodes, cols))
            if all(c[u] != c[v] for u, v in G.edges):
                return k

def numpy_conv(x, y, b):
    D_xy = np.sum(x ** 2, -1)[:, None] - 2 * (x @ y.T) + np.sum(y ** 2, -1)[None, :]

def torch_conv(x, y, b):
    D_xy = (x * x).sum(-1).unsqueeze(1) - 2 * (x @ y.T) + (y * y).sum(-1).unsqueeze(0)

def torch_chunked(x, y, b):

def hash_u(i, step, seed):
    i = np.asarray(i, dtype=np.uint32)
    s = (i * np.uint32(0x9E3779B1)) ^ np.uint32(((step + 1) * 0x85EBCA77) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ np.uint32(((seed + 7) * 0xC2B2AE3D) & 0xFFFFFFFF)
    s = s * np.uint32(747796405) + np.uint32(2891336453)
    w = ((s >> ((s >> np.uint32(28)) + np.uint32(4))) ^ s) * np.uint32(277803737)
    return ((w >> np.uint32(22)) ^ w).astype(np.float64) / 4294967296

def init(N, L, seed=44):
    r = Rng(seed); a = np.array([[L * r.uniform(), L * r.uniform(), 2 * np.pi * r.uniform() - np.pi] for _ in range(N)])
    return a[:, 0].astype(np.float32), a[:, 1].astype(np.float32), a[:, 2].astype(np.float32)

def step(x, y, th, L, eta, k, seed=5, brute=False):

        dy = y[None, :].astype(np.float64) - y[:, None]; dy -= L * np.round(dy / L)
        A = (dx * dx + dy * dy < 1.0); sc, ss = A @ co, A @ si
    else:

        pairs = cKDTree(P, boxsize=L).query_pairs(1.0, output_type="ndarray")
        sc, ss = co.copy(), si.copy()                                   # each particle is its own neighbour
        np.add.at(sc, pairs[:, 0], co[pairs[:, 1]]); np.add.at(sc, pairs[:, 1], co[pairs[:, 0]])
        np.add.at(ss, pairs[:, 0], si[pairs[:, 1]]); np.add.at(ss, pairs[:, 1], si[pairs[:, 0]])
    t = (np.arctan2(ss, sc) + eta * (hash_u(np.arange(N), k, seed) - 0.5)).astype(np.float32)
    nx = x.astype(np.float64) + 0.03 * np.cos(t.astype(np.float64)); ny = y.astype(np.float64) + 0.03 * np.sin(t.astype(np.float64))

    nx = np.where(nx >= L, 0, nx); ny = np.where(ny >= L, 0, ny)
    return nx.astype(np.float32), ny.astype(np.float32), t

Cheat sheet




What a GPU is (§1.3, §3.1)
About 100 streaming multiprocessors (A100: 108 of 128), each with 4 sub-partitions of 16 float32, 16 int32 and 8 float64 cores and a Tensor Core; warps of 32 threads. Identical blocks give redundancy against defects and binning.


Memory layers (§1.5)
Registers and shared memory: KB per core, a few cycles. Device memory: GB, hundreds of cycles, 1.5 TB/s on an A100. Host memory: across the bus. One random access to main memory costs the time of hundreds of arithmetic operations.


Four rules (§1.6)
1. Block-wise parallelism. 2. Few host ↔ device transfers. 3. Few device ↔ shared/register transfers: no intermediate arrays. 4. Contiguous, block-wise memory accesses.


CUDA and WGSL (§1.7)
Kernel = code of one thread. CUDA block = workgroup; __shared__ = var<workgroup>; __syncthreads() = workgroupBarrier(); blockIdx.x * blockDim.x + threadIdx.x = global_invocation_id. Host: allocate, copy, launch, synchronise, copy back, free.


Nearest neighbours, four ways (§1.9)
(M,N,D) arrays: memory bound. On the GPU: device traffic bound. Matrix product: (M,N) traffic, fast for D > 100, float32 cancellation. Tiled on-the-fly reduction: compute bound, nothing stored. Structured data: sort, cluster, skip blocks.


Compilation (§2.1–§2.4)
Register allocation = colouring the interference graph (NP-complete; Chaitin–Briggs heuristics; spill when colours run out). LLVM: front ends, common optimizer, back ends. JIT: specialise for the sizes known at run time, cache the binary.


Symbolic matrices (§2.5–§2.6)
Dense: M × N numbers. Sparse: (i, j, value) triplets. Symbolic: a formula F(xi, yj) and the data; entries computed in registers and reduced at once (sum, min, argmin, log-sum-exp). KeOps: LazyTensor, any formula, autodiff, linear memory.


The benchmark of p. 38 (§2.7)
Gaussian product of N 3D points on an RTX 2080 Ti: at 20k, NumPy 12.8 s, PyTorch 39.7 ms, KeOps 0.907 ms; PyTorch out of memory at 50k (10 GB per matrix); KeOps 1.41 s at 1M, 7×10¹¹ terms per second.


Kernel methods at scale (§2.9)
Solve (λ Id + Kxx) a = b by conjugate gradients: each iteration one kernel sum, the matrix never stored; iterations grow as λ shrinks or ℓ grows. Prediction f(x) = Σj k(x, xj) aj, another kernel sum.


Rooflines and formats (§3.1–§3.2)
Attainable rate = min(peak, intensity × bandwidth); A100 balance 12.5 FLOP/byte. float32 1/8/23 bits, ε = 2⁻²³; float64 1/11/52, ε = 2⁻⁵²; bfloat16 1/8/7, ε = 2⁻⁷; float16 1/5/10, max 65,504; int32 two’s complement, −2³¹ to 2³¹ − 1.









Self-test




1. Why did the AI revolution of 2010–15 depend on video games?
Games paid for chips that perform millions of independent geometric operations per frame; researchers learned to run scientific code on these affordable GPUs, which made large neural networks trainable (p. 4–5).


2. What does “memory bound” mean?
The program’s time is set by the bytes it moves between memory and cores rather than by its operations; its arithmetic intensity is below the machine balance.


3. Why are GPUs made of about a hundred identical blocks?
Blocks share the work without talking to each other, and a defective block can be switched off so that the rest of the die can still be sold (redundancy and binning, p. 13–14).


4. Name the memory layers of p. 16 from the slowest to the fastest.
Disk (HDD/SSD), host RAM, device RAM, shared block-wise memory, thread-wise registers.


5. State the four rules of GPU programming.
Promote block-wise parallelism; reduce host ↔ device transfers; reduce device ↔ shared/thread transfers; promote block-wise, contiguous memory accesses.


6. What does __syncthreads() do, and when is it needed?
It makes every thread of a block wait until all have reached it; it is needed between writing shared memory and reading what other threads wrote there.


7. What is wrong with shared_mem[i] = device_data[i] on p. 18?
i is the global index (up to N − 1), while each block has its own small shared array; the index must be threadIdx.x.


8. Why is the program of p. 24 not much faster than p. 23 in principle?
It still writes and reads two M × N × D arrays and one M × N array; on the GPU its bottleneck is the device ↔ thread memory traffic.


9. Why does the matrix-product trick lose accuracy?
|x|² − 2x·y + |y|² subtracts large, nearly equal numbers; in float32, far from the origin, the rounding error exceeds the gap between the nearest and the second nearest distance.


10. What does the tiled scheme of p. 26 store in device memory?
Only the inputs and the M results: the M × N values are computed in registers and reduced at once; the points yj are shared by the block through shared memory, tile by tile.


11. When can whole blocks of the distance matrix be skipped exactly?
When the points are sorted into compact clusters and the lower bound from the bounding boxes of a tile exceeds the current candidate distances of all its queries; it works in low dimension (p. 27, Lab 5).


12. What is an interference graph?
A graph with one node per value and an edge between two values that must be kept at the same time; a colouring with k colours is an allocation to k registers.


13. Why do compilers use heuristics for register allocation?
Every graph is the interference graph of some program (Chaitin et al., 1981), so optimal allocation is as hard as graph colouring, NP-complete.


14. What problem does LLVM solve?
It shares one intermediate representation and one optimizer between many languages (front ends) and many processors (back ends).


15. What is the advantage of just-in-time compilation?
The code is generated when the sizes and types are known, so it can be specialised (unrolled loops, values in registers); the cost is the compilation time at the first call.


16. What is a symbolic matrix?
A matrix given by a formula F(xi, yj) and data arrays, whose entries are computed when needed and reduced at once; memory is linear in M + N.


17. What triggers the computation in a KeOps script?
The reduction (.sum(), .argmin(), @…): the arithmetic on LazyTensors only builds the formula.


18. Why does PyTorch run out of memory at 50,000 points on p. 38?
Its program stores N × N matrices: 10 GB each in float32 at N = 50,000, beyond the 11 GB of the card once temporaries are counted.


19. Why is the hyperbolic formula of p. 37 enough for nearest neighbours?
In the upper half-space, the hyperbolic distance is arccosh(1 + |x − y|²/(2 x₀ y₀)), an increasing function of |x − y|²/(x₀ y₀): both have the same minimiser.


20. How does kriging use a kernel sum?
Conjugate gradients solve (λ Id + Kxx) a = b with one product Kxx v per iteration, and predictions are Σj k(x, xj) aj; both are kernel sums over pairs.


21. Why is brute force acceptable for 100k Vicsek swimmers on a GPU but not on a CPU?
10¹⁰ pair tests per step take tens of milliseconds at GPU rates; on a CPU, cell lists that only compare neighbouring cells are much faster.


22. How do you get the A100’s 19.5 TFLOP/s from p. 51?
108 SMs × 4 × 16 float32 cores × 2 operations per multiply–add × 1.41 GHz = 19.5 × 10¹² operations per second.


23. What is the machine balance, and what does it decide?
Peak operation rate divided by memory bandwidth (12.5 FLOP per byte on an A100); programs of lower arithmetic intensity are limited by memory.


24. Why do bfloat16 and float32 have the same range but not the same precision?
Both have 8 exponent bits; bfloat16 keeps 7 significand bits instead of 23, so the gap after 1 is 2⁻⁷ instead of 2⁻²³.


25. Why does physics simulation prefer float64?
Over millions of steps, float32 rounding errors accumulate: the energy of an orbit drifts at about 10⁻⁴ whatever the time step, while float64 keeps improving as the step shrinks (Lab 13).
