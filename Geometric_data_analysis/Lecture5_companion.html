§0 Where this lecture sits

Lecture 3 measured graphs: their dimension, their curvature, and three archetypes (cliques, grids and trees). Lecture 4 tried to learn on graphs and met, among other problems, graphs that do not look like grids. Lecture 5 changes point of view. Instead of working with the discrete graph, it describes a continuous space whose geometry behaves like the graph. The slogan is on p. 3: “Large graphs are best understood as continuous objects.” The central example is the Poincaré disk, a continuous version of a tree.

The first part builds the disk from a thought experiment of Henri Poincaré (1902) and turns it into a formula: a Riemannian metric, a way of measuring small steps that changes from point to point (p. 8–23). The second part finds the shortest paths, the geodesics, with almost no computation, by using symmetries (p. 25–34). The third part shows the same tools at work on surfaces, covariance matrices, rotations, probability distributions, shapes, neural networks and graph embeddings, and ends with the equation that computes geodesics in general (p. 36–48).

This lecture has more mathematics than the previous one, and almost every claim can be checked by computing. The page does so in thirteen labs.

This lecture’s stations. Four threads are central. Similarity is the model: a Riemannian metric says how far apart two nearby points are, and choosing it is choosing the geometry (§1.2); covariance matrices and Gaussians change geometry when the metric changes (§3.2, §3.3). Local → global: lengths of paths, geodesics and the exponential map all follow from the local metric (§3.7). A question that comes back

What depends on the coordinates, and what does not?

The slides’ recap gives the reason for asking: “Changes of coordinates are key to eloquent proofs. Don’t get stuck on one parameterization” (p. 34). A map of the Earth distorts shapes, but distances on the ground do not depend on the map (§1.3). The disk, the hemisphere and the half-plane look nothing alike, yet they measure every length identically (Lab 3). The same square of angles is flat or curved depending on the metric put on it (Lab 7). Gradient descent draws different paths when the parameters of a network are renamed; the natural gradient does not (Lab 10). Each lab separates what belongs to the space from what belongs to the chosen picture of it.

Earlier lectures. Lecture 1 asked what a model should keep and what it should throw away; Lecture 2, what a model assumes where there is no data; Lecture 3, what can be learned from the neighbours alone; Lecture 4, what a simple baseline would do. The question here is older than all of them: Poincaré’s claim that “geometry is only the summary of the laws by which these images succeed each other” (p. 8) says that what matters is what stays the same when the picture changes. Later lectures ask it of probability distributions (L6), where the same Gaussians are Euclidean for optimal transport and hyperbolic for the relative entropy.

Prologue: from graphs to continuous spaces p. 1–6 · fr. 1–6

P.1 Recap of the previous lectures p. 2 · fr. 2

The slide. “To mitigate the curse of dimensionality, we use: Expert knowledge: high-quality features. Relevant families of functions: kernels, convolutional networks. Relevant neighborhood structures: graphs. Main challenge: local implementation ⟹ global understanding. Produce guidelines and insights for practitioners.” Lecture 6 opens with the same slide.

What it means. The curse of dimensionality (L1 §1.1, L3 P.2) says that data alone cannot fill a high-dimensional space; a prior must fill the gaps. The first three items are the priors seen so far. Features choose what to measure (L2 §1.4). Kernels and convolutional networks choose which functions are plausible (L2 §6, L4 §1.1). Graphs choose who is a neighbour of whom (L3 §1.3). All three are local: they describe a point and its surroundings. The “main challenge” is to turn such local descriptions into global answers: how far apart two distant patients are, what path connects them, what their average is.

Where this lecture fits. A Riemannian metric is exactly such a local rule: at each point, it says how to measure a small step. Lengths of paths, shortest paths and averages then follow from it, as §1 to §3 show. The rule is local; its consequences are global.

P.2 Large graphs are best understood as continuous objects p. 3–4 · fr. 3–4

The slides. p. 3 shows three pictures: a “Simple graph” (the six-vertex graph of L3 §1.1), a K-NN graph on the swiss roll labelled “Manifold hypothesis” (L3 P.4, L3 Lab 5), and two meshes of a segmented heart labelled “Physical manifold” [EPW11]. p. 4, “Long history in physics”, adds three examples: the Solar system (an animated orrery [Dat18]), “The ideal gas model” (molecules pushing a piston [Bria]) and “Fluid simulation” (a smoke plume from Covector fluids [NWRC22]).

The idea. A litre of air contains about 3 × 10²² molecules, yet engineers describe it with two numbers, pressure and temperature, related by a continuous law. A fluid is a crowd of particles, yet it is simulated with continuous fields of velocity and pressure. The discrete description is exact but unmanageable; the continuous one is an approximation that is simple, stable and independent of how many particles there are. The slides propose the same move for data: a graph with a million vertices built from samples of a surface (or of a “manifold of plausible data”) is best studied through the continuous space it samples.

Why it helps. A continuous space comes with calculus: lengths, angles, areas, symmetries and differential equations. It also gives an answer that does not depend on the sampling. L4 Lab 3 showed the price of ignoring this: message passing on a graph spreads heat differently on two meshes of the same square, while the cotangent Laplacian, a discretisation of a continuous operator, does not.

When it fails. Not every graph samples a nice space. A social network, or the Cayley graph of §2.4, may have no continuous counterpart with the same large-scale shape. The lecture’s answer is to choose the continuous model to fit the graph: when the graph looks like a tree, use the Poincaré disk (§1–§2).

§1 The Poincaré disk p. 7–23 · fr. 7–22

§1.1 Poincaré’s thought experiment p. 8–12 · fr. 7–11

The problem. Is geometry a fact about space, or a fact about us? In Science and Hypothesis (1902, chapter IV), Henri Poincaré argued for the second answer. The slides quote him: “The Non-Euclidean World. – If geometrical space were a framework imposed on each of our representations considered individually, it would be impossible to represent to ourselves an image without this framework, and we should be quite unable to change our geometry. But this is not the case; geometry is only the summary of the laws by which these images succeed each other” (p. 8). And: “We may thus conceive that beings whose education has taken place in a medium in which those laws would be so different, might have a very different geometry from ours” (p. 9).

The picture next to the quotes. Two walks, with points numbered 0 to 9 (triangles_intro.png, not credited on the slide). Walk three steps, turn, walk three steps, turn, walk three steps. In the upper walk you are back at 0: that is what “our” laws of motion do. In the lower walk the last leg bends and step 9 misses the start. Poincaré’s point is that “straight”, “turn” and “back where I started” are learnt from how such sequences of experiences fit together. Change the laws, and a different geometry is learnt.

The experiment (p. 10–12, quoted from [Nor]). “Suppose, for example, a world enclosed in a large sphere and subject to the following laws: The temperature is not uniform; it is greatest at the centre, and gradually decreases as we move towards the circumference of the sphere, where it is absolute zero. The law of this temperature is as follows: if \(R\) be the radius of the sphere, and \(r\) the distance of the point considered from the centre, the absolute temperature will be proportional to \(R^2 - r^2\).” Further, “all bodies have the same coefficient of dilatation, so that the linear dilatation of any body is proportional to its absolute temperature”, and a body carried elsewhere is “instantaneously in thermal equilibrium with its new environment.” Then: “A moving object will become smaller and smaller as it approaches the circumference of the sphere. … although from the point of view of our ordinary geometry this world is finite, to its inhabitants it will appear infinite. … The steps they take are therefore also smaller and smaller, so that they can never reach the boundary of the sphere.”

From the story to a formula. Take R = 1. An inhabitant’s ruler at distance r from the centre has, in our units, a length proportional to its temperature, 1 − r². A segment of our length |dx| there is therefore worth |dx| / (c (1 − r²)) rulers, for some constant c that only fixes the unit. With c = ½ this is the Poincaré metric of p. 13:

\[ \text{length for the inhabitants} \;=\; 2\,\frac{|\mathrm{d}x|}{1 - r^2}. \]

The inhabitants cannot see the shrinking: their rulers, their bodies and their eyes shrink together. What they can observe is the geometry of their measurements, and it is not Euclidean. The figure of p. 10–12 (credited to [Nor]) shows it: the stick figures shrink towards the rim; the arcs C–C′, D–D′ and E–E′ all pass through the point P without ever meeting the line A–A′. Through a point outside a line there are infinitely many lines that never meet it: Euclid’s parallel postulate fails.

Beyond the slides

Why would the inhabitants call those arcs straight? In the book, Poincaré adds a law that the slides leave out: light crosses media whose refractive index is inversely proportional to R² − r². Light then travels along the same arcs, the circles that meet the boundary at right angles. A path is straight, for the inhabitants, when light follows it and when their rulers find it shortest; both criteria pick the arcs. These are the geodesics of §2.

What goes wrong with triangles. In the plane, three legs of equal length with two turns of 120° close up into an equilateral triangle. In Poincaré’s world they do not: to close the triangle you must turn by more, because the angles of a hyperbolic triangle add up to less than 180°. For the metric of p. 13, the defect is exactly the area of the triangle: area = π − (sum of the angles). An equilateral triangle with side L has angles α with

\[ \cos\alpha \;=\; \frac{\cosh L}{1 + \cosh L}, \]

which tends to ½ (α = 60°) when L is small and to 1 (α = 0) when L is large. Small triangles are nearly Euclidean; large ones are thin and have area close to π. Lab 1 makes the walk of p. 8 in the disk.

Watch out

Poincaré’s world is a ball in space (“a world enclosed in a large sphere”), with a temperature that depends on the distance to the centre; the slides draw a disk. Nothing is lost: every plane through the centre is a copy of the disk with the same law, and the three-dimensional version is the Poincaré ball. The slide also keeps the hyphen of the book’s line break (“tem- perature”, p. 11).

§1.2 A metric that is locally Euclidean p. 13–14 · fr. 12–13

The definition (p. 13). “With \(x^2 + y^2 \lt 1\), we define:”

\[ \mathrm{d}\Big( (x,y) \rightarrow (x,y) + (\mathrm{d}x, \mathrm{d}y) \Big) ~=~ 2 \,\frac{ \sqrt{\mathrm{d}x^2 + \mathrm{d}y^2}}{1 - (x^2 + y^2)} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathrm{d}\Big( (x,y) \rightarrow (x,y) + (\mathrm{d}x, \mathrm{d}y) \Big) \\ &\qquad =~ 2 \,\frac{ \sqrt{\mathrm{d}x^2 + \mathrm{d}y^2}}{1 - (x^2 + y^2)} \end{aligned} \]

“In other words:”

\[ \mathrm{d}^2\Big( (x,y) \rightarrow (x,y) + (\mathrm{d}x, \mathrm{d}y) \Big) ~=~ 4 \,\frac{ \mathrm{d}x^2 + \mathrm{d}y^2}{\big( 1 - (x^2 + y^2)\big)^2} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathrm{d}^2\Big( (x,y) \rightarrow (x,y) + (\mathrm{d}x, \mathrm{d}y) \Big) \\ &\qquad =~ 4 \,\frac{ \mathrm{d}x^2 + \mathrm{d}y^2}{\big( 1 - (x^2 + y^2)\big)^2} \end{aligned} \]

“This local Euclidean metric is the Riemannian metric:”

\[ \| (\mathrm{d}x, \mathrm{d}y) \|_{(x,y)}^2 ~=~ \big\langle\, (\mathrm{d}x, \mathrm{d}y)\,,\, g_{(x,y)}\,(\mathrm{d}x, \mathrm{d}y) \,\big\rangle, \]
\[ g_{(x,y)} ~=~ \begin{pmatrix} 4/\big( 1 - (x^2 + y^2)\big)^2 & 0 \\ 0 & 4/\big( 1 - (x^2 + y^2)\big)^2 \end{pmatrix}. \]

What a Riemannian metric is. At each point x of a space, a symmetric positive definite matrix gx, varying smoothly with x. It measures a small step v taken at x by the norm ‖v‖x = √(vᵀ gx v). In the plane, gx is the identity everywhere. Here it is the identity times 4/(1 − r²)², a factor that is 4 at the centre and grows without bound near the rim. The picture of p. 13 draws the same step (dx, dy) twice: large at the centre, small near the rim, where it has the same length for the inhabitants.

Why “locally Euclidean”. Near a given point, g is almost constant, so small distances are Euclidean distances multiplied by a constant factor. Angles are even exactly Euclidean, because g is a multiple of the identity (the metric is conformal): the picture shows angles faithfully and lengths not at all.

Lengths of curves (p. 14). “If \(\gamma : [0,1] \rightarrow B(0,1)\) is a smooth path, we define:”

\[ \ell(\gamma) ~=~ \int_0^1 \|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|_{\gamma(t)} \,\mathrm{d}t. \]

“For the straight path \(\gamma(t) = (t, 0)\), we find:”

\[ \begin{aligned} \ell(\gamma) ~&=~ \int_0^1 \|(1, 0)\|_{(t,0)} \,\mathrm{d}t ~=~ \int_0^1 \frac{2}{1 - t^2} \,\mathrm{d}t \\ &=~ \int_0^1 \frac{2}{(1 + t)(1 - t)} \,\mathrm{d}t ~=~ +\infty. \end{aligned} \]

“The Poincaré disk is a universe in a nutshell” (Hamlet: “I could be bounded in a nutshell, and count myself a king of infinite space”). The integral stopped at r gives the distance from the centre to a point at Euclidean radius r: 2 artanh r = ln((1 + r)/(1 − r)). Every factor of 10 closer to the rim adds about ln 10 ≈ 2.3 to the distance.

From lengths to distances. The distance between two points is the infimum of the lengths of the paths that join them; a path that achieves it is a geodesic (§2). For the disk there is a closed formula, which the slides do not give and the labs use:

\[ d(u, v) \;=\; \arcosh\Big( 1 + \frac{2\,|u - v|^2}{(1 - |u|^2)(1 - |v|^2)} \Big). \]

Circles grow exponentially. The circle of hyperbolic radius ρ about the centre is the Euclidean circle of radius r = tanh(ρ/2). Its hyperbolic circumference is 2πr × 2/(1 − r²) = 2π sinh ρ, and the disc it bounds has area 2π(cosh ρ − 1). In the plane these are 2πρ and πρ². For large ρ, sinh ρ ≈ eρ/2: the circumference grows like π eρ, like the number of vertices at depth k of a binary tree, 2k = ek ln 2. This is the growth that Lecture 3 measured on trees (L3 Lab 7), and the first sense in which the disk is a “continuous tree” (p. 34).

Key idea

A Riemannian metric is a rule for measuring small steps, one positive definite matrix gx per point. Everything else, lengths, distances, geodesics, areas and curvature, is computed from it. The disk of the picture is finite; the geometry defined on it is infinite. What a picture shows depends on the coordinates; what the metric measures does not.

Beyond the slides

The metric 4 |dx|² / (1 − |x|²)² has Gaussian curvature −1 at every point, whereas the sphere of radius 1 has +1. By the Theorema Egregium of L3 §3.3, this curvature can be computed from the metric alone, without looking at the disk from outside. For a conformal metric e2φ(dx² + dy²) the formula is K = −e−2φ Δφ; here eφ = 2/(1 − r²), and the computation gives K = −1. The Gauss–Bonnet theorem then turns the curvature into the angle defect of Lab 1: for a geodesic triangle, π − (sum of the angles) = −∫K dA = area.

§1.3 Tissot’s indicatrix p. 15 · fr. 14

The slide. A diagram of the Mercator projection from the Encyclopædia Britannica [Brib] and Stefan Kühn’s Mercator map with Tissot’s indicatrices [Kü04]: red discs of equal size on the ground, drawn larger and larger towards the poles. “Tissot’s indicatrix at location \((x,y)\) is a unit ball for the local metric. This ellipsoid allows us to depict distortions in cartography and fully describes a Riemannian metric on the 2D map.”

Why an ellipse describes a metric. A map sends each point of the Earth to a point (x, y) of the page. Distances on the ground define, at each (x, y), a norm on the small steps of the page: ‖v‖ = the ground length of the step v. This norm is √(vᵀ g v) for a symmetric positive definite 2 × 2 matrix g. Its unit ball {v : vᵀ g v ≤ ε²}, scaled to a visible size ε, is an ellipse: that is Tissot’s indicatrix, the image of a small circle drawn on the ground. Conversely, an ellipse centred at the point (its two axes and its orientation) determines g. Knowing the indicatrix everywhere is knowing the metric.

Mercator, measured. L1 Lab 4 computed the indicatrices of four world maps. On the Mercator map they are circles, because the projection preserves angles, but their area grows like 1/cos² of the latitude: × 4 at 60°, × 33 at 80°. Greenland looks as large as Africa. The Poincaré disk is the same kind of map: conformal, so its indicatrices are circles, with a radius (1 − r²)/2 times the step, shrinking towards the rim. Lab 3 draws the same indicatrices on three different maps of one geometry.

Watch out

§1.4 Three equivalent models p. 16–22 · fr. 15–21

The slides (p. 16–20). “Stereographic projections define bijections between the segment \(I\), the half-circle \(J\) and the half-line \(H\). If we endow \(I\) with the Poincaré metric:”

\[ \ABC{\|(\mathrm{d}x)\|_{(x)\in I} ~=~ 2\, \frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{d}x^2}}{1 - x^2}}~, \]

“then \(J\) and \(H\) are endowed with:”

\[ \SIM{\|(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}z)\|_{(x,z)\in J} ~=~ \frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{d}x^2+\mathrm{d}z^2}}{z}}, \qquad \DEF{\|(\mathrm{d}z)\|_{(z) \in H} ~=~ \frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{d}z^2}}{z}}. \]

The figure: \(I\) is the segment from (−1, 0) to (1, 0); \(J\) is the upper half of the unit circle; \(H\) is the vertical half-line x = 1, z > 0. A point x of \(I\) is sent to \(J\) by the line from (0, −1) through x; a point of \(J\) is sent to \(H\) by the line from (−1, 0) through it. Page 18 follows a small step d at x = 0: it becomes 2d on the half-circle, at (0, 1), and 4d on the half-line, at height 2.

Why the three metrics agree. Write the maps out. The first sends x to (X, Z) = (2x, 1 − x²)/(1 + x²), so a step dx has length 2|dx|/(1 + x²) on \(J\), where Z = (1 − x²)/(1 + x²): dividing by Z gives 2|dx|/(1 − x²). The composition of both maps sends x to height z = 2(1 − x)/(1 + x) on \(H\); a step dx has length 4|dx|/(1 + x)² there, and dividing by z gives 2|dx|/(1 − x²) again. Each model inherits its metric from the previous one through the map, so corresponding paths have equal lengths: the three are one geometry drawn three ways. At x = 0 the Euclidean lengths d, 2d and 4d are divided by 1, 1 and 2, giving 2d in all three.

In two dimensions (p. 21). The same construction gives the disk \(I\), the hemisphere \(J\) and the half-plane \(H\):

\[ \begin{aligned} &\ABC{\|(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)\|_{(x,y)\in I} = 2\, \frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{d}x^2+\mathrm{d}y^2}}{1 - (x^2+y^2)}}, \\ &\SIM{\|(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}z)\|_{(x,y,z)\in J} = \frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{d}x^2+\mathrm{d}y^2+\mathrm{d}z^2}}{z}}, \\ &\DEF{\|(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}z)\|_{(y,z) \in H} = \frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{d}y^2+\mathrm{d}z^2}}{z}}. \end{aligned} \]

The disk sits in the plane z = 0, the hemisphere above it, and the half-plane is the vertical plane x = 1 with coordinates (y, z). The maps are (x, y) ↦ (2x, 2y, 1 − x² − y²)/(1 + x² + y²), from the south pole, and (X, Y, Z) ↦ (2Y, 2Z)/(1 + X), from (−1, 0, 0). Page 22 shows Tissot’s indicatrices in the three models: discs of equal hyperbolic size, circles in every model because stereographic projections preserve angles. Each model also has a closed formula for the distance:

\[ \begin{aligned} d_I(u,v) &= \arcosh\Big(1 + \frac{2|u-v|^2}{(1-|u|^2)(1-|v|^2)}\Big), \\ d_J(X,Y) &= \arcosh\Big(1 + \frac{|X-Y|^2}{2\,Z_X Z_Y}\Big), \\ d_H(a,b) &= \arcosh\Big(1 + \frac{|a-b|^2}{2\,z_a z_b}\Big). \end{aligned} \]

Why three models? Each makes something easy. The disk is bounded and has a visible centre, handy for pictures and for rotations about the centre. The half-plane has the simplest formula, invariant under horizontal translations and scalings (§2.3). The hemisphere connects the other two and makes rotations about the vertical axis obvious (§2.3). The recap of p. 34 draws the lesson: “Don’t get stuck on one parameterization.”

§2 Geodesics on the Poincaré disk p. 24–34 · fr. 23–32

§2.1 Geodesics on the grid p. 25 · fr. 23

The problem. A geodesic is a path that is locally the shortest. In the plane geodesics are straight lines, and nobody needs a theory to find them. In the disk the metric changes from point to point, so the shortest path between two points has no reason to be straight in the picture. The slides first ask the question on the discrete grid of §1.5, where lengths are just numbers of steps.

The slide. Two pictures of the grid. On the left, two vertices of the row of four: the red path goes three steps sideways, the green path goes up, across and down. “The green and red paths have the same length”: three steps each. On the right, the first and last vertices of the row of eight: sideways takes seven steps, up two rows, across one and down two takes five. “Going up is faster than travelling sideways.”

Why. Moving sideways by n vertices costs n steps in the row. Going up m rows first costs m steps, but the gap there is only about n / 2m vertices, and coming down costs m more. The total, 2m + n/2m, is smallest for m ≈ log2 n, which gives about 2 log2 n steps instead of n. The first and last vertices of the row of 256 are 255 steps apart sideways and 15 steps apart through the top.

The continuous version. In the half-plane, two points at the same height z and horizontal distance Δy are at hyperbolic distance 2 asinh(Δy / 2z), about 2 ln(Δy / z) when Δy is large. The geodesic between them is a half-circle that climbs to height Δy/2. The number of steps on the grid, about 2 log2 n = 2 ln n / ln 2, is therefore about the hyperbolic distance divided by ln 2: the grid is a coarse copy of the half-plane. Lab 4 measures how coarse.

§2.2 Vertical geodesics p. 26 · fr. 24

The slide. “If the source and target points belong to the vertical axis \(y = 0\), the shortest path is straight:”

\[ \begin{aligned} \ABC{\ell\big(y(t),z(t)\big)} &=~ \int_0^1 \frac{\sqrt{\dot{y}(t)^2 + \dot{z}(t)^2}}{z(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t \\ &\leqslant~ \int_0^1 \frac{\sqrt{\dot{z}(t)^2}}{z(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t ~=~ \SIM{\ell\big( 0, z(t) \big)}~. \end{aligned} \]

The picture draws a red wiggly path between two points of the axis and the green straight segment that is its shadow on the axis.

The argument. Take any path (y(t), z(t)) between two points of the axis, and replace it by its shadow (0, z(t)), which has the same end points. At every instant √(ẏ² + ż²) ≥ |ż|, so the shadow is never longer. Among paths along the axis, one that goes straight without turning back is shortest, and its length is |ln(z1/z0)|. So the distance between (0, z0) and (0, z1) is |ln(z1/z0)|, and the straight segment is the geodesic. In Lab 5, with the preset of p. 26, the straight segment from height 2 to height 0.5 has length ln 4 = 1.386 and the red wiggly path 2.528.

Watch out

The inequality on p. 26 points the wrong way. Since √(ẏ² + ż²) ≥ |ż|, the length of the wiggly path is at least the length of its shadow:

\[ \ABC{\ell\big(y(t),z(t)\big)} ~\geqslant~ \int_0^1 \frac{|\dot{z}(t)|}{z(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t ~=~ \SIM{\ell\big( 0, z(t) \big)}. \]

The source slides.md has \leqslant. With ⩽ the slide would prove that the straight path is the longest. The argument and the conclusion are right; only the symbol is wrong.

§2.3 Symmetries give all the geodesics p. 27–32 · fr. 25–30

The strategy. A symmetry of the metric (an isometry) maps each path to a path of the same length, so it maps geodesics to geodesics. One geodesic is known: the vertical axis of the half-plane. The slides move it around with symmetries, and every geodesic of the disk appears. No differential equation is solved.

Centres (p. 27). “Using our stereographic projections, the North pole \((x,y,z) = (0,0,+1)\) of the hemisphere corresponds to: the center \((x,y) = (0,0)\) of the disk, the point \((y,z) = (0, +2)\) of the half-plane.” With the maps of §1.4: (0, 0) goes to (0, 0, 1), and (X, Y, Z) = (0, 0, 1) goes to (2Y, 2Z)/(1 + X) = (0, 2).

Rotations (p. 28–29). “The vertical axis in the half-plane is equivalent to the ‘Greenwich meridian’ in the hemisphere. Since the Poincaré metric

\[ \SIM{\|(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}z)\|_{(x,y,z)\in J} ~=~ \frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{d}x^2+\mathrm{d}y^2+\mathrm{d}z^2}}{z}} \]

is invariant by rotations: \((x,y,z) \mapsto (\cos(\theta) \, x, \sin(\theta)\,y, z)\), all great circles that pass through the North pole are also geodesic curves.” The vertical axis of the half-plane comes from the half great circle of the hemisphere in the plane Y = 0, through the north pole. Rotating the hemisphere about its vertical axis changes neither the height z nor the Euclidean length of a step, so it preserves the metric and turns this meridian into every other one.

Watch out

The map written on p. 28–29, (x, y, z) ↦ (cos(θ) x, sin(θ) y, z), is not a rotation: for θ = 90° it sends every point to the plane x = 0, and it does not preserve lengths. The rotation by θ about the vertical axis is

\[ (x, y, z) \;\mapsto\; \big(\cos(\theta)\,x - \sin(\theta)\,y,\;\; \sin(\theta)\,x + \cos(\theta)\,y,\;\; z\big). \]

It keeps z and the Euclidean length of every step, hence the metric |d(x, y, z)|/z. The conclusion of the slide stands.

Meridians in the three models (p. 30). The meridians of the hemisphere become the “Diameters.” of the disk and, in the half-plane, the “Half-circles that pass through \((y, z) = (0, +2)\).” (together with the vertical axis). They are circles because stereographic projections send circles to circles or lines; they meet the horizontal axis at right angles because the meridians meet the equator at right angles and the projections preserve angles.

Translations and scalings (p. 31). “Half-circles that are perpendicular to the horizontal axis are all geodesics. This is because the Poincaré metric

\[ \DEF{\|(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}z)\|_{(y,z) \in H} ~=~ \frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{d}y^2+\mathrm{d}z^2}}{z}} \]

is invariant by horizontal translations: \((y,z) \mapsto (y+\Delta y, z)\) and scalings with a positive constant \(a \gt 0\): \((y,z) \mapsto (ay, az)\).” A scaling multiplies both the step and the height by a, so their ratio is unchanged. Now take any half-circle of centre c on the axis and radius R: translate it by −c and scale it by 2/R. It becomes the half-circle of radius 2 centred at the origin, which passes through (0, 2) and is the image of a meridian. So every half-circle perpendicular to the axis is a geodesic, and so is every vertical line.

Summary (p. 32). “Up-and-down paths” on the grid, “Half-circles and vertical lines” on the half-plane, “Orthogonal circles and diameters” on the disk. Through two points of the disk there is exactly one such curve, so geodesics are unique, as in the plane.

Beyond the slides

Write a point of the half-plane as a complex number w = y + iz. Every map w ↦ (aw + b)/(cw + d) with real a, b, c, d and ad − bc > 0 is an isometry, and every orientation-preserving isometry is of this form. Translations are [[1, Δy], [0, 1]], scalings [[√a, 0], [0, 1/√a]], and the rotation by an angle φ about the point (0, 2), the image of the slides’ rotation about the north pole, is [[cos(φ/2), 2 sin(φ/2)], [−sin(φ/2)/2, cos(φ/2)]]. These matrices are the group SL2(ℝ), up to sign. The integer matrices among them form SL2(ℤ), the group of §2.4. Lab 5 applies all three kinds of isometries to a pair of points and their paths.

§2.4 A continuous tree p. 33 · fr. 31

The slide. Two pictures. “Circle Limit IV by M.C. Escher: a regular tiling of the Poincaré disk” (1960): angels and devils that shrink towards the rim, all of the same hyperbolic size and shape. And the Cayley graph of SL2(ℤ), already drawn in L3 §3.4: “The Cayley graph of \(\SL_2(\Z)\) is to the Poincaré disk what a regular grid is to the Euclidean plane.”

What the analogy means. The square grid is the orbit of a point under the translations of the plane by integer vectors, ℤ2; its edges join points that differ by one generator. SL2(ℤ), the 2 × 2 integer matrices of determinant 1, acts on the half-plane by the isometries of §2.3, w ↦ (aw + b)/(cw + d). The orbit of a point, with edges for the generators u = [[1, 1], [−1, 0]], s = [[0, −1], [1, 0]] and their inverses, is a drawing of the Cayley graph inside the hyperbolic plane. It grows exponentially, like the disk: its spheres have 1, 5, 10, 12, 16, 24, 32, 48 and 64 elements up to word length 8, about √2 times more at each step (L3 Lab 7).

Where the analogy is loose. Every point of the plane is within 0.71 of a grid point, and the number of grid steps between two points is their Euclidean distance up to a constant factor. SL2(ℤ) does not cover the hyperbolic plane that way. Its quotient, the “modular surface”, has a cusp: an infinitely long, ever thinner horn. In the plane, this shows as regions near the boundary that the orbit never enters. The matrix Tn = [[1, n], [0, 1]] is a product of 2n generators, yet it only moves a point by a hyperbolic distance of about 2 ln n. The slide’s own picture of the Cayley graph, a tree of little squares and hexagons, tells the truth: SL2(ℤ) is essentially a free product of a group of order 2 and a group of order 3, and its Cayley graph has the large-scale shape of a tree. A genuine “regular grid” of the disk is a regular tiling such as the triangulation with 7 equilateral triangles at each vertex, {3,7} (the combinatorial pattern already met in L3 Lab 7). §3 Riemannian geometry, in practice p. 35–48 · fr. 33–45

§3.1 A convenient way of working with surfaces: the torus p. 36–37 · fr. 33–34

The slides. “A convenient way of working with surfaces”: the torus “As a surface embedded in 3D”, with its equators and meridian, and “The torus as a flat 2D square”, whose opposite sides are glued (p. 36). Then “Geodesics on the donut” and “The torus as a curved 2D square” (p. 37): a fan of geodesics leaving one point of the outer equator, drawn on the donut and in the square.

One square, two metrics. Describe a point of the donut by two angles: u around the central axis and v around the tube (v = 0 on the outer equator, v = ±π on the inner one). With a central radius R and a tube radius r, the point is ((R + r cos v) cos u, (R + r cos v) sin u, r sin v), and a small step (du, dv) has length

\[ \mathrm{d}s^2 \;=\; (R + r\cos v)^2\,\mathrm{d}u^2 \;+\; r^2\,\mathrm{d}v^2. \]

The square of angles is the same in both pictures of the slides; what changes is the metric on it. With the metric above, the square is “curved”: the circles of constant v are longer outside than inside. With a constant metric it is “flat”, its geodesics are straight lines, and it is a different surface: a flat torus, which cannot be bent smoothly into a donut in space. Working on the square with the right metric lets one compute on the donut without ever leaving two dimensions.

What the geodesics do. The metric does not depend on u, so the momentum pu = (R + r cos v)² u̇ is conserved along every geodesic. This is Clairaut’s relation for surfaces of revolution: (distance to the axis) × cos(angle with the parallel) is constant. A geodesic that leaves the outer equator (distance R + r) at an angle α from it has pu = (R + r) cos α. It can reach the inner equator (distance R − r) only if (R + r) cos α ≤ R − r. Otherwise it oscillates around the outer equator forever.

The donut’s Gaussian curvature is K = cos v / (r (R + r cos v)): positive outside, negative inside, and its integral is zero, as Gauss–Bonnet demands for a torus (L3 Lab 9 found the same on a mesh). No smooth surface in space can be a flat torus, because every closed smooth surface in space has a point where K > 0; less smooth embeddings do exist (Nash and Kuiper), and were drawn for the first time by the Hévéa project in 2012.

§3.2 Covariance matrices and rotations p. 38 · fr. 35

The slide. “Covariance matrices: geodesics for the Euclidean, affine-invariant and log-Euclidean metrics” [AFPA06]: three rows of ellipsoids that morph from a flat ellipsoid into a thin tilted one, and three plots of their volume. “3D rotations: geodesics for the Lie group structure” [KSA19]: a frame of three arrows turning.

The problem. Covariance matrices describe the spread of data; in diffusion MRI, the tensors of Arsigny et al. describe how water diffuses in each voxel. They are symmetric positive definite (SPD) matrices, a curved set: the average of two SPD matrices is SPD, but their difference is not, and a straight line through two of them leaves the set when extended. Averaging them, interpolating them or measuring how far apart they are all need a metric.

Three metrics (and a fourth). For SPD matrices A and B:

  • Euclidean: d(A, B) = ‖A − B‖, geodesic (1 − t)A + tB.
  • Log-Euclidean: d(A, B) = ‖log A − log B‖, geodesic exp((1 − t) log A + t log B).
  • Affine-invariant: d(A, B) = ‖log(A−½ B A−½)‖, geodesic A½ (A−½ B A−½)t A½.
  • Wasserstein (Bures), not on the slide but central in Lecture 6: the optimal transport distance between the centred Gaussians N(0, A) and N(0, B).

Why it matters: the swelling effect. Along the Euclidean geodesic, the determinant, which measures the volume of the ellipsoid, can rise far above that of both ends: two thin ellipsoids pointing in different directions average into a fat one. For diffusion tensors this invents diffusion that neither voxel shows. Along the log-Euclidean and affine-invariant geodesics the determinant is exactly det(A)1−t det(B)t, a geometric interpolation. The slide’s volume plots show this qualitatively.

What “affine-invariant” means. Changing units or mixing the coordinates of the data by an invertible matrix M changes every covariance A into M A Mᵀ. The affine-invariant distance does not change; the others do. This is the recurring question of this page, answered by a metric: this distance depends on the data, not on the coordinates used to record them.

Rotations. The rotations of space form a curved set too, SO(3). Its geodesics, for the metric that treats all rotations alike (the “Lie group structure”), are rotations at constant speed about one fixed axis: R(t) = exp(t log R1) from the identity to R1. Averaging the matrices leaves the set of rotations; interpolating Euler angles stays in it but takes a detour.

§3.3 Probability distributions p. 39 · fr. 36

The slide. “Probability distributions – see Lecture 6!” [PC18]. Two panels of a figure from Peyré and Cuturi: “Gaussians + Wasserstein metric = Euclidean” and “Gaussians + relative entropy = Poincaré”. Each panel shows a path from a narrow Gaussian (red) to a wide one further right (blue), in the half-plane of means m and standard deviations σ, and the densities along the path.

The set of Gaussians is a half-plane. A one-dimensional Gaussian N(m, σ²) is a point (m, σ) with σ > 0. Which metric to put on this half-plane depends on how we compare distributions.

  • Optimal transport moves mass: the cost of turning N(m1, σ1²) into N(m2, σ2²) is W2² = (m1 − m2)² + (σ1 − σ2)². The half-plane is Euclidean; geodesics are straight segments, along which the Gaussian slides and widens steadily.
  • The relative entropy (Kullback–Leibler divergence) compares likelihoods. It is not a distance, but for nearby Gaussians it is half the square of one: KL(N(m, σ²) ‖ N(m + dm, (σ + dσ)²)) = (dm² + 2 dσ²) / (2σ²) + … The metric (dm² + 2 dσ²)/σ², the Fisher–Rao metric, is the Poincaré half-plane metric in the coordinates (m/√2, σ), up to a factor 2. Its geodesics are half-circles in those coordinates.

Going up is faster, again. Along the Fisher–Rao geodesic the Gaussian first widens, moves sideways while it is wide, and then narrows: exactly the up-and-down path of the grid (§2.1). A narrow Gaussian cannot move sideways cheaply, because the relative entropy between two narrow Gaussians with different means is large; a wide one can. Optimal transport sees no such penalty and slides the Gaussian directly.

§3.5 Network architectures are “projections” p. 43 · fr. 40

The slide. The Britannica cylinder of p. 15 again, and a drawing: a grid of equal blue discs in the “Space of parameters”, an arrow “Architecture”, and red ellipses of all sizes and orientations in the “Space of estimators”. “Standard gradient descent on the parameters ⟺ Riemannian gradient descent on the network. Core idea behind the natural gradient, neural tangent kernels, (Wasserstein) gradient flows…”

The map. A network with parameters θ computes a function fθ; the architecture is the map θ ↦ fθ. Like a map of the Earth, it distorts: a small disc of parameters becomes an ellipse of functions, stretched in some directions and squashed in others. These ellipses are the Tissot indicatrices of the architecture; they are the red ellipses of the slide.

Why gradient descent is Riemannian. Gradient descent moves θ against the gradient of the loss L(fθ). With J = ∂fθ/∂θ, this gradient is Jᵀ ∇fL, and the function moves by

\[ \dot f \;=\; J\,\dot\theta \;=\; -\,J J^{\mathsf{T}}\;\nabla_f L. \]

This is gradient descent in the space of functions for the metric (J Jᵀ)−1: the architecture decides which directions of function space are cheap. The matrix J Jᵀ, evaluated at the training points, is the neural tangent kernel. The natural gradient removes the distortion: it moves θ by (JᵀJ)−1 Jᵀ ∇fL, so that, where J is invertible, ḟ = −∇fL, the plain gradient in function space. It does not depend on how the network is parameterised. (Amari’s natural gradient uses the Fisher metric of the model’s output distribution; for a least-squares loss it reduces to this Gauss–Newton step.) Wasserstein gradient flows apply the same idea to distributions, with the metric of optimal transport (Lecture 6).

§3.6 Embedding graphs in the disk p. 44 · fr. 41

The slide. “Graph and point cloud embeddings” [SDSGR18, NK17]: “Embedding a tree in the Poincaré disk”, a small tree drawn with geodesic arcs (Sala, De Sa, Gu and Ré 2018), and the “WordNet mammals subtree” embedded by Nickel and Kiela (2017), with labels such as Mammal, Carnivore, Ungulate, Rodent. “UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projections) also works with Riemannian metrics.”

The problem. Hierarchies are everywhere: taxonomies, file systems, the WordNet dictionary. To use them in machine learning we want points whose distances match the distances in the tree. In the plane this is hopeless for large trees: a disc of radius ρ has room for about ρ² well-separated points, while a tree of branching b has bρ vertices within ρ of its root. In the Poincaré disk, room grows like eρ (§1.2): trees fit.

A construction that needs no training. Sala et al. put the root at the centre and every edge along a geodesic of the same length τ. Around each vertex, its children are spread evenly over the directions not taken by the edge to its parent. As τ grows, subtrees move apart exponentially fast and distances become τ times the tree distances, with a distortion that tends to zero. Nickel and Kiela instead optimise the positions of the points by Riemannian gradient descent, so that related words end up close. Both can work in as few as two dimensions.

The catch: precision. A vertex at hyperbolic distance ρ from the centre sits at Euclidean radius tanh(ρ/2), which differs from 1 by about 2e−ρ. Double-precision numbers resolve 1 − r down to about 10−16, so ρ cannot exceed about 35. Deep trees and large τ run out of digits; Sala et al. discuss this trade-off between precision and dimension.

§3.7 The geodesic equation and the exponential map p. 45–46 · fr. 42–43

The slide (p. 45). “If \(H(q,p)~=~ \tfrac{1}{2} \langle\, p \,,\, g_{q}^{-1} p\,\rangle\) denotes the Hamiltonian for the Riemannian metric \(g_q\), we can show that paths \(q(t)\) that minimize length locally and travel at constant speed follow a coupled Ordinary Differential Equation with a momentum vector \(p(t)\):”

\[ \begin{cases} \dot{q}(t) ~=~ +\tfrac{\partial H}{\partial p}(q(t),p(t)) &\quad\text{follow the velocity } v(t) = g_{q(t)}^{-1}p(t).\\ \dot{p}(t) ~=~ -\tfrac{\partial H}{\partial q}(q(t),p(t)) &\quad\text{steer the momentum to stay on a geodesic path.} \end{cases} \]
\[ \begin{cases} \dot{q}(t) ~=~ +\tfrac{\partial H}{\partial p}(q(t),p(t)), \\ \dot{p}(t) ~=~ -\tfrac{\partial H}{\partial q}(q(t),p(t)). \end{cases} \]

The first line follows the velocity v(t) = gq(t)−1 p(t); the second steers the momentum to stay on a geodesic path.

“Geodesic paths are fully determined by: the starting position \(q(t=0)\), the starting momentum \(p(t=0)\) ⟺ the velocity \(\dot{q}(t=0) = g_{q(t=0)} p(t=0)\).”

Where the equations come from. Among paths with fixed ends, those of least energy ∫ ½ q̇ᵀ gq q̇ dt are the geodesics travelled at constant speed. The momentum p = gq q̇ turns this minimisation into Hamilton’s equations, as in mechanics, where the metric plays the role of the mass. H(q, p) = ½ ‖q̇‖² is conserved: the speed is constant. The first equation only needs the inverse metric g−1, the cometric; the second needs its derivative with respect to q, which is what bends the path.

On the half-plane. There gq = I/z², so H = ½ z² (py² + pz²), and the equations read ẏ = z² py, ż = z² pz, ṗy = 0, ṗz = −z (py² + pz²). Their solutions are the half-circles of §2.3; Lab 12 checks this numerically.

Watch out

The last line of p. 45 contradicts the first equation of the same slide. Since q̇ = ∂H/∂p = gq−1 p, the initial velocity is

\[ \begin{aligned} \dot{q}(t=0) &\;=\; g_{q(t=0)}^{-1}\, p(t=0), \\ p(t=0) &\;=\; g_{q(t=0)}\,\dot q(t=0). \end{aligned} \]

The slide writes q̇(t = 0) = gq(t=0) p(t = 0): the inverse is missing. On the half-plane, where g = I/z², the difference is a factor z⁴.

The exponential map (p. 46). “\(\exp_{q_0}(p_0)\) denotes the solution \(q(t=1)\) of the geodesic equation with initial condition \(q(t=0) = q_0\), \(p(t=0)=p_0\).” Three pictures: “From the 1D line to the circle” (the tangent line at q = 1 wrapped around the circle by t ↦ eit), “From the 2D plane to the sphere” [Chr10], and the “Azimuthal equidistant projection” of the Earth centred on the North Pole [Rok08].

Why the map is the exponential map read backwards. On the azimuthal equidistant map, a place at distance ρ from the centre, in direction θ, is drawn at distance ρ in direction θ. That is exactly the inverse of the exponential map at the centre, the logarithm map. Distances from the centre are exact; everything else is distorted. A circle of radius ρ on the Earth has circumference 2π sin ρ (for a unit sphere) but is drawn with circumference 2πρ: Tissot’s indicatrices are stretched along the circles by ρ / sin ρ, not at all along the radii. The same picture for the hyperbolic plane shrinks them by ρ / sinh ρ. The difference between sin, identity and sinh is curvature: geodesics leaving a point converge on the sphere, stay parallel in the plane and diverge in the hyperbolic plane, as on L3 p. 47.

§3.8 Computing geodesics from A to B: three scenarios p. 47 · fr. 44

The slide. “1. The metric has a lot of structure – closed formulas: Just like the Poincaré disk: simple example + symmetries. Riemannian metric ⟺ Relevant kernel. 2. The metric \(g_x\) is simple – path shortening: Discretize the path energy”

\[ \ell^2(\gamma) \simeq \tfrac{1}{\text{N}}\sum_{i=1}^\text{N} \text{N}^2 \, \|\gamma(i/\text{N}) - \gamma((i-1)/\text{N}) \|^2_{\gamma(i/\text{N})}. \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \ell^2(\gamma) \simeq \tfrac{1}{\text{N}}\sum_{i=1}^\text{N} \text{N}^2 \, &\|\gamma(i/\text{N}) \\ &\; - \gamma((i-1)/\text{N}) \|^2_{\gamma(i/\text{N})}. \end{aligned} \]

“‘Mean curvature flow’: gradient descent with respect to the snapshot positions. 3. The cometric \(K_q = (g_q)^{-1}\) is simple – geodesic shooting: Implement the exponential \((q_0,p_0) \mapsto \exp_{q_0}(p_0)\) by integrating the Hamilton ODE. Solve the inverse problem \(p_0 \mapsto \exp_{A}(p_0) \simeq B\) with an optimizer or a network.”

Scenario 1. When the metric has enough symmetry, geodesics come in closed form, as in §2.3 and for the covariance metrics of §3.2. “Riemannian metric ⟺ Relevant kernel” refers to a family where the modelling choice is a kernel: in shape analysis (LDDMM), a configuration of landmarks q = (q1, …, qn) moves under a smooth velocity field, and the cometric is the kernel matrix Kq = (k(qi, qj))ij of Lecture 2 (L2 §6.3). Choosing the kernel is choosing the metric.

Scenario 2. Replace the path by N + 1 snapshots and minimise the discrete energy over the inner ones, with the ends fixed. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, energy ≥ length², with equality at constant speed, so the minimiser is a geodesic travelled at constant speed and the “≃” of the slide becomes an equality at the optimum. Gradient descent on this energy moves each snapshot towards the average of its neighbours: it is a discrete curve-shortening flow, the one-dimensional case of mean curvature flow. It needs the metric g at every snapshot.

Scenario 3. Integrate Hamilton’s equations (§3.7), which need the cometric K = g−1, and adjust the initial momentum until the path lands on B. Newton’s method does it in a few steps when started close enough; a network can learn the map from (A, B) to p0 when the same problem must be solved many times, as in registration.

Which to choose. Path shortening is simple and robust, but its gradient steps must shrink as N grows, and it converges slowly. A quasi-Newton optimiser helps without removing the problem: L-BFGS needs 572 iterations for N = 64 instead of plain gradient descent’s 3,325, and 1,755 for N = 256 (script 13). Shooting converges fast and produces the momentum, a vector at A that can be averaged or fed to statistics, but it can fail when the start is poor or the geodesic is long. Lab 13 runs both.

The message, measured. “Simple paths on a curved space” are the half-circles of the disk, the geodesics of the affine-invariant metric, the Fisher–Rao arcs: each is one formula or one ODE. “Complex paths on a flat space” are what the same objects look like in bad coordinates: the detour of gradient descent in function space (Lab 10), the swelling of the Euclidean average of covariances (Lab 8), the tree crushed into the plane (Lab 11). What the slides conclude · references

The slides end on three claims (p. 48). Riemannian metrics give an expressive vocabulary (“trees, balls, shapes and probability distributions”), a complete toolbox (“local metric ⟶ geodesics, exponentials and barycenters”), and a message: “simple paths on a curved space > complex paths on a flat space.” The recap of the first half (p. 34) adds two habits of mind: change coordinates when it helps, and let discrete and continuous pictures inform each other.

What to keep from this page. Poincaré’s world is a real geometry that walking reveals (Lab 1): a Riemannian metric turns it into a formula whose circles grow like trees (Lab 2), and whose three pictures disagree on everything except what the inhabitants can measure (Lab 3). Its geodesics, found by symmetry, are half-circles; a grid of halving rows is a faithful discrete copy (Lab 4, Lab 5). The same tools compute geodesics on a donut (Lab 7), average covariance matrices without swelling (Lab 8), make Gaussians blur before they move (Lab 9), explain why gradient descent wanders in function space (Lab 10), and fit a real hierarchy in two coordinates (Lab 11). When no formula exists, geodesic shooting and path shortening compute them (Lab 12, Lab 13). Five points of the slides needed correcting or qualifying: two formulas (the inequality of p. 26 and the velocity of p. 45), a map that is not a rotation (p. 28–29), the sense in which SL2(ℤ) is a “regular grid” of the disk (p. 33), and hand-drawn plots presented as data (p. 38).

0. The page’s random generator

"""The random generator of the companion pages (mulberry32 + Marsaglia's polar method), in Python.

The page's labs draw their random data with this generator; with the same seed, these functions return the
same numbers, so the snippets below reproduce the page's results instead of results "within sampling error".
"""
import math

M32 = 0xFFFFFFFF


def _imul(a, b):
    return (a * b) & M32


class Rng:
    def __init__(self, seed):
        self.a = (seed & M32) or 1
        self.spare = None

    def uniform(self):                       # uniform in [0, 1), bit-for-bit as the pages' G.rng(seed).uniform()
        self.a = (self.a + 0x6D2B79F5) & M32
        a = self.a
        t = _imul(a ^ (a >> 15), 1 | a)
        t = ((t + _imul(t ^ (t >> 7), 61 | t)) & M32) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & M32) / 4294967296

    def normal(self):                        # Marsaglia's polar method, keeping the spare value as the pages do
        if self.spare is not None:
            s0, self.spare = self.spare, None
            return s0
        while True:
            u, v = 2 * self.uniform() - 1, 2 * self.uniform() - 1
            s = u * u + v * v
            if 0 < s < 1:
                break
        f = math.sqrt(-2 * math.log(s) / s)
        self.spare = v * f
        return u * f

    def int(self, n):
        return math.floor(self.uniform() * n)

    def range(self, lo, hi):
        return lo + (hi - lo) * self.uniform()

    def normals(self, n):
        return [self.normal() for _ in range(n)]

    # ---- vectorised versions (same stream as repeated calls; used for millions of draws) ----
    def uniform_array(self, n):
        import numpy as np
        k = np.arange(1, n + 1, dtype=np.uint64)
        a = (np.uint64(self.a) + k * np.uint64(0x6D2B79F5)) & np.uint64(M32)
        self.a = int(a[-1]) if n else self.a
        m = np.uint64(M32)
        t = ((a ^ (a >> np.uint64(15))) * (a | np.uint64(1))) & m
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> np.uint64(7))) * (t | np.uint64(61))) & m)) & m) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> np.uint64(14))) & m).astype(np.float64) / 4294967296

    def normal_array(self, n):
        import numpy as np
        out = []
        if self.spare is not None and n > 0:
            out.append(self.spare); self.spare = None
        need = n - len(out)
        while need > 0:
            m = max(64, int(need * 0.66) + 64)             # pairs of uniforms to try
            u = self.uniform_array(2 * m)
            x, y = 2 * u[0::2] - 1, 2 * u[1::2] - 1
            s = x * x + y * y
            ok = (s < 1) & (s > 0)
            # keep the stream exact: stop at the pair that completes the request, rewind the rest
            idx = np.flatnonzero(ok)
            pairs_needed = (need + 1) // 2
            if len(idx) >= pairs_needed:
                last = int(idx[pairs_needed - 1])
                self.a = int((self.a - (2 * m - 2 * (last + 1)) * 0x6D2B79F5) & M32)
                idx = idx[:pairs_needed]
            f = np.sqrt(-2 * np.log(s[idx]) / s[idx])
            vals = np.empty(2 * len(idx)); vals[0::2] = x[idx] * f; vals[1::2] = y[idx] * f
            if len(vals) > need:
                self.spare = float(vals[-1]); vals = vals[:-1]
            out.extend(vals.tolist()); need -= len(vals)
        return np.array(out[:n])

Cheat sheet

Riemannian metric (§1.2–1.3)

At each point x, an SPD matrix gx; ‖v‖x = √(vᵀ gx v). Length ℓ(γ) = ∫‖γ̇‖γ dt; distance = inf of lengths. Drawn as Tissot’s indicatrix: the ellipse {vᵀ g v ≤ ε²}. Conformal: g = λ(x) I, angles exact.

Poincaré disk (§1.1–1.2)

2|dx|/(1 − |x|²), curvature −1. d(0, x) = 2 artanh|x|; the rim is at infinity. Circle of radius ρ: circumference 2π sinh ρ, area 2π(cosh ρ − 1): grows like a tree. d(u, v) = arcosh(1 + 2|u − v|²/((1 − |u|²)(1 − |v|²))).

Three models (§1.4)

Disk I, hemisphere J (|dX|/Z), half-plane H (|d(y, z)|/z), linked by stereographic projections; centre ↔ north pole ↔ (0, 2). Half-plane: d = arcosh(1 + |a − b|²/(2 az bz)); isometries w ↦ (aw + b)/(cw + d).

Geodesics (§2)

Vertical lines and half-circles perpendicular to the axis (half-plane); diameters and circles orthogonal to the rim (disk). Found by symmetry: the axis is a geodesic, isometries carry it everywhere. Grid: “going up is faster than travelling sideways”, ~2 log2 n steps for n sideways.

Discrete ↔ continuous (§1.5, §2.4)

Binary-tiling grid ≈ half-plane: steps ≈ distance / ln 2. A regular tiling ({3,7}) is a grid of the disk. SL2(ℤ)’s Cayley graph is a tree-like subset (cusp: Tⁿ has length 2n, displacement 2 asinh(n/4)).

Surfaces (§3.1)

Torus: (R + r cos v)² du² + r² dv². Clairaut: (distance to axis) × cos(angle with parallel) conserved. From the outer equator, the inner one is reached iff α ≥ arccos((R − r)/(R + r)).

SPD matrices, rotations (§3.2)

Euclidean (1 − t)A + tB swells. Log-Euclidean exp((1 − t)log A + t log B). Affine-invariant A½(A−½BA−½)tA½: invariant under A ↦ MAMᵀ. Bures: optimal transport between N(0, A), N(0, B). SO(3): exp(t log R).

Gaussians (§3.3)

W2² = Δm² + Δσ²: Euclidean half-plane, straight paths. KL ≈ ½ (dm² + 2dσ²)/σ²: Fisher–Rao = √2 × Poincaré in (m/√2, σ), curvature −½; geodesics blur, move, sharpen. KL is not symmetric.

Architectures, embeddings (§3.5–3.6)

GD on θ: ḟ = −JJᵀ∇fL (metric (JJᵀ)−1, NTK = JJᵀ). Natural gradient: ḟ = −∇fL, parameterisation-free. Trees embed in the disk with small distortion (Sala: edges of length τ); precision limits ρ ≲ 35 in double precision.

Computing geodesics (§3.7–3.8)

H = ½ pᵀ g−1 p; q̇ = g−1p, ṗ = −∂H/∂q. expq(p) = q(1). 1. closed formulas (symmetry); 2. path shortening (needs g; slow, robust); 3. shooting + Newton (needs K = g−1; fast). Kernel Kq = (k(qi, qj)) ⇒ landmark metric.

Self-test

1. What does “Large graphs are best understood as continuous objects” mean, and why would we want that?
A large graph often samples a continuous space (a surface, a manifold of data). Studying the space gives calculus, symmetries and answers that do not depend on the sampling, as a gas is described by pressure and temperature rather than by its molecules.
2. What did Poincaré mean by “geometry is only the summary of the laws by which these images succeed each other”?
Geometry is learnt from how our experiences follow each other when we move: straight, turning, back where we started. Beings raised in a world with other laws of motion would learn another geometry, such as the non-Euclidean world of p. 10–12.
3. In Poincaré’s world, why can the inhabitants never reach the boundary?
Their rulers and steps shrink like 1 − r² as they approach it, so the number of steps to the boundary, ∫ 2 dr/(1 − r²), is infinite: the world is finite for us and infinite for them.
4. Write the Poincaré metric of the disk and its matrix g.
d = 2√(dx² + dy²)/(1 − (x² + y²)); g(x,y) = 4/(1 − (x² + y²))² times the identity.
5. What is a Riemannian metric, in one sentence?
A smooth choice of a Euclidean norm (a symmetric positive definite matrix gx) at every point, used to measure small steps; lengths, distances and geodesics follow from it.
6. How long is the circle of hyperbolic radius ρ, and why does that make the disk a “continuous tree”?
2π sinh ρ, which grows like π eρ: exponentially, like the number of vertices at depth k of a tree. There is room for trees without crowding.
7. What is Tissot’s indicatrix, and how does it describe a metric?
The image on the map of a small circle on the ground: the ellipse {v : vᵀ g v ≤ ε²}. Its axes and orientation give g, so the field of indicatrices is the metric.
8. Is the Mercator projection the projection from a light source at the centre of the globe onto a tangent cylinder?
No. The light-source projection puts latitude φ at height tan φ and does not preserve angles; Mercator puts it at ln tan(π/4 + φ/2), chosen so that it does.
9. Why do the segment, the half-circle and the half-line describe the same geometry?
The stereographic projections are bijections that carry each metric onto the next: a step of length d on the segment at x = 0 becomes 2d and 4d in the pictures, but its metric length is 2d in all three.
10. Where is the centre of the disk in the other two models?
At the north pole (0, 0, 1) of the hemisphere and at the point (y, z) = (0, 2) of the half-plane.
11. Why is “going up faster than travelling sideways” on the Poincaré grid?
Each row up halves the horizontal gap, so climbing m rows, crossing and coming down costs about 2m + n/2m steps, around 2 log2 n instead of n.
12. What is wrong with the inequality of p. 26?
Its direction: the wiggly path is at least as long as its vertical shadow, so the sign must be ⩾. With ⩽ it would say the straight path is the longest.
13. Which map is written as a rotation on p. 28, and what should it be?
(x, y, z) ↦ (cos θ x, sin θ y, z), which is not a rotation (for θ = 90° it flattens everything onto x = 0). The rotation about the vertical axis is (x cos θ − y sin θ, x sin θ + y cos θ, z).
14. Which symmetries of the half-plane give all its geodesics from the vertical axis?
Horizontal translations, scalings (y, z) ↦ (ay, az), and the rotations about (0, 2) inherited from the hemisphere. Together they move the axis onto every half-circle perpendicular to the boundary.
15. In what sense is the Cayley graph of SL2(ℤ) not a “regular grid” of the disk?
SL2(ℤ) acts on the disk with a non-compact quotient (a cusp), so word length does not measure hyperbolic distance: Tⁿ has length 2n but moves a point by about 2 ln n. Its Cayley graph is tree-like; a regular tiling such as {3,7} is the true analogue of the square grid.
16. What is the difference between the “flat” and the “curved” square of p. 36–37?
The same square of angles with two metrics: constant (a flat torus, straight geodesics) or (R + r cos v)² du² + r² dv², the metric of the donut, whose geodesics bend.
17. Which geodesics from the outer equator of the donut reach the inner equator?
Those whose angle α with the equator satisfies (R + r) cos α ≤ R − r (Clairaut’s relation): above 70.53° for R = 2, r = 1.
18. What is the swelling effect?
The Euclidean average of two SPD matrices can have a much larger determinant (volume) than either: in Lab 8, 4.04 at the midpoint between determinants 1 and 0.36. Log-Euclidean and affine-invariant geodesics interpolate the determinant geometrically.
19. Why is the affine-invariant metric called that?
Its distance is unchanged when every matrix A becomes M A Mᵀ for an invertible M, that is, when the data are expressed in other units or coordinates.
20. What are the geodesics between two Gaussians for optimal transport and for the Fisher–Rao metric?
Straight segments in (m, σ) for optimal transport (slide and widen steadily); half-circles in (m/√2, σ) for Fisher–Rao (blur, move sideways, then sharpen).
21. How is the relative entropy related to the Fisher–Rao metric?
For nearby Gaussians, KL ≈ half the squared Fisher–Rao distance: (dm² + 2 dσ²)/(2σ²). Far apart, KL is not symmetric and not a distance.
22. In what sense is standard gradient descent on the parameters “Riemannian gradient descent on the network”?
The function moves by ḟ = −J Jᵀ ∇fL, a gradient flow in function space for the metric (J Jᵀ)−1 set by the architecture (J Jᵀ is the neural tangent kernel). The natural gradient cancels this distortion.
23. Why do trees embed well in the disk and badly in the plane?
Trees grow exponentially and so do hyperbolic discs, while Euclidean discs grow like ρ². In Lab 11 the WordNet mammals keep a precision of 0.770 in the disk and 0.039 in the plane.
24. What are the two equations of the geodesic flow in Hamiltonian form, and what is wrong on p. 45?
q̇ = ∂H/∂p = gq−1 p and ṗ = −∂H/∂q, with H = ½ pᵀ gq−1 p. The slide’s last line writes q̇(0) = g p(0) instead of g−1 p(0).
25. Compare path shortening and geodesic shooting.
Path shortening minimises a discrete energy over snapshots: it needs the metric g, is robust, but converges slowly (thousands of steps in Lab 13). Shooting integrates Hamilton’s equations and solves for the initial momentum: it needs the cometric g−1, converges in a few Newton steps, but needs a reasonable start.
