§0 Where this lecture sits

Lecture 1 ended on a table: N patients in rows, D measurements in columns, and one quantity to predict (♥). Lecture 2 asks the practical question that follows: if the columns are good, which formula should we fit? It reviews six families of models — trees, nearest neighbours, linear models, neural networks, polynomials and kernels — on the same two toy problems (9 points on a line, 20 points in a square), and compares how each family fills the space between the data points. The slide’s own summary of the day is “well-rounded methods for high-quality features” (p. 5).

“Flat” in the title means that each patient is a point of the vector space \(\R^\D\), and that distances are measured with the Euclidean norm, possibly after rescaling the columns. Nothing here is curved or discrete yet: graphs come in Lecture 3, curved spaces in Lecture 5, spaces of distributions in Lecture 6.

A question that comes back

What does the model assume where there is no data?

With 9 or 20 data points, almost all of the input space is empty. Every model must still output a number there, and each family answers with its own prior. A tree assumes the answer is constant on boxes aligned with the axes (§1). K-NN copies the nearest patients, which gives constant values on Voronoi cells (§2). Linear regression assumes one global slope (§3). A ReLU network assumes straight pieces joined at hinges (§4). A polynomial assumes one smooth global formula, which explodes away from the data (§5). A kernel method lets the user choose: smooth or peaky, local or global (§6). The 1D panels of the slides show the answers between the points; the 2D panels show them everywhere else. Lab 13 scores them against fresh data.

Earlier lectures. Lecture 1 asked “what should the model keep, and what should it throw away?” Its answer to the empty space was the curse of dimensionality: 5128·128 samples would be needed to fill the space of small images (L1 §1.2), so a prior must fill it instead. In L1 Lab 3, the prior “an area is a sum of pixels” did that with 5 images. Later lectures ask the same question with more geometry: data may only fill a thin manifold (L3), a ReLU network continues in a straight line beyond the data (L4 Lab 11), “between two points” depends on the metric (L5 geodesics), and interpolating between two distributions depends on the distance chosen (L6 §2.5, §2.9).

§1 Decision trees p. 6–32 · fr. 6–31

§1.1 Trees and greedy training p. 7–8 · fr. 6–7

The problem. Medical data are tables of heterogeneous columns: an age in years, a tumour grade from 1 to 3, a yes/no answer, a laboratory value in mg/L. No distance between two patients is obvious, because the columns have nothing in common. Experts nevertheless decide with simple rules: “if the tumour is larger than 2 cm and the patient is older than 50, then…”. Page 7 shows such rules, drawn as trees, from the Dutch national breast-cancer guideline (“Transformation of the national breast cancer guideline into data-driven clinical decision trees”, Hendriks et al., 2019).

The model. A regression tree asks a sequence of yes/no questions of the form “is column j at most t?”. Each answer sends the patient left or right, and the final box (a leaf) predicts one number: the mean of the training values yi that fall in it. Geometrically, the tree cuts \(\R^\D\) into boxes whose sides are parallel to the axes, and F is constant on each box.

The training criterion (p. 8). The slide writes the goal as a fit term plus a price per leaf:

\[\Fit_{\text{x},\text{y}}(\text{F}) ~+~ \Reg(\text{F}) ~~=~~ \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{\text{i}} \| \text{F}(\text{x}_\text{i}) - \text{y}_\text{i} \|^2 ~+~ \text{T} \cdot \Leaves(\text{F})~.\]
\[\begin{aligned}&\Fit_{\text{x},\text{y}}(\text{F}) + \Reg(\text{F})\\ &\quad= \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{\text{i}} \| \text{F}(\text{x}_\text{i}) - \text{y}_\text{i} \|^2 + \text{T} \cdot \Leaves(\text{F}).\end{aligned}\]

and states: “Recursive splits that stop if improvements < T ⟺ greedy minimization” of this sum. T is a number chosen by the user: the price of one more leaf, in the units of the squared error.

One greedy step. Take a leaf that contains n points with mean \(\bar{\text{y}}\), and cut it into a left part (nL points, mean \(\bar{\text{y}}_\text{L}\)) and a right part (nR points, mean \(\bar{\text{y}}_\text{R}\)). The sum of squared errors (SSE) of a set is \(\sum \text{y}_\text{i}^2 - \text{n}\,\bar{\text{y}}^2\), so the split lowers the SSE by

\[\Delta ~=~ \text{n}_\text{L}\,\bar{\text{y}}_\text{L}^2 + \text{n}_\text{R}\,\bar{\text{y}}_\text{R}^2 - \text{n}\,\bar{\text{y}}^2 ~=~ \frac{\text{n}_\text{L}\,\text{n}_\text{R}}{\text{n}}\,\big(\bar{\text{y}}_\text{L} - \bar{\text{y}}_\text{R}\big)^2,\]

using \(\text{n}\,\bar{\text{y}} = \text{n}_\text{L}\bar{\text{y}}_\text{L} + \text{n}_\text{R}\bar{\text{y}}_\text{R}\). A good split separates two groups with very different means, and prefers balanced groups. The split adds one leaf, so the objective decreases exactly when \(\tfrac12\Delta \gt \text{T}\): this is the stopping rule of the slide. The candidates are finite: for each column, sort the values and try every midpoint between two consecutive values. With running sums, all n − 1 candidates of a column cost one pass after sorting, so a level of the tree costs about D·n·log n operations.

Beyond the slides

Why greedy? Finding the smallest tree that fits a dataset is NP-complete (Hyafil & Rivest, 1976), so the classical algorithm, CART (Breiman, Friedman, Olshen & Stone, 1984), grows the tree one split at a time. The rule “stop when the improvement is below T” is short-sighted: a split with no gain can enable large gains one level below (think of y = x₁·x₂ with signs: no single cut on x₁ or x₂ helps, two cuts solve it). CART therefore grows a deep tree first and then prunes it back with exactly the penalty of p. 8, T·#Leaves (“cost-complexity pruning”). scikit-learn’s DecisionTreeRegressor, used for every tree of this lecture, implements the greedy growth; pruning is its ccp_alpha option.

§1.2 Two toy regression problems p. 9 · fr. 8

Every model of the lecture is fitted to the same two datasets, and every figure shows both side by side. The data come from the author’s script code/regression.py, which also produced all the model figures.

  • D = 1: 9 points chosen by hand, shaped like a bowl. In the code, x = (1.0, 1.4, 2.0, 2.3, 2.9, 4.0, 4.5, 5.0, 5.4)/6 − 0.5 and y = (1.5, 0.8, 0.7, 0.2, 0.42, 1.0, 1.4, 1.1, 1.9)/6 − 0.25.
  • D = 2: 20 points produced by scikit-learn’s make_regression(n_samples=20, n_features=2, noise=100, random_state=0), then rescaled so that x lies in [−0.4, 0.4]² and y in [−1, 1]. The folder name 2D_n100 refers to noise=100, not to 100 points.

The slide says “the unit interval [0, 1]” and “the unit square [0, 1]²”; the code uses coordinates centred on 0. This page uses the code’s coordinates, so that every number can be compared with the author’s figures.

Watch out

The 2D problem is a plane plus a lot of noise. make_regression draws x from a standard Gaussian and sets y = 36.37·x₁ + 31.54·x₂ + 100·ε with Gaussian ε. In the rescaled coordinates of the slides, the truth is the plane y = 0.815·x₁ + 0.808·x₂ − 0.305, and the noise has standard deviation 0.425. The noise variance is 4.3 times the variance of the signal, and the true plane explains only 30% of the variance of the 20 values. Consequences for reading the slides:

  • every structure of a 2D map beyond a tilted plane is fitted noise;
  • no model can have a test mean squared error below 0.180 (the noise variance);
  • because the generator is known, this page can measure an honest error: the true test MSE, on 20,000 fresh points drawn from the same generator and rescaled with the same constants.

The 1D points have no generator, so they are judged by leave-one-out cross-validation: fit the model on 8 points, predict the ninth, repeat for each point, and average the 9 squared errors.

§1.3 Depth: from one constant to full overfit p. 10–17 · fr. 9–16

Pages 10–16 grow trees of depth 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 10 (DecisionTreeRegressor(max_depth=d)). A tree of depth d has at most 2d leaves, hence the slides’ “1 constant value”, “2 distinct values”, “up to 4 distinct values”, …, “up to 1,024 distinct values”. Depth 0 is the mean of y. In 1D the model is a staircase; in 2D it is a patchwork of rectangles whose edges are parallel to the axes — “the model follows the D axes of the feature space” (p. 12). The slides flag overfitting from depth 4 (“Starting to clearly overfit”) and call depth 10 a “Full overfit on both datasets”. Lab 1 puts numbers on this: in 1D, depth 3 has the best leave-one-out error; in 2D, every split makes the true test error worse.

Strengths and weaknesses (p. 17). Decision trees are “Interpretable”, “Easy to train and deploy”, “Fast and CPU-friendly”, and “Robust”, because they:

  • “Only use a few columns at a time.” Each split reads one column; columns that never help are never used. Lab 13 shows the benefit when many columns are useless.
  • “Work well with heterogenous information.” A split compares a column with a threshold of its own unit; no column is ever added to another.
  • “Only rely on the ordering of the features.” Only the ranks of the values within each column matter. Measuring height in metres instead of centimetres, or replacing a column by its logarithm, leaves the fitted values at the training points unchanged. Lab 4 measures this.

“However, trees also overfit quickly and produce blocky results. Regularization methods mitigate these issues, at the cost of interpretability.” The next two subsections are those regularisations.

Watch out

The deeper 2D trees are not unique. When two candidate splits lower the SSE by exactly the same amount — frequent once a leaf holds two or three points — scikit-learn picks one at random, and regression.py does not fix the random seed. Over 50 seeds, scikit-learn grows 2 different depth-3 trees, 37 different depth-5 trees and 45 different depth-10 trees on the same 20 points, all with the same training error. Even the leave-one-out error of a depth-2 tree ranges from 0.117 to 0.180 over 40 seeds. The maps of p. 13–16 are one draw each; this page reproduces those exact draws (the tie-breaking orders were found by decoding the colours of the slide images).

§1.4 First regularisation: a boosted sequence of trees p. 18–22 · fr. 17–21

The idea. Instead of one deep tree, add up many shallow ones, each correcting what the previous ones missed. Page 18 draws the first steps on five points: fit Tree 1, compute the residuals y − Tree 1, fit Tree 2 to the residuals, add the two trees. The slide’s rule:

\[\text{\BR{Residual}}_\text{\,\BR{i}} = \text{y}_\text{i} - \text{\textbf{0.1}}\cdot \sum_\text{k} \Tree_\text{k} (\text{x}_\text{i})~.\]

“Iterative fits on the prediction residuals with shallow trees. Use a small learning rate for better regularization.” As an algorithm: start from F0 = 0; at step m, compute the residuals ri = yi − Fm−1(xi), fit a shallow tree to the pairs (xi, ri), and set Fm = Fm−1 + 0.1·Treem.

Why it works. The residual is minus the derivative of the squared error with respect to the prediction: \(\text{r}_\text{i} = -\partial_{\text{F}(\text{x}_\text{i})}\,\tfrac12(\text{y}_\text{i}-\text{F}(\text{x}_\text{i}))^2\). Each tree is a rough, piecewise-constant copy of the negative gradient, and the learning rate 0.1 is a step size: boosting is gradient descent on the training error, in the space of functions (Friedman, 2001, “gradient boosting”). Small steps need more trees but add smoother corrections, which acts like early stopping. With 100 trees of depth 3, the training error on both toy problems becomes essentially zero (“We reach a high training accuracy with a relatively smooth model”, p. 22) — which, on the 2D noise, is not a good sign (Lab 2).

Watch out

The figures of p. 19–22 were not made with the formula of p. 18. In regression.py, the “boosted” models are AdaBoostRegressor(DecisionTreeRegressor(max_depth=3), n_estimators=...). That is AdaBoost.R2 (Drucker, 1997), a different algorithm: each tree is fitted to a random resample of the data in which badly predicted points are drawn more often; each tree gets a weight log(1/β) that grows with its accuracy; the prediction is the weighted median of the trees, not a sum; and the learning rate is 1, not 0.1. It explains why the “1 tree of depth 3” of p. 19 differs from the depth-3 tree of p. 13: it was grown on a resample. The residual-fitting algorithm of the formula is scikit-learn’s GradientBoostingRegressor, whose default learning rate is 0.1. Two smaller points: the trees of the p. 18 diagram are labelled “Depth 2” but have two leaves each (depth 1 in the convention of p. 11), and the diagram adds Tree 1 + Tree 2 without the factor 0.1. Lab 2 runs both algorithms.

§1.5 Second regularisation: random forests p. 23–27 · fr. 22–26

The idea (p. 23). “Parallel fits on bootstrap samples of the original dataset. The final model is the average of a forest of independent trees.” A bootstrap sample draws N points from the N data points with replacement: some points appear twice, about a third not at all. Each tree sees a slightly different dataset, so the trees disagree where the data are weak, and averaging cancels part of that disagreement. This is Breiman’s bagging (1996); a random forest (Breiman, 2001) also restricts each split to a random subset of columns, but scikit-learn’s RandomForestRegressor, used here, considers all columns by default, so the slides show bagged trees.

Why averaging helps. If each tree’s prediction at a point has variance σ², and two trees have correlation ρ, the average of M trees has variance

\[\operatorname{Var}\Big(\frac1M\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Tree_m(\text{x})\Big) ~=~ \rho\,\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{M}\,\sigma^2 .\]

More trees remove the second term, never the first: trees grown on overlapping resamples stay correlated. Averaging also smooths the staircase, since the jumps of different trees fall at different places, but every jump is still parallel to an axis: “The model still follows the axes of the feature space, but is smoother” (p. 27).

§1.6 Some features require more work: understand the context p. 28–31 · fr. 27–30

A tree only thresholds columns. If the useful information is a combination of columns, or a transformation of one, the tree must approximate it with many small boxes. Four slides show the problem and one answer: build the right feature with someone who knows the context.

Body-mass index (p. 28). “The Body Mass Index = weight / height² is a good indicator for many health problems.” The obesity boundary BMI = 30 is the curve weight = 30·height², which no small set of horizontal and vertical cuts can follow. Given the BMI as a column, one threshold suffices. The slide’s chart (credited to Withings) tabulates the index for 21 weights and 28 heights.

Postcodes (p. 29). “Applying thresholds on postal codes is mostly useless. Other statistics may be much more informative.” The left map shows the two-digit codes of the French départements; the right one, from INSEE, the share of people with very high incomes (“Part des personnes à très haut revenu”). The départements were numbered in the alphabetical order of their names (Ain 01, Aisne 02, Allier 03, …), so a threshold such as “code ≤ 38” groups them by the alphabet. A statistic of the area — its income level, its distance to a hospital — carries the information that a code only names. Lecture 6 meets the same trap with class labels coded 1, 2, 3: “Massive bias depending on the ordering of the labels” (L6 §1.4; L6 Lab 3 finds that no ordering of the codes classifies the slide’s fifteen points).

Timestamps (p. 30). The slide shows the counter 1665491486, “SECONDS SINCE JAN 01 1970. (UTC)”, and a clock reading 2:31:28 PM. That count is Tuesday 11 October 2022, 12:31:26 UTC, i.e. 14:31:26 in Paris: the clock was photographed two seconds after the counter. “Applying thresholds on UNIX timestamps is mostly useless. We must first apply periodic transforms to get hours-days-months.” A threshold on the timestamp cuts time into intervals: the tree memorises the past, then predicts one constant for the whole future. The hour of the day and the day of the week turn every Monday at 9:00 into the same value, so that a pattern learnt on past Mondays applies to the next one.

Raw pixels (p. 31). “Tree models cannot process raw pixel values. §2 K-nearest neighbours p. 33–42 · fr. 32–40

§2.1 The Euclidean metric is isotropic p. 34 · fr. 32

The problem. Trees favour the axes. For homogeneous features, such as the coordinates of a point in space, the axes are an accident of the scanner’s orientation, and a model should not depend on them.

The slide’s contrast. On the left, a threshold x₁ < 1 cuts the plane along an axis: “Thresholding features promotes decisions along the axes of the feature space.” On the right, the disk x₁² + x₂² < 4: “The squared Euclidean metric \(\|(\text{x}_\text{1}, \dots, \text{x}_\text{D})\|^2 = \text{x}_\text{1}^\text{2} + \dots + \text{x}_\text{D}^\text{2}\) is invariant to rotations.”

Why. A rotation is a matrix Q with \(\t{Q}Q = \Id\), so \(\|Q\text{x}\|^2 = \t{\text{x}}\t{Q}Q\,\text{x} = \|\text{x}\|^2\). A model that only uses distances between points — nearest neighbours, Gaussian kernels — therefore gives the same predictions after the whole dataset is rotated, and also after it is translated. Such a model is called isotropic: no direction is special. A tree uses coordinates, not distances, and changes when the axes turn. The price of isotropy is the opposite dependence: a distance adds up the columns, so it depends on their units, which trees ignore (§2.3).

Link

Each model has a group of transformations it ignores, exactly as Kendall’s shape space ignored translations, rotations and scalings of triangles (L1 §2.2). Trees ignore increasing changes of each column separately; Euclidean methods ignore rotations and translations of the whole space. Choosing a model means choosing which of these should not matter. Lecture 4 meets the same question for neural networks on surfaces (“How do we deal with the lack of orientation?”, L4 §1.4, slide p. 23).

§2.2 Average value among the K nearest neighbours p. 35–40 · fr. 33–38

The model. To predict at a new point x, find the K training points closest to x and average their values:

\[\text{F}(\text{x}) ~=~ \frac{1}{\text{K}} \sum_{\text{j} \in \mathcal{N}_\text{K}(\text{x})} \text{y}_\text{j},\]

where \(\mathcal{N}_\text{K}(\text{x})\) holds the indices of the K data points nearest to x.

“With K = 1 neighbor, we retrieve a simple nearest neighbor interpolation. This model is piecewise constant on the Voronoi diagram of x” (p. 35). The Voronoi cell of xj is the set of points closer to xj than to any other data point; with K = 1 the model copies yj on that cell. With K neighbours, the regions of constant value are the sets of points that share the same K nearest neighbours; there are more of them, so “the cells of the diagram become smaller” (p. 36–38), and with K = 5 “the model looks smoother and smoother but is still piecewise constant” (p. 39).

Why people like it (p. 40). K-NN models are “Interpretable” — a prediction is justified by K real patients — “Isotropic – which may or may not be a good thing!”, “Easy to deploy”, “Fast, parallel and GPU-friendly – see our MVA Lecture 7 on algorithms”, and “Well-packaged and scalable: FAISS, KeOps, (big-)ann-benchmarks.com…”. There is no training: the model is the data. A query costs one distance per training point (N·D operations), a computation that GPUs do extremely well (L7 §1.9; L7 Lab 4 times four ways of doing it).

§2.3 Major weakness: K-NNs need a good scaling of the features p. 41 · fr. 39

Unlike tree-based models, the Euclidean distance is sensitive to the precise values of the features x. Out-of-the-box, K-NNs are not even robust to the choice of the units for the columns of our dataset!slide p. 41 (fr. 39)

A height in centimetres varies by about 10 between patients; the same height in metres varies by 0.1. In the first case height dominates the distance, in the second it is invisible. The slide lists three normalisations, to apply before computing distances:

  1. Feature-wise rescaling “using e.g. the standard deviation”: replace each column xj by (xj − meanj)/sdj. Every column then varies by about 1.
  2. Multivariate normalisation “using e.g. Principal Component Analysis. The Euclidean distance with a normalized PCA is known as the Mahalanobis metric.” Write the covariance matrix of the columns as \(\Sigma = U\Lambda\t{U}\) (PCA: U holds the principal directions, Λ the variances along them). Normalised PCA coordinates are \(\text{z} = \Lambda^{-1/2}\t{U}(\text{x} - \mu)\), and
    \[\begin{aligned}\|\text{z} - \text{z}'\|^2 &= \t{(\text{x}-\text{x}')}\,U\Lambda^{-1}\t{U}\,(\text{x}-\text{x}')\\ &= \t{(\text{x}-\text{x}')}\,\Sigma^{-1}\,(\text{x}-\text{x}').\end{aligned}\]
    Unlike rescaling, this also removes correlations: two columns that measure nearly the same thing (weight and height) are not counted twice.
  3. Equalisation “a robust equalization of the feature histograms”: replace each value by its rank among the training values, divided by N (the empirical cumulative distribution). Every column becomes uniform on [0, 1]; outliers and heavy tails no longer matter, and — like a tree — the result only depends on the ordering of each column.
Watch out

None of the three normalisations knows which columns matter. They give every column the same influence, including a useless one. A column that carries no information about y (an income, in Lab 5) is harmless to a tree but costs K-NN accuracy after any rescaling. Only context removes it — the message of p. 4.

§3 Linear regression p. 43–64 · fr. 41–45

§3.1 A simple model: least squares p. 44 · fr. 41

The model. Page 44 reuses Lecture 1’s picture (L1 §1.1): one weight per column, plus a constant, “We choose the weights a, b, …, f by minimizing a least squares error.” With the N inputs stacked as the rows of a matrix X (a column of ones added for the constant) and the weights in a vector w, the model is F(x) = x·w, and least squares minimises

\[\begin{aligned}\text{E}(\text{w}) &= \frac1{\text{N}}\,\|X\text{w} - \text{y}\|^2\\ &= \frac1{\text{N}}\Big(\t{\text{w}}\,\t{X}X\,\text{w} - 2\,\t{\text{y}}X\text{w} + \t{\text{y}}\text{y}\Big).\end{aligned}\]

E is a convex quadratic: its gradient \(\tfrac{2}{\text{N}}(\t{X}X\text{w} - \t{X}\text{y})\) vanishes at the unique minimiser (when \(\t{X}X\) is invertible)

\[\text{w}^\star = (\t{X}X)^{-1}\,\t{X}\text{y}.\]

In one dimension this is the familiar slope = covariance(x, y)/variance(x). On the 9 points of the 1D problem: slope 0.1534, intercept −0.0872, mean squared error 0.005698 (Lab 6, “closed form”). Convexity is what makes this model reliable: one minimum, found by a formula, identical on every computer. The neural networks of §4 lose exactly this property.

§3.2 Iterative least squares fitting p. 45–62 · fr. 42–43

Pages 45–53 introduce the 1D dataset one point at a time: an input x (blue circle), an output y (red circle), and the pair (xi, yi) marked on the axes. Pages 54–62 then fit the line y = a·x + b not by the formula, but by an iterative optimiser, and print the two parameters on each frame: (a, b) = (+0.00, +0.25), (+0.12, +0.01), (+0.23, −0.01), (+0.28, +0.04), (+0.23, +0.12), (+0.07, +0.10), (+0.18, +0.08), (+0.16, +0.09), (+0.15, +0.09).

Why iterate when a formula exists? Because the same loop — compute the error, compute its gradient, take a step — fits any differentiable model. The author’s scripts regression_old.py and model_fitting.py use one routine for the line, the polynomials and the neural networks of §4. The optimiser is Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2015) with learning rate 0.2 and ε = 0.01, on the cost \(\text{C} = \frac1{9}\sum_\text{i}(\text{y}_\text{i}-\text{F}(\text{x}_\text{i}))^2\), starting from zero parameters. For each parameter θ with gradient g, at step t = 1, 2, …:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{m} &\gets 0.9\,\text{m} + 0.1\,\text{g},\qquad \text{v} \gets 0.999\,\text{v} + 0.001\,\text{g}^2,\\ \theta &\gets \theta - \frac{0.2}{1-0.9^\text{t}}\cdot\frac{\text{m}}{\sqrt{\text{v}/(1-0.999^\text{t})} + 0.01}. \end{aligned}\]

At the first step m/√v = g/|g|, so each parameter moves by about 0.2 in the direction of −g, whatever the size of the gradient: the slope jumps from 0 to 0.12 at once. Adam normalises each coordinate by the size of its recent gradients; it is not a descent method, and with a large learning rate the cost can go up: here it rises from 0.0141 to 0.0162 at the first step, and oscillates before settling.

Watch out

The “b” printed on p. 54–62 is not the intercept. The script prints b = 0.25 + F(−0.45): the height of the line where it crosses the drawn y-axis (x = −0.45), measured from the drawn x-axis (y = −0.25). The model’s own intercept F(0) is −0.087 at convergence, while the slide prints +0.09. The slope a is the true slope, because the plot has equal scales on both axes. With this reading, Lab 6 reproduces all nine printed pairs.

§4 Neural networks p. 65–95 · fr. 46–59

§4.1 Intermediate variables p. 66–68 · fr. 46–47

The idea (p. 66). “Domain experts may have suggested a step-by-step process to compute the quantity of interest – say, the perimeter of an organ.” The picture goes from an input image, through two intermediate images, to an output y = 8.3 cm. A model can copy this structure: compute intermediate variables g1, g2 from x, then y from them.

Linear intermediate variables (p. 67–68). The first attempt uses g1 = a1·x + b1, g2 = a2·x + b2 and y = c1·g1 + c2·g2 + d. Substituting,

\[\text{y} = (\text{c}_1\text{a}_1 + \text{c}_2\text{a}_2)\,\text{x} + (\text{c}_1\text{b}_1 + \text{c}_2\text{b}_2 + \text{d}),\]

a straight line again. Seven parameters describe a family with two degrees of freedom, so infinitely many parameter vectors give the same model. §4.2 Intermediate variables and non-linearities: hinges p. 69–80 · fr. 48–49

The fix (p. 69). Pass each intermediate variable through a non-linearity: gk = (ak·x + bk)+, where (·)+ keeps positive values and replaces negative ones by 0 (the rectified linear unit, ReLU). Each unit is now a hinge: flat on one side of the point x = −bk/ak, straight on the other. The output y = c1g1 + c2g2 + d is continuous and piecewise linear, with at most one kink per unit. (In regression_old.py the floor of the hinge is −0.25, the height of the drawn x-axis, instead of 0; this shifts the parameters, not the family of functions.)

Two units (p. 69–73). From the same start as p. 67, training bends the line into a V that follows the bowl: the frames at iterations 10, 20, 50, 100 and 194 show the kink appearing and sliding into place. The cost falls from 0.005698 (a line) to 0.001013.

Four units (p. 74–80) — “We can then increase the number of neurons…”. The final frame (iteration 173) is worse than with two units: cost 0.003695, one kink instead of two. Three of the four units have died. A unit is dead when a·xi + b is below the floor for all 9 data points: it then outputs a constant, its gradient with respect to a and b is zero, and no later step can revive it. More neurons made the model more flexible on paper and worse in practice.

How many hinges? For a one-dimensional input, a first layer of n1 units creates at most n1 kinks, so each unit of the second layer sees a piecewise linear function with at most n1 + 1 pieces. Its ReLU adds at most one kink per piece, and the output adds up the second layer:

\[\#\text{kinks} ~\le~ \text{n}_1 + \text{n}_2\,(\text{n}_1 + 1).\]

For two layers of 100 units this is 10,200, the order of magnitude of the slide’s “at most 100 x 100 hinges”. With more layers the bound multiplies again, which is why depth can create many more pieces than width (Montúfar et al., 2014). Trained networks use very few of them: Lab 8 counts 12 kinks for the 100 + 100 network of seed 1.

§4.4 Neural networks: strengths and weaknesses p. 95 · fr. 59

“Modular and easy to extend. Simplest way of implementing high-dimensional piecewise linear models. Extremely well-supported on CPU and GPU: PyTorch, TensorFlow…”

Unfortunately, the optimization of the “neural” weights corresponds to a non-convex optimization problem. We must rely on non-deterministic, stochastic solvers. Piecewise-linear models also extrapolate along their last straight piece, which Lecture 4 names as a limit: “Fitting piecewise linear functions to parabolas or exponentials is fine for interpolation, but very limiting for extrapolation” (L4 §3.3, slide p. 65; L4 Lab 11 measures it on the slide’s cannonball). §5 Polynomial interpolation p. 96–104 · fr. 60–67

§5.1 Degree 0 to 10 p. 97–103 · fr. 60–66

The model. Keep the convex least-squares machinery of §3, but feed it more columns: all the monomials of degree at most d. The slides list them: in 1D, 1, x, x², …, xd; in 2D, 1, x, y, x², xy, y², x³, x²y, xy², y³, and so on. The number of monomials of degree at most d in D variables is

\[\binom{\text{D}+\text{d}}{\text{d}}:\qquad \text{D} = 2:~ 1,~3,~6,~10,~15,~21 \text{ for d} = 0,\dots,5.\]

In 2D, degree 4 already has 15 coefficients for 20 points — “Starting to overfit in dimension D=2” (p. 101) — and degree 5 has 21, more than the data: “Full overfit in dimension D=2” (p. 102). In 1D, degree 10 has 11 coefficients for 9 points: “Full overfit in both examples” (p. 103).

How the figures were computed. regression.py uses KernelRidge(alpha=0, kernel="polynomial", degree=d), a kernel method (§6) with kernel \(\text{k}(\text{x},\text{x}') = (\gamma\,\text{x}\cdot\text{x}' + 1)^\text{d}\), γ = 1/D. By the binomial formula this kernel is the inner product of the monomial features, each with a fixed weight. Two regimes follow. When there are fewer monomials than points, the kernel matrix is singular, scikit-learn falls back to least squares, and the result is exactly the least-squares polynomial fit (checked here to 6×10⁻¹¹ for degree 3 in 2D). When there are at least as many monomials as points, the fit interpolates, and among the many interpolating polynomials it picks the one of smallest norm in the kernel’s weighted feature space. The degree-10 curve of p. 103 is therefore one choice among infinitely many polynomials through the 9 points; another weighting of the monomials would draw another curve.

Why high degrees fail. A polynomial is global: every coefficient acts everywhere. To pass through points that are not on a smooth trend, a high-degree polynomial must oscillate, and the oscillations grow near and beyond the ends of the data (Runge’s phenomenon, 1901). Numerically, the monomials x7 and x8 are almost proportional on [−0.5, 0.5], so the least-squares system becomes ill-conditioned: its condition number grows from 22 at degree 2 to 1.3×10⁵ at degree 7 on the 1D data (Lab 9).

§5.2 Summary of the models seen so far p. 104 · fr. 67

  • “Non-parametric” methods: “Tree-based models – robust, but with a bias along the axes. K-Nearest Neighbors models – isotropic, but requires a good scaling.”
  • “Parametric” methods: “Linear regression – useful, but often too simplistic. Neural networks – expressive, but unreliable.”
  • “Polynomial regression: Linear regression with polynomial features. Quadratic regression is fine – but we badly overfit beyond degree 4-5.”

The quotation marks around “non-parametric” are deserved. Trees and K-NN do have parameters (splits, stored points); what the word means is that their number grows with the data, so the model can become as complex as the dataset allows. A parametric model fixes its number of parameters in advance. Kernel methods, next, are non-parametric in this sense — one weight per data point — but convex like least squares.

§6 Kernel interpolation p. 105–129 · fr. 68–91

§6.1 User-specified smoothness p. 106–107 · fr. 68–69

The idea. Instead of choosing a family (trees, polynomials) and hoping its prior fits, write the prior directly. Page 106 starts from a linear combination of fixed functions,

\[\text{F}(\text{a}_\text{1}, \dots, \text{a}_\text{J} ; \text{x}) ~=~ \text{a}_\text{1} \text{F}_\text{1}(\text{x}) ~+~ \cdots ~+~ \text{a}_\text{J} \text{F}_\text{J}(\text{x}),\]

and chooses one function per data point, a copy of the same bump centred on xj:

\[\text{F}(\text{a}_\text{j} ; \text{x}) ~=~ \sum_{\text{j}} \text{a}_\text{j}\, \text{k}(\text{x} - \text{x}_\text{j}),\]

“and say that k(x − y) is the kernel of our method.” The kernel says how far, and how smoothly, the value at one data point influences its surroundings: it is a similarity between locations, chosen by the user. F is linear in the weights aj, so fitting them is a linear problem, as in §3; but there are N weights, as many as data points, so the model can follow the data as closely as the kernel allows.

Choosing a kernel (p. 107). “Two main criteria: is the kernel smooth or peaky? Does the kernel have compact support or a heavy tail?” A smooth kernel (Gaussian, Cauchy) is differentiable at 0 and gives smooth models; a peaky one (exponential) has a corner at 0 and gives models with corners at the data points. A kernel with compact support is exactly zero beyond some radius, so distant points have no influence at all; a heavy tail (Cauchy, which decays like 1/r²) lets every point influence the whole domain.

§6.2 First method: Nadaraya–Watson and Shepard p. 108–116 · fr. 70–78

The formula (p. 108). “First method – just use a fraction instead of a linear combination”:

\[\text{F}(\text{x}) ~=~ \frac{\sum_{\text{j}} \text{k}(\text{x} - \text{x}_\text{j}) \, \text{y}_\text{j}}{\sum_{\text{j}} \text{k}(\text{x} - \text{x}_\text{j})}\]

“The Nadaraya–Watson method assumes that k(x−y) takes positive values. It corresponds to a barycentric interpolation between the values yj, with weights that are proportional to k(x − xj).” (Nadaraya, 1964; Watson, 1964.) There is nothing to fit: the weights \(\text{w}_\text{j}(\text{x}) = \text{k}(\text{x}-\text{x}_\text{j})/\sum_\text{l}\text{k}(\text{x}-\text{x}_\text{l})\) are positive and add up to 1, so F(x) is a weighted average of the data values.

Consequences.

  • F never leaves the range of the data: no overshoot, and no extrapolation of a trend beyond the last point.
  • Far from the data, the tail of the kernel decides. With a heavy tail (Cauchy, Shepard), all weights become nearly equal and F tends to the mean of the yj — “Dampened towards the global average” (p. 111). With a Gaussian, the weight of the nearest point beats the others by a factor that grows without bound, and F tends to the value of the nearest point, like 1-nearest-neighbour. (Very far away all Gaussian weights underflow to 0 in floating point, and the fraction becomes 0/0.)
  • K-NN is the special case where k is 1 on the K nearest points and 0 elsewhere — a box whose width adapts to the local density.

Shepard’s inverse distance weighting (p. 113–116). With k(x, y) = 1/‖x − y‖p the weight of a data point becomes infinite on the point itself, so F(xj) = yj: “Perfect fit to the training data” (Shepard, 1968). The exponent sets the shape in between. Between two neighbouring points, at relative position t ∈ [0, 1], and ignoring the others, the weight of the right-hand point is

\[\frac{\text{t}^{\,\text{p}}}{\text{t}^{\,\text{p}} + (1-\text{t})^{\text{p}}}.\]

For p = 1 this is t itself (linear interpolation); as p → ∞ it becomes a step (nearest neighbour).

For p > 1 the curve is flat at both ends, so F has a zero slope at every data point; for p ≤ 1 the other points pull hard and F has cusps (p. 113–114). The slides’ captions — p = 2 “close to a linear interpolation”, p = 4 “close to a nearest neighbor interpolation” — are measured in Lab 10. (The code computes 1/(‖x − y‖p + 10⁻⁸) to avoid dividing by zero; this changes nothing visible for p ≤ 4.)

Watch out

The Gaussian Nadaraya–Watson figures use a narrower kernel than their caption. Pages 109–110 print k(x, y) = exp(−‖x − y‖²/2σ²) with σ = 0.2 and 0.1, but regression.py builds them with gamma = 1/σ², i.e. exp(−‖x − y‖²/σ²): in the slide’s notation the widths are σ/√2 = 0.141 and 0.071. The Cauchy and exponential captions match the code, and so do the Gaussian kernel-ridge figures of p. 123–126. Lab 10 offers both conventions; the page’s maps match the slides only with the code’s.

§6.3 Second method: solve a linear system — kernel ridge regression p. 117–119 · fr. 79–81

The interpolation system (p. 117). Ask the model \(\text{F}(\text{x}) = \sum_\text{j}\text{a}_\text{j}\,\varphi(\text{x}-\text{x}_\text{j})\) to reproduce the data:

\[\begin{alignat*}{6} \text{F}(\text{x}_\text{1}) ~&=~ \textbf{a}_\textbf{1} \, \varphi(\text{x}_\text{1} -\text{x}_\text{1}) ~&+~& \cdots ~&+~& \textbf{a}_\textbf{N}\, \varphi(\text{x}_\text{1} -\text{x}_\text{N}) ~&\simeq~& \text{y}_\text{1} \\ \text{F}(\text{x}_\text{2}) ~&=~ \textbf{a}_\textbf{1}\, \varphi(\text{x}_\text{2} -\text{x}_\text{1}) ~&+~& \cdots ~&+~& \textbf{a}_\textbf{N}\, \varphi(\text{x}_\text{2} -\text{x}_\text{N}) ~&\simeq~& \text{y}_\text{2} \\ \vdots~~~ ~&=~ ~~~~~~~~~\vdots ~&+~& \ddots ~&+~& ~~~~~~~~~\vdots ~&\simeq~& \vdots \\ \text{F}(\text{x}_\text{N}) ~&=~ \textbf{a}_\textbf{1}\, \varphi(\text{x}_\text{N} -\text{x}_\text{1}) ~&+~& \cdots ~&+~& \textbf{a}_\textbf{N}\, \varphi(\text{x}_\text{N} -\text{x}_\text{N}) ~&\simeq~& \text{y}_\text{N} \end{alignat*}\]
\[\text{F}(\text{x}_\text{i}) = \sum_{\text{j}=1}^{\text{N}} \textbf{a}_\textbf{j}\,\varphi(\text{x}_\text{i}-\text{x}_\text{j}) ~\simeq~ \text{y}_\text{i},\qquad \text{i} = 1,\dots,\text{N}.\]

“Linear system \(\Phi\,\textbf{a} = \text{y}\)”, where Φ is the N × N matrix \(\Phi_{\text{ij}} = \varphi(\text{x}_\text{i}-\text{x}_\text{j})\). Solving it exactly interpolates the data, noise included.

Approximate regression (p. 118). “Enforcing a perfect fit to the data may not be reasonable. Instead, we target a trade-off between accuracy and smoothness:”

\[\min_\text{a} ~~\|\Phi \text{a} - \text{y}\|^\text{2} + \Reg(\text{a}).\]

“Popular regularization terms are convex:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{Ridge: } \alpha \|\text{a}\|^\text{2} = \alpha ( \text{a}_\text{1}^\text{2} + \cdots + \text{a}_\text{N}^\text{2} ). \\ &\text{Lasso: } \lambda \|\text{a}\|_\text{1} = \lambda ( |\text{a}_\text{1}| + \cdots + |\text{a}_\text{N}|). \\ &\text{Elastic Net: } \lambda \|\text{a}\|_\text{1} + \alpha \|\text{a}\|^\text{2}.\text{”} \end{aligned} \]

These are the penalties of L1 Lab 2, now on the weights of the bumps.

Kernel ridge regression (p. 119). With the ridge penalty, the minimisation has a closed form. The slide’s derivation:

\[\begin{aligned} \min_\text{a}~ \|\Phi\,\text{a} - \text{y}\|^2 ~+~ \alpha\,\|\text{a}\|^2 ~~&=~~ (\text{a}^\intercal \Phi^\intercal - \text{y}^\intercal)(\Phi \text{a} - \text{y}) + \alpha\, \text{a}^\intercal \text{a} \\ ~~&=~~ \text{a}^\intercal ( \Phi^\intercal \Phi + \alpha\,\Id_\text{N} )\, \text{a} - 2\,\text{y}^\intercal \Phi \text{a} + \text{y}^\intercal \text{y} \\ \Longrightarrow ~~ \text{a} ~~&=~~ ( \Phi^\intercal \Phi + \alpha\,\Id_\text{N} )^{-1}\, \Phi^\intercal \text{y} ~~=~~ \Phi^\intercal ( \Phi \Phi^\intercal + \alpha\,\Id_\text{N} )^{-1} \text{y} \\ \Longrightarrow ~~ \text{F}(\text{x}) ~~&=~~ \Phi\,\text{a} ~=~ \Phi \Phi^\intercal ( \Phi \Phi^\intercal + \alpha\,\Id_\text{N} )^{-1} \, \text{y}~. \end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned} &\|\Phi\text{a}-\text{y}\|^2 + \alpha\|\text{a}\|^2 \\ &\quad= \text{a}^\intercal(\Phi^\intercal\Phi + \alpha\Id_\text{N})\,\text{a} - 2\,\text{y}^\intercal\Phi\text{a} + \text{y}^\intercal\text{y}\\ &\text{a} = (\Phi^\intercal\Phi + \alpha\Id_\text{N})^{-1}\Phi^\intercal\text{y}\\ &\phantom{\text{a}} = \Phi^\intercal(\Phi\Phi^\intercal + \alpha\Id_\text{N})^{-1}\text{y}\\ &\text{F}(\text{x}) = \Phi\text{a} = \Phi\Phi^\intercal(\Phi\Phi^\intercal + \alpha\Id_\text{N})^{-1}\text{y}. \end{aligned}\]

The first implication sets the gradient 2(Φ⊺Φ + α Id)a − 2Φ⊺y to zero. The equality in the third line is the “push-through” identity: \(\Phi^\intercal(\Phi\Phi^\intercal + \alpha\Id) = (\Phi^\intercal\Phi + \alpha\Id)\Phi^\intercal\); multiply on the left by \((\Phi^\intercal\Phi + \alpha\Id)^{-1}\) and on the right by \((\Phi\Phi^\intercal + \alpha\Id)^{-1}\). “A fundamental object appears: the symmetric, positive, semidefinite kernel matrix \(\K = \Phi \Phi^\intercal\).” Symmetric and positive semidefinite because \(\t{\text{u}}\Phi\Phi^\intercal\text{u} = \|\Phi^\intercal\text{u}\|^2 \ge 0\) for every vector u. It is the N-by-N similarity matrix of L1 §3: the whole fit only depends on the data through K.

Watch out

Two notational shortcuts in p. 119–121. (1) “F(x) = Φa” is the vector of fitted values at the N data points, not the model at a new x. At a new point, \(\text{F}(\text{x}) = \sum_\text{j}\text{a}_\text{j}\,\varphi(\text{x}-\text{x}_\text{j})\) with the same weights a. (2) The letter k changes role. On p. 106 it is the bump placed on each data point (called φ from p. 117 on). From p. 120 on, k is the function behind the kernel matrix, \(\text{k} = \varphi\star\varphi\), and the model is written with it: \(\text{F}(\text{x}) = \sum_\text{i}\text{c}_\text{i}\,\text{k}(\text{x}-\text{x}_\text{i})\) with \(\text{c} = (\K + \alpha\Id_\text{N})^{-1}\text{y}\). The captions of p. 123–129, and regression.py, give this second k.

§6.4 The kernel trick and the computational perspective p. 120–121 · fr. 82–83

The kernel trick (p. 120).

\[\K = \Phi \Phi^\intercal ~~~~\text{i.e.}~~~~ \K(\text{x}_\text{i},\text{x}_\text{j}) ~=~ \langle ~\Phi(\text{x}_\text{i}), \Phi(\text{x}_\text{j})~\rangle ~=~ \sum_{\text{s}=\text{1}}^\text{N} \varphi(\text{x}_\text{i} - \text{x}_\text{s})\,\varphi(\text{x}_\text{j} - \text{x}_\text{s})\]
\[\begin{aligned}\K &= \Phi \Phi^\intercal, \quad\text{i.e.}\\ \K(\text{x}_\text{i},\text{x}_\text{j}) &= \langle \Phi(\text{x}_\text{i}), \Phi(\text{x}_\text{j})\rangle\\ &= \sum_{\text{s}=\text{1}}^\text{N} \varphi(\text{x}_\text{i} - \text{x}_\text{s})\,\varphi(\text{x}_\text{j} - \text{x}_\text{s})\end{aligned}\]

“This may be expensive: N terms for every coefficient of K. Fortunately, we may use the continuous limit instead:”

\[\text{k}(\text{x}_\text{i},\text{x}_\text{j}) ~=~ \int_{\text{x}} \varphi(\text{x}_\text{i} - \text{x})\,\varphi(\text{x}_\text{j} - \text{x})\,\text{dx}\]

“This dot product between two translated copies of φ is often known in closed form.” The sum places one bump on each data point; the integral places one bump everywhere — infinitely many features, one per location — and replaces the sum by an integral. For a Gaussian bump φ(x) = exp(−x²/2s²), the integral is \(\sqrt{\pi}\,\text{s}\,\exp(-(\text{x}_\text{i}-\text{x}_\text{j})^2/4\text{s}^2)\): a Gaussian of width s√2. The two kernels agree only up to a factor (the sum over N points approximates N × density × the integral) and differ near the edges of the data, where the sum sees bumps on one side only; Lab 11 measures both effects.

Which functions k are allowed? (p. 121) “Criterion: if the Fourier transform \(\widehat{\text{k}}(\omega)\) is real-valued and positive, then \(\widehat{\varphi}(\omega) = \sqrt{\widehat{\text{k}}(\omega)}\) works.” Indeed, for a symmetric φ the Fourier transform of φ ⋆ φ is \(\widehat{\varphi}^{\,2}\), so choosing \(\widehat{\varphi} = \sqrt{\widehat{\text{k}}}\) gives back k. The criterion also guarantees that every kernel matrix is positive semidefinite, because

\[\sum_{\text{i},\text{j}}\text{a}_\text{i}\,\text{a}_\text{j}\,\text{k}(\text{x}_\text{i}-\text{x}_\text{j}) ~=~ \int \Big|\sum_\text{i}\text{a}_\text{i}\,\varphi(\text{x}_\text{i}-\text{x})\Big|^2\,\text{dx} ~\ge~ 0 .\]

With the convention \(\widehat{\text{k}}(\omega) = \int\text{k}(\text{x})\,e^{-\text{i}\omega\text{x}}\,\text{dx}\) in 1D: the Gaussian exp(−x²/2σ²) has \(\widehat{\text{k}} = \sigma\sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{-\sigma^2\omega^2/2}\); the exponential exp(−|x|/σ) has \(2\sigma/(1+\sigma^2\omega^2)\); the Cauchy kernel 1/(1 + x²/σ²) has \(\pi\sigma\,e^{-\sigma|\omega|}\). All three are positive. The box (1 for |x| < σ, 0 elsewhere) has \(2\sin(\sigma\omega)/\omega\), which changes sign: the box is not a valid kernel, even though it looks like the most natural “neighbourhood”.

Beyond the slides

The criterion is also necessary. Bochner’s theorem (1933) says that a continuous function k(x − y) gives positive semidefinite matrices for every set of points if and only if k is the Fourier transform of a non-negative measure. The dimension matters: the triangle (1 − |x|/σ)+ has a non-negative transform in 1D, \(4\sin^2(\sigma\omega/2)/(\sigma\omega^2)\), but the cone (1 − ‖x‖/σ)+ is not positive definite in 2D (Askey, 1973); Lab 11 finds negative eigenvalues. The distance kernel −‖x − y‖ of p. 129 fails the criterion in the ordinary sense but is conditionally positive definite: \(\sum\text{a}_\text{i}\text{a}_\text{j}\text{k}(\text{x}_\text{i}-\text{x}_\text{j}) \ge 0\) whenever \(\sum\text{a}_\text{i} = 0\). This is the kernel behind the energy distance of Székely & Rizzo (2013), listed in the lecture’s biblio.bib, and a kernel norm of Lecture 6.

Computation (p. 121). “Then, kernel ridge regression simply relies on the model: \(\text{F}(\text{x}) = \K\,(\K + \alpha\,\Id_\text{N})^{-1}\,\text{y}\). On GPUs, we may solve this linear system efficiently using: KeOps – bruteforce methods scale to N = 1,000,000 in seconds. FalkonML – approximate methods scale to N = 1,000,000,000 in hours.” The obstacle is size: for N = 10⁶, storing K in double precision takes 8 terabytes and a direct solve costs about 10¹⁸ operations. Iterative solvers (conjugate gradients) only need products Kv; KeOps computes them on the fly, with O(N) memory. Falkon (Rudi, Carratino & Rosasco, 2017; Meanti et al., 2020) replaces the N centres by M ≪ N random ones and preconditions the iterations. Lecture 7 returns to this with GPyTorch on the 3DRoad dataset: “7h with 8 GPUs → 15mn with 1 GPU” (L7 §2.9; L7 Lab 10 solves an exact Gaussian process on all 278,319 training points on a laptop GPU).

§6.5 Kriging and Gaussian process regression p. 122 · fr. 84

Kernel ridge regression has a rich history in applied mathematics. It is especially popular in geostatistics to estimate smooth terrain models: the approximation parameter α controls the nugget effect. This theory is also behind Sobolev norms and Gaussian processes… More about this in the MVA Lecture 6 on probability distributions!slide p. 122 (fr. 84), figure from Leclercq (2018)

The probabilistic reading. Suppose the unknown function f is random, a Gaussian process with covariance s²·k(x − x′), and that each observation adds independent Gaussian noise of variance s²·α. Given the data, f(x) is still Gaussian, with

\[\begin{aligned}\text{mean}(\text{x}) &= \t{\text{k}_\text{x}}(\K + \alpha\Id_\text{N})^{-1}\text{y},\\ \text{var}(\text{x}) &= \text{s}^2\big(\text{k}(0) - \t{\text{k}_\text{x}}(\K + \alpha\Id_\text{N})^{-1}\text{k}_\text{x}\big),\end{aligned}\]

where kx is the vector of the k(x − xi). The mean is exactly the kernel ridge prediction. The variance gives the “2σ credible region” drawn on p. 122: narrow near the data, wide far from it. In geostatistics this is kriging (after the mining engineer D. G. Krige; formalised by G. Matheron in the 1960s). The nugget is a jump of the covariance at distance 0, caused by measurement error or by variations smaller than the sampling: it is the α of the ridge penalty, and it sets how much the model is allowed to miss each data point. With α = 0 the model interpolates; with α > 0 the credible band never pinches to zero at the data. Lab 12 draws this band, with the amplitude s² set by maximum likelihood.

Sobolev norms. Each valid kernel defines a norm on functions, and kernel ridge regression minimises “error + α × norm²”. For the exponential kernel in 1D, the norm is \(\int(\text{f}^2 + \sigma^2\text{f}'^2)\) up to a constant — a Sobolev norm, which penalises slopes. Lecture 6 uses these norms to compare distributions (“Hilbert or Sobolev norms, Maximum Mean Discrepancies”, L6 §2.5).

§6.6 Kernel ridge regression with α = 0.1 p. 123–129 · fr. 85–91

The last seven model slides fit kernel ridge regression with α = 0.1 and seven kernels (KernelRidge(alpha=0.1, kernel="rbf", gamma=1/(2σ²)) for the Gaussian, the code’s own MyKernelRidge for the others):

  • Gaussian, σ = 1.0: “Only models a global linear trend.” A kernel much wider than the data is nearly a low-degree polynomial on it (exp(−r²/2σ²) ≈ 1 − r²/2σ²), so the model can only bend gently.
  • σ = 0.5: “Starts to discern different regions.” σ = 0.2: “Well-suited to the sampling density” — the points are about 0.1 apart. σ = 0.1: “Overfits on individual sample values.”
  • Cauchy, σ = 0.2: “Extrapolates with more confidence” — its heavy tail carries the influence of the data further.
  • Exponential, σ = 0.2: “Closer fit to the training data” — its corner lets the fit bend sharply at each point.
  • Distance, k(x, y) = −‖x − y‖: “Models both local and global trends. Excellent parameter-free baseline.”

Why the distance kernel works. In 1D, F(x) = Σ ci·(−|x − xi|) is piecewise linear with a kink at each data point, like a linear interpolation softened by α. Beyond the data, every |x − xi| grows at the same rate, so F is a straight line with slope −Σci on the right and +Σci on the left. The kernel matrix has one large negative eigenvalue whose eigenvector is nearly constant, which pushes Σci towards 0: here Σci = 0.025, so the fit extrapolates almost flat (slopes ±0.025), as on p. 129. There is no width to tune — the kernel is the same at every scale — hence “parameter-free”; α remains.

Question. How do the kernel, its width σ and the ridge α trade accuracy for smoothness, and is the distance kernel really an excellent baseline?

Leave-one-out errors use the closed form ei/(1 − Hii), where H = K(K + α Id)−1 maps y to the fitted values; the effective degrees of freedom are the trace of H.

What the lab shows (default: Gaussian, σ = 0.2, α = 0.1, as on p. 125)
  • The slide’s setting is good but not the best. With σ = 0.2 the 1D fit has training MSE 0.0011, leave-one-out 0.0043 and 4.2 effective degrees of freedom; in 2D, leave-one-out 0.153 and true test MSE 0.244. On the grid of widths, leave-one-out is lowest at σ = 0.3 in both problems (0.0039 and 0.132).
  • Width goes from global to local. In 1D, σ = 1 uses 1.9 degrees of freedom (a gentle curve, leave-one-out 0.0086) and σ = 0.1 uses 6.4 (leave-one-out 0.0075). In 2D, σ = 1 has a true test MSE of 0.207, the second-best value on this page after Nadaraya–Watson with a wide Gaussian (0.204, Lab 10).
  • The distance kernel is a strong baseline. Without any width: 1D leave-one-out 0.0068 (Gaussian σ = 0.2: 0.0043; linear: 0.0107) and 2D true test MSE 0.222 (linear 0.219). It is competitive everywhere and best nowhere — which is what a baseline should be. Its matrix K + α Id has a negative eigenvalue (−6.7 in 2D), yet the system is solved without trouble.
  • The nugget. With the band on (α = 0.1), the ±2 sd band has a half-width of 0.049 to 0.064 at the data points and 0.128 at x = 0.5. With α = 0.001 the band is still 0.039 to 0.051 wide at the data points: maximum likelihood raises the amplitude from s² = 0.016 to 0.69 to compensate. The band never pinches to zero while α > 0.

# Greedy CART split from scratch, and the depth sweep of p. 10-16 on the 1D toy data (compare with scikit-learn).
import numpy as np
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeRegressor

x = np.array([1.0, 1.4, 2.0, 2.3, 2.9, 4.0, 4.5, 5.0, 5.4]) / 6 - 0.5      # regression.py, D = 1
y = np.array([1.5, 0.8, 0.7, 0.2, 0.42, 1.0, 1.4, 1.1, 1.9]) / 6 - 0.25

def best_split(x, y):
    """Scan the midpoints between sorted values; return (threshold, SSE after the split)."""
    o = np.argsort(x); xs, ys = x[o], y[o]
    best = (None, np.sum((y - y.mean()) ** 2))
    for p in range(1, len(x)):
        if xs[p] <= xs[p - 1]: continue
        L, R = ys[:p], ys[p:]
        sse = np.sum((L - L.mean()) ** 2) + np.sum((R - R.mean()) ** 2)
        if sse < best[1]: best = ((xs[p - 1] + xs[p]) / 2, sse)
    return best

t, sse = best_split(x, y)
print(f"one leaf: SSE = {np.sum((y - y.mean())**2):.5f}; best first split x <= {t:.4f}, SSE = {sse:.5f}")
print("sklearn root threshold:", DecisionTreeRegressor(max_depth=1).fit(x[:, None], y).tree_.threshold[0].round(4))

def loo(depth):
    err = []
    for i in range(len(x)):
        k = np.arange(len(x)) != i
        m = DecisionTreeRegressor(max_depth=depth).fit(x[k, None], y[k])
        err.append((m.predict(x[i:i + 1, None])[0] - y[i]) ** 2)
    return np.mean(err)

Cheat sheet

Trees (§1)

Boxes aligned with the axes; leaf value = mean. Greedy split gain \(\Delta = \frac{\text{n}_\text{L}\text{n}_\text{R}}{\text{n}}(\bar{\text{y}}_\text{L}-\bar{\text{y}}_\text{R})^2\); split if ½Δ > T. Depth d → ≤ 2d leaves (≤ N). Ignore increasing warps of each column; not rotations. Use for heterogeneous tables; fails on combinations of columns, raw pixels, extrapolation.

Boosting and forests (§1.4–1.5)

Boosting: \(\text{F}_\text{m} = \text{F}_{\text{m}-1} + \nu\,\Tree_\text{m}(\text{residuals})\), ν = 0.1 — gradient descent in function space. AdaBoost.R2 (the slides’ figures): weighted resampling + weighted median. Forest: average of bootstrap trees; variance ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ²/M.

K-NN (§2)

\(\text{F}(\text{x}) = \frac{1}{\text{K}}\sum_{\text{j}\in\mathcal{N}_\text{K}(\text{x})}\text{y}_\text{j}\); constant on (order-K) Voronoi cells. Isotropic: ignores rotations and translations, not units. Normalise: z-scores, Mahalanobis \(\t{(\text{x}-\text{x}')}\Sigma^{-1}(\text{x}-\text{x}')\), or ranks. Useless columns hurt after any normalisation.

Least squares (§3)

\(\text{w}^\star = (\t{X}X)^{-1}\t{X}\text{y}\); convex, one minimum. Gradient descent needs η < 2/λmax; Adam normalises each coordinate, first step ≈ lr·sign(g), not monotone. The slides’ printed b = 0.25 + F(−0.45).

ReLU networks (§4)

Linear layers compose to a line; hinges (x)+ give piecewise linear functions, ≤ n1 + n2(n1+1) kinks in 1D for 2 layers. Non-convex: dead units, local minima; results depend on seed, library version, rounding. Extrapolate along the last piece.

Polynomials (§5)

Least squares on the \(\binom{\text{D}+\text{d}}{\text{d}}\) monomials. Interpolates when that number ≥ N. Runge oscillations, ill-conditioning. Here: best degree 2 (1D), 1 (2D).

Kernels (§6)

Nadaraya–Watson: \(\text{F} = \sum\text{k}(\text{x}-\text{x}_\text{j})\text{y}_\text{j}/\sum\text{k}(\text{x}-\text{x}_\text{j})\) — weighted average, stays in the data range; tails decide far away. Kernel ridge: \(\text{F}(\text{x}) = \t{\text{k}_\text{x}}(\K+\alpha\Id)^{-1}\text{y}\), K = ΦΦ⊺; LOO residual ei/(1 − Hii), H = K(K + αId)−1. Valid kernel ⇔ \(\widehat{\text{k}} \ge 0\) (Bochner); φ̂ = √k̂.

Kriging (§6.5)

Same mean as kernel ridge; variance \(\text{s}^2(\text{k}(0) - \t{\text{k}_\text{x}}(\K+\alpha\Id)^{-1}\text{k}_\text{x})\). α = nugget = noise-to-signal ratio; band never zero when α > 0. Distance kernel −|x − y|: conditionally positive definite, parameter-free baseline.

Evaluating honestly

Training error always falls with complexity. Use leave-one-out (1D) or fresh data from a known generator (2D: noise variance 0.180 is the floor). On 20 noisy points leave-one-out is itself noisy (rank correlation 0.64 with the true ranking). Always compare with the mean (0.224) and with a line (0.219).

Self-test

1. What does a regression tree predict inside a leaf, and why that value?
The mean of the training values yi in the leaf. For a fixed partition, the constant c minimising \(\sum(\text{y}_\text{i}-\text{c})^2\) over the leaf is the mean (set the derivative −2Σ(yi − c) to zero).
2. Why is tree training greedy rather than exact?
Finding the optimal tree is NP-complete (Hyafil & Rivest). Greedy growth evaluates each split by its immediate SSE decrease \(\frac{\text{n}_\text{L}\text{n}_\text{R}}{\text{n}}(\bar{\text{y}}_\text{L}-\bar{\text{y}}_\text{R})^2\), which is cheap (sort once per column), but can miss splits that only pay off deeper (checkerboard). Practice grows deep and prunes with the penalty T·#Leaves.
3. The slide says depth 10 gives “up to 1,024 distinct values”. How many does it give on the toy data?
9 in 1D and 20 in 2D: a leaf must contain at least one training point, so a tree has at most N leaves. From depth 5 in 1D the tree already interpolates the 9 points (Lab 1).
4. Which transformations of the inputs leave a tree’s fitted values unchanged, and which leave K-NN’s unchanged?
Trees: any strictly increasing transformation of each column separately (units, logarithms, ranks), because only the order of values matters; not rotations. K-NN with the Euclidean distance: rotations and translations of the whole space (distances are preserved); not a change of units of one column (Lab 4).
5. Write the boosting rule of p. 18 and explain why the residual is a gradient.
Fm = Fm−1 + 0.1·Treem, with Treem fitted to ri = yi − Fm−1(xi). Since \(-\partial_{\text{F}}\tfrac12(\text{y}-\text{F})^2 = \text{y}-\text{F}\), the residuals are the negative gradient of the squared loss with respect to the predictions: each tree approximates a gradient step, 0.1 is the step size.
6. What algorithm actually produced the boosting figures of p. 19–22, and how does it differ?
AdaBoost.R2 (AdaBoostRegressor): each tree is fitted on a weighted bootstrap resample (badly predicted points drawn more often), trees get weights log(1/β), and the prediction is a weighted median with learning rate 1 — not a sum of trees with shrinkage 0.1 fitted to residuals.
7. Why does averaging bootstrap trees reduce the error, and what limits the gain?
Averaging M predictions of variance σ² with pairwise correlation ρ gives variance ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ²/M: the second term vanishes with M, the first does not, because trees grown on overlapping resamples are correlated. Averaging does not change the bias (the axis-aligned staircase shape).
8. Why are thresholds on UNIX timestamps useless for forecasting?
A threshold on time cuts it into intervals; every future time falls beyond the last threshold, so the tree predicts one constant for the whole future (test R² −1.06 in Lab 3). Periodic features (hour of day, day of week) map future times onto values already seen in training (R² 0.68).
9. Why is a threshold on French postcodes (département numbers) mostly meaningless?
The numbers follow the alphabetical order of the départements’ names; a threshold groups départements by the alphabet, not by geography or wealth. A statistic of the area (income, distance to a hospital) carries the information that the code only names.
10. State the three normalisations of p. 41. Which one would you choose with a heavy-tailed column?
Feature-wise standardisation ((x − mean)/sd); Mahalanobis (normalised PCA, distance \(\t{(\text{x}-\text{x}')}\Sigma^{-1}(\text{x}-\text{x}')\)); equalisation (replace values by their ranks). With a heavy tail, equalisation: the standard deviation is dominated by a few extreme values, ranks are not (Lab 5: 0.759 vs 0.706).
11. Derive the Mahalanobis distance from “Euclidean distance in normalised PCA coordinates”.
With \(\Sigma = U\Lambda\t{U}\) and \(\text{z} = \Lambda^{-1/2}\t{U}(\text{x}-\mu)\): \(\|\text{z}-\text{z}'\|^2 = \t{(\text{x}-\text{x}')}U\Lambda^{-1}\t{U}(\text{x}-\text{x}') = \t{(\text{x}-\text{x}')}\Sigma^{-1}(\text{x}-\text{x}')\).
12. On p. 62 the slide prints (a, b) = (+0.15, +0.09) but the least-squares intercept is −0.087. Explain.
The script prints b = 0.25 + F(−0.45): the height of the line at the drawn y-axis (x = −0.45) above the drawn x-axis (y = −0.25). With slope 0.1534 and intercept −0.0872: 0.25 − 0.0872 − 0.45 × 0.1534 = 0.0937 ≈ +0.09.
13. Why does Adam’s first step move every parameter by about the learning rate?
After one step, the bias-corrected moments are m̂ = g and v̂ = g², so the update is lr·g/(|g| + ε) ≈ lr·sign(g) when |g| ≫ ε. With ε = 0.01 and small gradients the step is somewhat smaller (0.12 for the slope on p. 55).
14. Why is a network with two linear intermediate variables no better than a line?
y = c1(a1x + b1) + c2(a2x + b2) + d = (c1a1 + c2a2)x + const: compositions of affine maps are affine. Training converges to the least-squares line (cost 0.005698).
15. How can four hinge units fit worse than two?
Training is non-convex. From the slides’ start, three of the four units die: their pre-activation stays below the floor at all data points, their gradients are zero, and they never recover. The network ends with one active hinge (cost 0.003695 vs 0.001013 for two units).
16. What is the maximum number of kinks of a 2-layer ReLU network with 1D input and widths n₁, n₂?
n₁ + n₂(n₁ + 1): n₁ kinks from the first layer; each second-layer unit sees at most n₁ + 1 linear pieces and its ReLU adds at most one kink per piece. For 100 + 100: 10,200. The trained network of Lab 8 (seed 1) uses 12.
17. How many coefficients does a degree-3 polynomial have in 10 variables? Why does it matter?
\(\binom{13}{3} = 286\). Polynomial regression is least squares on monomials; with more coefficients than data points it interpolates noise. The count grows like Dd/d!, which is the curse of dimensionality for this family.
18. What does KernelRidge(kernel="polynomial", alpha=0) compute when there are fewer monomials than points? And when there are more?
Fewer: the kernel matrix is singular, scikit-learn falls back to least squares, and the result is the least-squares polynomial fit. More: the matrix is invertible and the fit interpolates, choosing among the infinitely many interpolating polynomials the one of smallest norm in the kernel’s weighted monomial features.
19. Why does Nadaraya–Watson never overshoot the data?
Its weights k(x − xj)/Σ k(x − xl) are non-negative and sum to 1, so F(x) is a convex combination of the yj and lies between their minimum and maximum.
20. Which Shepard exponent gives an interpolant closest to linear interpolation, and why?
Between two points at relative position t, the weight of the right-hand point is tp/(tp + (1 − t)p), which is exactly linear for p = 1 if the other points are ignored; but the other points pull hard for p ≤ 1 (cusps). Measured on the 9 points, p = 2 is closest to linear (mean distance 0.0083) and p = 4 closest to nearest-neighbour (0.0062).
21. Derive the kernel ridge solution and explain the push-through identity.
Setting the gradient of \(\|\Phi\text{a}-\text{y}\|^2 + \alpha\|\text{a}\|^2\) to zero gives \((\Phi^\intercal\Phi+\alpha\Id)\text{a} = \Phi^\intercal\text{y}\). Since \(\Phi^\intercal(\Phi\Phi^\intercal+\alpha\Id) = (\Phi^\intercal\Phi+\alpha\Id)\Phi^\intercal\), \(\text{a} = \Phi^\intercal(\K+\alpha\Id)^{-1}\text{y}\) with K = ΦΦ⊺: only the N × N kernel matrix is needed.
22. State the Fourier criterion of p. 121 and use it to decide whether the box kernel is valid.
If \(\widehat{\text{k}}\) is real and non-negative, φ with \(\widehat{\varphi} = \sqrt{\widehat{\text{k}}}\) satisfies φ ⋆ φ = k, and all kernel matrices are positive semidefinite (Bochner: also necessary). The box has transform 2 sin(σω)/ω, which is negative on intervals: not valid; its kernel matrices have negative eigenvalues (48 of 200 in Lab 11).
23. In kriging, what is the nugget and what does it do to the credible band?
A jump of the covariance at distance 0, caused by measurement noise or micro-scale variation; it is the α of kernel ridge (noise-to-signal ratio). With α > 0 the model does not interpolate and the posterior variance at the data points stays positive: the band never pinches to zero.
24. Why does the distance kernel −|x − y| extrapolate almost flat on p. 129?
In 1D the fit Σci(−|x − xi|) is linear beyond the data with slope ∓Σci. The kernel matrix has one large negative eigenvalue with a nearly constant eigenvector, which drives Σci towards 0 (0.025 here), so the slopes are ±0.025.
25. Which models survive 48 useless columns in Lab 13, and why only when the useful directions are axes?
Random forests (R² 0.87 at D = 50) — trees split only on columns that reduce the error, so useless columns are rarely used. K-NN and Gaussian kernels add all columns into a distance, so useless ones drown the useful ones (0.08 and 0.36). When the two useful directions are random combinations of all columns, no single column is useful and the forest fails too (0.06).
