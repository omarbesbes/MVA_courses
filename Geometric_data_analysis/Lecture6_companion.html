§0 Where this lecture sits

Lecture 5 replaced large graphs by continuous spaces. Lecture 6 makes the same move for data sets: a sample of patients, a histogram of curvatures or a cloud of points on an organ is treated as a probability distribution, a set of weights placed at locations (p. 5). The slogan is on p. 3: “From discrete samples to continuous distributions.”

The first part explains why this point of view is useful: to test whether two groups of patients differ, to balance a clinical trial, to train a classifier, to fit a generative model, to register two shapes and to compare populations of histograms (p. 5–18). The second part, the core of the lecture, compares the four families of distances that data scientists use: total variation, the relative entropy (with its geometry on Gaussians, Fisher–Rao), kernel norms, and optimal transport (p. 19–39). It ends with two open problems and a reading of all these distances as dual norms, which leads to the losses of generative adversarial networks and diffusion models (p. 40–44).

The slides have few numbers but many figures, drawn from real computations or by hand. Most of the fourteen labs below start from the data of those figures, read from the SVG and PNG files of the course folder.

This lecture’s stations. Four threads are central. Similarity is the model: the whole second part compares distances, and each one decides differently which of two predictions is closest to the truth (§2.1, Lab 1, Lab 5). Discrete ↔ continuous: a histogram, a sample and a weighted cloud are three encodings of one object, and the same blur can be computed on each of them (§1.1, §1.6). Curse of dimensionality: in high dimension, the distance between two samples of the same law hides the signal (§2.13). Scale & computation: transport became usable when its solvers became fast (§2.10). Three appear in passing: transport ignores neighbourhoods (§2.12); the invariances of the relative entropy make Gaussians hyperbolic (§2.4); and a baseline that the slides do not show, least squares on one-hot vectors, is as invariant as logistic regression (§1.4).

A question that comes back

What does this distance notice, and what does it ignore?

The slides ask it about every distance, in almost the same words: the total variation “pays no attention to ‖xi − xj‖” and “pays no attention to unlikely events” (p. 21); the relative entropy “pays a lot of attention to unlikely events” (p. 24); kernel norms “pay a lot of attention to ‖xi − yj‖” (p. 27); optimal transport is “extremely faithful to the ground metric” (p. 39). Two questions organise the answers (p. 20): should the distance use the geometry of the space where the data live, and how harshly should it punish a model that puts no mass where the data have some? The labs measure the answers. Lab 1 and Lab 5 show two distances disagreeing on the same pair of predictions. Lab 6 shows a single letter, the “ö” of a German name, making a relative entropy infinite. Lab 8 shows a kernel norm that no longer sees particles farther than a few kernel widths away.

Lecture 1 asked what a model should keep and what it should throw away; Lecture 2, what a model assumes where there is no data; Lecture 3, what can be learned from the neighbours alone; Lecture 4, what a simple baseline would do; Lecture 5, what depends on the coordinates. The question here is Lecture 1’s, asked of distances: a distance between distributions is a model of what matters in a difference. Lecture 7 asks what these distances cost to compute, on real hardware.

P.2 A continuous vocabulary for data sciences p. 3 · fr. 3

The idea. Lecture 5 argued that a graph with a million vertices is best studied through the continuous space it samples. The same argument applies to data sets. A sample of 36 patients, the 100,000 points of a scanned organ or the pixels of a segmentation are finite and irregular. The distribution they come from is a single, stable object, and it does not depend on how many points were drawn or where exactly they fell. Comparing two data sets then means comparing two distributions, and the lecture is about how to do that.

§1 What is a probability distribution? p. 4–18 · fr. 4–17

§1.1 Weights at locations p. 5 · fr. 4

The slide. “Probability distribution \(\alpha =\) weights \(a_i\) at locations \(x_i\).” Three pictures of the same elongated blob: a “Histogram: variable weights \(a_i\), fixed locations \(x_i\)”; a “Sample: fixed weights \(1/\text{N}\), variable locations \(x_i\)”; a “Weighted point cloud: variable weights \(a_i\), variable locations \(x_i\).” Then:

\[ \textbf{Discrete sum } \alpha = \sum_{i=1}^\text{N} a_i \delta_{x_i} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \textbf{Continuous density } \alpha = \int_x a(x) \, \mathrm{d} x~. \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &\textbf{Discrete sum } \alpha = \sum_{i=1}^\text{N} a_i \delta_{x_i} \\ &\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \textbf{Continuous density } \alpha = \int_x a(x) \, \mathrm{d} x~. \end{aligned} \]

“Today, we assume that \(a \geqslant 0\) and sums up to 1.”

The problem. Data come in many forms: a table of patients, a scanned surface, a segmentation mask, a list of words. Most statistical tools need them as vectors of a fixed length, and none of these forms is one. A distribution is a common format for all of them: a list of places \(x_i\) and a list of non-negative weights \(a_i\) that add up to 1.

What the symbols mean. \(\delta_{x}\) is the Dirac mass at \(x\): a unit of weight concentrated at one point. \(\alpha = \sum_i a_i \delta_{x_i}\) puts the weight \(a_i\) at \(x_i\). A histogram fixes the places (the centres of the bins) and lets the weights vary. A sample of N observations fixes the weights (each one counts \(1/\text{N}\)) and lets the places vary. A weighted cloud lets both vary; a mesh with one weight per triangle is an example (§1.5). When the points become infinitely many and infinitely close, the sum becomes a density \(a(x)\): the weight of a small region around \(x\) is \(a(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\).

Why it helps. The three encodings describe the same kind of object, so a method written for one applies to the others (§1.6). The weights also make the description independent of the sampling: two different samplings of the same surface give nearly the same distribution, while their lists of vertices have nothing in common (§1.5, Lab 7).

When it fails. A distribution forgets the order of the points and who was next to whom. For a curve, the list of vertices says which vertex follows which; the distribution of its segments does not. Distances between distributions inherit this blindness, which is the first open problem of the lecture (§2.12).

Watch out

Read “\(\alpha = \int_x a(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\)” as “\(\alpha\) has density \(a\)”, that is \(\mathrm{d}\alpha(x) = a(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\), or \(\alpha(A) = \int_A a(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\) for every region \(A\). Taken literally, the integral over the whole space is the number 1, not a distribution.

§1.2 Which distribution is closer? p. 6 · fr. 5

The slide. “Today’s focus: quantifying distances between probability distributions.” Left: a red histogram over a blue curve, “We must handle both discrete and continuous distributions.” Right: three curves, “We must choose if \(\source{\alpha}\) is closer to \(\target{\beta}\) (same mean value) or to \(\other{\gamma}\) (same support).”

The problem. A distance between distributions must first accept both kinds of objects: a sample of 36 observations and a smooth model curve, which do not even live on the same points. It must then take a position on what matters in a difference. On the right of p. 6, the red \(\alpha\) and the blue \(\beta\) have the same mean, but \(\beta\) spreads beyond both ends of \(\alpha\). The green \(\gamma\) lives exactly where \(\alpha\) does, but its mass is piled up on the left. Whether \(\alpha\) is closer to \(\beta\) or to \(\gamma\) is not a fact about the curves; it is a choice made by the distance.

What the lab measures. The three curves are Bézier paths in the slide’s SVG file (images/distances_2.svg); the lab reads them, turns them into densities on the drawn axis (rescaled to [0, 1]), and computes every distance of the lecture. The drawing is faithful to its caption: \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) have means 0.583 and 0.573, and \(\gamma\) has the support of \(\alpha\), up to a sliver of hand-drawn outline.

Lab 1

Closer to β or to γ? (p. 6)

Question. On the three curves drawn on p. 6, which distances call α closer to β (same mean), and which to γ (same support)?

The three densities of p. 6, read from the SVG file
Relative entropy with α as the model, as the floor ε shrinks
What the lab shows (default: σ = 0.05, no floor)
  • Almost every distance says β. Total variation: 0.550 for β against 1.266 for γ. Hellinger: 0.355 against 0.539. Wasserstein W₁: 0.062 against 0.226, and W₂: 0.073 against 0.235. Energy distance: 0.0121 against 0.2210. The Gaussian kernel norm: 0.047 against 0.354, and β stays closer for every width from 0.005 to 1. Even the relative entropy with α as the data, KL(α | ·), says β: 0.297 against 1.199.
  • Only the relative entropy with α as the model separates the curves by their support. KL(β | α) is infinite, because 20.6% of β’s mass lies where α is exactly zero: the model α calls those observations impossible. KL(γ | α) is infinite too, but only through a sliver of 0.01% of γ’s mass, where the hand-drawn outline of γ ends a fraction of a millimetre after that of α. Cut γ to the support of α and KL(γ | α) = 1.408: finite, while KL(β | α) stays infinite. This is the sense in which γ, “same support”, can be closer.
  • How harshly to pay for support errors is a dial. Give α a floor ε (mix it with ε times the uniform density). KL(γ | αε) settles at 1.41 whatever ε, while KL(β | αε) grows like 0.206 × ln(1/ε): 0.82 at ε = 10⁻², 1.77 at 10⁻⁴, 3.66 at 10⁻⁸. The two cross at ε = 5.8 × 10⁻⁴. With a larger floor β is closer; with a smaller one γ is. The slope 0.206 is exactly the share of β that α considers impossible.

What to take from it. The slide’s question has no answer until the distance is chosen, and the distances fall into two camps. Those that look at where the mass is (total variation, Hellinger, the transport distances, the kernel norms) agree that \(\beta\) is closer: it overlaps \(\alpha\) more and its mass sits at the same place on average. The relative entropy with \(\alpha\) as the model looks at something else: whether the model gives a positive probability to everything that happens. That is the second question of p. 20, “How harshly should be we penalize errors on the estimation of the support?”, and §2.3 explains where the answer comes from.

§1.3 Application 1: tests, and a clinical trial p. 7–8 · fr. 6–7

The slides. p. 7: “One-sample test: discrete observation \(\source{\alpha}\), continuous model \(\target{\beta}\). Two-sample test: two discrete observations \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\). Null hypothesis: \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) come from the same distribution. Test: reject if \(\text{d}(\alpha, \beta)\) is too large.” p. 8, “Splitting a population evenly for a clinical trial”: “Problem 1: ensure that the treatment and control groups have similar characteristics. Problem 2: given a large population, pick a group of control patients that have similar characteristics to our treated patients.” The axes of p. 8 are a beer mug and a heart: two characteristics of each patient.

The idea of a test. Any distance between distributions gives a test. Compute it between the two data sets; then ask how large it would typically be if both came from the same distribution. For a one-sample test, simulate samples of the same size from the model. For a two-sample test, pool the two samples, split them at random many times, and recompute the distance each time: if the true split does not stand out, there is no evidence of a difference. The p-value is the share of simulated or permuted distances at least as large as the observed one.

What the drawings contain. The bars of p. 7 are drawn on a grid, and their heights are whole numbers of grid units: the red histogram counts (5, 8, 6, 7, 3, 2, 3, 1, 1) observations, 36 in all, and the blue one (6, 6, 5, 9, 2, 1, 4, 2, 1), also 36. The blue curve on the left is a smooth model. The patients of p. 8 are circles in the SVG files, five treated and five controls on the left, five treated and fourteen candidates on the right. The lab tests the histograms and matches the patients.

What the lab shows (default: the slide’s 36 + 36 observations)
  • The drawings are consistent with “same distribution”. Against the model curve, the red histogram gives a Pearson χ² of 4.40 on 8 degrees of freedom (asymptotic p-value 0.820; 0.812 by 2,000 simulations); the total variation 0.222 and the binned W₁ 0.513 mm give p-values 0.897 and 0.892.   • The same discrepancies become significant with more data, but not for every distance at once. With four copies of the red histogram (144 observations), the χ² test rejects the model (p = 0.032) while the total variation (0.165) and W₁ (0.356) do not. With eight copies (288), χ² gives 0.0005, total variation 0.0085 and W₁ still 0.108. The two-sample difference needs eight copies before the total variation rejects it (p = 0.036). A test is only as sensitive as its distance is to the kind of difference present: here, small excesses in the tail bins, which χ² weighs heavily and W₁ hardly at all.
  • The slide’s patients are well chosen. The split drawn for Problem 1 has an energy distance of 1.220 between the two groups: rank 3 of the 126 ways to split the ten patients into two groups of five (best 1.128, median 3.19). For Problem 2, the optimal assignment of the five treated patients among the fourteen candidates picks exactly the five blue controls of the slide (total squared distance 41.86 mm²).
  • With more patients, greedy matching falls behind. In a synthetic population of 30 treated patients and 200 candidates (seed 3), matching each treated patient in turn to its nearest free candidate costs 14.5% more than the optimal assignment (mean squared distance 0.00274 against 0.00240), and its worst pair is 0.118 apart against 0.087.
Beyond the slides

Matching controls to treated patients by optimal assignment is standard practice in observational studies (“optimal matching”, Rosenbaum 1989), and checking the balance of the matched groups with a distance between their distributions is its natural companion. Permutation tests built on the energy distance [Székely and Rizzo 2013] or on kernel norms (the “maximum mean discrepancy” test of Gretton et al. 2012) are the two-sample tests of p. 7 in their modern form.

§1.4 Application 2: classification is regression in a space of distributions p. 9–10 · fr. 8–9

The slides. p. 9, “Linear regression: Encode class labels as integer numbers \(l(x) \in \{1, 2, 3\}\). Predict a score \(s(x)\) at every location \(x\). Minimize the least square error:”

\[ \frac{1}{\text{N}} \sum_{i=1}^\text{N} \big|l(x) - s(x) \big|^2~. \]

“Massive bias depending on the ordering of the labels.” p. 10, “Logistic regression: Encode class labels as probability distributions \(\delta(x) \in \mathbb{P}(\{1, 2, 3\})\). Predict a vector of scores \(s_i(x)\) at every location \(x\) and turn it into a probability distribution using the SoftMax: \(\alpha(x) = \big( e^{s_1(x)}, e^{s_2(x)}, e^{s_3(x)} \big)~/~ \sum e^{s_i(x)}\). Minimize the relative entropy:”

\[ \frac{1}{\text{N}} \sum_{i=1}^\text{N} \mathrm{KL}\big( \delta(x), \alpha(x)\big)~. \]

“Invariant to the ordering of the labels.” Both slides show the same fifteen points of three colours (“2 input features, 3 classes”) with the regions each model predicts. In both formulas the sum runs over the observations \(i\) while the summand is written at \(x\): read \(l(x_i) - s(x_i)\) and \(\mathrm{KL}\big(\delta(x_i), \alpha(x_i)\big)\).

The problem with numbers. Coding three classes as 1, 2, 3 and regressing the code declares that class 2 lies between classes 1 and 3. A linear score has parallel level lines, so the plane is cut into three parallel bands, and class 2 must be the middle band. If the class drawn in blue is not “between” the red and the green in the plane, some points must be misclassified. Re-labelling the classes changes the answer, which is absurd for categories such as lion, tiger and taxi.

The fix. Code each label as a distribution: \(\delta(x_i)\) puts all its mass on the observed class, (1, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0) or (0, 0, 1). The model predicts a distribution \(\alpha(x)\) through the softmax, and the loss compares two distributions. With \(\delta\) concentrated on the observed class \(c\), \(\mathrm{KL}(\delta, \alpha) = \log[1/\alpha_c(x)]\): the familiar cross-entropy of classifiers, the logarithmic cost of having given the true class a small probability. Nothing in this loss depends on the order of the classes.

When it fails. The relative entropy knows nothing about which mistakes are worse; confusing a lion with a tiger costs as much as confusing it with a taxi. §2.1 comes back to this.

§1.5 Applications 3–5: generative models, registration, meta-analyses p. 11–14 · fr. 10–13

Generative modelling (p. 11). “Generative Adversarial Networks and Variational Auto-Encoders minimize a distance between a synthetic sample and a reference data sample.” “Diffusion and score-based models estimate a gradient of the distance to the support of a reference data sample.” In the first picture, green arrows push each red synthetic point towards the blue data; in the second, level lines of a distance to the data and their gradients. A generator is a machine that turns noise into a sample; training it means moving its sample closer to the data, so the choice of distance decides what “closer” means and which gradients the generator receives. §2.13–§2.15 return to this.

Shape registration (p. 12–13). “Curve: one weight per segment. Surface: one weight per triangle. Segmentation mask: one weight per voxel. Encoding shapes as distributions guarantees an invariance to resamplings. We may work with basic \((x,y,z)\) coordinates or with better features.” The figure of the triangle labels its centre \(x_i\), its normal \(\vec{n}_i\) and its weight \(\alpha_i\) (its area), as in the varifolds of [KCC17]. p. 13 reuses the lung registration of Lecture 4 (L4 §2.3): “Registration algorithms minimize a distance between a deformable model \(\alpha\) and the fixed target \(\beta\).” The weights are what makes the encoding independent of the mesh: a large triangle carries as much weight as the many small triangles that cover the same area. Lab 7 measures this invariance on the curves of p. 26.

Meta-analyses (p. 14). “3D shape texture \(\simeq\) Distribution of curvatures \(\kappa_1 \geqslant \kappa_2\) on the surface. UMAP representation of a population of textures, from the matrix of Wasserstein distances between curvature histograms. Distances enable the processing of populations of histograms. This is relevant to make group-level analyses.” The textures are the curvatubes of Lecture 3 (L3 §3.2); each surface becomes a two-dimensional histogram of its principal curvatures, and a distance between histograms turns the population into a matrix of distances, the input of UMAP (L3 §4). The same recipe applies to a cohort of patients, each described by a histogram of cell sizes or of cortical thicknesses.

§1.6 One operation, three implementations p. 15–17 · fr. 14–16

The slides. p. 15 repeats the three pictures of p. 5 with new captions: a histogram has “explicit weights \(a_i\), implicit locations \(x_i\)”; a sample “implicit weights \(1/\text{N}\), explicit locations”; a weighted cloud, both explicit. “Depending on the application, we may choose a different encoding for our distributions.” p. 16 shows one distribution with three atoms in the three encodings. p. 17 blurs it in three ways: “Convolution of the density map \(a[\,i,\,j\,]\) with a filter \(g[\,i,\,j\,]\).” “Additive noise: \(x_i \mapsto x_i + w_i\) where \(w_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)~.\)” “Soft distance: log-likelihood”

\[ \ell(x) = \log \big( \textstyle\sum_i a_i e^{-\|x - x_i\|^2 / 2\sigma^2} \big)~. \]

“Understanding that different implementations correspond to the same operation is key to insightful research in the field.”

Why the three are one operation. Adding independent Gaussian noise of variance \(\sigma^2\) to every point of a distribution replaces each Dirac mass \(\delta_{x_i}\) by a Gaussian bump centred at \(x_i\): the distribution of the noisy points is the convolution of \(\alpha\) with the Gaussian. On a grid, the same convolution is a weighted average of neighbouring cells, the filter of Lecture 1 (L1 §2.3). And the logarithm of the blurred density at a point \(x\) is, up to a constant, the log-likelihood \(\ell(x)\) of the slide: the plausibility of \(x\) under a Gaussian mixture with centres \(x_i\) and weights \(a_i\).

Why “soft distance”. Multiply \(\ell\) by \(-\sigma^2\). When \(\sigma\) is small, the sum is dominated by its largest term, the nearest atom, and

\[ -\sigma^2\,\ell(x) \;\xrightarrow[\sigma \to 0]{}\; \min_i \tfrac{1}{2}\|x - x_i\|^2. \]

The log-sum-exp is a smooth minimum, and \(\sigma\) sets how smooth. This operation, a sum of exponentials followed by a logarithm, is the “log-sum-exp reduction” that Lecture 7 implements on GPUs (L7 §2.6), and the building block of the Sinkhorn algorithm (§2.10).

What the lab measures. The atoms of p. 16 sit at (0.3, 0.7), (0.7, 0.6) and (0.5, 0.2) in the unit square (the centres of the dots of the right picture), with weights in the ratio 1 : 0.7 : 0.5 (the areas of the dots, as in a scatter plot where the area is proportional to the weight). The left picture of p. 17 is best fitted by a blur of σ ≈ 0.09 on a 24 × 24 grid. The lab computes the blurred distribution three ways and compares each with the exact masses of the grid cells.

§1.7 A point about notations p. 18 · fr. 17

The slide. “If \(\alpha = \sum_{i=1}^\text{N} a_i\delta_{x_i}\) is a probability distribution and \(f:x\mapsto f(x)\in\mathbb{R}\) is a continuous function,”

\[ \underbrace{\vphantom{\int_x}\sum_{i=1}^\text{N}a_i f(x_i)}_{\text{Programming}} ~~=~~ \underbrace{\int_x f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\alpha(x)}_{\text{Integration}} ~~=~~ \underbrace{\vphantom{\int_x} \langle\, \alpha\,,\, f\,\rangle}_{\text{Analysis}} ~~=~~ \underbrace{\vphantom{\int_x}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim \alpha}\big[ f(X)\big]}_{\text{Probability}}~. \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^\text{N}a_i f(x_i)}_{\text{Programming}} &~=~ \underbrace{\int_x f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\alpha(x)}_{\text{Integration}} \\ &~=~ \underbrace{\langle\, \alpha\,,\, f\,\rangle}_{\text{Analysis}} ~=~ \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{X\sim \alpha}\big[ f(X)\big]}_{\text{Probability}}~. \end{aligned} \]

“To study spaces of probability distributions, the \(\langle\, \alpha \,, \, f \,\rangle\) notation is superior as it highlights the linearity with respect to both distributions and functions:”

\[ \begin{aligned} \langle\, \tfrac{1}{2}\alpha + \tfrac{1}{2}\beta \,, \, f \,\rangle ~&=~ \tfrac{1}{2}\langle\, \alpha \,, \, f \,\rangle + \tfrac{1}{2}\langle\,\beta \,, \, f \,\rangle~, \\ \langle\, \alpha \,, \, f + g\,\rangle ~&=~ \langle\, \alpha \,, \, f \,\rangle + \langle\,\alpha \,, \, g \,\rangle~. \end{aligned} \]

Why it matters. The four expressions are the same number: the average of \(f\) under \(\alpha\). The pairing notation treats \(\alpha\) and \(f\) symmetrically: a distribution is something that eats a function and returns a number, linearly. This is the point of view of the whole second part. A distribution is known once all its averages \(\langle \alpha, f\rangle\) are known, so two distributions can be compared through their averages. Choosing which test functions \(f\) to use is choosing a distance, and §2.14 shows that the four families of the lecture are four such choices.

How it is used. The blur of §1.6 is a pairing: the blurred density at \(y\) is \(\langle \alpha, g(\cdot - y)\rangle\). The kernel norms of §2.5 are double pairings, \(\langle \alpha - \beta, k \star (\alpha - \beta)\rangle\). The mixture \(\frac12\alpha + \frac12\beta\) of the first line is the kernel geodesic of p. 27, a fading from one distribution to the other.

§2.2 The total variation distance p. 21 · fr. 19

The slide. “The space of probability distributions on \(\{x_1, \dots, x_\text{K}\}\) is a simplex of dimension \(\text{K}-1\). The Total Variation is the L1–Manhattan distance:”

\[ \mathrm{TV}(\source{\alpha},\target{\beta}) ~=~ \sum_i |\source{a(x_i)} - \target{b(x_i)}|~. \]

“This distance: Maxes out at 2 with disjoint supports. Pays no attention to \(\|x_i-x_j\|\). Pays no attention to unlikely events.” The figure draws the simplex for three labels, a triangle in the corner of three axes a(x₁), a(x₂), a(x₃) (lion, tiger, chicken), and two histograms over four labels with green arrows for the differences.

The simplex. A distribution over K labels is a vector of K non-negative numbers that add up to 1. For K = 3 these vectors fill a triangle whose corners are the three certain predictions (1, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0), (0, 0, 1); its dimension is K − 1 = 2 because of the constraint. The total variation measures distances on this triangle with the Manhattan norm.

Why it works as a distance. It is a norm of the difference, so it is symmetric, zero only when the histograms coincide, and satisfies the triangle inequality. Half of it has a clean meaning (Scheffé’s identity): \(\tfrac12 \mathrm{TV}(\alpha, \beta) = \max_A |\alpha(A) - \beta(A)|\), the largest difference of probability that the two distributions assign to the same event. It is the distance a statistician uses to say that two sampling schemes are indistinguishable.

When it fails. The three bullets are three blind spots. Disjoint supports always give 2, however close the supports: two Dirac masses 1 mm apart or 1 km apart are at the same distance, so the total variation gives no gradient to a model whose sample must move towards the data (§2.13). The labels are interchangeable (Lab 5). And a label of probability 0.001 can change the total variation by at most 0.002, so rare events hardly count, which §2.3 turns upside down.

Watch out

Many textbooks define the total variation as half this sum, so that it lies between 0 and 1 and equals \(\max_A |\alpha(A) - \beta(A)|\). The slide’s convention, with its maximum at 2, is the L1 norm, and it is the one that makes the dual formula of p. 42 exact (§2.14). The two histograms drawn on the right of p. 21 are illustrations: their bars add up to 28.0 and 29.6 grid units, not to the same total.

§2.3 Maximum likelihood and the relative entropy p. 22–24 · fr. 20–22

The slides. p. 22: “If \(\target{\beta = (b(1), \dots, b(\text{K}))}\) is a model distribution on \(\{1, \dots, \text{K}\}\), the likelihood of observing a sample \(x\) is \(\target{L_\beta}(x)~=~ \target{b(x)}\). Assuming independence, the joint likelihood of a sample \((x_1, \dots, x_\text{N})\) is: \(\target{L_\beta}(x_1, \dots, x_\text{N}) ~=~ \target{b(x_1) \cdots b(x_\text{N})}\). Finding a sample \((x_1, \dots, x_\text{N})\) that maximizes the likelihood is equivalent to minimizing:”

\[ \target{\ell_\beta}(x_1, \dots, x_\text{N}) ~=~ - \frac{1}{\text{N}} \log \big[ L_\beta(x_1, \dots, x_\text{N}) \big] ~=~ \frac{1}{\text{N}} \sum_{i=1}^\text{N} \log\big[ 1 / \target{b(x_i)} \big] \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \target{\ell_\beta}(x_1, \dots, x_\text{N}) ~&=~ - \frac{1}{\text{N}} \log \big[ L_\beta(x_1, \dots, x_\text{N}) \big] \\ ~&=~ \frac{1}{\text{N}} \sum_{i=1}^\text{N} \log\big[ 1 / \target{b(x_i)} \big] \end{aligned} \]

“If the \(x_i\) are drawn independently according to a data distribution \(\alpha\), this converges to:”

\[ \target{\ell_\beta}(\source{\alpha}) ~=~ \lim_{\text{N}\rightarrow +\infty} \sum_{k=1}^\text{K} \frac{\#\{\,i ~|~ x_i = k \,\} }{\text{N}} \log\big[ 1 / \target{b(k)}\big] ~=~ \sum_{k=1}^\text{K} \source{a(k)} \,\log\big[ 1 / \target{b(k)}\big] \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \target{\ell_\beta}(\source{\alpha}) ~&=~ \lim_{\text{N}\rightarrow +\infty} \sum_{k=1}^\text{K} \frac{\#\{\,i ~|~ x_i = k \,\} }{\text{N}} \log\big[ 1 / \target{b(k)}\big] \\ ~&=~ \sum_{k=1}^\text{K} \source{a(k)} \,\log\big[ 1 / \target{b(k)}\big] \end{aligned} \]

p. 23: “In practice, the data distribution \(\alpha\) is fixed and we try to find a model distribution \(\beta\) which is as likely as possible. This is equivalent to minimizing the relative entropy or Kullback–Leibler divergence:”

\[ \mathrm{KL}(\source{\alpha}, \target{\beta}) ~=~ \target{\ell_\beta}(\source{\alpha}) - \source{\ell_\alpha}(\source{\alpha}) ~=~ \sum_{k=1}^\text{K} \source{a(k)} \,\log \big[ \source{a(k)} / \target{b(k)} \big]. \]

“We have that \(\mathrm{KL}(\source{\alpha}, \source{\alpha}) = 0\) and \(\mathrm{KL}(\source{\alpha}, \target{\beta}) \geqslant 0\), since \(\log\) is concave:”

\[ \begin{aligned} \log\big[ \target{b(k)} / \source{a(k)}\big] ~&\leqslant~ \target{b(k)} / \source{a(k)} - 1 \\ \Longrightarrow \quad \log\big[ \source{a(k)} / \target{b(k)}\big] ~&\geqslant~ 1 - \target{b(k)} / \source{a(k)} \\ \Longrightarrow \quad \sum_{k=1}^\text{K} \source{a(k)} \, \log\big[ \source{a(k)} / \target{b(k)}\big] ~&\geqslant~ \sum_{k=1}^\text{K} \source{a(k)} \,\big[ 1 - \target{b(k)} / \source{a(k)}\big] ~=~0~. \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &\log\big[ \target{b(k)} / \source{a(k)}\big] \leqslant \target{b(k)} / \source{a(k)} - 1 \\ &\Longrightarrow \log\big[ \source{a(k)} / \target{b(k)}\big] \geqslant 1 - \target{b(k)} / \source{a(k)} \\ &\Longrightarrow \sum_{k=1}^\text{K} \source{a(k)} \, \log\big[ \source{a(k)} / \target{b(k)}\big] \\ &\qquad \geqslant \sum_{k=1}^\text{K} \source{a(k)} \,\big[ 1 - \target{b(k)} / \source{a(k)}\big] ~=~0~. \end{aligned} \]

p. 24: “\(\mathrm{KL}(\source{\alpha}, \target{\beta}) = \sum_{k} \source{a(k)} \log [\source{a(k)} / \target{b(k)}] = \int_x \source{a(x)} \log [ \source{a(x)} / \target{b(x)} ] \,\mathrm{d}x\): Is not symmetric – remember it as \(\mathrm{KL}(\source{\text{data}} \,|\, \target{\text{model}})\). Is tied to an assumption of independence. Historically: compression on communication networks ⟹ .zip format. Crucially, the relative entropy: Pays no attention to \(\|x_i-x_j\|\). Pays a lot of attention to unlikely events: \(\log(0^+) = -\infty\).”

From likelihood to entropy. The likelihood of a sample under a model is the product of the probabilities of its observations; its negative logarithm, divided by N, is an average of \(\log[1/b(x_i)]\). As the sample grows, each label k appears a share \(a(k)\) of the time, and the average becomes the cross-entropy \(\sum_k a(k)\log[1/b(k)]\). The best possible model is the data distribution itself, whose cross-entropy with itself is the entropy \(H(\alpha)\). The relative entropy is the excess, \(\mathrm{KL}(\alpha, \beta) = \ell_\beta(\alpha) - H(\alpha)\): maximising the likelihood of a model on a large sample is minimising its relative entropy to the data. The proof of positivity uses only \(\log u \leqslant u - 1\).

Why bits. With logarithms in base 2, \(\log_2[1/b(k)]\) is the length in bits of the codeword that an optimal code built for \(\beta\) gives to the symbol k (Shannon, 1948). The cross-entropy is then the average length of a message from \(\alpha\) encoded with a code designed for \(\beta\), and \(\mathrm{KL}(\alpha, \beta)\) is the number of bits wasted per symbol by using the wrong code. That is the link to compression on the slide: the .zip format uses Huffman codes, which realise these lengths to within one bit per symbol.

What the lab measures. Real data: the two versions of Henri Poincaré’s Science and Hypothesis, the book quoted by Lecture 5: the French original (Flammarion edition of 1917, from the Internet Archive’s scan) and George Bruce Halsted’s English translation of 1913 (Project Gutenberg). Each text becomes a stream of letters, accented letters and spaces; each language becomes a distribution over these symbols.

§2.4 Information geometry: the Fisher–Rao metric p. 25 · fr. 23

The slide. “The Gauss map defines a parametric surface: \(\mathcal{N}:(m,\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_+ \mapsto \mathcal{N}(m,\sigma) \in \mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R})\). Direct computations show that:”

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathrm{KL}\big( \source{\mathcal{N}(m+\mathrm{d}m,\sigma+\mathrm{d}\sigma)}, \target{\mathcal{N}(m,\sigma)}\big) \\ &~=~ \frac{\source{\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}m^2 + \mathrm{d}\sigma^2} }{\target{\sigma^2}}~+~ o(\source{\mathrm{d}m^2,\mathrm{d}\sigma^2}) \end{aligned} \]

“⟹ Poincaré metric on the upper half-plane. With its invariance to translation and scalings, the relative entropy induces a hyperbolic geometry on the surface of Gaussian distributions.” [Fey17]. The figure is the right panel of Lecture 5’s p. 39.

Checking the formula. The relative entropy between two Gaussians has a closed form, \(\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{N}(m_1,\sigma_1), \mathcal{N}(m_2,\sigma_2)) = \log(\sigma_2/\sigma_1) + \frac{\sigma_1^2 + (m_1-m_2)^2}{2\sigma_2^2} - \frac12\). Put \(m_1 = m + \mathrm{d}m\), \(\sigma_1 = \sigma + \mathrm{d}\sigma\), \(m_2 = m\), \(\sigma_2 = \sigma\) and expand \(\log(1+u) = u - u^2/2 + \dots\): the first-order terms cancel and what remains is \((\tfrac12\mathrm{d}m^2 + \mathrm{d}\sigma^2)/\sigma^2\), the slide’s formula. The relative entropy is not a distance, but between nearby distributions it behaves like half the square of one: \(\mathrm{KL} \approx \frac12\,\mathrm{d}s^2\) with \(\mathrm{d}s^2 = (\mathrm{d}m^2 + 2\,\mathrm{d}\sigma^2)/\sigma^2\), the Fisher–Rao metric.

Why hyperbolic. The relative entropy does not change when the units change: replacing \(x\) by \(ax + b\) moves \(\mathcal{N}(m,\sigma)\) to \(\mathcal{N}(am+b, a\sigma)\) for both distributions at once, and the integral \(\int a\log(a/b)\) is unaffected by a change of variables. A metric on the half-plane that is invariant under all the maps \((m, \sigma) \mapsto (am + b, a\sigma)\) must be of the form \((c_1\,\mathrm{d}m^2 + c_2\,\mathrm{d}\sigma^2)/\sigma^2\) (invariance under \(x \mapsto -x\) rules out a cross term): the Poincaré half-plane of Lecture 5, stretched horizontally. In the coordinates \((m/\sqrt2, \sigma)\), \(\mathrm{d}s^2 = 2 \times (\text{Poincaré metric})\). Lecture 5 measured the consequences (L5 Lab 9): the Fisher–Rao geodesic from \(\mathcal{N}(0, 0.1)\) to \(\mathcal{N}(4, 1)\) rises to σ = 1.59 before coming down, and the relative entropy between the two is 9.81 one way and 847.2 the other.

§2.5 Kernel norms: recover compatibility with the addition p. 26–27 · fr. 24–25

The slides. p. 26: “For sources \(\alpha = \sum_i a_i \delta_{x_i}\) and targets \(\beta = \sum_j b_j \delta_{y_j}\), choose a symmetric function \(g\) that induces a convolution kernel \(k = g\star g\) and use:”

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}_k(\source{\alpha}, \target{\beta}) ~&=~ \|\, g\star \source{\alpha} - g\star \target{\beta}\,\|_{L^2}^2 \\ ~&=~ \langle \, \source{\alpha} - \target{\beta}\,,\, k\star (\source{\alpha} - \target{\beta}) \,\rangle \\ ~&=~ \source{\sum_i \sum_j a_i a_j \,k(x_i,x_j)} \\ &- 2 \source{\sum_i} \target{\sum_j} \source{a_i} \target{b_j} \,k(\source{x_i},\target{y_j}) \\ &+ ~~ \target{\sum_i \sum_j b_i b_j \,k(y_i,y_j)}~. \end{aligned} \]

The figure shows two closed curves, red \(\alpha\) and blue \(\beta\), and the “Blurred signal \(g \star (\source{\alpha} - \target{\beta})\)”, red where \(\alpha\) dominates and blue where \(\beta\) does. p. 27: “Kernel norms (aka. Hilbert or Sobolev norms, Maximum Mean Discrepancies): Are quadratic with respect to the weights \(\source{a_i}\) and \(\target{b_j}\). Are compatible with the addition – the geodesic from \(\source{\alpha}\) to \(\target{\beta}\) is: \(t\in[0,1] \mapsto (1-t)\,\source{\alpha} + t\,\target{\beta}\). Have wildly different behaviors depending on \(k(x,y)\): see the lab session. Crucially, these formulas: Pay a lot of attention to \(\|x_i-y_j\|\). Pay little attention to unlikely events, except if they are associated to large values of \(k(x,y)\).”

The idea. Two clouds of points that are close but not identical have disjoint supports: the total variation sees them as maximally different. Blur both with the same bump \(g\): the blurred densities overlap, and their L² distance is small when the clouds are close. The formula of the second line comes from expanding the square: blurring twice by \(g\) is blurring once by \(k = g \star g\). The last line is what a computer evaluates: three double sums of kernel values, with no grid and no density estimation. This is the maximum mean discrepancy of the statistics literature and the “energy” of shape registration.

Why it works. Because \(k = g \star g\), the Fourier transform of \(k\) is \(|\hat g|^2 \geqslant 0\): the kernel is positive definite, the criterion of Lecture 2 (L2 §6.4). Then \(\mathrm{d}_k\) is the squared norm of \(\alpha - \beta\) in a Hilbert space, so it is zero only when \(\alpha = \beta\) (for kernels such as the Gaussian), symmetric, and it satisfies the triangle inequality after a square root. It is “compatible with the addition” because the space is linear: the shortest path from \(\alpha\) to \(\beta\) is the straight line of mixtures, along which mass fades out at \(\alpha\) and fades in at \(\beta\) without moving.

When it fails. The kernel sets a scale. Features smaller than its width are blurred away; points farther apart than a few widths do not interact at all. Lab 8 shows what this does to gradient descent: the “screening” of p. 42.

§2.6 Optimal transport generalises sorting to spaces of dimension D > 1 p. 28–29 · fr. 26–27

The slides. p. 28: “If \(\ared{\text{A} = (\text{x}_\text{1}, \dots, \text{x}_\text{N})}\) and \(\bblue{\text{B} = (\text{y}_\text{1}, \dots, \text{y}_\text{N})}\) are two clouds of N points in \(\mathbb{R}^\text{D}\), we define:”

\[ \OT(\ared{\text{A}},\bblue{\text{B}}) ~=~ \min_{\sigma \in \mathcal{S}_\text{N}}~ \frac{1}{2\text{N}} \sum_{i=1}^\text{N} \vgreen{\|~\ared{\text{x}_i} - \bblue{\text{y}_{\sigma(i)}}\|^2} \]

“Generalizes sorting to metric spaces. Linear problem on the permutation matrix \(\ggrey{\text{P}}\):”

\[ \OT(\ared{\alpha},\bblue{\beta}) ~=~ \min_{\ggrey{\text{P}} \in \mathbb{R}^{\text{N}\times \text{N}}} \sum_{i, j=1}^{\text{N}} \ggrey{\text{P}_{i,j}} \cdot \vgreen{ \tfrac{1}{2} \|~ \ared{\text{x}_i} - \bblue{\text{y}_j} \|^2 }~, \]
\[ \text{s.t.}\quad \ggrey{\text{P}_{i,j}} \geqslant 0, \qquad \underbrace{\bblue{\textstyle\sum_j} \ggrey{\text{P}_{i,j}} = \ared{a_i}}_{\text{Each source point…}}, \qquad \underbrace{\ared{\textstyle\sum_i} \ggrey{\text{P}_{i,j}} = \bblue{b_j}.}_{\text{is transported onto the target.}} \]

The drawing shows five red points x₁ … x₅ on one vertical line and five blue points on another, labelled y₃, y₅, y₂, y₄, y₁ from top to bottom, with the “assignment σ: ⟦1, 5⟧ → ⟦1, 5⟧” drawn as lines. p. 29, “Practical use”: “Alternatively, we understand OT as: Nearest neighbor projection + incompressibility constraint. Fundamental example of linear optimization over the transport plan \(\ggrey{\text{P}_{i,j}}\). This theory induces two main quantities: The transport plan \(\ggrey{\text{P}_{i,j}} \simeq\) the optimal mapping \(\ared{x_i} \mapsto \bblue{y_{\sigma(i)}}\). The “Wasserstein” distance \(\sqrt{\OT(\ared{\text{A}},\bblue{\text{B}})}\).”

The idea. Match each source point with one target point, every target used once, so that the total squared displacement is as small as possible. In one dimension the answer is known to everyone who has sorted a list: match the smallest source with the smallest target, the second with the second, and so on. The drawing of p. 28 is exactly this: x₁ goes to the highest target (y₃), x₅ to the lowest (y₁), and no two lines cross. In two or more dimensions there is no order, and the matching must be computed; optimal transport is the name of this “sorting” in any dimension.

Why a linear program. Replace the permutation by a matrix \(\text{P}\) whose entry \(\text{P}_{i,j}\) is the mass sent from \(x_i\) to \(y_j\). The constraints say that each source sends all its mass \(a_i\) and each target receives exactly \(b_j\); the cost is linear in \(\text{P}\). With \(a_i = b_j = 1/\text{N}\), the feasible matrices are the permutation matrices divided by N and all their averages (the Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem), and a linear function reaches its minimum at a corner: some optimal plan is a permutation. The linear form also handles weights that are not equal, where no permutation exists and the mass of a point must be split.

“Projection + incompressibility”. Sending every source point to its nearest target minimises each displacement separately but piles many sources onto a few targets. The constraint \(\sum_i \text{P}_{i,j} = b_j\) forbids this, like the incompressibility of a fluid forbids particles from crowding.

§2.8 Gaussians: Euclidean or hyperbolic p. 34–35 · fr. 29–30

The slides. p. 34: “The Gauss map defines a parametric surface: \(\mathcal{N}:(m,\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_+ \mapsto \mathcal{N}(m,\sigma) \in \mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R})\). Direct computations show that:”

\[ 2\, \mathrm{OT}\big( \source{\mathcal{N}(m_1,\sigma_1)}, \target{\mathcal{N}(m_2,\sigma_2)}\big) ~=~ (\source{m_1} - \target{m_2})^2 ~+~ (\source{\sigma_1} - \target{\sigma_2})^2~. \]

“⟹ Euclidean metric on the upper half-plane. Optimal transport lifts the geometry of the sample space to the surface of Gaussian distributions.” [PC18]. p. 35 puts both panels side by side: “Gaussians + Wasserstein metric = Euclidean. Gaussians + relative entropy = Poincaré.”

Why Euclidean. In one dimension, transport is sorting (§2.6): the optimal map sends the quantile of level t of the first distribution to the quantile of level t of the second. For Gaussians the quantiles are \(m + \sigma z_t\), so the map is \(x \mapsto m_2 + \frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}(x - m_1)\), and the squared displacement averages to \((m_1 - m_2)^2 + (\sigma_1 - \sigma_2)^2\). Along the geodesic, each point moves in a straight line at constant speed; the densities translate and widen, as in the left panel. The Fisher–Rao geodesic of §2.4 instead flattens the distribution in the middle, because the relative entropy finds it cheaper to become vague than to move.

What the choice changes. Transport measures differences in the units of the data: a shift of 1 mm costs the same whether the Gaussians are narrow or wide. The relative entropy measures differences in units of the spread: a shift of 1 mm between two narrow Gaussians is enormous, between two wide ones negligible. Lecture 5’s Lab 9 (L5 Lab 9) measured both on the same pair: 4.100 for transport and 6.365 for Fisher–Rao from \(\mathcal{N}(0, 0.1)\) to \(\mathcal{N}(4, 1)\).

Both figures come from Peyré and Cuturi’s book [PC18], and the Fisher–Rao geodesic is drawn as a half-circle in the (m, σ) plane without scales on the axes. With \(\mathrm{d}s^2 = (\mathrm{d}m^2 + 2\,\mathrm{d}\sigma^2)/\sigma^2\), the geodesics are half-circles only in the coordinates \((m/\sqrt2, \sigma)\), as Lecture 5 noted (L5 §3.3).

§2.10 How should we solve the OT problem? p. 37–38 · fr. 32–33

The slides. p. 37: “Key dates for discrete optimal transport with N points: [Kan42]: Dual problem of Kantorovitch. [Kuh55]: Hungarian methods in \(O(\text{N}^3)\). [Ber79]: Auction algorithm in \(O(\text{N}^2)\). [KY94]: SoftAssign = Sinkhorn + simulated annealing, in \(O(\text{N}^2)\). [GRL+98, CR00]: Robust Point Matching = Sinkhorn as a loss. [Cut13]: Start of the GPU era. [Mér11, Lév15, Sch19]: multi-scale solvers in \(O(\text{N}\log \text{N})\). Solution, today: Multiscale Sinkhorn algorithm, on the GPU. ⟹ Generalized QuickSort algorithm, \(\simeq O(\text{N}\log \text{N})\) if D is small, fast \(O(\text{N}^2)\) otherwise.” p. 38, “Scaling up optimal transport to anatomical data”: “Progresses of the last decade add up to a \(\times \textbf{100}\) - \(\times \textbf{1000}\) acceleration: Sinkhorn GPU \(\xrightarrow{\times 10}\) + KeOps \(\xrightarrow{\times 10}\) + Annealing \(\xrightarrow{\times 10}\) + Multi-scale. With a precision of 1%, on a gaming GPU: pip install geomloss + modern GPU (1 000 €) ⟹ 10k points in 30-50ms, 100k points in 100-200ms” (a Stanford dragon at two resolutions).

The three families of solvers. The Hungarian method builds the optimal assignment by augmenting paths, maintaining dual potentials (the prices of Kantorovich’s dual problem) so that the partial matching stays optimal; the modern version takes \(O(\text{N}^3)\) operations. The auction algorithm lets source points bid for targets: each unassigned source raises the price of its favourite target by the margin over its second choice plus ε, and takes it. It is naturally parallel, and with ε-scaling it finds the exact optimum. Sinkhorn’s algorithm solves the transport problem with an entropic penalty \(\varepsilon\,\mathrm{KL}(\text{P}\,|\,a \otimes b)\): the optimal plan has the form \(\text{P}_{ij} = a_i b_j \exp((f_i + g_j - C_{ij})/\varepsilon)\), and alternately fitting the row sums and the column sums updates \(f\) and \(g\) by log-sum-exp reductions, the soft minimum of §1.6. Each iteration costs \(O(\text{N}^2)\) simple operations that a GPU performs in parallel; decreasing ε along the iterations (“annealing”, “ε-scaling”) keeps their number small, and coarse-to-fine “multi-scale” schemes avoid most of the \(\text{N}^2\) pairs. The debiased Sinkhorn divergence \(S_\varepsilon(\alpha,\beta) = \OT_\varepsilon(\alpha,\beta) - \frac12 \OT_\varepsilon(\alpha,\alpha) - \frac12\OT_\varepsilon(\beta,\beta)\) removes the blurring bias and is what GeomLoss computes.

What the lab measures. The page implements the three solvers in JavaScript and times them on the ellipse and paisley of p. 30. Its Sinkhorn divergence reproduces GeomLoss’s SamplesLoss("sinkhorn", p=2, blur, scaling=0.9) to 10⁻¹⁵, value and gradient (checked in the page’s verification scripts).

§2.12 Open problem 1: topology-aware distances p. 40 · fr. 35

The slide. “‘OT that preserves the neighborhood structure’? The problem has been studied for decades: Optimization: Quadratic assignment… Optimal Transport: Gromov–Wasserstein… Fluid mechanics: Camassa–Holm equation… Shape analysis: LDDMM, SVF… Statistics: Stein Variational Gradient Descent… Deep learning: Neural ODEs… Mature tools exist but remain \(\geqslant\) 100 slower than Optimal Transport.” The figure shows four landmarks with momenta on a grid (“Initial configuration”, φ⁰ = Id) and the deformed grid after the flow (“Diffeomorphic displacement”).

The problem. Optimal transport looks at each point separately: it minimises a sum of displacements and does not care whether two neighbours end up far apart. Lab 10 measured it: only about half of the nearest neighbours survive the transport of an ellipse onto a paisley, and in the moons the rainbow folds over itself. For an organ, tearing the tissue is not an option.

The alternatives. The listed methods all add a notion of neighbourhood. The quadratic assignment problem and the Gromov–Wasserstein distance compare the matrix of distances within the source to the one within the target, so that neighbours must map to neighbours (an NP-hard problem in general). LDDMM, the method of the figure, moves the whole space by a smooth flow: the points are carried by a velocity field that a kernel keeps smooth, and the grid of the picture deforms without folding. Lecture 5 computed such a flow for three landmarks by geodesic shooting (L5 Lab 13). The price is computational: each gradient step integrates an ordinary differential equation over all points, where transport solves one matching.

§2.13 Open problem 2: the curse of dimensionality p. 41 · fr. 36

The slide. “In high dimension, the matrix of Euclidean distances stops being informative. Standard kernels and OT metrics are overwhelmed by statistical noise. How can we compute meaningful distances and gradients?” The figure is the generative-model picture of p. 11.

The problem. Lecture 3 showed that independent Gaussian samples in dimension D are all at roughly the same distance from each other (L3 Lab 3). A distance between two samples is then dominated by the randomness of the samples themselves. Two samples of the same distribution are far apart in transport distance, because matching N random points to N other random points in dimension D leaves each point about \(\text{N}^{-1/\text{D}}\) times the diameter from its partner, a quantity that shrinks absurdly slowly when D is large. A real difference between the distributions then has to stand out against this noise floor.

What the lab measures. Two samples of 200 points from the standard Gaussian in dimension D, and a third from an alternative: the first coordinate shifted by 1 (a change of mean), or multiplied by 2 (a change of spread). For each D the lab computes the distance between the two samples of the same law and between the different laws, with W₂ (exact assignment), the energy distance, and the Gaussian kernel norm with the median of the pairwise distances as width, and runs a permutation test for each.

§2.14 Dual norms: a fundamental insight from functional analysis p. 42–43 · fr. 37–38

The slides. Both pages start from the same formula:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Loss}(\ared{\alpha},\bblue{\beta})~=~ &\max_{f\,\in\, B}~ \langle\,\ared{\alpha}-\bblue{\beta}\,,\,f\,\rangle, \\ \text{look for}~~ \ared{\theta^*} ~=~\arg\min_{\ared{\theta}} &\max_{f\,\in\, B} ~ \langle\,\ared{\alpha(\theta)}-\bblue{\beta}\,,\,f\,\rangle \end{aligned} \]

p. 42: “\(B = \{ \, \|\,f\,\|_\infty \leqslant 1\,\}\) ⟹ Loss = TV norm: Zero geometry, always saturates on disjoint samples. Too many test functions. \(B = \{ \, \|\,f\,\|_{L^2}^2 + \|\,\nabla f\,\|_{L^2}^2 + \cdots \leqslant 1\,\}\) ⟹ Loss = kernel norm: Screening artifacts – see lab session. In high dimension, samples are at equal distance from each other. “Smooth” functions are either “constant” or “bounded”: fall back on TV behavior.” p. 43: “\(B = \{ \, f \text{ is 1-Lipschitz}\,\}\) ⟹ Loss = Wasserstein-1: Modern solvers are nearly as efficient as a closed formula. Useless in \((\mathbb{R}^{512\times 512}, \|\cdot\|_2)\): the ground cost makes no sense. \(B \simeq \{ \, f \text{ is 1-Lipschitz}\,\} \cap \{ \, f \text{ is a } \textbf{CNN}\,\}\) ⟹ Loss = Wasserstein–GAN: Use perceptual test functions. No simple formula: use gradient ascent. Leads to a cumbersome min-max optimization.”

The idea. §1.7 said that a distribution is known through its averages \(\langle \alpha, f \rangle\). To compare two distributions, look for the test function \(f\) whose averages differ most, within a set \(B\) of allowed functions. The choice of \(B\) is the choice of distance. Bounded functions give the total variation: the best \(f\) is +1 where \(\alpha\) exceeds \(\beta\) and −1 elsewhere. Functions of bounded Sobolev norm give a kernel norm (its square root), with the best \(f\) proportional to the blurred signal \(k \star (\alpha - \beta)\) of p. 26. 1-Lipschitz functions, whose slope never exceeds 1, give the Wasserstein-1 distance (Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality); in one dimension the best \(f\) climbs with slope +1 where the cumulative distribution of \(\beta\) is ahead of that of \(\alpha\), and descends elsewhere.

Why GANs. A generative adversarial network trains a generator \(\alpha(\theta)\) against a “critic”, a network \(f\) that tries to separate generated samples from real ones: exactly the min-max of the second line, with \(B\) the functions a network can represent. The Wasserstein GAN restricts the critic to be (roughly) 1-Lipschitz, by clipping its weights or penalising its slopes. A convolutional critic on images compares textures and shapes rather than pixel-wise Euclidean distances (“perceptual test functions”), which is why it works where the Wasserstein-1 distance with the pixel metric is “useless”. The price is that the inner maximum is never solved exactly: it is chased by gradient ascent, step after step.

Lab 14

Test functions: the dual view (p. 42–43)

Question. What do the optimal test functions of the total variation, a kernel norm and W₁ look like for the curves of p. 6? How close does a small critic trained by gradient ascent get to W₁, with a slope penalty or with weight clipping?

What the lab shows (default: α against β, σ = 0.05, critics of 16 tanh units with a slope penalty, 1,500 Adam steps, eight random initialisations)
  • Each ball of test functions gives its distance exactly. The sign function achieves the total variation, 0.550; the normalised blurred signal achieves the kernel norm √dk = 0.216 for σ = 0.05; the 1-Lipschitz zig-zag built from the two cumulative distributions achieves W₁ = 0.0619. The three optimal functions look nothing alike: the first jumps, the second is smooth, the third has slopes ±1.
  • A small critic with a slope penalty nearly finds W₁, but not reliably. Over eight random initialisations, sixteen tanh units reach a median of 96.9% of W₁, from 92.9% to 100.1% (the penalty is soft, so a slope slightly above 1 can overshoot W₁). Four units reach a median of 94.1%; sixty-four units, slower to train in the same number of steps, 89.1%, with one run stopping at 77.5%.
  • Single runs are fragile. With Adam and a penalty, differences in the last digit of the arithmetic lead to different critics: the page’s Python script finds medians of 98.1% (16 units) and 89.7% (64 units) with the same algorithm and the same initialisations, and another browser may give other values again. Chasing the inner maximum by gradient ascent is the “cumbersome min-max optimization” of p. 43.
  • Weight clipping gives a value, not a distance. With weights clipped at 0.1, every initialisation ends at the same critic, with all its weights at the clip value: value 0.0017, largest slope 0.16; even rescaled by that slope it gives only 17% of W₁. Clipped at 1, the value is 0.262, four times W₁, because the critic is 16-Lipschitz. The number that a clipped WGAN reports is W₁ times an unknown factor, and it can be far from W₁ even after that factor is removed.
  • Easier pairs are easier. Between α and γ, whose W₁ is 0.226, the penalised critics of 16 units reach 101.5% to 103.4% of W₁ (their slopes slightly exceed 1), and weight clipping at 0.1 recovers W₁ exactly once rescaled by the slope.

§2.15 Toy models and experiments p. 44 · fr. 39

What it means. A diffusion model learns, at every noise level, the direction in which a noisy image should move to become more plausible: the gradient of the log-density of the blurred data distribution, which is the gradient of the soft distance of §1.6 to the data (the “score”). The network that predicts it, a U-Net, plays the role of the test function: its architecture decides which features of images count. The lecture ends where it started: the choice of a distance is a choice of what to notice, and in high dimension that choice is made by the architecture of a network, which toy models help to understand.

What the slides conclude · references

The deck has no conclusion slide; its recap (p. 39) and its last page (p. 44) play that role. The recap sorts the four families by two properties: whether they use the ground metric (total variation and relative entropy do not, kernel norms “more or less”, optimal transport “extremely”) and whether they are cheap (kernels are, transport has become so). The last page states a method: combine “mathematical insights derived from toy models” with “numerical experiments on modern hardware”, and “play with gradient descent to build an intuition about classical formulas”.

What to keep from this page. A data set is a distribution, and the same distribution can be stored as a histogram, a sample or a weighted cloud (Lab 4); distributions make shapes independent of their sampling (Lab 7) and labels independent of their order (Lab 3). Comparing two distributions requires a choice, and the choices disagree on real examples (Lab 1, Lab 5). The relative entropy is the language of likelihood and compression, and it treats an impossible event as infinitely costly (Lab 6). Kernel norms are cheap and linear but see nothing beyond their width (Lab 8). Optimal transport is sorting in any dimension (Lab 9), turns into a displacement in one gradient step (Lab 10), gives geometric averages (Lab 11), and is now fast to compute (Lab 12), but it tears neighbourhoods apart and drowns in noise in high dimension (Lab 13). All four families are dual norms, maximisations over test functions, which is how GANs and diffusion models enter the picture (Lab 14). Points of the slides that needed correcting or qualifying are listed in the report of each section: the density “α = ∫ a(x) dx” (p. 5), the index of the sums on p. 9–10, the likelihood maximised over the sample (p. 22), the equality “= 0” of p. 23, the “.zip” shortcut (p. 24), the factor ½ of the Wasserstein distance (p. 29), the “After” panels that are ten steps of gradient descent (p. 30–33), and the complexities of p. 37.

"""The random generator of the companion pages (mulberry32 + Marsaglia's polar method), in Python.

The page's labs draw their random data with this generator; with the same seed, these functions return the
same numbers, so the snippets below reproduce the page's results instead of results "within sampling error".
"""
import math

M32 = 0xFFFFFFFF


def _imul(a, b):
    return (a * b) & M32


class Rng:
    def __init__(self, seed):
        self.a = (seed & M32) or 1
        self.spare = None

    def uniform(self):                       # uniform in [0, 1), bit-for-bit as the pages' G.rng(seed).uniform()
        self.a = (self.a + 0x6D2B79F5) & M32
        a = self.a
        t = _imul(a ^ (a >> 15), 1 | a)
        t = ((t + _imul(t ^ (t >> 7), 61 | t)) & M32) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & M32) / 4294967296

    def normal(self):                        # Marsaglia's polar method, keeping the spare value as the pages do
        if self.spare is not None:
            s0, self.spare = self.spare, None
            return s0
        while True:
            u, v = 2 * self.uniform() - 1, 2 * self.uniform() - 1
            s = u * u + v * v
            if 0 < s < 1:
                break
        f = math.sqrt(-2 * math.log(s) / s)
        self.spare = v * f
        return u * f

    def int(self, n):
        return math.floor(self.uniform() * n)

    def range(self, lo, hi):
        return lo + (hi - lo) * self.uniform()

    def normals(self, n):
        return [self.normal() for _ in range(n)]

    # ---- vectorised versions (same stream as repeated calls; used for millions of draws) ----
    def uniform_array(self, n):
        import numpy as np
        k = np.arange(1, n + 1, dtype=np.uint64)
        a = (np.uint64(self.a) + k * np.uint64(0x6D2B79F5)) & np.uint64(M32)
        self.a = int(a[-1]) if n else self.a
        m = np.uint64(M32)
        t = ((a ^ (a >> np.uint64(15))) * (a | np.uint64(1))) & m
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> np.uint64(7))) * (t | np.uint64(61))) & m)) & m) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> np.uint64(14))) & m).astype(np.float64) / 4294967296

    def normal_array(self, n):
        import numpy as np
        out = []
        if self.spare is not None and n > 0:
            out.append(self.spare); self.spare = None
        need = n - len(out)
        while need > 0:
            m = max(64, int(need * 0.66) + 64)             # pairs of uniforms to try
            u = self.uniform_array(2 * m)
            x, y = 2 * u[0::2] - 1, 2 * u[1::2] - 1
            s = x * x + y * y
            ok = (s < 1) & (s > 0)
            # keep the stream exact: stop at the pair that completes the request, rewind the rest
            idx = np.flatnonzero(ok)
            pairs_needed = (need + 1) // 2
            if len(idx) >= pairs_needed:
                last = int(idx[pairs_needed - 1])
                self.a = int((self.a - (2 * m - 2 * (last + 1)) * 0x6D2B79F5) & M32)
                idx = idx[:pairs_needed]
            f = np.sqrt(-2 * np.log(s[idx]) / s[idx])
            vals = np.empty(2 * len(idx)); vals[0::2] = x[idx] * f; vals[1::2] = y[idx] * f
            if len(vals) > need:
                self.spare = float(vals[-1]); vals = vals[:-1]
            out.extend(vals.tolist()); need -= len(vals)
        return np.array(out[:n])

Cheat sheet

Distributions (§1.1, §1.7)

α = Σ aᵢ δxᵢ, aᵢ ≥ 0, Σ aᵢ = 1. Histogram: fixed places; sample: fixed weights 1/N; weighted cloud: both vary. ⟨α, f⟩ = Σ aᵢ f(xᵢ) = ∫ f dα = E[f(X)], linear in α and in f.

Uses (§1.3–1.5)

Tests: reject if d(α, β) is large (simulate or permute for the p-value). Matching controls = assignment. Classification: labels as distributions, loss KL(δ, α) = −log αc. Generative models, registration, populations of histograms: minimise or compare distances.

Blur (§1.6)

Convolution of a histogram = adding N(0, σ²) noise to a sample = Gaussian mixture. ℓ(x) = log Σ aᵢ e−|x − xᵢ|²/2σ²; −σ²ℓ(x) → minᵢ |x − xᵢ|²/2: a soft minimum (log-sum-exp).

Total variation (§2.2)

TV = Σ |a − b| ∈ [0, 2] (half of it = maxA |α(A) − β(A)|). Ignores the ground metric; 2 for any disjoint supports; rare events count little. Dual: max over |f| ≤ 1.

Relative entropy (§2.3)

KL(α, β) = Σ a log(a/b) = cross-entropy − entropy ≥ 0 (log u ≤ u − 1). = extra bits per symbol when coding α with β’s code. Not symmetric: KL(data | model). +∞ if the model gives 0 to an observed event.

Fisher–Rao (§2.4)

KL(N(m + dm, σ + dσ), N(m, σ)) ≈ (½dm² + dσ²)/σ² = ½ ds², ds² = (dm² + 2dσ²)/σ²: hyperbolic (Poincaré in (m/√2, σ)), from the invariance under x ↦ ax + b.

Kernel norms (§2.5)

dk = ‖g⋆α − g⋆β‖² = ⟨α − β, k⋆(α − β)⟩ = ΣΣ aa k − 2ΣΣ ab k + ΣΣ bb k, k = g⋆g positive definite. Quadratic, linear geodesics (fading). Sees ‖x − y‖ up to the kernel’s width; screening beyond it. Energy distance: k = −|x − y|.

Optimal transport (§2.6–2.8)

OT = minσ (1/2N) Σ ‖xᵢ − yσ(i)‖² = minP ≥ 0, P1 = a, Pᵀ1 = b ⟨P, ½‖x − y‖²⟩. 1D: sort. W₂² = 2 OT. Plan = displacement (one gradient step lands). Gaussians: W₂² = Δm² + Δσ²: Euclidean.

Solvers (§2.10)

Hungarian O(N³) exact; auction (bids, ε-scaling) exact; Sinkhorn: P = a b e(f + g − C)/ε, log-sum-exp updates O(N²)/iteration, ε-scaling, multiscale. Debiased Sε(α, β) = OTε(α, β) − ½OTε(α, α) − ½OTε(β, β) (GeomLoss).

Open problems, dual norms (§2.12–2.15)

Transport ignores neighbourhoods (Gromov–Wasserstein, LDDMM keep them, ≥ 100× slower). High dimension: sample noise ~N−1/D swamps W₂. Loss = maxf∈B ⟨α − β, f⟩: |f| ≤ 1 → TV; Sobolev ball → kernel; 1-Lipschitz → W₁; Lipschitz CNN → WGAN (min-max).

Self-test

1. What is a probability distribution, in the slides’ sense?
A set of non-negative weights aᵢ, adding up to 1, placed at locations xᵢ: α = Σ aᵢ δxᵢ; in the continuous limit, a density a(x) with dα = a(x) dx.
2. What is the difference between a histogram, a sample and a weighted point cloud?
A histogram has fixed locations (the bins) and variable weights; a sample has fixed weights 1/N and variable locations; a weighted cloud lets both vary.
3. Why is “α = ∫ a(x) dx” not literally correct?
The integral of a density over the whole space is the number 1. The intended meaning is that α has density a: α(A) = ∫A a(x) dx for every region A.
4. How does any distance between distributions give a two-sample test?
Compute the distance between the two samples, then pool them, split them at random many times and recompute it; the p-value is the share of random splits with a distance at least as large as the observed one.
5. Why does least squares on the codes 1, 2, 3 misclassify on p. 9?
A linear score has parallel level lines, so the codes force class 2 to be a band between classes 1 and 3; if the classes are not arranged that way in the plane, some points must fall in the wrong band. Changing the codes changes the result.
6. Where does the ordering invariance of logistic regression come from?
From encoding each label as a distribution (a one-hot vector). Least squares on one-hot vectors is also invariant; the softmax and the relative entropy add valid probabilities.
7. Why is KL(δ(x), α(x)) the cross-entropy loss?
δ(x) puts all its mass on the observed class c, so Σ δ log(δ/α) reduces to log(1/αc(x)), the negative log-probability of the true class.
8. In what sense are a convolved histogram, a noisy sample and a Gaussian mixture the same thing?
Adding independent N(0, σ²) noise to points drawn from α gives the distribution α convolved with a Gaussian; on a grid that is a convolution of the histogram; its density is the mixture Σ aᵢ N(xᵢ, σ²), whose log is the soft distance.
9. What is the maximum of the total variation Σ|a − b|, and when is it reached?
2, when the two distributions have disjoint supports, however close those supports are.
10. What does the total variation ignore?
The distances between locations (lion and tiger are as different as lion and taxi) and the rare events (a label of probability 0.001 moves it by at most 0.002).
11. Why does maximising the likelihood of a model amount to minimising KL(data, model)?
The average negative log-likelihood of a large sample converges to the cross-entropy Σ a log(1/b), which equals the entropy of the data (a constant) plus KL(α, β).
12. Prove that KL(α, β) ≥ 0.
log(b/a) ≤ b/a − 1, so Σ a log(a/b) ≥ Σ a (1 − b/a) = 1 − Σa>0 b ≥ 0.
13. What does KL(α, β) mean in bits?
The number of extra bits per symbol needed to encode data from α with a code optimised for β (for instance a Huffman code built on β’s frequencies).
14. Why is KL(English | French) infinite with maximum-likelihood models of the two books?
The English text contains one “ö” (in the name König), which never occurs in the French text: the French model gives it probability 0, so its log-likelihood is −∞.
15. What is the Fisher–Rao metric on Gaussians, and why is it hyperbolic?
ds² = (dm² + 2dσ²)/σ², the second-order term of the relative entropy. It is invariant under changes of units x ↦ ax + b, which forces the form (c₁dm² + c₂dσ²)/σ²: a rescaled Poincaré half-plane.
16. Write the kernel norm between two weighted clouds.
dk(α, β) = Σᵢ Σⱼ aᵢaⱼ k(xᵢ, xⱼ) − 2 Σᵢ Σⱼ aᵢbⱼ k(xᵢ, yⱼ) + Σᵢ Σⱼ bᵢbⱼ k(yᵢ, yⱼ), with k = g ⋆ g.
17. What is the “screening” of kernel norms?
A point farther than a few kernel widths from the target feels almost no attraction, because the kernel and its derivative are nearly zero there: gradient descent leaves it stranded (57.7% of the points in Lab 8 with a Gaussian of width 0.05).
18. Why is optimal transport “sorting in D dimensions”?
In one dimension the cheapest matching of two samples for a squared cost is the sorted one; optimal transport defines the cheapest matching in any dimension, where there is no order.
19. Why does the linear program of p. 28 have a permutation as a solution?
With equal weights 1/N, the feasible plans are the doubly stochastic matrices divided by N, whose corners are the permutation matrices (Birkhoff–von Neumann); a linear cost is minimised at a corner.
20. What is the relation between the slides’ OT and the usual Wasserstein distance W₂?
OT = ½ W₂², so √OT = W₂/√2; the formula of p. 34 says W₂² = (m₁ − m₂)² + (σ₁ − σ₂)².
21. What are the “After” pictures of p. 30–33?
The positions after ten steps of gradient descent on a transport loss (frames 001 and 002 of the folder are steps 1 and 2). One step on the exact transport cost with step size N moves every point onto its target; the entropic version leaves a few stragglers.
22. What is the difference between the Euclidean and the Wasserstein barycentre of images?
The Euclidean barycentre averages pixel values: the shapes are superimposed as ghosts. The Wasserstein barycentre averages positions along transport maps: a single shape moves and bends.
23. Why is Sinkhorn’s algorithm fast on GPUs?
Each iteration is a pair of log-sum-exp reductions over an N × N matrix of costs, the same simple operation for every entry, which GPUs perform in parallel; ε-scaling and multiscale schemes reduce the number of iterations and of pairs.
24. Why is W₂ between samples useless in high dimension?
Two samples of the same distribution are far apart (matching errors of order N−1/D): 20.6 in dimension 256 for 200 points in Lab 13, while a real shift of 1 adds only 0.05%.
25. What is the dual formula of p. 42, and which distances does it give?
Loss(α, β) = max over f in a ball B of ⟨α − β, f⟩. Bounded functions give TV, a Sobolev (RKHS) ball gives a kernel norm, 1-Lipschitz functions give W₁, and Lipschitz convolutional networks give the Wasserstein-GAN loss.
