3. Projections: PCA and UMAP

PCA is a linear projection onto the directions of largest variance: exact, interpretable, used for the per-image colour maps. UMAP is a non-linear embedding that keeps neighbourhoods, used for the maps of the 32,000 atoms: good for clusters, unreliable for distances and shapes.

Principal component analysis

Problem. $256$ patch tokens of dimension $768$ cannot be looked at. We want the few directions along which they differ most.

Definition

Let $\mX\in\R^{n\times d}$ have rows $x_i$ and $\bar x=\frac1n\sum x_i$; $\mX_c=\mX-\mathbf 1\bar x$. The SVD $\mX_c=\mU\mS\mV\T$ gives the principal directions (columns $v_j$ of $\mV$), the scores $\mX_cv_j=\sigma_ju_j$, and the explained variance of component $j$: $\sigma_j^2/\sum_l\sigma_l^2$. The first $r$ directions span the best $r$-dimensional affine subspace (through $\bar x$) in the least-squares sense (Eckart–Young); the residual error is $\sum_{l>r}\sigma_l^2$.

In the paper §6, Figs. 12–13 · p. 14–15, Fig. 14 · p. 16

For one image the $256$ patch tokens of the last layer are projected on their first three principal components and displayed as RGB; the maps are smooth and follow objects. Fifteen components explain about $81\%$ of the variance; the position correlates with components 3 and 5 (cosines $0.19$, $0.24$).

Example — by hand, then at the paper's size

Points $(2,1),(-2,-1),(1,2),(-1,-2),(0,0)$ (mean $0$): covariance $\tfrac15\sum x x\T=\begin{pmatrix}2&1.6\\1.6&2\end{pmatrix}$, eigenvalues $3.6$ and $0.4$ with directions $(1,1)/\sqrt2$ and $(1,-1)/\sqrt2$: the first component explains $90\%$.

  • Centring $256$ tokens costs one dimension: the centred matrix has rank at most $255$, so at most 255 non-zero components, whatever $d=768$.
  • For $256$ random Gaussian tokens in $\R^{768}$ (no structure), the top 3 components explain $2.8\%$ and the top 15 explain $12.9\%$ of the variance (against $3/255=1.2\%$ and $5.9\%$ if all were equal). For tokens that are a rank-5 structure plus a little noise, 3 components already explain $86\%$ and 15 explain $97\%$. So "15 components explain $81\%$" in the paper is far above random and compatible with low-dimensional structure.
  • RGB map: the scores of components 1–3 for the 256 tokens are each rescaled to $[0,1]$ and read as (R,G,B) on the $16\times16$ grid. Two tokens get similar colours when their projections on the three components are similar.

Linear maps send segments to segments. PCA is $x\mapsto(x-\bar x)\mV_r$, an affine map. For an affine map $L$, $L((1-t)a+tb)=(1-t)L(a)+tL(b)$: a line segment between two tokens is projected onto the segment between the projections. A curved or broken path in a PCA plot therefore comes from the data, not from the projection; conversely, three collinear projections do not prove collinearity in $\R^{768}$.

Pitfalls
  • The sign of a component is arbitrary (colours can flip between two images); the same RGB colour in two images means nothing unless the components are tied across images.
  • Maps are per image: a component is "first" because it has the largest variance in this image.
  • Variance is not importance: low-variance directions can carry the information that matters for a task.

UMAP

Problem. The paper draws the 32,000 atoms in the plane (Figs. 2, 5, 6, 10D) to see which concepts a task uses and whether they cluster.

What it is. UMAP (McInnes, Healy, Melville 2018) builds a weighted k-nearest-neighbour graph of the points in high dimension (a fuzzy simplicial set), then finds 2-D positions whose own neighbour graph is as close as possible (cross-entropy of edge weights), by stochastic gradient descent. We recall the idea and do not reproduce the construction.

  • Preserved (approximately): who is near whom (local neighbourhoods), and often the coarse existence of clusters.
  • Not preserved: distances between far points, the sizes of clusters, the empty space between them, the density; the relative position of two clusters may change with the random seed or the number of neighbours. The map is non-linear, so segments are not segments.
Our reading — how to use a UMAP figure

Read "these concepts are close" only if the same holds in the original space (check a few cosines). Read "this task uses a compact region" (Fig. 2) as a statement about neighbourhoods in the atom graph; the paper backs it with the cosine and eigenvalue statistics of Fig. 3, which do not depend on UMAP. A colour map over a UMAP is a statement about a smooth function on the graph, nothing more.
