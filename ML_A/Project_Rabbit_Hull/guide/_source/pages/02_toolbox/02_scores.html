2. Scores: stable rank, Hoyer, entropy, cosine

Four numbers summarise a matrix or a vector: how many directions a matrix really uses (stable rank), how spread over coordinates a vector is (Hoyer), how spread over tokens a distribution is (entropy), and how aligned two vectors are (cosine).

Stable rank

Problem. The rank of a matrix counts its non-zero singular values, so it jumps from 768 to 1 because of numerical noise. The paper (Fig. 14, App. I) wants a smooth measure of "how many directions" the position basis spans.

Definition

For a non-zero matrix $\mathbf{M}$ with singular values $\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\dots$, $$\operatorname{srank}(\mathbf{M})=\frac{\norm{\mathbf{M}}_F^2}{\norm{\mathbf{M}}_2^2}=\frac{\sum_i\sigma_i^2}{\sigma_1^2}.$$

In the paper Fig. 14 · p. 16, App. I · p. 45

The paper reports the stable rank of the "position basis" across layers: $9.0,8.1,5.4,7.2,5.6,5.4,4.9,4.4,4.4,2.8,2.6,2.7$ (layers 1–12), read as the effective number of dimensions needed to encode position.

Properties
  1. $1\le\operatorname{srank}(\mathbf{M})\le\rank(\mathbf{M})$; it is invariant under scaling $\mathbf{M}\to s\mathbf{M}$ and under $\mathbf{M}\to\mU\mathbf{M}\mV$ with $\mU,\mV$ orthogonal.
  2. $\operatorname{srank}=1$ iff $\mathbf{M}$ has rank 1; $\operatorname{srank}=r$ iff its $r$ non-zero singular values are all equal.
  3. If the rows of $\mathbf{M}$ are centred data, $\sigma_i^2/\sum_j\sigma_j^2$ is the fraction of variance of the $i$-th principal component; so the first component explains exactly $1/\operatorname{srank}$ of the variance.
  4. A matrix with two non-zero singular values has $\operatorname{srank}=1+\sigma_2^2/\sigma_1^2\le2$.
Proof

(1) $\sigma_1^2\le\sum_{i\le r}\sigma_i^2\le r\sigma_1^2$. (2) equality cases of the two inequalities. (3) $\norm{\mathbf{M}}_F^2=\sum\sigma_j^2$ and $\sigma_1^2$ is the variance (times $n$) along the first component. (4) $\sum\sigma_i^2=\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2$ and $\sigma_2\le\sigma_1$.

Example
  • $\mI_5$: srank $5$. $\operatorname{diag}(3,1)$: $(9+1)/9=1.11$ (almost rank 1). $\operatorname{diag}(1,1)$: $2$. Singular values $(4,3,2,1)$: $30/16=1.875$.
  • Random $3000\times768$ matrix with unit rows: $\sigma_1^2\approx8.8$, srank $=3000/8.8=340$: far below the rank 768 yet large. A dictionary of $32{,}000$ unit atoms with $\sigma_1=22.5$ has srank $\le32{,}000/506=63$.
  • Two singular values $(1,0.8)$: srank $1.64$; the top component explains $1/1.64=61\%$ of the variance.
Our reading — Fig. 14: stable rank 2.6 and R² of the top-2 PCs at 1.00

In layers 10–12 the paper reports a stable rank of $2.6$–$2.8$ and, in the panel next to it, that two principal components explain $R^2\approx1.00$ of the position basis. For one and the same centred matrix this is impossible: top-2 explaining everything means two non-zero singular values, hence srank $\le2$ by property 4. The two panels cannot be computed on the same object (for instance one centred, one not, or one with PCA per image). See errata.

Pitfall

The stable rank depends on whether the matrix is centred: the mean adds a large singular value along the mean direction, which drives srank towards 1. Always say which matrix.

Hoyer sparsity

Problem. Is an atom $\mD_i$ aligned with a few neurons (coordinates) or spread over all of them? The paper uses this to argue that concepts are not "neuron-aligned".

Definition (Hoyer 2004)

For $x\in\R^n$, $x\ne0$: $$H(x)=\frac{\sqrt n-\norm{x}_1/\norm{x}_2}{\sqrt n-1}\in[0,1].$$

In the paper Fig. 10C–D · p. 12

"Scores remain below the neuron-aligned limit (1.0)", compared with the Gaussian baseline "$1-\sqrt{2/\pi}\approx0.2$". The atoms score about $0.22$–$0.25$ (read off the figure).

  1. $\norm{x}_2\le\norm{x}_1\le\sqrt n\norm{x}_2$ Cauchy–Schwarz for the right inequality; $(\sum\abs{x_i})^2\ge\sum x_i^2$ for the left. Hence $1\le\norm{x}_1/\norm{x}_2\le\sqrt n$ and $H\in[0,1]$.
  2. one-hot vector: $\norm{x}_1/\norm{x}_2=1$, $H=1$ maximal sparsity.
  3. constant vector (all $\abs{x_i}$ equal): ratio $=\sqrt n$, $H=0$ maximal spread (equality case of Cauchy–Schwarz).
  4. Gaussian $x\sim\N(0,\mI_n)$: $\norm{x}_1\approx n\,\E\abs{g}=n\sqrt{2/\pi}$ and $\norm{x}_2\approx\sqrt n$ law of large numbers; $\E\abs g=\sqrt{2/\pi}=0.798$.
  5. $H\approx\dfrac{\sqrt n\,(1-\sqrt{2/\pi})}{\sqrt n-1}\to1-\sqrt{2/\pi}=0.202$ insert and let $n\to\infty$. At $n=768$ the formula gives $0.2097$ and a simulation of 2000 vectors gives $0.2094\pm0.008$ (standard deviation): the baseline in dimension 768 is $0.21$, not $0.202$.
Example

$n=4$, $x=(3,4,0,0)$: $\norm{x}_1/\norm{x}_2=7/5=1.4$, $H=(2-1.4)/(2-1)=0.6$. $x=(1,1,0,0)$: $H=0.586$. In $n=768$: a vector with 8 equal non-zero entries scores $0.93$; a geometric profile $2^{-i}$ scores $0.97$; the absolute value of a Gaussian vector scores $0.21$, like the signed one ($H$ ignores signs).

Pitfall

$H$ is invariant under signs and scale, but not under rotations of the basis: it measures alignment with the coordinate axes. A sparse vector in a rotated basis looks dense. In a residual stream the coordinates are not privileged by the architecture in the same way for all layers, which is why a low Hoyer score does not prove that the atom is a "distributed" feature in any basis-independent sense. A score of $0.22$–$0.25$ against a baseline of $0.21$ says "close to random in the coordinate basis".

Entropy of a distribution over tokens

Problem. A concept fires on some tokens of the $261$ of an image. Is its footprint concentrated on one token (the cls token, a register, a position) or spread? Entropy gives one number.

Definition

For a probability vector $p\in\simplex^{n-1}$, $H(p)=-\sum_ip_i\ln p_i$ (with $0\ln0=0$), in nats (natural logarithm; divide by $\ln2$ for bits). $0\le H(p)\le\ln n$, with $H=0$ iff $p$ is one-hot and $H=\ln n$ iff $p$ is uniform. $e^{H}$ is the "effective number" of tokens.

In the paper §4, Fig. 7, App. G · p. 9, 39

The footprint $\omega_i$ of concept $i$ is its mean code over images at each of the $261$ token positions; its entropy (in nats, maximum $\ln261=5.56$) is histogrammed: a spike at $0$ (about 110 concepts) and a bulk at $5$–$5.5$.

Pitfall

Entropies near the maximum are indistinguishable at a glance: $5.0$ is $e^{5.0}\approx148$ effective tokens, $5.5$ is $245$. Between "all tokens" and "all patches but no register" the difference is $0.02$ nats. A specific structure (the 5 special tokens carry weight) hides in the last digits; the paper looks at the spike at 0 for that reason.

Cosine similarity

Problem. Compare directions, not lengths. $\cos(u,v)=\dfrac{\ps uv}{\norm u\norm v}\in[-1,1]$; it is the inner product when both are unit vectors, and $\cos(\lambda u,\mu v)=\cos(u,v)$ for $\lambda,\mu>0$.

  • $\cos=1$: same direction; $0$: orthogonal; $-1$: opposite. Antipodal pair: $v=-\lambda u$, $\lambda>0$ ($\cos=-1$). Example: $u=(1,2,2)$, $v=(2,1,-2)$ both have norm $3$ and $\ps uv=2+2-4=0$: orthogonal.
  • In $\R^{768}$ two independent random directions have $\cos\sim\N(0,1/768)$: standard deviation $0.036$, and $P(\abs{\cos}>0.2)\approx3\times10^{-8}$. So $\cos=0.2$ between two atoms is a strong alignment.
  • Use $\abs{\cos}$ when the sign of a direction is arbitrary (the two ends of an axis are the same feature; the paper's antipodal pairs, Fig. 11A, are "opposite poles of the same feature axis", e.g. white shirt / black shirt). Use $\cos$ itself when the sign matters (a non-negative code only moves along $+\mD_i$, so $\mD_i$ and $-\mD_i$ are different concepts).
Pitfall

A strongly negative cosine between two atoms does not make them "anti-correlated concepts" in use: the codes are non-negative, so the two atoms never subtract; they can still be active together on different tokens. Coactivation and geometry are separate statistics (paper §5, Fig. 11B).
