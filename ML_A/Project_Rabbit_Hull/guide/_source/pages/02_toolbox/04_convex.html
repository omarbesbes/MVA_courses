4. Convex geometry: hulls, simplices, faces

The MRH describes tokens as convex combinations of archetypes. Everything rests on four notions: the convex hull, the simplex with its barycentric coordinates, the faces of a polytope, and the behaviour of hulls in high dimension.

Convex sets and the convex hull

Definitions

A set $K\subseteq\R^d$ is convex if $x,y\in K$ and $t\in[0,1]$ imply $(1-t)x+ty\in K$ (the segment between any two points stays in $K$). A convex combination of $a_1,\dots,a_m$ is $\sum_j\lambda_ja_j$ with $\lambda\in\simplex^{m-1}$ ($\lambda_j\ge0$, $\sum_j\lambda_j=1$). The convex hull $\conv(S)$ is the set of all convex combinations of finitely many points of $S$; it is the smallest convex set containing $S$ (the intersection of all convex sets containing $S$).

In the paper Def. 2 · p. 18

Each tile $\Tc_i$ is a set of archetypes, its polytope is $\Pc_i=\conv(\Ac_{\Tc_i})$, and a token in the tile is a convex combination $\sum z_j\va_j$ of its archetypes, $z\in\simplex$. Archetypal-SAE atoms are convex combinations of data points ($\mD=\mS\mC$ with $\mS$ row-stochastic): $\mD_i\in\conv(\text{centroids})$.

Example

$\conv\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)\}$ is the triangle; $(0.25,0.5)=0.25\,(1,0)+0.5\,(0,1)+0.25\,(0,0)$. A circle is not convex (its chord leaves it) and its hull is the disc. The hull of the two points $a,b$ is the segment $[a,b]$.

Pitfall

A convex combination has non-negative weights summing to 1. Drop non-negativity: the affine hull (a whole subspace, e.g. the line through $a,b$). Drop the sum condition: a cone or a span. The distinction between a non-negative combination (the paper's LRH codes, $\vz\ge0$ unbounded) and a convex combination (MRH, weights summing to one) is central: see page 6.

The simplex and barycentric coordinates

Problem. Given a point of a polytope, which weights express it as a combination of the vertices? Unique weights make the code identifiable; non-unique weights do not.

Definitions

The probability simplex $\simplex^{n-1}=\{\lambda\in\R^n:\lambda\ge0,\sum_j\lambda_j=1\}=\conv(e_1,\dots,e_n)$. Points $v_0,\dots,v_k$ are affinely independent if $\sum\mu_jv_j=0$ and $\sum\mu_j=0$ force $\mu=0$. Then $\conv(v_0..v_k)$ is a $k$-simplex (segment, triangle, tetrahedron…).

Barycentric coordinates
  1. If $v_0,\dots,v_k$ are affinely independent, every point $p$ of their affine hull is written in exactly one way $p=\sum\lambda_jv_j$, $\sum\lambda_j=1$ (the barycentric coordinates), and $p$ is in the simplex iff all $\lambda_j\ge0$.
  2. If they are affinely dependent (e.g. four points in the plane, such as the vertices of a square) the representation of an interior point is not unique: it is a family $\lambda+t\mu$, where $\mu\ne0$ satisfies $\sum\mu_jv_j=0$, $\sum\mu_j=0$.
Proof

(1) Two representations $\lambda,\lambda'$ differ by $\mu=\lambda-\lambda'$ with $\sum\mu_jv_j=0$, $\sum\mu_j=0$, hence $\mu=0$. (2) Adding $t\mu$ keeps $\sum_j\lambda_jv_j$ and $\sum_j\lambda_j$; for a point in the relative interior all weights can be taken $>0$ and a small $t$ keeps them $\ge0$.

Examples
  • Triangle $(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)$ and $p=(0.25,0.5)$: solving $\lambda_1(0,0)+\lambda_2(1,0)+\lambda_3(0,1)=p$ with $\sum\lambda=1$ gives $\lambda=(0.25,0.25,0.5)$, and no other.
  • Unit square $v_1=(0,0),v_2=(1,0),v_3=(1,1),v_4=(0,1)$, $p=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$: $\tfrac12(v_1+v_3)$, $\tfrac12(v_2+v_4)$, $\tfrac14\sum v_j$ and $(0.3,0.2,0.3,0.2)$ all give $p$; here $\mu=(1,-1,1,-1)$.

Carathéodory

If $p\in\conv(S)$ with $S\subseteq\R^d$, then $p$ is a convex combination of at most $d+1$ points of $S$.

Proof

Take a representation $p=\sum_{j=1}^m\lambda_ja_j$ with all $\lambda_j>0$ and $m$ minimal. Suppose $m>d+1$. Then $a_1..a_m$ are affinely dependent: there is $\mu\ne0$ with $\sum\mu_ja_j=0$, $\sum\mu_j=0$, so some $\mu_j>0$. Put $t=\min_{\mu_j>0}\lambda_j/\mu_j$: the weights $\lambda_j-t\mu_j$ are $\ge0$, sum to 1, give the same point, and at least one is $0$, contradicting minimality.

In $\R^{768}$ a point of the hull of any number of archetypes needs at most $769$ of them: far more than the $k=8$ active codes of the SAE, which is why "sparse" and "convex combination" are different constraints.

Polytopes, faces, relative interior

Definitions

A polytope is the convex hull of finitely many points; equivalently (Minkowski–Weyl theorem, quoted) a bounded intersection of finitely many half-spaces. For a direction $u\ne0$ let $h_P(u)=\max_{x\in P}\ps xu$ (the support function). The supporting hyperplane is $\{x:\ps xu=h_P(u)\}$ and the face of $P$ in direction $u$ is $F(P,u)=P\cap\{\ps xu=h_P(u)\}$: a vertex, an edge, a facet… A vertex (extreme point) is a point of $P$ that is not in the open segment between two other points of $P$; for $P=\conv(S)$ the vertices are among the points of $S$. The relative interior $\relint P$ is the interior of $P$ inside its affine hull: for a triangle in $\R^{768}$ it is the open triangle, though its interior in $\R^{768}$ is empty.

Affine maps

If $L(x)=xM+b$ is affine, then (i) $L(\conv S)=\conv L(S)$: images of hulls are hulls of images; (ii) $L(\relint P)=\relint L(P)$ (Rockafellar, Convex Analysis, Thm 6.6, quoted); (iii) the vertices of $L(P)$ are among the images of vertices of $P$, but not every image of a vertex stays a vertex.

Proof of (i) and (iii)

(i) $L(\sum\lambda_ja_j)=\sum\lambda_jL(a_j)$ when $\sum\lambda_j=1$. (iii) $L(P)=\conv L(V)$ for the vertex set $V$ of $P$ and the vertices of a hull of finitely many points are among these points. Counter-example for the converse: projecting the cube $[0,1]^3$ on the plane along $(1,1,1)$ gives a regular hexagon with 6 vertices; the images of the two vertices $(0,0,0)$ and $(1,1,1)$ land at the centre.

Used in the paper Lemma 2 · p. 19, Lemma 6 · p. 50

Lemma 2: an affine map sends a convex code to a convex code (a bias is absorbed into the archetypes). Lemma 6: restricting the support of the weights to a subset gives a sub-polytope, which is a face when the subset is the argmax set of a linear functional.

Pitfall

Faces of a polytope are not "subsets of vertices": only subsets selected by a linear functional (the argmax set of $u$) are faces. In a square, two opposite vertices span a diagonal, which is not a face.

Hulls in high dimension

Problem. Is it surprising that tokens (a new sample) lie in the hull of the others? The paper claims it is not: in high dimension a new point is almost never in the hull, so reconstructing tokens from $\sim10$ archetypes in a hull is a strong statement (§7.2, p. 20).

In the paper §7.2 · p. 20

"This is particularly striking in high dimensions, where the probability that a random point lies in the convex hull of a small set of samples decays exponentially with dimension (Bárány–Füredi 1988; Balestriero–Pesenti–LeCun 2021)".

What we verified of the references. Bárány and Füredi, On the shape of the convex hull of random points, Probab. Th. Rel. Fields 77 (1988), 231–240: for $n$ points drawn independently and uniformly in the $d$-dimensional ball, the hull has exactly $n$ vertices with probability tending to $1$ when $n=2^{d/2}d^{-\eps}$ and to $0$ when $n=2^{d/2}d^{3/4+\eps}$ ($d\to\infty$, $\eps>0$ fixed): the threshold is exponential in $d$ (this is the statement of the abstract; we did not read the proofs). Balestriero, Pesenti and LeCun, Learning in high dimension always amounts to extrapolation (arXiv:2110.09485), invoke this result to argue that the number of samples needed for a new point to fall in the hull of the sample grows exponentially with the intrinsic dimension; we did not verify the hypotheses of their Theorem 1, and we do not state it more precisely than that.

Our reading — what this does and does not say about the tokens

These results concern i.i.d. samples from a full-dimensional distribution. Tokens live on a low-dimensional structure (a few components explain most of the variance), so the relevant dimension is the intrinsic one; "reconstruction from 10 archetypes of the same image" tests a very different thing, and being in the hull of the 256 tokens of an image where those archetypes are themselves mixtures of those tokens is, by construction, easy (see the sandwich).
