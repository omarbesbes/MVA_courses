5. Minkowski sums, support functions, decomposability

The Minkowski sum $A\msum B$ adds every point of $A$ to every point of $B$. Its support function is the sum of the support functions, its edges are the edges of the summands sorted by angle, and writing a polytope as a sum is far from unique.

The Minkowski sum

Problem. The MRH writes the activation space as $\X=\Pc_1\msum\dots\msum\Pc_m$: one polytope per tile, a point of the space being one point per tile added together (§7, Def. 2). We need to know what such a sum looks like.

Definition

$A\msum B=\{a+b:\ a\in A,\ b\in B\}$ (also written $A+B$). The sum of several sets is defined by induction.

In the paper Def. 2 · p. 18

(i) $\X=\Pc_1\msum\dots\msum\Pc_m$ with $\Pc_i=\conv(\Ac_{\Tc_i})$; (ii) block-convex coding: $x=\sum_{i\in S}z_i\Ac_{\Tc_i}$ with $z_i\in\simplex$ and $\abs S\ll m$ (one point of each active tile).

Properties (A, B, P, Q convex compact sets in $\R^d$)
  1. $A\msum\{p\}=A+p$: adding one point is a translation.
  2. $A\msum B$ is convex and compact; $\conv(A)\msum\conv(B)=\conv(A+B)$ for any sets $A,B$ (finite or not, with hulls taken of the sets).
  3. $L(A\msum B)=L(A)\msum L(B)$ for every linear map $L$ (for an affine map $x\mapsto L_0(x)+b$ one gets $L_0(A)\msum L_0(B)+b$); and $(\lambda A)\msum(\mu A)=(\lambda+\mu)A$ for convex $A$ and $\lambda,\mu\ge0$.
  4. Vertices: every vertex of $P\msum Q$ is a sum $v+w$ of a vertex $v$ of $P$ and a vertex $w$ of $Q$; hence $P\msum Q$ has at most $\abs{V(P)}\cdot\abs{V(Q)}$ vertices.
  5. In the plane: the edges of $P\msum Q$ are the edges of $P$ and the edges of $Q$, merged when parallel and equally oriented, in the order of their angle; so $P\msum Q$ has at most $\abs{V(P)}+\abs{V(Q)}$ vertices.
Proofs

(1) trivial. (2) $(1-t)(a+b)+t(a'+b')=((1-t)a+ta')+((1-t)b+tb')$. For hulls: $\sum\lambda_ia_i+\sum\kappa_jb_j=\sum_{i,j}\lambda_i\kappa_j(a_i+b_j)$ since $\sum\kappa_j=\sum\lambda_i=1$, which gives $\subseteq\conv(A+B)$; conversely $a_i+b_j\in\conv A\msum\conv B$ which is convex, giving $\supseteq$. (3) $L(a+b)=L(a)+L(b)$ for linear $L$. For $\lambda A\msum\mu A\supseteq(\lambda+\mu)A$ take $a=a'$; for $\subseteq$, $\lambda a+\mu a'=(\lambda+\mu)\bigl(\tfrac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu}a+\tfrac{\mu}{\lambda+\mu}a'\bigr)\in(\lambda+\mu)A$ by convexity. (4) Let $x=a+b$ be a vertex of $P\msum Q$. If $a=\tfrac12(a_1+a_2)$ with $a_1\ne a_2$ in $P$, then $x=\tfrac12(a_1+b)+\tfrac12(a_2+b)$ is in the open segment between two distinct points of the sum: contradiction. So $a$, and likewise $b$, are vertices. (5) Using support functions (next entry), the face of the sum in direction $u$ is $F(P\msum Q,u)=F(P,u)\msum F(Q,u)$. In the plane a face is a point or a segment orthogonal to $u$; a sum of two faces is a segment iff at least one of them is, and then its length is the sum of the lengths. So the edges of the sum have the directions of edges of $P$ or $Q$, with a length equal to the sum when both are present; walking around the sum visits them in the order of the outward normal $u$, i.e. by angle. The number of vertices equals the number of edges.

Examples (all computed in check_02.py)
  • Two orthogonal unit segments: the unit square. A disc plus a polygon: the polygon with rounded corners (the offset body).
  • Unit square $\msum$ unit square rotated by $45^\circ$: an octagon (8 vertices $=4+4$) of area $2+2\sqrt2=4.83$. Unit square $\msum$ itself: the square of side 2 (4 vertices: the parallel edges merge), area 4.
  • A polygon with 4 vertices plus one with 6 (random): 10 vertices ($=4+6$, the maximum).
  • Triangle $T=\conv\{(0,0),(2,0),(0,1)\}$ plus the unit square $Q$: edge vectors $(2,0),(-2,1),(0,-1)$ and $(1,0),(0,1),(-1,0),(0,-1)$; merging the parallel ones and sorting by angle gives $(3,0),(0,1),(-2,1),(-1,0),(0,-2)$: a pentagon with vertices $(0,0),(3,0),(3,1),(1,2),(0,2)$, area $5$.

What to look at: drag vertices of $P$ and $Q$. Count the edges of $P\msum Q$: they are the edges of $P$ and $Q$ rearranged by angle, and two parallel edges merge into one. Make an edge of $P$ parallel to an edge of $Q$ and the number of vertices of the sum drops.

Pitfall

$A\msum B$ is not the union $A\cup B$ and not a product of independent coordinates unless $A$ and $B$ live in independent subspaces; it has "more room" than either summand: $\dim(A\msum B)\ge\max(\dim A,\dim B)$. Also, the set of sums is a statement about sets: a point $x\in P\msum Q$ can be written $p+q$ in many ways (for instance $P=Q=[0,1]$, $x=1$: $p+q=1$ for all $p\in[0,1]$).

Zonotopes

Definition

A zonotope is a Minkowski sum of segments: $Z=\bigmsum_{i=1}^m[0,g_i]=\{\sum_it_ig_i:\ t\in[0,1]^m\}$, the image of the cube $[0,1]^m$ under $t\mapsto\sum t_ig_i$ (the generators $g_i\in\R^d$). The cube $[0,1]^d$ is the zonotope of the $d$ coordinate segments; the regular hexagon is the zonotope of three unit segments at $0^\circ,60^\circ,120^\circ$.

  • Zonotopes are centrally symmetric (about $\tfrac12\sum g_i$); in the plane with $m$ pairwise non-parallel generators they have $2m$ vertices (by the edge rule); in $\R^d$ with generators in general position, $2\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\binom{m-1}{i}$ vertices (known formula, quoted): $m=4$, $d=3$: $14$ (the rhombic dodecahedron). The cube of $m$ axis segments has $2^m$ vertices: $2^{768}\approx10^{231}$ for $m=768$.
  • Link with bounded linear codes. A sparse code with bounded non-negative coefficients, $x=\sum_{i\in S}z_i\mD_i$, $0\le z_i\le M$, ranges over the zonotope of the segments $[0,M\mD_i]$, $i\in S$. So the LRH, with bounds on the intensities, is a union over supports of zonotopes: a special case of the MRH with segment tiles (page 6, our reading).

Support functions

Problem. To compare convex sets (and sums of them) with numbers rather than pictures, use a function that records, for each direction, how far the set extends.

Definition

For a non-empty compact set $K\subset\R^d$: $h_K(u)=\max_{x\in K}\ps xu$, $u\in\R^d$. For $\norm u=1$, $h_K(u)$ is the signed distance from the origin to the supporting line of $K$ with outward normal $u$; the supporting line touches $K$ on the face $F(K,u)$.

Properties
  1. Sublinear: $h_K(\lambda u)=\lambda h_K(u)$ for $\lambda\ge0$ and $h_K(u+v)\le h_K(u)+h_K(v)$.
  2. $h_{\conv K}=h_K$; $h_{K+p}(u)=h_K(u)+\ps pu$; $h_{LK}(u)=h_K(L\T u)$ for a linear $L$.
  3. Additivity: $h_{A\msum B}=h_A+h_B$.
  4. Determination: a compact convex set $K$ is determined by $h_K$: $K=\bigcap_u\{x:\ps xu\le h_K(u)\}$. Hence $A=B\iff h_A=h_B$ for compact convex sets.
Proofs

(1) $\max_x\ps x{\lambda u}=\lambda\max_x\ps xu$; and $\max_x(\ps xu+\ps xv)\le\max_x\ps xu+\max_x\ps xv$. (2) a linear functional attains its maximum over $\conv K$ at a point of $K$; the other two are one line. (3) $h_{A\msum B}(u)=\max_{a,b}(\ps au+\ps bu)=\max_a\ps au+\max_b\ps bu$ because the two maxima are over independent variables. (4) "$\subseteq$" is the definition. If $y\notin K$, the separation theorem (a compact convex set and a point outside it are strictly separated by a hyperplane, quoted) gives $u$ with $\ps yu>h_K(u)$, so $y$ is not in the intersection.

Examples
  • A point $p$: $h(u)=\ps pu$. The segment $[0,g]$: $h(u)=\max(0,\ps gu)$. The unit disc: $h(u)=\norm u$.
  • Square $[0,1]^2$: $h(u)=\max(0,u_1)+\max(0,u_2)$; $h(1,1)=2$, $h(1,-1)=1$, $h(-1,0)=0$ (it touches the line $x=0$ from the right). The triangle $\conv\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)\}$: $h(2,1)=2$.
  • Zonotope: $h(u)=\sum_i\max(0,\ps{g_i}u)$ by additivity. Regular hexagon from three unit segments at $0^\circ,60^\circ,120^\circ$: $h(1,0)=1+\tfrac12+0=1.5$ (computed).

Where this is used

Paper Prop. 2/4 (non-identifiability of the Minkowski decomposition, App. J p. 50) is stated through support functions: the sum of two sets has the sum of their support functions, so a decomposition of a polytope $P$ is a decomposition of $h_P$ into two sublinear functions.

Decomposability and non-uniqueness

Problem. Given only the support $X=P_1\msum\dots\msum P_m$, can one recover the $P_i$? (§7.3, "implication: non-identifiability".) We recall what is true.

Definitions

$A$ is a homothet of $P$ if $A=\lambda P+t$ with $\lambda\ge0$ (a scaled, translated copy; a point is a homothet with $\lambda=0$). A convex compact set $P$ is indecomposable if every decomposition $P=A\msum B$ has $A$ and $B$ homothets of $P$ (necessarily $\lambda_A+\lambda_B=1$); otherwise decomposable.

Trivial non-uniqueness

For every convex $P$ and $\lambda\in[0,1]$: $P=\lambda P\msum(1-\lambda)P$ (property 3 above). And $A\msum B=(A+t)\msum(B-t)$. Neither says anything about the structure of $P$.

Segments and triangles are indecomposable
Proof

Segment $S=[0,e]$. If $S=A\msum B$, the width in a direction $u\perp e$, $h(u)+h(-u)$, is $0$ for $S$ and is additive, so it is $0$ for $A$ and for $B$: both lie in lines parallel to $e$. They are segments (or points) parallel to $e$, i.e. homothets of $S$.

Triangle $T$ with outward unit edge normals $n_1,n_2,n_3$ and counter-clockwise edge vectors $e_1,e_2,e_3$, $e_1+e_2+e_3=0$. Let $T=A\msum B$. Then $F(T,u)=F(A,u)\msum F(B,u)$ for every $u$ (additivity of $h$: the maximisers of $\ps{a+b}u$ are the sums of maximisers). For $u\notin\{n_1,n_2,n_3\}$, $F(T,u)$ is a single point, so $F(A,u)$ is a point. Hence the only faces of $A$ of positive length have normals among $n_1,n_2,n_3$, and since $T$ has no two opposite normals, $A$ is not a segment: it is a point or a polygon whose counter-clockwise edge vectors are $\lambda_ie_i$, $\lambda_i\ge0$ (the face $F(A,n_i)$ is parallel to $e_i$). A closed polygon has edge vectors summing to $0$: $\lambda_1e_1+\lambda_2e_2+\lambda_3e_3=0$, i.e. $(\lambda_1-\lambda_3)e_1+(\lambda_2-\lambda_3)e_2=0$; $e_1,e_2$ are independent, so $\lambda_1=\lambda_2=\lambda_3=\lambda$, and $A$ has the edge vectors of $\lambda T$: it is a translate of $\lambda T$. The same holds for $B$. $\square$

Example — the hexagon, two genuinely different decompositions

$H$ = regular hexagon of side 1 (area $3\sqrt3/2=2.598$).

  • $H=[0,g_1]\msum[0,g_2]\msum[0,g_3]$ with unit generators at $0^\circ,60^\circ,120^\circ$; the sum of the three segments has 6 vertices and area $2.598$ (computed).
  • $H=T\msum(-T)$ with $T$ the equilateral triangle of side 1 up to a translation: 6 vertices, area $2.598=6\cdot\operatorname{area}(T)$ (computed), and the two sets have the same support function after centring (maximum difference $3\times10^{-16}$).

The summands of the two decompositions are not homothets of each other (segments versus triangles), both being indecomposable by the theorem. So a decomposition into indecomposables is not unique: it is a genuine failure of identifiability, not the trivial one.

Our reading — what the paper's Proposition 2 can mean

The trivial splittings above ($P=\lambda P\msum(1-\lambda)P$, translations) already show that the summands of a sum are not determined by it. The hexagon shows the non-trivial case, and the fact that $\X$ alone cannot distinguish "three bounded linear features" from "two three-way categorical tiles". Details, the gap in the paper's proof and what identifies a decomposition (the distribution of the codes, not the support) are on page 5 and page 6.
