1. Self-test

Forty questions, from recall to critique. Answer aloud before you open the fold; each answer links to the place that explains it.

Questions of a group go from recall to understanding; the last of each group is often a critique. Answers marked “our reading” are not the paper's.

Page 1 — Background

1. What is a register token, and why does DINOv2-with-registers have four of them?

Answer

An extra learnable token appended to the input sequence. It is not an image patch and is not read out as the image feature; it gives the network room for global computation, so that the patch tokens stay clean. Here it brings the token count to $t=1+4+256=261$. See: registers.

2. Where do the 256 patch tokens come from?

Answer

A 224 px image is cut into $14\times14$ px patches: a $16\times16$ grid. Each patch becomes one token of width $d=768$. See: tokens.

3. What does the KoLeo term of DINOv2 do, and why does the paper mention it?

Answer

It spreads the features of a batch by penalising small nearest-neighbour distances. The paper uses it, with the 128k prototypes of the DINO and iBOT heads, to suggest that tokens are mixtures of prototypes (a conjecture). See: KoLeo.

4. Superposition: say it in two sentences.

Answer

A layer can represent more features than it has dimensions by giving each feature a direction and letting the directions be only nearly orthogonal. This works if each input uses only a few features at a time (sparsity). See: superposition.

5. What is TopK in a sparse autoencoder, and what does it replace?

Answer

The encoder keeps only the $k$ largest codes and sets the others to zero, so sparsity is imposed directly instead of through an $\ell_1$ penalty. The paper uses $k=8$ (BatchTopK: the selection is made over a batch, $k$ per token on average). See: TopK.

6. A heat map and a dictionary of concepts both “explain” a prediction. What does each tell you that the other does not?

Answer

The heat map says where in the image the evidence is, but not what it is. A concept dictionary says what the evidence is (an object part, a texture, a global property), but not by itself where it matters for the prediction. See: attribution vs concepts.

Page 2 — Toolbox

7. State the Welch bound and give its value for $c=32{,}000$, $d=768$.

Answer

For $c$ unit vectors in $\R^d$ the coherence is at least $\sqrt{(c-d)/(d(c-1))}$; here $0.0356$. The best frame computed in the paper reaches $0.0659$, random atoms reach about $0.23$. See: Welch bound.

8. Define the stable rank. How does it relate to the rank?

Answer

$\mathrm{srank}(M)=\norm{M}_F^2/\norm{M}_2^2$, the sum of squared singular values over the largest one. It is at most $\rank M$, and it is robust to small singular values. A centred matrix with two non-zero singular values has stable rank at most $2$. See: stable rank.

9. Hoyer score: range and meaning of $0.22$.

Answer

It goes from $0$ (all coordinates equal in magnitude) to $1$ (one-hot). A Gaussian vector gives $1-\sqrt{2/\pi}\approx0.20$, so atoms at $0.22$–$0.25$ are spread over many coordinates, not sparse in the neuron basis. See: Hoyer score.

10. What is the Minkowski sum of two non-parallel segments? Of two parallel ones?

Answer

A parallelogram (every point is a point of the first plus a point of the second); a longer segment. In general the vertices of the sum are sums of vertices. See: Minkowski sum.

11. What can softmax outputs be, geometrically, and what happens when the temperature goes to $0$?

Answer

Softmax weights are strictly positive and sum to one, so $\val\mV$ is a point in the relative interior of $\conv(\mV)$. At low temperature the weights concentrate on the largest logit and the output approaches a vertex. See: softmax.

12. Order PCA with $K-1$ components, archetypal analysis with $K$ archetypes and $k$-means with $K$ centroids by reconstruction error.

Answer

$\mathrm{PCA}(K-1)\le\mathrm{AA}(K)\le k\text{-means}(K)$ on the training error: $k$-means solutions are feasible for AA, and AA reconstructions lie in a $(K-1)$-dimensional affine subspace. See: AA sandwich.

Page 3 — Part I: concepts and tasks

13. State Definition 1 (LRH) and the role of each of its three conditions.

Answer

$\va=\vz\mD$ with (i) overcompleteness $c\gg d$: more concepts than dimensions; (ii) quasi-orthogonality: unit atoms with $\max_{i\ne j}\abs{\ps{\mD_i}{\mD_j}}\le\eps$, so that concepts interfere little; (iii) sparsity $\abs{\supp\vz}\le k\ll c$, so that few concepts are active at once. See: LRH setup.

14. How is the dictionary of the paper built, and what is special about its atoms?

Answer

An archetypal SAE: $\min\norm{\mA-\mZ\mD}_F^2$ with $\mZ\ge0$, at most $8$ non-zeros per token and every atom in $\conv(\mA)$, i.e. $\mD=\mS\mC$ with $\mS$ row-stochastic and $\mC$ the $128{,}000$ k-means centroids. Atoms are therefore combinations of real activations. See: LRH setup.

15. Derive the importance of concept $i$ for output $j$ of a linear probe.

Answer

$\mY=\mA\mW\T\approx\mZ\mD\mW\T=\mZ\mW'$, so output $j$ is $\sum_iZ_iW^{\prime}_{ij}$. The importance is the mean contribution $\E[Z_i]W^{\prime}_{ij}$, which is gradient $\times$ input in the concept basis. See: importance.

16. What does the classification head of the paper take as input?

Answer

The concatenation of the cls token and the mean of the patch tokens (App. B.2). See: importance.

17. What is an “elsewhere” concept, and why can a classifier use one?

Answer

A concept that fires on tokens away from the object but only when the object is present (“not the object, but the object exists”). The classifier pools over tokens, so a token away from the object can still carry the evidence. RISE shows that the causal region is the object. See: elsewhere.

18. Read Fig. 3 right: what does a faster-decaying Gram spectrum of the top-100 task concepts mean?

Answer

The normalised eigenvalues of the Gram matrix measure how the atoms spread over directions. A flat spectrum would give $1/100$ everywhere; random atoms start at about $0.047$, task atoms at $0.11$–$0.16$. The task atoms occupy a lower-dimensional subspace. See: subspace.

19. Define the footprint of a concept and its entropy. What do $0$ and $\ln261$ mean?

Answer

$\vom_i$ is the mean over images of the code of concept $i$ at each of the $261$ token positions. Entropy $0$: the concept fires on a single token (about $110$ concepts); $\ln261\approx5.565$: it is spread evenly over all tokens (the bulk is at $5$–$5.5$). See: footprint.

20. The text of §4 and the bar chart of Fig. 7 disagree. On what?

Answer

The text speaks of a unique cls-only concept, while the bar chart shows about $32$ cls-only concepts (about $150$ register-only, about $31.8$k spatial-only). See: Fig. 7.

Page 4 — Part II: statistics and geometry

21. Which concepts are dense, and what share of the activations do they take?

Answer

Three positional concepts (left, right, bottom), each firing on roughly a quarter to a third of all tokens (our estimate from Fig. 9), which together take about a tenth of all activations: the code is hybrid sparse plus dense. See: occurrence.

22. Why does a non-negative code need two atoms for a signed feature?

Answer

A code coefficient cannot be negative, so one atom can only push in its own direction. To represent “vertical lines” and “horizontal lines” as the two ends of one axis the dictionary needs two opposite atoms, each used for one sign. Hence antipodal pairs (Fig. 11 A). See: antipodal pairs.

23. Compare the inner products of the SAE atoms with the random and Grassmannian references (Fig. 10 A).

Answer

SAE: from about $-0.85$ to $+0.92$, heavy tails, positive tail heavier; random: within about $\pm0.2$; Grassmannian: within $\pm0.066$. The dictionary is far from a frame of minimal coherence, and some atoms are close to others. See: Fig. 10.

24. What does a singular value spectrum from $22.5$ down to $0$ tell you, compared with $\sqrt{c/d}=6.455$?

Answer

A tight frame has all singular values equal to $\sqrt{c/d}$, a random matrix lies in a Marchenko–Pastur band around it ($6.455\,[0.845,1.155]$). The SAE spectrum spans a far wider range: a few dominant directions, and some nearly absent: strong anisotropy. See: geometry spectrum.

25. The correlation between $\mZ\T\mZ$ and $\mD\mD\T$ is $r=0.28$. Is that surprising?

Answer

A positive relation is expected: $\tr[\mZ\T\mZ\,\mD\mD\T]=\norm{\mZ\mD}_F^2\approx\norm{\mA}_F^2$ is positive, so the two matrices cannot be unrelated. $R^2=0.08$ says usage and geometry are largely independent. Whether $0.28$ itself is explained by the identity is not settled by the paper. See: usage vs geometry.

26. How well can a linear decoder read token position across layers (Fig. 14)?

Answer

Accuracy is about $1.0$ in layers 0–2 and falls to $0.97$, $0.95$, $0.92$, $0.82$ and then $0.44$, $0.34$, $0.23$ in the last layers; cls and register tokens remain decodable. See: Fig. 14.

27. Which numbers of Fig. 14 do not fit together?

Answer

The stable rank of the position basis ($9.0\to2.7$) and the $R^2$ of its top-two PCs ($0.63\to1.0$): if two PCs explain $\approx100\%$ of a centred matrix, its stable rank is at most about $2$. The panels are not computed on the same object (for instance centred and uncentred). See: Fig. 14.

28. What does a smooth per-image PCA map show, and what does it not?

Answer

The first three PCs of the $256$ patch tokens, shown as RGB, vary smoothly and follow objects. A linear projection sends segments to segments, so it cannot create curvature, but it can hide differences; it shows that the top-3 coordinates are smooth, not that the cloud is connected in $\R^{768}$. See: PCA maps.

Page 5 — Part III: the Minkowski Representation Hypothesis

29. State Definition 2 (MRH) in words.

Answer

Archetypes $\Ac=(\va_1,\dots,\va_c)$ with $c\gg d$ are partitioned into tiles $\Tc_i$, with polytopes $\Pc_i=\conv(\Ac_{\Tc_i})$. (i) $\X=\bigmsum_i\Pc_i$; (ii) an activation is $\vx=\sum_{i\in S}\vz_i\Ac_{\Tc_i}$ with each $\vz_i$ in a simplex and $\abs{S}\ll m$ (block-convex coding). See: definition.

30. State Lemma 1 and what the proof really gives.

Answer

If $\mY=\val\mV$ with $\val=\softmax(\mQ\mK\T)$ then each output is in $\conv(\mV)$, and equality needs the logits to reach all of $\R^m$. Since softmax is strictly positive, what is attained is the relative interior; its closure is the hull. See: single head.

31. Why is the output of a multi-head attention a Minkowski sum?

Answer

Concatenating the heads and projecting equals $\sum_h\vy_h\mW_O^{(h)}$. Each $\vy_h$ lies in $\conv(\mV_h)$, and a linear map of a polytope is a polytope, so $\vy\in\bigmsum_h\mW_O^{(h)}\conv(\mV_h)$, with codes $(\val_1,\dots,\val_H)$. See: multi-head.

32. Describe the three panels of Fig. 17.

Answer

Left: distance to the nearest sample along a path between two tokens; the linear chord peaks at about $13$ at the midpoint, the kNN-graph path stays at $4$–$5$. Middle: per-image AA MSE ($0.32$ with $3$ archetypes down to $0.116$ with $30$) against the SAE ($0.227$). Right: $\mZ\T\mZ$ of an AA with about $1000$ archetypes, reordered by clustering: block-diagonal. See: evidence.

33. How do LRH and AA sit in MRH?

Answer

Archetypal analysis is MRH with one tile. A bounded non-negative linear code is MRH with segment tiles $\{0,M\vd_j\}$ anchored at the origin. The new content is the simplex constraint inside a tile (features of a tile compete) instead of a box (independent features). This is our reading. See: nested.

34. What is Proposition 2 and the simplest true example?

Answer

The Minkowski decomposition of a set is not unique, so the tiles of a layer are not identifiable from its support. True trivially by splitting a summand into homothetic copies; genuinely for the regular hexagon, which is a sum of three segments and also $T\oplus(-T)$ for an equilateral triangle $T$. The paper's proof has a gap: $h_\X-s$ need not be sublinear. See: identifiability.

35. The paper compares archetypal analysis with the SAE: what would be a fair baseline?

Answer

The comparison is a per-image dictionary against a global $32$k dictionary with $8$ active atoms. Fair: match the number of atoms and active codes, compare with $k$-means and PCA (the sandwich), and with null data of the same covariance (Gaussian) to see where AA sits. This is our critique. See: diagnostics.

36. Why does “the nearest sample to the chord midpoint is far” not show that the support is non-convex?

Answer

For $N$ samples in high dimension the nearest sample to the midpoint of two samples is typically one of the endpoints, even for a convex support (Gaussian, simplex). The chord leaves the sample, not the support. Our reading, see page 6. See: chords.

Page 6 — Critical reading

37. Why does Proposition 1 carry no information about what DINOv2 learned?

Answer

The value vectors are functions of the current image's tokens, so the polytope is rebuilt for each image, and the proposition holds for any softmax attention with any weights on any input. MRH needs a fixed archetype dictionary; this is the gap. See: architecture.

38. What does the final LayerNorm do to the MRH picture?

Answer

LayerNorm is not affine (per-token statistics): after it tokens lie on an ellipsoid of dimension $d-2$, which contains no segment, so every chord between two tokens leaves the set. MRH can only hold approximately after the last layer, and Fig. 17 left is then true by construction. Our proposition. See: LayerNorm.

39. Which block structure of $\mZ\T\mZ$ does a Minkowski sum predict, and which does a union of polytopes predict?

Answer

A sum of simultaneously active tiles predicts dense co-activation between tiles with a Kronecker structure; a union of polytopes (each token uses one group) predicts block-diagonal co-activation, which is what Fig. 17 right shows. Our reading. See: block diagnostic.

40. Is non-identifiability specific to MRH?

Answer

No. By the same support-only standard LRH is non-identifiable (a cone does not determine its generators). A dictionary is identified by a prior on the codes (sparsity, independence, simplex), not by the support. See: identifiability.

Opening and conclusion

Three sentences to open
  1. Interpretability tools often ask where a model looks; this paper asks what a vision model represents, with a dictionary of $32{,}000$ concepts extracted from DINOv2.
  2. It uses the dictionary in three ways: to see what each task relies on, to describe the statistics and geometry of the concepts, and to propose a new picture of the representation.
  3. I will go through the three parts, say what each figure shows, and say how strong I think the evidence is.
Three sentences to close
  1. Tasks use the dictionary differently and concepts are organised by token type; the geometry is far from a random or an optimal frame, which makes the pure “sparse orthogonal directions” picture incomplete.
  2. The Minkowski Representation Hypothesis says activations are sums of convex polytopes, motivated by attention; the authors present it as a working hypothesis supported by compatible evidence.
  3. In my reading the measurements are solid, while the diagnostics for the hypothesis would also be produced by simpler pictures, so the next step is a test that separates them.

Twelve questions an examiner may ask

E1. Why a sparse dictionary rather than neurons or PCA directions?

Short answer

Neurons are polysemantic under superposition, and PCA directions are orthogonal, so they cannot give more concepts than $d=768$. A sparse overcomplete dictionary can. See: superposition.

E2. Why archetypal atoms, and what do you lose?

Short answer

Atoms in $\conv(\mA)$ are combinations of real activations, hence more interpretable and stable; the constraint reduces flexibility, which costs some reconstruction. See: archetypal SAE.

E3. Why is the importance score $\E[Z_i]W^{\prime}_{ij}$ reasonable, and what does it leave out?

Short answer

It is gradient $\times$ input in the concept basis, exact for a linear probe; it ignores the reconstruction error and the aggregation over outputs is not specified. See: importance.

E4. What does “elsewhere” change for heat maps?

Short answer

A concept can fire away from the object yet depend on it; a heat map shows where it fires, not what it is about. RISE tests the dependence. See: elsewhere.

E5. Why are segmentation concepts border concepts, and is it surprising?

Short answer

Segmentation asks for the boundary between regions, so concepts detecting boundaries are what the probe needs (Fig. 5); the paper reports a tight cluster of the top-50. See: borders.

E6. Why compare the dictionary with the Grassmannian frame?

Short answer

It is the minimal-coherence arrangement of $c$ directions, the ideal of the LRH; the SAE is far from it, so the quasi-orthogonality in Def. 1 holds only loosely. See: Welch and Grassmannian.

E7. Is the correlation $r=0.28$ between co-activation and geometry meaningful?

Short answer

It is weak, and a positive value is expected from the trace identity; it shows usage and geometry are largely independent, not that the geometry is irrelevant. See: usage vs geometry.

E8. What does the paper actually prove about attention?

Short answer

Lemma 1 and Prop. 1: outputs of softmax attention lie in the convex hull of the values, and for several heads in a Minkowski sum of per-head polytopes. This holds for any attention. See: multi-head.

E9. Does Fig. 17 prove the MRH?

Short answer

No: the authors say it is compatible evidence. Chords leave the sample even for convex supports, AA with a per-image dictionary is not a like-for-like comparison with the SAE, and block-diagonality suggests clusters. See: evidence.

E10. Is MRH a contradiction of LRH?

Short answer

The authors refrain from saying so. A bounded non-negative linear code is the special case of segment tiles at the origin (our reading). See: limits.

E11. What is non-identifiability, and why does it matter for interpretation?

Short answer

The Minkowski decomposition of a layer is not unique, so tiles cannot be read from the support; the authors conclude one should explain a layer through the sequence of transformations that built it. See: identifiability.

E12. What would you do next?

Short answer

Test on the layer, not on the attention: a null (random-weight) twin of DINOv2, control for LayerNorm, compare AA with PCA and $k$-means baselines, and test position additivity (a sum of two segments is a zonotope). See: lab.
