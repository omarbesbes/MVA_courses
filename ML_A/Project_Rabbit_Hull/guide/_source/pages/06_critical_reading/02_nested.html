2. Box or simplex: the two hypotheses are nested

Repaired, the Minkowski hypothesis describes a union of polytopes over active sets. A bounded linear code is the case where every tile is a segment. What is left to tell the two apart is the constraint that couples the codes inside a group: a box (free) or a simplex (a shared unit budget).

The definition needs one repair

In the paper Def. 2 · p. 18, App. J · p. 46

Archetypes $\Ac=(\va_1,\dots,\va_c)$ are partitioned into tiles $\Tc_1,\dots,\Tc_m$ with polytopes $\Pc_i=\conv(\Ac_{\Tc_i})$. The set of activations satisfies (i) $\X=\Pc_1\oplus\dots\oplus\Pc_m$, and (ii) every activation is block-convex: $\vx=\sum_{i\in S}\vz_i\Ac_{\Tc_i}$ with each $\vz_i$ in a simplex and $|S|\ll m$. The text adds “a union over attention patterns when different tiles are selected across inputs”. Detail on page 5.

The problem. A Minkowski sum of convex sets is convex, and a point of $\Pc_1\oplus\dots\oplus\Pc_m$ receives one contribution from every tile. Item (ii) lets only a few tiles contribute. The two items agree only if an inactive tile contributes a fixed point (for instance $\{0\}$). The set the text really describes, with its “union”, is

$$\X=\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal S}\ \bigmsum_{i\in S}\Pc_i,$$

a union of polytopes indexed by the admissible active sets $\mathcal S$. This is the exact counterpart of sparse coding, whose activations fill a union of low-dimensional cones.

Our reading — why this matters for the evidence

Under the full sum, $\X$ is convex, so “a straight line between two tokens leaves the data” cannot be evidence for (i): a convex set contains the whole chord. Under the union it can, but only if the two tokens use different active sets. The repair makes the hypothesis precise enough to be tested; it is not an objection to it.

A bounded linear code is a Minkowski code with segment tiles

Proposition — nested hypotheses own

(a) Let $\vx=\sum_{j\in S}z_j\vd_j$ with $|S|\le k$ and $0\le z_j\le M$. Then $\vx$ satisfies the block-convex form (ii) with the same active set and the two-point tiles $\Tc_j=\{\mathbf 0,\,M\vd_j\}$. With signed codes $|z_j|\le M$, use $\Tc_j=\{-M\vd_j,\,M\vd_j\}$.

(b) Conversely, a code satisfying (ii) is a non-negative linear code over the dictionary of archetypes, with at most $\sum_{i\in S}|\Tc_i|$ non-zero entries, subject to the $|S|$ equalities $\sum_{j\in\Tc_i}z_j=1$.

Idea

A number in $[0,M]$ is a convex combination of its two ends $0$ and $M$. So a bounded coefficient times a direction is a point of the segment from the origin to $M\vd_j$, and the segment is a tile with two archetypes. Every other statement follows from this one line.

Proof, step by step
  1. Take $j\in S$ and $\lambda_j=z_j/M\in[0,1]$.because $0\le z_j\le M$
  2. $z_j\vd_j=(1-\lambda_j)\,\mathbf 0+\lambda_j\,(M\vd_j)$.multiply out: $\lambda_jM\vd_j=z_j\vd_j$
  3. This is a convex combination of the two archetypes of $\Tc_j=\{\mathbf 0,M\vd_j\}$, with code $(1-\lambda_j,\lambda_j)$ in the simplex $\simplex^2$.the weights are non-negative and sum to 1
  4. Sum over $j\in S$: $\vx=\sum_{j\in S}\big((1-\lambda_j)\mathbf 0+\lambda_j M\vd_j\big)$, one point per active tile. This is (ii).definition of block-convex coding
  5. Signed case: for $|z_j|\le M$ put $\lambda_j=(z_j+M)/(2M)\in[0,1]$. Then $(1-\lambda_j)(-M\vd_j)+\lambda_j(M\vd_j)=(2\lambda_j-1)M\vd_j=z_j\vd_j$.$2\lambda_j-1=z_j/M$
  6. (b): a block-convex code writes $\vx=\sum_{i\in S}\sum_{j\in\Tc_i}z_j\va_j$ with $z_j\ge0$ and $\sum_{j\in\Tc_i}z_j=1$ for each $i\in S$. Read it as $\vx=\vz\Ac$ with $\vz\ge0$ supported on $\bigcup_{i\in S}\Tc_i$.it is a rewriting of (ii): a non-negative linear code on the archetypes with $|S|$ sum-to-one constraints
Worked example with numbers

In the plane take $\vd_1=(1,0)$, $\vd_2=(0.6,0.8)$ (unit vectors), $M=2$ and $\vz=(1.5,\,0.5)$. The linear code gives $\vx=1.5\,(1,0)+0.5\,(0.6,0.8)=(1.8,\,0.4)$. In the tile form: $\lambda=(0.75,\,0.25)$; tile 1 is $\{\mathbf 0,(2,0)\}$ and contributes $0.75\,(2,0)=(1.5,0)$; tile 2 is $\{\mathbf 0,(1.2,1.6)\}$ and contributes $0.25\,(1.2,1.6)=(0.3,0.4)$; the sum is $(1.8,0.4)$. With a signed code, $z_1=-0.5$ gives $\lambda_1=(-0.5+2)/4=0.375$ on the tile $\{-2\vd_1,2\vd_1\}$: $0.625\,(-2,0)+0.375\,(2,0)=(-0.5,0)$.

Since activations are bounded, the proposition says that every representation satisfying the linear hypothesis satisfies the Minkowski one, with segments as tiles: its set of activations is a union of zonotopes. Conversely a tile with two archetypes $\{\va,\vb\}$ contributes $\va+\lambda(\vb-\va)$: a bounded linear feature along $\vb-\va$ with an offset. The two hypotheses therefore part ways only at tiles with three archetypes or more.

What is left: the budget inside a tile

For a tile $\{\va,\vb,\vc\}$ the points are $\va+\lambda_b(\vb-\va)+\lambda_c(\vc-\va)$. Under the Minkowski hypothesis the coordinates $(\lambda_b,\lambda_c)$ satisfy $\lambda_b,\lambda_c\ge0$ and $\lambda_b+\lambda_c\le1$: a triangle. Under the linear hypothesis, where the two features are free to be large together, they fill the square $[0,1]^2$. Inside a tile, features share a unit budget; across tiles they do not. A tile is an irreducible multi-dimensional feature in the sense of Engels et al., and the hypothesis is the linear one plus a statement of mutual exclusion inside groups of atoms: a categorical variable relaxed to its simplex.

Worked example with numbers — the budget decides

Tile $\va=(0,0)$, $\vb=(2,0)$, $\vc=(0,2)$. The point with $\lambda=(0.5,\,0.25)$ is $(1,0.5)$, budget $0.75\le1$: both hypotheses allow it. The point with $\lambda=(0.75,\,0.75)$ is $(1.5,1.5)$, budget $1.5>1$: only the box allows it. The set of activations is the triangle of area $2$ for the simplex and the square $[0,2]^2$ of area $4$ for the box. If the codes were uniform on the box, half of them would violate the budget.

This is the quantity the hypothesis adds, and it concerns the codes, not the set.

The hexagon: one support, two hypotheses

The budget is a property of the codes, not of the set they generate. Let $\vg_1,\vg_2,\vg_3$ be unit vectors at $0^\circ,60^\circ,120^\circ$, $S_k=[-\vg_k/2,\vg_k/2]$, and $T$ an equilateral triangle of unit side centred at the origin. The regular hexagon $H$ of unit circumradius satisfies

$$H\;=\;S_1\oplus S_2\oplus S_3\;=\;T\oplus(-T).$$

The first expression is a linear code with three bounded features in superposition in the plane; the second is a block-convex code with two ternary tiles. We checked it numerically (t4_hexagon.json): both sums have six vertices, and their support functions agree with the hexagon's on 720 directions to $6\times10^{-16}$. In direction $0^\circ$, for example, the three segments give $\tfrac12(1+\tfrac12+\tfrac12)=1$ and the two triangles give $h_T+h_{-T}=\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1$.

  • Panels (c)–(d). Two clouds of 4,000 points with the same hexagonal support. (d) block-convex codes, Dirichlet(0.3) on $T$ and on $-T$: mass near the nine sums of vertices, the diagonals and the boundary.

  • Conclusion. A test of the Minkowski hypothesis against the linear one must bear on the joint statistics of the codes; a test that only asks where activations lie cannot succeed.
  • What it does not show. It is a toy in the plane; it does not say which law real codes follow.
