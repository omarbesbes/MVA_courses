5. The three signatures under null models

For each of the paper's three empirical signatures we ask what a model without archetypes gives. Chords leave the sample of a Gaussian too; archetypal analysis sits inside its sandwich where a Gaussian puts it; block co-activation follows image classes, the mark of a union, not of a sum.

The method is the same three times: (1) say what the paper shows; (2) build data whose geometry we control (a toy) and see what the diagnostic gives; (3) run the diagnostic on DINOv2 tokens and on a null model (a distribution with no archetype but the same first two moments) and on a twin network with random weights. The DINOv2 data are the patch tokens of 1,000 Imagenette images (ten classes), after the final normalisation; the toy scripts are in code/toy, the DINOv2 ones in code/dino (section 8). Signature 1 — chords leave the sample, not the support

In the paper §7.2 · p. 20, Fig. 17 left · p. 21

Take two tokens. Walk along the straight segment between them and, at each step, measure the distance to the nearest token of the data set; do the same along a shortest path in the $k$-nearest-neighbour graph. The straight path gives a triangle peaking at the midpoint (about 13), the graph path a plateau at 4–5. The authors read this as support for criterion (i), “compatible evidence, not proof”. Page 5 reads the panel: chords.

Idea

In high dimension a finite sample does not fill any body. The midpoint of two samples is farther from every sample than the two endpoints are, even when the midpoint belongs to a convex support. The graph path is within half an edge of a sample by construction, so it cannot leave the data. The triangle then says “the sample is finite and the dimension is large”, not “the support is non-convex”.

A heuristic with numbers. Draw samples from $N(0,\mI_d)$. Two samples are at distance about $\sqrt{2d}$, so the endpoint is at $\sqrt{d/2}$ from the midpoint. A third independent sample is at distance about $\sqrt{d+d/2}=\sqrt{1.5d}$ from the midpoint, which is $0.87$ times the chord whatever $d$. For this third sample to beat an endpoint it must be at less than $0.5$ times the chord; the nearest of $N$ samples is closer than a typical one by a factor of about $N^{-1/d}$. At $d=50$ and $N=20{,}000$ that factor is $0.82$, giving $0.87\times0.82\approx0.7>0.5$: an endpoint stays the nearest sample. At $d=2$ the factor is $0.007$ and the midpoint is close to some sample. (An order-of-magnitude argument: the measured $\rho$ is $0.01$ at $d=2$ and $0.49$ at $d=50$.)

Our reading — caveat: the ellipsoid is a separate reason

For normalised tokens the chord leaves the support as well, for a reason unrelated to polytopes: the support is on an ellipsoid (section 4, Proposition). So the signature is automatic in two independent ways. The sphere row of the table shows that a curved support gives a large $\rho$ even in low dimension, but a convex one gives it at $d\ge50$ anyway, so the figure cannot tell the two cases apart.

Signature 2 — archetypal analysis in its sandwich

In the paper §7.2 · p. 20, Fig. 17 middle · p. 21

Archetypal analysis (AA) fitted on the 256 tokens of one image reconstructs them with a mean squared error that falls with the number of archetypes: $0.32$ ($K=3$), $0.26$ (5), $0.19$ (10), $0.138$ (20), $0.116$ (30), against $0.227$ for the SAE (dashed). AA is the single-tile case of the hypothesis, so this is read as a direct test of convex coding. Reading of the panel: page 5.

Reminder — archetypal analysis and its sandwich

AA with $K$ archetypes writes each data point as a convex combination of $K$ archetypes, themselves convex combinations of data points. It has two natural neighbours: PCA and $k$-means. More.

Lemma — sandwich own

For any data set and any $K$, the optimal reconstruction errors satisfy $\mathrm{err}[\mathrm{PCA}_{K-1}]\le\mathrm{err}[\mathrm{AA}_K]\le\mathrm{err}[k\text{-means}_K]$.

Proof, step by step
  1. Left inequality. The reconstructions of $\mathrm{AA}_K$ are convex combinations of $K$ points, so they lie in the affine hull of these $K$ points, of dimension at most $K-1$.$K$ points span an affine space of dimension at most $K-1$
  2. $\mathrm{PCA}_{K-1}$ is the best reconstruction in an affine subspace of dimension $K-1$ (the mean plus $K-1$ principal directions).Eckart–Young for centred data
  3. Hence $\mathrm{err}[\mathrm{PCA}_{K-1}]\le\mathrm{err}[\mathrm{AA}_K]$.AA's reconstructions are one candidate for PCA's minimisation
  4. Right inequality. A $k$-means solution has $K$ centroids, each the average of the data points of its cluster. An average of data points is a convex combination of data points, so each centroid is an admissible archetype.archetypes must lie in the convex hull of the data
  5. The $k$-means reconstruction of a point is its centroid, which is the archetype with the one-hot code $(0,\dots,1,\dots,0)$, a point of the simplex.codes of AA are in the simplex
  6. So every $k$-means solution is feasible for AA with the same error; the optimum of AA is at most that.minimum over a larger set
Worked example — a tiny data set

Five points in the plane, $K=2$: $(0,0),(2,0),(4,0),(1,1.5),(3,1.5)$, errors as sums of squares. PCA with one component (a line through the mean $(2,0.6)$): error $2.70$. Best $k$-means with two centroids: clusters $\{(0,0),(2,0),(1,1.5)\}$ and $\{(4,0),(3,1.5)\}$, centroids $(1,0.5)$ and $(3.5,0.75)$, error $3.5+1.625=5.125$. AA with two archetypes reconstructs on a segment between two points of the hull; a numerical optimisation (hill climbing, 60 restarts) gives an error of about $2.97$, with archetypes near $(0.34,0.51)$ and $(3.66,0.51)$. Order: $2.70\le2.97\le5.125$; the gap ratio is $g=(2.97-2.70)/(5.125-2.70)=0.11$. The $k$-means centroids are feasible archetypes, which is why AA cannot do worse than $5.125$.

What the sandwich implies for the paper's figure. Per-image AA is a model refitted on each image, with $K$ free archetypes for 256 tokens. The SAE is a single dictionary shared by all images, with 8 active atoms per token. We reproduce the paper's AA errors ($0.32$, $0.25$, $0.18$ against $0.32$, $0.26$, $0.19$ for $K=3,5,10$), which lets us place the SAE's $0.227$ on our axes. A $k$-means with ten centroids per image already passes below it ($0.21$), and so does PCA with nine components ($0.15$). “Matching the SAE with ten archetypes” is therefore not specific to convex mixing: it rewards any model refitted on each image.

The informative number is where AA sits inside its sandwich: $g=(\mathrm{AA}-\mathrm{PCA})/(k\text{-means}-\mathrm{PCA})$, between $0$ (as good as PCA) and $1$ (as bad as $k$-means), calibrated on data of known geometry.

Toy experiment t2_sandwich. 256 points in $\R^{50}$ with identical sample mean and covariance: mixtures of ten random Gaussian vertices with Dirichlet(0.5) weights plus noise (PCA(9) error $\approx0.05$), against a Gaussian with exactly the same mean and covariance. At $K=10$: $g=0.19$ for the polytope, $g=0.49$ for the Gaussian (20 repetitions); the polytope is below the Gaussian in all repetitions for $K\ge5$. No sandwich violation in any run.

Our reading — the honest caveat

The test excludes “the token cloud of an image is a polytope with about ten vertices”, not “convex coding” in general. The tokens are somewhat closer to the polytope end than the Gaussian is ($0.47$ against $0.51$), and the pre-norm tokens more so ($0.40$); the difference is small next to the planted polytope ($0.26$). We make no claim beyond that.

Signature 3 — a sum is not a union

In the paper §7.2 · p. 20, Fig. 17 right · p. 21

The matrix $\mZ\T\mZ$ of the codes of an archetypal analysis with about 1,000 archetypes, reordered by clustering, is block-diagonal. This is offered as support for block structure. Reading of the panel: page 5.

Idea

Block-diagonal means: a token uses archetypes of one group only. That is a union of polytopes (clusters). A Minkowski sum of tiles that are active together predicts the opposite: an AA, whose codes live in a single simplex, has to take the sums of vertices as archetypes, and every token uses all of them at once.

Why a sum gives product weights
  1. Let $\vx=\vp+\vq$ with $\vp=\sum_i\alpha_i\va_i\in\Pc_1$ and $\vq=\sum_j\beta_j\vb_j\in\Pc_2$, $\sum_i\alpha_i=\sum_j\beta_j=1$.two tiles active together
  2. $\vx=\sum_i\alpha_i\va_i\cdot\big(\sum_j\beta_j\big)+\big(\sum_i\alpha_i\big)\cdot\sum_j\beta_j\vb_j=\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\beta_j(\va_i+\vb_j)$.multiply $\vp$ by $\sum_j\beta_j=1$ and $\vq$ by $\sum_i\alpha_i=1$
  3. The weights $\alpha_i\beta_j$ are non-negative and sum to $1$: a point of the simplex over the archetypes $\va_i+\vb_j$.$\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\beta_j=(\sum_i\alpha_i)(\sum_j\beta_j)=1$
  4. Therefore the single-simplex AA of a sum has the vertex sums as archetypes, and the code of $\vx$ is $\val\otimes\vbe$.Kronecker product of the two codes
  5. The co-activation of this token is $(\val\otimes\vbe)(\val\otimes\vbe)\T=(\val\val\T)\otimes(\vbe\vbe\T)$, which has no zero entry as soon as all $\alpha_i,\beta_j>0$.product of positive numbers
Worked example

Two tiles, each a segment: $\Pc_1=[\va_1,\va_2]$, $\Pc_2=[\vb_1,\vb_2]$, with $\val=(0.7,0.3)$ and $\vbe=(0.6,0.4)$. Sum. The four archetypes are $\va_1+\vb_1,\va_1+\vb_2,\va_2+\vb_1,\va_2+\vb_2$ and the code is $(0.42,0.28,0.18,0.12)$, which sums to 1. The co-activation matrix of this token is $$\begin{pmatrix}0.176&0.118&0.076&0.050\\0.118&0.078&0.050&0.034\\0.076&0.050&0.032&0.022\\0.050&0.034&0.022&0.014\end{pmatrix},$$ with no zero entry: every archetype co-activates with every other. Union. If the four archetypes are $\va_1,\va_2,\vb_1,\vb_2$ and a token uses one tile, the code is $(0.7,0.3,0,0)$ and the matrix has a non-zero block $\begin{pmatrix}0.49&0.21\\0.21&0.09\end{pmatrix}$ and zeros elsewhere: blocks.

Our reading — honest caveat

Block structure is evidence for the union in the repaired definition, and against a reading where several tiles are active at once in this basis. It is not evidence for “additive tiles”, which is what the Minkowski sum needs. The global AA with 200 archetypes leaves $40\%$ of the variance unexplained, against $46\%$ for $k$-means with 200 clusters.
