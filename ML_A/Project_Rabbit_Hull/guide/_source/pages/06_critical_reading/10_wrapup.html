10. Summary and reflexes

What to remember
  1. Verdict. Parts I and II are measurements and stand. In Part III the attention lemma is a theorem; the arguments for the hypothesis do not separate it from simpler pictures; one exact consequence fails after the last normalisation.
  2. Nested. The definition needs one repair, to a union over active sets, $\X=\bigcup_S\bigmsum_{i\in S}\Pc_i$. A bounded non-negative linear code, as an SAE produces it, is the case of segment tiles ($\{0,M\vd_j\}$ with its own copy of the origin, or $\{-M\vd_j,M\vd_j\}$ for signs). What separates the hypotheses is a box or a simplex inside a tile; the hexagon is one support for both.
  3. Attention. The statement holds for random weights; the polytopes are rebuilt per image; rank $64\ll261$; attention spread over 33–78 tokens with 10–49% on the five special tokens; tokens are 0.65 attention / 0.35 MLP, but 0.46 / 0.54 within an image; the last block carries 82%.
  4. LayerNorm. Outputs lie on an ellipsoid ($\|\vu\|^2=d$ to $10^{-7}$, $0.19\le\gamma\le14.9$) which contains no segment: no non-trivial Minkowski sum fits. A chord's midpoint is $(1-\cos\frac\theta2)\sqrt d$ below the sphere; measured exactly, 10% (same image) and 12.5% (different images) of the half-chord. The per-activation statements survive.
  5. Signatures. (1) $\rho=0.50$ and $0.16$ on tokens, $0.50$ and $0.19$ on a Gaussian: chords leave a sample, not a support; what separates the tokens is clustering (nearest neighbour at 0.35 chord against 0.80), and the peak is ten times the ellipsoid's depth, so it is not automatic. (2) $\mathrm{PCA}_{K-1}\le\mathrm{AA}_K\le k\text{-means}_K$; $g=0.47$ on tokens, $0.51$ Gaussian, $0.26$ planted polytope; large polytopes are not excluded. (3) A sum admits the product code $\alpha_i\beta_j$ and a dense matrix (97% of pairs co-activate; a three-block cut: 42% against 36%); a union gives blocks; DINOv2 has blocks that largely follow the classes (AMI 0.62): one group per token, no tiles that add up.
  6. Identifiability. The proof is flawed (counter-example 0.45, 0.45, 1.0). A sum of segments does determine the directions and total lengths of its generators; the ambiguity is between families (hexagon: three segments or two triangles), so a support cannot tell a linear code from a block-convex one. Sparsity and the law of the codes identify dictionaries; that sparsity identifies tiles is a conjecture.
  7. Position. $P_{ij}\approx r_i+c_j$: 97.4% (held-out 94.7% against a ceiling of 95.2%; 11.8% for a generic table), each summand an arc (72% / 77% of variance on one direction, 96% / 97% on two; linear-in-index fit $R^2=0.71$): a genuine Minkowski sum, positive for the hypothesis, though the test cannot tell a box from a simplex (position is 4.8% of the variance; a random twin reaches 0.90). It explains the dense and the antipodal atoms.

Reflexes

  • When you see “sum of tiles, few active” → union over active sets; a full sum is convex.
  • When you see “a bounded feature” → a segment tile; the hypotheses differ only by the budget inside a group of three or more archetypes.
  • When you see “softmax output is a convex combination” → true for any weights; ask what is fixed across images, whether the polytope can be filled (rank), and what else is added to the token (MLPs, LayerNorm).
  • When you see “LayerNorm” → centre, scale: points on a sphere in $\mathbf 1^\perp$, then an invertible affine map: an ellipsoid; no segments.
  • When you see “the midpoint of two samples is far from the data” → try a Gaussian with the same moments.
  • When you see “AA with $K$ archetypes” → place it between PCA($K-1$) and $k$-means($K$); report $g$.
  • When you see “block-diagonal co-activation” → union of clusters; a sum gives a dense Kronecker pattern.
  • When you see “decomposition is not unique” → true of supports, for zonotopes too; ask what the law of the codes says.
  • When you see “a dense, signed or antipodal feature” → try an additive code of signed axes first.
