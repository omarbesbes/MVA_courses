0. Before you start

Parts I and II of the paper are measurements and stand. Part III proposes a hypothesis and offers evidence for it; this page, which follows the project report (sections 4 and 5), shows that the evidence does not separate the hypothesis from simpler pictures, and that one exact consequence of it fails after the last layer normalisation. It does not show that the hypothesis is false, and on the one factor we can observe, position, the additive composition it postulates holds.

How to read this page

The page is the project's own reading. Boxes marked In the paper quote or paraphrase the paper, with a page reference; they are kept short because pages 3 to 5 develop them. Everything else, in particular every box marked “Our reading” and every experiment, is ours: we say “we” for the arguments of the report. Three statuses are used for a claim: solid (proved, or measured directly), suggestive (consistent with the data, not separated from rivals) and speculative (not tested, or contradicted by our checks). own marks a result of the project.

Pitfall — “we showed the paper is wrong”

The report does not say that. It says three things: the definition needs a repair; the arguments given do not discriminate between the hypothesis and simpler models; one exact consequence (a Minkowski sum for normalised tokens) is excluded. The authors call their hypothesis “a working hypothesis” and their evidence “compatible, not proof”; the report agrees with that wording and asks what would count as evidence.

The argument of the report in six steps

  1. Repair and nest. The definition as written clashes with itself (a full sum is convex; the text describes a union). Repaired, it contains bounded linear codes as the case where every tile is a segment. The two hypotheses differ by one thing: inside a group of features, a box or a simplex (section 2).
  2. Delimit the attention argument. “Attention outputs convex combinations” is true for any weights and any input; the polytopes are rebuilt for every image; the condition for filling a polytope fails (rank 64 against 261); a token is mostly not an attention update (section 3).
  3. Layer normalisation. After the last normalisation all tokens lie on an ellipsoid, which contains no segment: no non-trivial Minkowski sum describes them exactly (section 4).
  4. Null models for the three signatures. Chords leave a sample of a Gaussian too, and what separates the tokens from it is clustering; per-image k-means matches the reported error and archetypal analysis sits where a Gaussian puts it; blocks follow image classes, the mark of a union, not of a sum (section 5).
  5. Identifiability. The proof is flawed (counter-example); what holds is narrower and concerns supports: a support does not tell a linear code from a block-convex one (the hexagon); what identifies a dictionary is sparsity and the law of the codes, and that sparsity identifies tiles in general is a conjecture (section 6).
  6. A tile one can observe. The position code is additive in rows and columns to 97%: a genuine Minkowski sum of two arcs, a positive result for the hypothesis, though the test cannot tell a box from a simplex (section 7).
