7. A tile that can be observed: position

One factor of variation has a known value for every token: its position on the $16\times16$ grid. Its code in the last layer is a sum of a row effect and a column effect to 97.4%: two one-dimensional factors, a curved box, not a simplex.

In the paper §6 · p. 15–16, Fig. 14–15 · p. 16, §5 · p. 11, Fig. 9

The position of a token can be decoded linearly in the early layers and less in the last ones; the stable rank of the position basis falls from about 9 to about 2.7 and the two leading principal components explain about all of its variance in the last layers: a two-dimensional sheet. In the dictionary, three dense atoms (“left”, “right”, “bottom”) fire on roughly a quarter of all tokens each, and some atoms form antipodal pairs. See page 4.

The paper reads this as a first hint of dense, non-sparse structure. We ask which of the two geometries of section 2 the position code has.

The additive test

Reminder — an additive two-way table

A table $P_{ij}$ with $i$ indexing rows and $j$ columns is additive if $P_{ij}=r_i+c_j$: the effect of the row and that of the column simply add. Here each entry is a vector of $\R^{768}$. The best additive fit of a table is given by the row means and column means (after subtracting the overall mean), and the share of variance it explains is $R^2=1-\|P-\hat P\|^2/\|P\|^2$. A table that is a sum of a row effect and a column effect is a Minkowski sum of two sets: the set of row effects and the set of column effects, with no coupling between the two.

Worked example

The $2\times3$ table $\begin{pmatrix}1&2&4\\3&4&6\end{pmatrix}$ is additive: $r=(0,2)$, $c=(1,2,4)$, $R^2=1$. Change one entry: $\begin{pmatrix}1&2&4\\3&5&6\end{pmatrix}$. The additive fit now leaves the residual $\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac16&-\tfrac13&\tfrac16\\-\tfrac16&\tfrac13&-\tfrac16\end{pmatrix}$ and $R^2=0.981$. For a $16\times16$ table with 255 degrees of freedom after centring, the additive model has $15+15=30$: a table with no structure (random) would give $R^2=30/255=11.8\%$, the baseline against which $97.4\%$ is judged.

Experiment (r6_position_additive). Let $P_{ij}\in\R^{768}$ be the mean token at position $(i,j)$ over the 1,000 images, centred. Last layer, after the final normalisation.

The held-out figure is the honest one: the tables of two halves of the images are noisy, so even the exact table of one half explains only $95.2\%$ of the other's (the ceiling). The additive fit reaches $94.7\%$, within half a point of it. Each factor is close to one-dimensional:

one directiontwo directions
row effects $r_i$ (16 vectors)72%96%

The coordinate along the leading direction is monotone in the index (rank correlation $1.0$ for both), and the two leading directions are orthogonal (cosine $0.05$). A straight line in the index would explain only $71\%$ and $75\%$: the factors are arcs, not segments, as the curvature of the ellipsoid imposes. The two rank-one factors together explain $72.8\%$ of $P$.

How to read this figure
  • Axes. $x$: first principal component of $P$ (41% of its variance); $y$: second (32%); together 73%. Each blue dot is the mean token at one of the 256 positions.
  • Grey lattice. The additive fit $r_i+c_j$ plotted in the same plane: 16 curves for fixed $i$ (varying $j$) and 16 for fixed $j$.
  • Look at. The dots form a curved sheet and sit on the lattice: each position is reached by combining a row arc and a column arc.
  • Conclusion. The sheet that the paper finds is a product: a sum of two one-dimensional factors.
  • What it does not show. The scatter around the lattice (about 3% of the variance outside the additive model); and that training created additivity: the in-sample $R^2$ is also $0.91$ before the last normalisation and $0.90$ for the random-weights twin, so the test establishes the shape of the table, not its origin. Position is also only 4.8% of the variance of a token.

Why this is “a box”

The $16\times16$ grid of positions is the product of a row range and a column range, and the code is the sum of the corresponding factors. The row and the column vary freely and independently: there is no unit budget shared between them. In the terms of section 2, this is a linear code with two bounded features (a zonotope, here curved), not a tile with a simplex of archetypes: a box, not a simplex. A simplex tile would predict that tokens sit near archetypes (corners); the position grid is uniform.

Our reading — it explains two anomalies of the paper's dictionary
  • Dense atoms. A non-negative, sparse code has to represent a signed axis (left-to-right) by two opposite atoms; and an axis on which every token has a value is always active. This explains atoms such as “left”, “right”, “bottom” that fire on about a quarter of all tokens each.
  • Antipodal pairs. Two atoms $\vd_i\approx-\vd_j$ are the two signs of one axis.

This is the axis code the paper itself describes; it does not need polytopes. Where the paper uses position as a first hint of non-linear structure, we read it as the linear special case.
