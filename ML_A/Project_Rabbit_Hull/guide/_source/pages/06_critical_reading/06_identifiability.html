6. Identifiability: a flawed proof and a narrower statement

The paper's proof that a Minkowski sum does not determine its summands has a gap, which a numerical counter-example shows. We do not repair that proof: we show that it is flawed and state what does hold, which concerns supports with all tiles active and is narrower than the paper's claim. The real ambiguity is between families of summands, so a support does not tell whether it comes from a linear code or a block-convex one. What identifies a dictionary in practice is sparsity and the law of the codes; that sparsity identifies tiles in general is a conjecture, not a proved result.

In the paper Prop. 2 · p. 21, App. J Prop. 4 · p. 50

Consequence (I3): the decomposition of $\X$ into a Minkowski sum is not unique, so concepts cannot be recovered from the activations of one layer; one should use the attention weights and earlier layers. The proof passes to support functions, $h_{A\oplus B}=h_A+h_B$ with $h_{\mathcal C}(\vu)=\sup_{\vx\in\mathcal C}\ps{\vu}{\vx}$, so that decomposing $\X$ means writing $h_\X$ as a sum of sublinear functions. It then picks “any sublinear $s$ with $0\le s\le h_\X$” and writes $h_\X=(h_\X-s)+s$. Full reading on page 5.

The gap

$s$ is a valid summand only if $h_\X-s$ is itself sublinear, i.e. the support function of a convex set. It is so exactly when the convex set with support function $s$ is a summand of $\X$. Being sublinear and below $h_\X$ is not enough.

Counter-example with numbers own

Take for $\X$ the equilateral triangle of unit side centred at the origin, with vertices $(\pm\tfrac12,-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{6})$ and $(0,\tfrac{\sqrt3}3)$, and the short segment $Q=[-0.1,0.1]\times\{0\}$, which lies inside the triangle (its inradius is $0.289$). Let $s=h_Q$, i.e. $s(\vw)=0.1\,|w_1|$. Then $0\le s\le h_\X$ (because $Q\subset\X$). Now take the directions $\vu=(\cos60^\circ,\sin60^\circ)=(0.5,\,0.866)$ and $\vv=(\cos120^\circ,\sin120^\circ)=(-0.5,\,0.866)$ and $f=h_\X-s$:

  • $h_\X(\vu)=0.5$ (attained at a vertex), $s(\vu)=0.05$, so $f(\vu)=0.45$; the same for $\vv$: $f(\vv)=0.45$.
  • $\vu+\vv=(0,\sqrt3)$: $h_\X(\vu+\vv)=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{3}\sqrt3=1$, $s(\vu+\vv)=0$, so $f(\vu+\vv)=1.0$.

Subadditivity would require $f(\vu+\vv)\le f(\vu)+f(\vv)=0.9$. But $1.0>0.9$: $f$ is not sublinear, so $h_\X-s$ is not a support function, and $s$ is not a summand. In general with $Q=[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]\times\{0\}$ the violation is exactly $\varepsilon$ ($0.1$ here, $0.2$ for $\varepsilon=0.2$). A grid of 3,600 directions finds a maximal violation of $0.173$ (at $150^\circ$ and $30^\circ$) and $6.6\%$ of the direction pairs violate it (t4_hexagon, part c).

A triangle has no summand other than its own homothets (and points), so no short segment is a summand of it: this is the reason (see page 5 for the proof).

What does hold

Decompositions are never unique, for three trivial reasons: summands can be translated in opposite directions ($\Pc\oplus\Qc=(\Pc+\vt)\oplus(\Qc-\vt)$); a summand can be split, $\Pc=\lambda\Pc\oplus(1-\lambda)\Pc$ for a convex $\Pc$ (the segment $[0,2]=[0,1]\oplus[0,1]$); summands can be regrouped. The rectangle that the paper cuts into three segments is an instance of the second and third. Up to these operations, a polytope is a sum of indecomposable ones, and that decomposition is not unique either: the hexagon (section 2) is the classical example, $H=S_1\oplus S_2\oplus S_3=T\oplus(-T)$, segments and triangles being indecomposable.

Remark — a flawed proof, not a repaired one

The paper's conclusion (decompositions of a support are not unique) survives, but not through its proof: “any sublinear $s$” would have to be “any $s$ such that $h_\X-s$ is sublinear”, and the example that works is the hexagon, not a rectangle cut in segments. Within one family the paper says too little and in one place too much: a sum of segments does determine the directions and total lengths of its generators (Schneider 2014), so the segments cannot “vary in direction” as the paper states; only their lengths can be split.

What a support cannot tell

The statement that holds concerns the support of the activations when all tiles are active, and the hexagon gives its true scope: a support does not tell whether it was generated by a linear code (three segments) or by a block-convex one (two triangles). This is an ambiguity between families, not within the linear one, where the generators' directions and total lengths are determined. Nobody concludes from it that sparse dictionaries cannot be learned from one layer.

What makes dictionaries identifiable in practice

  • Sparsity. If each input uses at most $k$ atoms, the data lie on a union of low-dimensional subspaces, and under conditions on the dictionary (small coherence, see coherence) the dictionary is unique up to sign and permutation. This is a property of the law on the support, not of the support.
  • Independence and non-Gaussianity. Independent non-Gaussian coordinates identify the mixing matrix up to scale and permutation (independent component analysis).

The hypothesis has the same resources. Item (ii) is a sparsity assumption; it turns the support into the union of section 2, whose low-dimensional pieces expose the individual polytopes when a tile is sometimes active alone. That sparse activity identifies the tiles more generally is a conjecture, which we do not prove. And the two clouds of the hexagon figure, which share their support, are told apart at a glance.

Our reading — consequence (I3) is too strong as stated

“Concepts cannot be recovered from the activations of one layer” does not follow from non-uniqueness of the support's decomposition: what identifies a dictionary is the law of the codes, not the support. The practical advice of the paper stands on its own merits: attention weights are available, and they are the codes of the decomposition of Proposition 1.
