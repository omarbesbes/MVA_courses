4. Dictionary learning and sparse autoencoders

Given a matrix of activations $\mA$, find sparse codes $\mZ$ and a dictionary $\mD$ with $\mA\approx\mZ\mD$. The classical methods differ by the constraint they put on $\mZ$ and $\mD$; sparse autoencoders learn $\mZ$ with an encoder; the paper uses a stable variant whose atoms are convex combinations of data.

4.1 A family of factorisations

Problem. Under the LRH, concepts are the rows of an unknown $\mD$. Many methods fit a factorisation; they differ in the constraint, hence in what a concept is.

From LRH to dictionary learning App. B · p. 33
$$(\mZ^\star,\mD^\star)=\argmin_{\mZ,\mD}\ \norm{\mA-\mZ\mD}_F^2$$

subject to one of:

MethodConstraintAtoms are
ACE · k-meanseach $\mZ_i\in\{\ve_1,\dots,\ve_k\}$ (one-hot)cluster centres
ICE · PCA$\mD\mD\T=\mI$orthonormal axes
CRAFT · NMF$\mZ\ge0$, $\mD\ge0$non-negative parts
Archetypal analysis$\mZ_i\in\simplex^c$, $\mD_i\in\conv(\mA)$points on the border of the data cloud

"These approaches trade off fidelity ($\norm{\mA-\mZ\mD}_F$) against sparsity (e.g. $\norm{\mZ}_0$), yielding a Pareto frontier"; SAEs are attractive at scale "because the encoder $\Psi_\theta$ enables amortized, batched inference".

one row per token (over all images).
$\mZ\in\R^{n\times c}$

$\mD\in\R^{c\times d}$

4.2 Sparse autoencoders

Problem. With $n$ in the hundreds of millions of tokens and $c=32{,}000$, classical optimisation is slow and gives no function to apply to a new activation. A sparse autoencoder (SAE; Cunningham et al. 2023, Bricken et al. 2023) learns an encoder that maps an activation to its sparse code in one pass.

$$\begin{aligned}\vz&=\Psi_\theta(\va),\qquad \hat\va=\vz\mD,\\ \mathcal L&=\norm{\va-\vz\mD}_2^2\ (+\lambda\norm{\vz}_1\text{ for ReLU}).\end{aligned}$$
$\Psi_\theta$
one linear layer followed by a non-linearity that makes most outputs exactly 0.
$\vz\mD=\sum_i z_i\mD_i$
reconstruction as a combination of the atoms that fire.

Reading a code. If $z_i=2.3$ on token $t$, concept $i$ "fires on token $t$ with strength 2.3", and token $t$'s activation contains $2.3\,\mD_i$. Over a heat map, one colours each of the 256 patches by $z_i$ (§5.1). Quality of the reconstruction is measured by $R^2=1-\norm{\mA-\mZ\mD}_F^2/\norm{\mA-\bar\mA}_F^2$, and the fraction of variance unexplained is $\mathrm{FVU}=1-R^2$. The paper's dictionary has $R^2>88\%$, i.e. $\mathrm{FVU}<0.12$.

4.3 How sparsity is enforced: ReLU+L1, TopK, BatchTopK

  • ReLU + L1. $\vz=\max(0,\va\mW_e+\vb)$ and a penalty $\lambda\norm{\vz}_1$ in the loss. Sparsity is indirect, tuned by $\lambda$, and the L1 penalty shrinks the codes (a bias).
  • TopK. Keep the $k$ largest pre-activations of each token, set the others to 0. Exactly $k$ active codes per token; no penalty; no shrinkage.
  • BatchTopK (Bussmann et al. 2024). Keep the $k\cdot B$ largest pre-activations in a batch of $B$ tokens, over the whole batch. The average is still $k$ per token, but a complicated token may use more and a simple one fewer.

The paper writes: "Codes $\mZ$ are produced via a single-layer encoder with BatchTopK projection" with $k=8$ (§2 · p. 5). The toy below uses 3 tokens and 5 concepts.

What to look at: with ReLU every positive entry survives. With TopK, $k=2$, every row keeps exactly two entries (token 2 keeps two tiny ones). With BatchTopK the same six survivors are chosen over all tokens: token 2 keeps none, token 3 keeps four. Check the printed counts per token.

Worked example (the default matrix)

Rows $(2,-0.5,1,0.3,0.9)$, $(0.2,0.1,-0.3,0.4,0)$, $(3,1.5,0.8,2.2,1.9)$. Top-2 per token keeps $\{2,1\}$, $\{0.4,0.2\}$, $\{3,2.2\}$. BatchTopK with $k=2$ keeps the 6 largest positive entries of all: $3,2.2,2,1.9,1.5,1$, i.e. 2, 0 and 4 codes per token.

4.4 The archetypal SAE used in the paper

Problem. Plain SAEs are unstable: two runs from different seeds give different dictionaries, which undermines any conclusion drawn from "the" concepts (the paper cites Paulo et al. 2025 and Papadimitriou et al. 2025). Archetypal SAEs (Fel et al. 2025, arXiv:2502.12892) anchor each atom geometrically: atoms must lie in the convex hull of the data, which the authors report "significantly enhances the stability" of the dictionary; a "mildly relaxed" version (RA-SAE) is the one that matches state-of-the-art reconstruction. The DinoVision demo says its concepts come from an RA-SAE.

The paper's configuration §2 · p. 5
$$\begin{aligned}\min_{\mZ,\mD}\ &\norm{\mA-\mZ\mD}_F^2\\ \text{s.t. }\ &\mZ\ge0,\ \norm{\mZ_i}_0\le k,\ \mD_i\in\conv(\mA).\end{aligned}$$

"We set $c=32{,}000$ atoms and $k=8$ active codes per token, approximate $\conv(\mA)$ by $128{,}000$ centroids extracted via $k$-means over 1.4M ImageNet-1K images (with augmentation), and parametrize $\mD=\mS\mC$ with $\mS$ row-stochastic. Codes $\mZ$ are produced via a single-layer encoder with BatchTopK projection. Training with Adam for 50 epochs yields $R^2>88\%$."

$\mC\in\R^{128000\times768}$
fixed: centroids of $k$-means on the activations; a cheap stand-in for the convex hull of all tokens.
$\mS\in\R^{32000\times128000}$
learned; each row is $\ge0$ and sums to 1 (row-stochastic), so row $i$ of $\mS\mC$ is a convex combination of centroids.
$\mD=\mS\mC$
atom $i$ is in $\conv(\text{centroids})\subset\conv(\mA)$ (the centroids are averages of data, so are inside the hull).
$\mZ\ge0,\ \norm{\mZ_i}_0\le8$
concept strengths are non-negative: a concept is present or absent, not "negative".
Reminder — convex hull

$\conv(\mathcal P)$ is the set of all convex combinations (non-negative weights summing to 1) of points of $\mathcal P$. A point of the hull is never outside the range of the data. more

Why it matters. Atoms in the hull are in-distribution (they look like real activations) and reproducible. Three consequences for the rest of the guide: atoms are not forced to be orthogonal, the code $\mZ\ge0$ only adds, and an atom is "an average of real activations" (the paper's page 4 and 5 arguments build on this). Counting: $\mS$ has $32{,}000\times128{,}000\approx4.1\times10^9$ entries, against $2.5\times10^7$ for a free $\mD$; the training set has $1.4\text{M}\times261\approx3.65\times10^8$ tokens per epoch (our arithmetic).

Our reading — a dictionary is one fit among many

The concepts come from one training run of one method; a different $c$, $k$ or constraint gives other atoms. The paper's statements about "the" dictionary are statements about this one. See page 6.
