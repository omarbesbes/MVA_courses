5. Implications for interpretability

If MRH holds, concepts are points and regions rather than directions, steering has a natural stopping point at an archetype, and the decomposition into tiles cannot be recovered from the activations of one layer alone.

In the paper §7.3 · p. 20

“If, and this is an assumption, the MRH holds, three immediate implications follow: (i) Concepts are no longer Directions, but Points and Regions. (ii) Steering admits a strict identifiable maximum. (iii) Decomposition is fundamentally non-identifiable.”

(i) Concepts are points and regions

In the paper §7.3 · p. 20

“In the context of the Archetypal representation framework, the very idea of a concept diverges from the classical linear factor picture in which semantic evidence is quantified by the magnitude of an inner product with a preferred direction. A concept is better understood as a landmark, an extremal point of the convex polytope that tessellates the latent manifold, or as a small constellation of such landmarks whose hull supports a coherent semantic region.”

Decoded: in the LRH the evidence for concept $i$ is $\ps{\va}{\mD_i}$ (or the code $z_i$): a magnitude along a direction. In MRH the evidence is where the activation sits in the polytope of the tile: the barycentric weight on an archetype, or the distance to a landmark. Two activations with the same inner product with a direction can be at different places of the polytope. The nested relation of the hypotheses (box against simplex) is in §2 and page 6.

(ii) Steering has a strict maximum

In the paper §7.3 · p. 20

“In a landmark based geometry probing no longer involves sliding indefinitely along a vector but steering an activation toward a specific point on the manifold. Once the activation reaches that landmark (or the convex cell surrounding it) the semantic signal saturates (further movement would drive the embedding off-manifold). This bounded trajectory could explain why current SAE probing gains plateau or even reverse when the scaling coefficient is pushed too far: the useful range ends at the landmark's basin of attraction. Practical probes should therefore measure how close an activation can get to the landmark set and stop once convergence is achieved, for instance by estimating barycentric weight or geodesic distance inside the convex cell rather than by extrapolating along an unbounded direction.”

Derivation of the two proximity curves. Let $\vx$ be the activation, $\va$ an archetype, and take as proximity $\exp(-\|\vx_t-\va\|)$, the quantity written in Fig. 16 (the guide uses this formula for both curves; the paper only draws them).

  1. Directional. $\vx_t=\vx+t\vv$, $t\ge0$. $\|\vx_t-\va\|^2=\|\vx-\va\|^2+2t\ps{\vv}{\vx-\va}+t^2\|\vv\|^2$.expand the square
  2. This is a parabola in $t$ with minimum at $t^\star=\ps{\vv}{\va-\vx}/\|\vv\|^2$ (if this is positive, i.e. if $\vv$ points somewhat towards $\va$).set the derivative $2\ps{\vv}{\vx-\va}+2t\|\vv\|^2$ to zero
  3. The minimal distance is the component of $\va-\vx$ orthogonal to $\vv$: $d_\perp=\|(\va-\vx)-\frac{\ps{\vv}{\va-\vx}}{\|\vv\|^2}\vv\|$, so the proximity first increases up to $e^{-d_\perp}$ at $t^\star$ and then decreases, tending to $0$ like $e^{-t\|\vv\|}$: a bell. It reaches $1$ only if $\va-\vx$ is parallel to $\vv$.distance is convex in $t$, so its exponential of minus is unimodal
  4. Archetypal. $\vx_t=\vx+t(\va-\vx)$, $t\in[0,1]$. Then $\vx_t-\va=(1-t)(\vx-\va)$, so $\|\vx_t-\va\|=(1-t)\|\vx-\va\|$.the point moves along the segment to $\va$
  5. The proximity is $\exp(-(1-t)\|\vx-\va\|)$: strictly increasing and convex in $t$, equal to $1$ at $t=1$, and the path stays in the hull of any polytope containing $\vx$ and $\va$ (convexity).exponential of an increasing affine function
Numbers (checked)

$\vx=(0,0)$, $\va=(3,1)$, $\|\va-\vx\|=\sqrt{10}\approx3.162$; directional steering with $\vv=(1,0)$ so $t^\star=3$ and $d_\perp=1$. Proximity: directional at $t=0,1,2,3,4,6$: $0.042,\ 0.107,\ 0.243,\ \mathbf{0.368},\ 0.243,\ 0.042$ (the peak is $e^{-1}$, symmetric about $t^\star$ because the distance is $\sqrt{(3-t)^2+1}$). Archetypal at $t=0,\tfrac14,\tfrac12,\tfrac34,1$: $0.042,\ 0.093,\ 0.206,\ 0.454,\ \mathbf{1}$. Our reading — a prediction, not a measurement

The paper runs no steering experiment here: the saturation is a consequence of the hypothesis, offered as a possible explanation for the plateaus and reversals reported for SAE steering. The curves above show that the picture follows from the definition of the two paths, not from data.

(iii) The decomposition is not identifiable

In the paper — Proposition 2 (non-identifiability of Minkowski decomposition) §7.3 · p. 21; App. J, Prop. 4 · p. 50

Let $\X=\bigmsum_{i=1}^m\Pc_i$ be a Minkowski sum of convex polytopes. Given only observations from $\X$, the decomposition $\{\Pc_1,\dots,\Pc_m\}$ is generally non-unique: there exist distinct collections $\{\mathcal Q_1,\dots,\mathcal Q_k\}$ such that $\X=\bigmsum_{j=1}^k\mathcal Q_j$. In particular, even very simple polytopes admit infinitely many decompositions as sums of line segments (zonotope generators) with varying directions and lengths.

Reminder — support function

For a non-empty closed convex $\mathcal C$, $h_{\mathcal C}(\vu)=\sup_{\vx\in\mathcal C}\ps{\vu}{\vx}$: how far $\mathcal C$ extends in direction $\vu$. It is sublinear (positively homogeneous: $h(\lambda\vu)=\lambda h(\vu)$, $\lambda\ge0$; subadditive: $h(\vu+\vu')\le h(\vu)+h(\vu')$), it determines $\mathcal C$ (the closed convex sets are in bijection with the sublinear lower-semicontinuous functions), and it is additive on Minkowski sums: $h_{A\oplus B}=h_A+h_B$ (the largest $\ps{\vu}{\va+\vb}$ is the sum of the largest $\ps{\vu}{\va}$ and the largest $\ps{\vu}{\vb}$). More.

The paper's proof, decoded

  1. Support functions are sublinear and additive on sums, so $\X=\bigmsum_i\Pc_i$ is equivalent to $h_\X=\sum_ih_{\Pc_i}$: a decomposition of one sublinear function into sublinear summands.correct: the correspondence set $\leftrightarrow$ support function is bijective
  2. “Fix any sublinear $s$ with $0\le s\le h_\X$. Then $h_\X=(h_\X-s)+s$ is a valid decomposition.”this is the step that fails: it needs $h_\X-s$ to be sublinear as well
  3. Each sublinear $h_i$ is the support function of a unique closed convex $\mathcal Q_i$, so $h_\X=\sum_ih_i\iff\X=\bigmsum_i\mathcal Q_i$.correct, with the bijection of step 1
  4. “There are infinitely many ways to split $h_\X$, hence infinitely many Minkowski decompositions.”only as strong as step 2
Remark — the gap in the paper's proof: $h_\X-s$ need not be sublinear

$0\le s\le h_\X$ does not make $h_\X-s$ subadditive. Counter-example (checked numerically). $\X=\conv\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)\}$, so $h_\X(\vu)=\max(0,u_1,u_2)$. Let $s=h_{[0,(0.2,0.2)]}$, the support function of a short segment inside $\X$: $s(\vu)=\max(0,\,0.2(u_1+u_2))$. Then $0\le s\le h_\X$ (because the segment is inside $\X$) and both are sublinear. But $g=h_\X-s$ is not: with $\vu=(1,1)$, $\vu'=(-1,0)$, $$g(\vu)=1-0.4=0.6,\qquad g(\vu')=0-0=0,\qquad g(\vu+\vu')=g(0,1)=1-0.2=0.8>0.6+0.$$ So $g$ is not a support function, and the triangle is not a sum “segment $\oplus$ something”: a triangle has no segment as a summand (Exercise 6). A random test of $10^5$ pairs finds $15{,}846$ violations of subadditivity. The correct condition is that $s$ is the support function of a summand, which is far from automatic. The proposition itself is true as stated (“there exist distinct collections”), but the proof must exhibit them, as follows.

What is true: trivial and genuine non-uniqueness

Trivial non-uniqueness (always available, for any sum with a non-point summand):

  • Translate in opposite directions: $\Pc\oplus\mathcal Q=(\Pc+\vt)\oplus(\mathcal Q-\vt)$ for any $\vt$.
  • Split a summand: for convex $\Pc$ and $\lambda\in(0,1)$, $\Pc=\lambda\Pc\oplus(1-\lambda)\Pc$. ($\supseteq$ by convexity; $\subseteq$: $\vp=\lambda\vp+(1-\lambda)\vp$.) Iterating gives collections of any size $k>m$.
  • Regroup: $(\Pc\oplus\mathcal Q)\oplus\mathcal R=\Pc\oplus(\mathcal Q\oplus\mathcal R)$: the same set with a different partition into tiles.
In the paper — the rectangle example App. J · p. 50

In $\R^2$, $\X=[-a,a]\times[-b,b]=([-a,a]\ve_1)\oplus([-b,b]\ve_2)$. For any $\alpha\in(0,1)$, $[-a,a]\ve_1=[-\alpha a,\alpha a]\ve_1\oplus[-(1-\alpha)a,(1-\alpha)a]\ve_1$, so $\X$ is also the sum of these two segments and $[-b,b]\ve_2$: “uncountably many distinct 3-segment decompositions”, and iterating gives $k$-segment decompositions for every $k$.

This is the second trivial case: splitting a summand into homothetic copies. The directions of the generators do not change (the paper says “varying directions and lengths”, but only the lengths vary here; the two directions are those of the sides of the rectangle).

A genuine example: the regular hexagon. Let $\vd_1,\vd_2,\vd_3$ be the unit vectors at $0^\circ,60^\circ,120^\circ$ and $T$ an equilateral triangle of side $1$. Then $$\underbrace{[-\tfrac12,\tfrac12]\vd_1\oplus[-\tfrac12,\tfrac12]\vd_2\oplus[-\tfrac12,\tfrac12]\vd_3}_{\text{three segments}}\;=\;\mathcal H\;=\;T\oplus(-T)\qquad(T\text{ centred at }0),$$ where $\mathcal H$ is the regular hexagon of side $1$ (vertices $(\cos k60^\circ,\sin k60^\circ)$) and the vertices of $T$ are $\vv_0=(-\tfrac12,-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{6})$, $\vv_1=(\tfrac12,-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{6})$, $\vv_2=(0,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{3})$.

Proof (edge vectors)

Fact: the Minkowski sum of convex polygons is the convex polygon whose edge vectors, taken in increasing order of angle, are those of the summands, merged and added when parallel (a point-reflection of a segment contributes its edge twice, once in each direction). A convex polygon is determined by its edge vectors up to translation.

  1. Each segment $[-\tfrac12,\tfrac12]\vd_k$ has edge vectors $\vd_k$ and $-\vd_k$. The three give the six unit vectors at angles $0^\circ,60^\circ,\dots,300^\circ$ (since $-\vd_1,-\vd_2,-\vd_3$ are at $180^\circ,240^\circ,300^\circ$).$\vd_k$ at $0,60,120$; opposites at $180,240,300$
  2. $T$ has edge vectors $\ve_1,\ve_2,\ve_3$ of length 1 at $0^\circ,120^\circ,240^\circ$ ($\ve_1+\ve_2+\ve_3=0$); $-T$ has $-\ve_1,-\ve_2,-\ve_3$ at $180^\circ,300^\circ,60^\circ$. Together: the same six unit vectors at multiples of $60^\circ$.a triangle with exterior angles $120^\circ$; its reflection is rotated by $180^\circ$
  3. The two polygons have the same edge vectors, hence are translates of one another; both are centrally symmetric about $0$ (a centred segment, $T\oplus(-T)$), so they are equal. A convex polygon with six unit edges at successive $60^\circ$ is the regular hexagon of side 1. $\square$equal edge sequences up to translation, same centre
Segments and triangles are indecomposable (a segment is a sum of parallel segments only; a triangle only of homothetic triangles, Exercise 6), so this is a factorisation into irreducible pieces in two ways, with different numbers of factors (3 against 2): not a matter of splitting or translating. The numerical check: the vertex sets and the support functions of the two sums agree to $2\cdot10^{-16}$ on 721 directions, and the area is $3\sqrt3/2\approx2.598$ for both. More on decomposability.

Our reading — a support cannot say which hypothesis, but a sum of segments is determined

The hexagon is also three bounded linear features in superposition (a zonotope) or two three-way categorical tiles. The support cannot distinguish them; the distribution of codes (mass on three lines through the centre, or near the six corners and the centre) can. Within the linear family the support is not ambiguous: a sum of segments does determine the directions and total lengths of its generators (only their lengths can be split), contrary to the paper's remark that the segments may vary in direction. The real ambiguity is between families of summands. In practice a dictionary is identified by sparsity and the law of the codes; that sparsity identifies tiles in general is a conjecture, not a proved result. Page 6.

In the paper — the way out §7.3 · p. 21; §8 · p. 21

“This means that, under the MRH, any method attempting to recover individual concept contributions $\vz$ or concept polytopes $\Pc$ only from activations from a single layer alone faces a non-unique solution. However, this impossibility, at its core, also hint a possible direction. While the final Minkowski sum hide its constituent factors, the information to find the decomposition is available within previous intermediate representations. Accessing attention weights and activations from earlier layers may render the factorization tractable, and pointing interpretability efforts toward exploiting architectural structure rather than treating learned representations as opaque geometric objects.” In §8: “If MRH holds, then this very non-identifiability becomes instructive: it tells us that concept extraction cannot stop at a layer's activations. To recover meaningful structure, we must explain a layer through the sequence of transformations that formed it.”

Why this follows: the attention weights $\val_h$ are the codes of the tiles in Proposition 1, and the values $\mW_O^{(h)}\mV_h$ the archetypes; so the head-wise split is read off the forward pass instead of being inferred from the sum. This is also why the paper's non-identifiability and its attention argument fit together: the tile structure comes from the architecture. The paper gives no experiment that uses attention weights this way.
