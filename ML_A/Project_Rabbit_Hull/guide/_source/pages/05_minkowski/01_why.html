1. Why a geometry: what a method can see

A dictionary method comes with an assumption about the shape of the set of activations; it can only reveal structure that this shape can express. Part II found structure that the linear shape expresses badly; Part III proposes another shape.

In the paper §7 · p. 17

“While hypotheses about representational composition are rarely stated explicitly, the geometry assumed by our methods is foundational: it constrains both their validity and the phenomena they can reveal. If sparse autoencoders implicitly answer to the Linear Representation Hypothesis, then we understand the importance of specifying the correct ambient geometry: it not only conditions how we interpret representations, but also determines the very form that our analytical methods of concept extraction can take.”

Example. A sparse autoencoder writes every activation as $\va=\vz\mD$ with few non-zero non-negative codes: the activations are assumed to lie near a union of low-dimensional cones. Whatever it finds, it reports as directions with magnitudes. If the true structure is “a point somewhere between these prototypes”, the SAE will cut it into several directions that fire together, and a reader will take that for several concepts.

The anomalies of Part II, in three lines

  1. Atoms are neither quasi-orthogonal nor neuron-aligned (large coherence, non-flat singular values, low Hoyer score), and concept co-activation barely follows atom geometry ($r=0.28$).
  2. A few dense positional atoms fire on a quarter of all tokens, and position lives in a 2-D sheet that shrinks with depth: not what a sparse code predicts.
  3. Within one image tokens vary smoothly (per-image PCA maps), and local neighbourhoods stay connected after removing position: tokens look like interpolations between prototypes.

Details and numbers: page 4, beyond the LRH. The paper's proposal is that the third line is “the surface of a deeper organization”: the activation space behaves as a sum of convex hulls.

Gärdenfors' conceptual spaces, in five lines

The cognitive scientist Peter Gärdenfors models a concept as a convex region in a space whose axes are perceptual or functional qualities (colour, weight, temperature). A point is one object. The most typical members of the region, its prototypes, are the focal, often extreme, points. Membership varies with proximity, and convexity means: if two objects belong to the category, every point on the segment between them does too. This is the picture the paper turns into a testable hypothesis (§3 below, Gärdenfors).
