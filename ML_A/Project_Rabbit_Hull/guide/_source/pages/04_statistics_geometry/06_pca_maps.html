7. The shape of an image: PCA maps

The paper stops looking at the dictionary and looks at the 256 patch tokens of one image. Coloured by their first three principal components, they give smooth maps that follow objects. Since PCA is linear, the paper says it "cannot fabricate curvature"; this is true, but needs a precise statement.

The problem

All of Part I and §5 went through a dictionary factorisation $\mA\approx\mZ\mD$. §6 asks what the tokens look like without it ("model-native geometry"), in the simplest setting: the tokens of one image. The tool is the PCA map that Oquab et al. (DINOv2) and Darcet et al. (registers) already show to illustrate semantic structure.

How a PCA map is made

Reminder — PCA

For a data matrix $\mX\in\R^{n\times d}$, centre the rows, take the eigenvectors of the covariance (equivalently the right singular vectors) as principal directions $\vv_1,\vv_2,\dots$ ordered by variance. The coordinate of row $\vx$ on component $r$ is $\ps{\vx-\bar\vx}{\vv_r}$. more

  1. Take the $256$ patch tokens of one image: $\mX\in\R^{256\times768}$. the paper's maps use patch tokens; cls and registers are left out.
  2. Centre them and compute the top three principal directions of this image's tokens. "per-image basis": one PCA per image.
  3. Project: each token gets three numbers $(u_1,u_2,u_3)$. $256\times3$ matrix.
  4. Rescale each of the three channels to $[0,1]$ and read them as $(r,g,b)$. the caption says "normalized to $[0,1]$"; whether per channel or jointly is not stated.
  5. Put the $256$ colours back on the $16\times16$ grid of patches. token $(i,j)$ keeps its position; the map is an image.
Signs and colours are only meaningful inside one image

A principal direction is defined up to sign, and its rank depends on the image. The colour "red" in two different per-image maps does not mean the same thing. That is why Fig. 22 (below) uses one shared basis.

"PCA is linear, so it cannot fabricate curvature", made precise

Fact

Let $L:\R^d\to\R^3$ be affine, $L(\vx)=\mP\vx+\vb$. For two points $\va,\vb'$, the image of the segment $[\va,\vb']$ is the segment $[L\va,L\vb']$, or a single point when $L\va=L\vb'$.

  1. A point of the segment is $\vx_t=(1-t)\va+t\vb'$, $t\in[0,1]$. definition of the segment.
  2. $L(\vx_t)=\mP((1-t)\va+t\vb')+\vb=(1-t)(\mP\va+\vb)+t(\mP\vb'+\vb)=(1-t)L\va+tL\vb'$. linearity of $\mP$, and $(1-t)\vb+t\vb=\vb$.
  3. So $t\mapsto L(\vx_t)$ is the parametrisation of the segment from $L\va$ to $L\vb'$; if the two endpoints coincide, a single point. $\square$ same formula as the segment.

Consequence: if the colours of the PCA map trace a curved surface, that curvature was already present in the projection of the data onto the three directions. A projection can lose shape (flatten, as in the right panel), never add curvature to a straight set. This is the correct content of the paper's sentence.

Our reading — what a smooth map does and does not prove

A projection onto orthonormal directions can only shrink distances: $\norm{\mP\vx-\mP\vy}\le\norm{\vx-\vy}$. So if neighbouring patches are close in $\R^{768}$, they are close in colour. The converse fails: two tokens can have the same colour and differ in all the other $765$ directions. A smooth map therefore shows that the top-3 coordinates vary slowly across the image; it is consistent with, but does not prove, that the tokens form a connected set in $\R^{768}$. Moreover, neighbouring patches of a natural image look alike, so some smoothness is expected from the content; the paper does not give a null comparison (for example, the same maps for pixel patches). See page 6.

"Locally connected"

The paper uses "locally connected" and "smooth" without defining them. The operational meaning in the figures: tokens of adjacent patches are neighbours in the embedding, so that the 256 tokens trace a connected low-dimensional set (a curve or a sheet) rather than a cloud, and colour boundaries follow object boundaries.

How to read Figures 12 and 13
  • What is drawn. A photo and, next to it, a $16\times16$ map of colours (RGB $=$ the first three per-image principal components).
  • What to look at. Regions of nearly constant colour with sharp edges at object contours; the object and its background get different colours; a few noisy patches remain.

Why a shared basis shows the geometry is "not purely relative"

A purely relative representation would be one in which only the distances between tokens of the same image are meaningful; then the position of the clouds in the ambient space of different images would be arbitrary, and a PCA basis pooled over images would not align them. Here the clouds overlap and the colours agree. It is a statement about these five images, not a proof for arbitrary ones.

A linear map sends a straight line of tokens (two tokens and all their convex combinations) to a curve in the PCA map. Is that possible?

Answer

No: the image of a segment under a linear (or affine) map is a segment or a point. A curved trace in a PCA map means the tokens themselves do not lie on a straight line; it says nothing about the original curvature in the discarded directions.
