3. Geometry of the dictionary

The 32,000 atoms are not spread like random vectors and not like a Grassmannian frame: inner products have heavy tails (up to about $0.9$), singular values decay fast (the dictionary is anisotropic), and atoms are spread over many coordinates (Hoyer score about $0.22$–$0.25$).

The problem and the reference points

The LRH (Definition 1, page 3) asks for unit atoms with small coherence. "Small" needs a scale. The paper builds two reference dictionaries with the same $c=32{,}000$ and $d=768$.

Baselines for concept geometry App. F · p. 38

"Random vectors on sphere: we sample a Gaussian random matrix $\mH\in\R^{c\times d}$, $H_{ij}\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ i.i.d., and then we normalized each row to have L2 norm 1. [...] Grassmannian frame: [...] we numerically computed the Grassmannian frame of $c$ atoms in $d$ dimension. [...] we adapted the algorithm from recent work TAAP, [...] adapted it to CUDA [...] solving the frame once still takes 6hr on an A100 GPU. In the end, we reached maximal coherence of $0.065897$."

Reminder — coherence, Welch bound, Grassmannian frames

Coherence of unit vectors: $\mu(\mD)=\max_{i\ne j}\abs{\ps{\mD_i}{\mD_j}}$. For $c>d$ unit vectors in $\R^d$, the Welch bound says $\mu\ge\sqrt{\dfrac{c-d}{d(c-1)}}$; a Grassmannian frame is a set of $c$ unit vectors minimising $\mu$ (it reaches the bound only for special $(c,d)$). more

Recomputed — the numbers behind the baselines
  1. Welch bound for $c=32{,}000$, $d=768$: $\sqrt{(32000-768)/(768\times31999)}\approx0.0356$. plug in; no frame can be below this.
  2. The computed Grassmannian frame has $\mu=0.0659$: $1.85$ times the Welch bound. $0.065897/0.03565$. The bound is not tight for these sizes, so the numerical frame is only an approximation of the optimum (as the paper says).
  3. Random unit vectors: an inner product is approximately $\mathcal N(0,1/d)$, standard deviation $1/\sqrt{768}=0.0361$. the inner product of two independent uniform points on the sphere has variance $1/d$.
  4. There are $c(c-1)/2\approx5.1\times10^{8}$ pairs. The largest of $P$ approximately Gaussian values is about $\sqrt{2\ln P/d}=0.229$. Gaussian tail: $P\,e^{-d\mu^2/2}\approx1$. Matches the paper's "random within $\pm0.2$" (a sample of 4000 atoms gives $0.196$ in check_04.py).

(B) Singular values: tight frame, Marchenko–Pastur, anisotropy

Reminder — tight frames and the spectrum of random matrices

$\mD\in\R^{c\times d}$ with unit rows has $\sum_{i\le d}\sigma_i^2=\norm{\mD}_F^2=c$. If all singular values are equal, $\sigma_i=\sqrt{c/d}$ and the rows form a tight frame ($\mD\T\mD=\tfrac cd\mI$). For a random matrix with i.i.d. rows, the singular values fall in the Marchenko–Pastur band $\sqrt{c/d}\,(1\pm\sqrt{d/c})$. more

  1. Tight-frame level: $\sqrt{c/d}=\sqrt{32000/768}=6.455$. $\sum\sigma^2=c$ spread evenly over $d$ values.
  2. Marchenko–Pastur band: $6.455\times[1-0.155,\,1+0.155]=[5.45,\,7.45]$. $\sqrt{d/c}=0.155$. A simulated random $32000\times768$ dictionary gives $[5.47,7.45]$, matching the figure's "$7.4\to5.5$".
  3. The Grassmannian frame is (almost) tight: flat at $\approx6.5$. minimal coherence goes with a nearly flat spectrum.
  4. The SAE: $\sigma_1\approx22.5$, so $\sigma_1^2\approx506$, i.e. $12$ times the average $c/d=41.7$ and $1.6\%$ of the total mass in one direction. $22.5^2/41.7=12.2$ and $506/32000=1.6\%$.
Anisotropy

A set of atoms is anisotropic when their mass is not spread evenly over directions: some directions of $\R^d$ carry much more squared mass $\sigma^2$ than others. Isotropic means all $\sigma_i^2$ equal to $c/d$. Here: a few directions hold much more mass, and the tail of singular values goes to almost $0$, i.e. some directions of the space are practically unused by the dictionary.

Interactive figure (needs JavaScript).

What to look at: at 0 the atoms are uniform on the sphere and the singular values fill the Marchenko–Pastur band. Raise anisotropy: the first singular values grow above the band and the last fall below it, while $\sum\sigma^2$ stays equal to $c$. Both are small-size versions ($c=160$, $d=32$) of the $32000\times768$ case.

Global anisotropy: two tentative explanations §5 · p. 12 and footnote

"One potential explanation is that the activations $\mA$ themselves have anisotropic covariance, thus to reconstruct them $\mA\approx\mZ\mD$ with L2 error, more atoms are allocated to the more important and higher variance subspace." Footnote: "A speculative hypothesis is that the affine components of the last LayerNorm deforms the activation space into an ellipsoidal geometry, locally increasing curvature along some directions. In such regions, dictionary learning may require more atoms to adequately cover the manifold with low reconstruction error. While this remains to be formalized [...]"

  • First explanation. The atoms are in $\conv(\mA)$ (archetypal SAE) and the loss is $\norm{\mA-\mZ\mD}_F^2$; high-variance directions of $\mA$ matter more for the loss, so more atoms point there. This is checkable (compare the spectrum of $\mD$ with the covariance spectrum of $\mA$); the paper does not do it.
  • Second explanation. After the last LayerNorm, $\mathrm{LN}(x)=\gamma\odot u+\beta$ with $u$ centred and scaled to norm $\sqrt d$. If the entries of $\gamma$ are non-zero, the output lies on an ellipsoid. The paper calls this speculative and unformalised.
Our reading — the two explanations are not exclusive, and the second is a real constraint

Both can hold: the ellipsoid shape is itself a source of anisotropic covariance. We test the ellipsoid claim in our experiments (page 6); it also bears on the Minkowski hypothesis of page 5.

(C–D) Hoyer scores: atoms are distributed

Reminder — Hoyer sparsity

For $\vx\in\R^n$, $\mathrm{Hoyer}(\vx)=\dfrac{\sqrt n-\norm{\vx}_1/\norm{\vx}_2}{\sqrt n-1}\in[0,1]$: $1$ for a one-hot vector, $0$ for a constant one. more

For a Gaussian vector, $\norm{\vx}_1/\norm{\vx}_2\approx\sqrt{2n/\pi}$, so the score tends to $1-\sqrt{2/\pi}=0.202$ (simulation at $n=768$: $0.210$). An atom aligned with one neuron would score near $1$; a vector with $77$ equal non-zero entries ($10\%$ of $768$) scores $0.71$. The SAE atoms score $0.22$–$0.25$: almost as spread as random vectors, slightly sparser. So each atom is a combination of many of the $768$ coordinates: a distributed representation, not neuron-aligned.

Our reading — Hoyer depends on the basis

The score is measured in the coordinate basis of the residual stream, which the model has no reason to privilege except through LayerNorm's per-coordinate $\gamma,\beta$ (and known high-norm coordinates in some ViTs). A score close to that of Gaussian vectors says the atoms are not axis-aligned; it does not say they are "as random as" Gaussian ones (they are not, by panels A and B).

Johnson–Lindenstrauss footnote §5 · p. 12

"The Johnson–Lindenstrauss (JL) lemma implies that $p=\exp(\mathcal O(m\eps^2))$ vectors can be embedded into $\R^m$ such that all pairwise inner products are preserved up to a small distortion $\eps>0$. This supports the feasibility of near-orthogonality even in regimes where $p\gg m$."

Decoded: with $m=d=768$ dimensions one can place exponentially many ($\exp(O(768\eps^2))$) unit vectors with all $\abs{\ps{\cdot}{\cdot}}\le\eps$. Equivalently $d\gtrsim\eps^{-2}\ln c$: with $\ln c=10.4$, near-orthogonality at the $0.2$ level needs a few hundred dimensions. So the LRH's "overcomplete but quasi-orthogonal" is feasible, and the paper's point is that the learned dictionary does not use this freedom (it prefers "structured redundancy"). more

What the paper concludes, and what to keep

The paper: the dictionary is "neither maximally incoherent nor uniformly distributed", with clusters, reduced effective rank, and groups of atoms tied to tasks (page 3). Keep apart: (A) is a statement about pairs (some are far above $0.066$); (B) about the global spectrum; (C–D) about coordinates. None of them alone contradicts the LRH, since the LRH (Def. 1) only asks for small coherence $\varepsilon$ to be some number; the paper's argument is that $\mu\approx0.9$ is not small.

Why must the singular values of a unit-row dictionary with $c=32000$, $d=768$ have mean square $41.7$, whatever its geometry?

Answer

$\sum_{i=1}^d\sigma_i^2=\norm{\mD}_F^2=\sum_j\norm{\mD_j}^2=c$ since rows are unit. Divide by $d$: $c/d=41.7$. A larger first singular value must be paid by smaller ones.

Reflex

When you see "the singular values decay" for a dictionary → compare with $\sqrt{c/d}$ and the Marchenko–Pastur band, not with a flat line at $1$.
