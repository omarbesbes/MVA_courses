1. How often and how strongly concepts fire

For almost every concept, the rarer it is, the higher its energy when it fires (a triangular envelope in Fig. 9 A). Three concepts break the pattern: they fire on roughly a quarter of all tokens and encode position (left, right, bottom).

The problem

The LRH says a token uses few concepts out of many. That is a statement about the support of the code. It says nothing about two other things one can measure directly on $\mZ$: how often each concept is on (its frequency), and how large it is when on (its energy). If the dictionary behaved like a pure sparse code, we would expect every concept to be rare. The paper looks for exceptions.

What the paper plots

Concept occurrence statistics §5 · p. 11, Fig. 9 · p. 11

"[...] its conditional energy $\E(Z_i\mid Z_i>0)$, the expected activation given that it fires, plotted against its firing count, i.e., the number of datapoints where $Z_i$ is nonzero. For the majority of concepts, the points are distributed within a triangular envelope [...]. [...] three outlier concepts that exhibit an anomalously dense activation pattern, firing across the entire dataset. [...] they encode positional information: specifically indicating whether a token lies on the left, right, or bottom of the input image grid. [...] we observe a hybrid representational regime where a small set of universally-active (here spatially grounded) features coexists with a broader ensemble of highly selective, image-contingent activations."

$Z_i$
the code of concept $i$ on one token: a non-negative number, zero on most tokens.
$\E(Z_i\mid Z_i>0)$
conditional energy: the mean of $Z_i$ over the tokens where it is non-zero. It ignores the zeros, unlike $\E[Z_i]$ used for importance on page 3.
number of firings
the count of tokens (not images) with $Z_i>0$. Maximum: the number of tokens, about $3.65\times10^8$.
"energy"
the size of the code, hence of the contribution $Z_i\mD_i$ to $\va=\vz\mD$ (atoms have unit norm). The paper writes "norm regimes".
"dense"
active on a large fraction of tokens (reference: Sun et al. 2025, dense latents in SAEs).
How to read this figure
  • Axes. Horizontal: firing count (the bulk is below about $2\times10^{6}$, the outliers are near $0.95$–$1.15\times10^{8}$). Vertical: conditional energy, from $0$ to about $5$.
  • The bulk. A triangle: at low counts the energies are spread from small to large values; as the count grows, the largest energies disappear. Frequent concepts are weak when they fire.
  • The three outliers. Energy about $1.2$–$1.3$, count about $10^8$: far right, at a modest height. Panels B and C show them on example images: they light up a side of the image (left, right, bottom) regardless of the content.
  • What the authors conclude. Concepts live in different energy regimes, and the code is a hybrid: sparse selective concepts plus a few universal positional ones.
  • What the figure does not show. The shape of the triangle is not explained (no model, no axis scale given for the energy of the typical token); and it says nothing about which concepts sit in the bulk.

How dense is "dense"? A back-of-envelope estimate

Our estimate — from the numbers of the paper

The paper trains on $1.4$M ImageNet images ($t=261$ tokens each) and reads outlier counts of about $0.95$ to $1.15\times10^8$ off the figure. The following uses these two readings; the exact counts are not in the text.

  1. Tokens: $1.4\times10^6\times261\approx3.65\times10^8$.a row of $\mZ$ per token.
  2. An outlier firing $0.95$–$1.15\times10^8$ times is active on $26\%$ to $31\%$ of all tokens.ratio of the count to the number of tokens.
  3. A typical concept fires fewer than $2\times10^{6}$ times, i.e. on less than $0.55\%$ of the tokens.the same ratio, using the right edge of the bulk.
  4. Total activations: $k\times3.65\times10^8\approx2.9\times10^9$ (if every token uses exactly its $k=8$ slots).BatchTopK keeps $k$ per token on average, so this is an average count.
  5. Three outliers at $1.05\times10^8$ each take $3.15\times10^8$ activations: about $11\%$ of all activations (range $10$–$12\%$).$3.15\times10^8/2.9\times10^9$.
  6. The mean concept fires $2.9\times10^9/32{,}000\approx9\times10^4$ times, so the three outliers fire about $1000$ times more often than the average one.$1.05\times10^8/9.1\times10^4\approx1150$.
Why an always-on feature is awkward for a sparse code

A code with $k=8$ active entries per token has a budget of 8 slots per token. A concept that is on in 29% of the tokens occupies one of those slots in 29% of the tokens; three such concepts occupy $3\times0.287\approx0.86$ slots per token on average (our estimate; the three are probably not independent, since "left" and "right" cannot hold at once). So about one slot in nine is not spent on image content but on position. More basically, a feature that is almost always on carries little information per token about the token: it behaves like a bias or a slowly varying coordinate, which is what a dense low-rank component is. The paper's analogy is "sparse plus dense low-rank" representations.

What the paper links to

The text connects the three outliers to two other results: positional information is linearly decodable up to intermediate layers (section 8, Fig. 14) and forms a 2-dimensional subspace at the penultimate layer. A 2-D position subspace fits with three dense concepts: "left" and "right" are two opposite ends of the horizontal coordinate and "bottom" is one end of the vertical one (see the antipodal section for why one signed coordinate needs two non-negative atoms).

Our reading — "dense" is a threshold, and the triangle is not explained

The paper does not give a definition of dense, nor the exact counts. The triangle is plausible from a norm budget (tokens have a roughly bounded norm, so a concept cannot be both frequent and large), but this is our intuition, not a result. Also, the measured quantity is per token over all token types: it does not say whether the three concepts fire on registers and cls. See page 6.

A concept fires on $0.3\%$ of the tokens with mean code $3$. Another fires on $30\%$ with mean code $1.2$. Which has the larger $\E[Z_i]$, and which the larger conditional energy?

Answer

$\E[Z]=\text{frequency}\times\text{conditional energy}$: $0.003\times3=0.009$ against $0.3\times1.2=0.36$. The dense one has $40$ times the mean code, the rare one a $2.5$ times larger conditional energy. That is why the paper plots conditional energy against the count: $\E[Z]$ would mix the two.

Reflex

When you see "mean activation" of a sparse code → ask whether zeros are included. $\E[Z_i]$ includes them, $\E[Z_i\mid Z_i>0]$ does not.
