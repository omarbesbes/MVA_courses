9. Exercises

Exercise 1 — Shapes ★

With $n$ images, $t=261$, $c=32{,}000$, $d=768$ and a head with $o=1000$ outputs, give the shapes of $\mA$, $\mZ$, $\mD$, $\mW$, $\mW'$, $\mY$. How many non-zero codes does one image have at most?

Hint

$\mY=\mA\mW\T$ must be $nt\times o$.

Solution

$\mA:\ nt\times768$; $\mZ:\ nt\times32000$; $\mD:\ 32000\times768$; $\mW:\ 1000\times768$; $\mW'=\mD\mW\T:\ 32000\times1000$ (32 million entries); $\mY:\ nt\times1000$. One image: $8\times261=2088$ non-zeros at most, out of $261\times32000$.

Exercise 2 — Toy dictionary ★

With the atoms of the toy example ($\mD_1=(1,0)$, $\mD_2=(0,1)$, $\mD_3=(1,1)/\sqrt2$), compute $\vz\mD$ for $\vz=(0,1,1)$. Does the dictionary satisfy condition (ii) for $\eps=0.1$?

Solution

$\vz\mD=(0,1)+(0.7071,0.7071)=(0.7071,\,1.7071)$. The coherence is $\max(0,0.7071,0.7071)=0.7071>0.1$: condition (ii) fails. In $\R^2$ any three unit vectors have coherence at least $0.5$ (the Welch bound), so no choice of atoms would work.

Exercise 3 — Importance ★★

Same $\mW'$ as in the worked example but $\E[\vz]=(0.4,\,0.1,\,0.5)$. Compute the score matrix and the mean outputs. Which concept is most important for output 1 and for output 2? What changed with respect to the example, and why?

Hint

Multiply each row of $\mW'$ by the matching mean code.

Solution

Scores: concept 1: $(0.80,-0.40)$; concept 2: $(0.05,0.10)$; concept 3: $(-1.50,\,2.00)$. Sums: $(-0.65,\,1.70)$. Concept 3 now dominates both outputs in absolute value (1.5 and 2.0), and the mean output 1 becomes negative. Its weights were already the largest; the mean code went from 0.05 to 0.5, ten times more. Importance is the product of frequency × intensity and alignment.

Exercise 4 — Gradient × input ★★

Show that $\E[Z_i]W'_{ij}$ is the average of gradient × input of $Y_j$ with respect to the codes. Why does the equality fail for the true model (the SAE decoder is linear but the network is not)?

Solution

$Y_{n,j}=\sum_iZ_{n,i}W'_{ij}$, so $\partial Y_{n,j}/\partial Z_{n,i}=W'_{ij}$ and gradient × input is $Z_{n,i}W'_{ij}$; averaging over $n$ gives $\E[Z_i]W'_{ij}$. For a non-linear function of the codes (for instance a later transformer layer between the codes and the output), the gradient depends on the input and the average of products is not the product of averages. Here the probe is linear in $\mA$ and the reconstruction $\mA\approx\mZ\mD$ is linear in $\mZ$, so only the reconstruction error breaks it.

Exercise 5 — Entropy of footprints ★

Compute the entropy (nats) of a footprint that is (a) equal on the five special tokens and zero on patches; (b) half on cls, half spread uniformly over the 256 patches.

Hint

Normalise to a probability vector first; $H=-\sum p\ln p$.

Solution

(a) $H=\ln5=1.609$. (b) $H=-\tfrac12\ln\tfrac12-256\cdot\tfrac{1}{512}\ln\tfrac1{512}=\tfrac12\ln2+\tfrac12\ln512=\tfrac12(0.693+6.238)=3.466$. A single token that carries half the mass lowers the entropy by 2.1 nats from the 5.545 of the uniform patches.

Exercise 6 — A broad but biased concept ★★

A concept puts 80% of its footprint mass uniformly on 80 patches (the left third) and 20% uniformly on the other 176 patches, nothing on special tokens. Compute $H$ and $e^H$. Where in the histogram of Fig. 7 does it fall, and would the paper call it position-specific?

Solution

$p=0.8/80=0.01$ on 80 patches, $p=0.2/176=0.001136$ on 176. $H=-0.8\ln0.01-0.2\ln0.001136=3.684+1.356=5.040$, $e^H=154$: as if the concept were uniform on 154 tokens. It lies in the bulk (between the orange and red boxes of Fig. 7), not in the "low-entropy" tail, though a clear left bias exists. High entropy does not mean "no position preference".

Exercise 7 — Reading the Gram spectrum ★★

The normalised Gram eigenvalues of 100 atoms sum to 1. (a) What is the flat value? (b) If the largest is 0.15 and the other 99 are equal, what is each, and what is $e^{H}$ of the spectrum ($H=-\sum\lambda\ln\lambda$)? (c) Why is 0.15 not impossible for 100 atoms of mean cosine 0 pairwise?

Solution

(a) $1/100=0.01$. (b) $0.85/99=0.00859$ each; $e^H=75.8$ "effective dimensions" out of 100. (c) Cosines averaging 0 do not mean a flat spectrum: a few strongly correlated pairs and many nearly orthogonal ones still give a top eigenvalue well above 0.01; and for 100 random directions of $\R^{768}$ the top is already about 0.018 (computed). The paper's 0.11–0.16 is about 15 times the flat value.

Exercise 8 — Why Elsewhere concepts help a pooled classifier ★★★

A classifier logit is the mean over 16 patch tokens of $w\,z_p$ for one concept with weight $w=2$. (a) The concept fires with code 1 on the 12 patches off the object. What is the logit contribution? (b) The same concept firing only on the 4 object patches? (c) Only 4 of the 12 off-object patches still fire (the rest are occluded). Explain what pooling does with locations, and what would break if the logit used one token only.

Hint

The mean is invariant under permutations of tokens.

Solution

(a) $2\cdot12/16=1.5$. (b) $2\cdot4/16=0.5$. (c) $2\cdot4/16=0.5$: the loss is proportional to the number of tokens that stop firing. The mean only counts how much evidence there is, not where; so a concept firing on the many off-object tokens contributes more than one on the few object tokens, as long as it fires on them. With one token only (the cls, say) location would matter: an off-object token could not use a concept whose evidence sits elsewhere unless attention carried it, which is precisely what the Elsewhere concepts show it does. Caution: this explains why such concepts can help; the paper does not measure that they do.
