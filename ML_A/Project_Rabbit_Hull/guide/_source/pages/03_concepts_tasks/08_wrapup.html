8. Summary and reflexes

Part I in ten lines
  1. Definition 1 (LRH): $\va=\vz\mD$, with $c\gg d$, unit atoms of low coherence, and at most $k$ non-zero codes.
  2. Operationalised by an archetypal SAE: $\min\norm{\mA-\mZ\mD}_F^2$, $\mZ\ge0$, $\norm{\mZ_i}_0\le8$, $\mD_i\in\conv(\mA)$; $d=768$, $t=261$, $c=32{,}000$, 1.4M images, $R^2>88\%$.
  3. $\mZ$ = statistics, $\mD$ = geometry. ACE, ICE, CRAFT, AA and SAE are the same problem with different constraints (App. B).
  4. A probe $\mW$ becomes $\mW'=\mD\mW\T$ on codes; concept $i$ has score $\E[Z_i]W'_{ij}$ for output $j$ (gradient × input in the concept basis; optimal in the linear case by Thm. 3 of Fel et al. 2023, according to the paper). The paper's $\vphi=\E(\mZ)\mW'$ is the sum of these scores; aggregation over outputs and the dropped reconstruction error are not specified.
  5. Classification uses many concepts; segmentation and depth few (Figs. 2, 3 left). Top-100 concepts of a task are more aligned than random ones and have a faster-decaying Gram spectrum (Fig. 3, App. D, Fig. 19).
  6. "Elsewhere" concepts fire off the object but depend on it (Fig. 4, RISE with 8000 masks): where a concept fires is not what it is about.
  7. Segmentation: border concepts, one tight cluster (Fig. 5). Depth: projective, shadow and frequency families, many mixed (Figs. 6, 18).
  8. Footprint $\vom_i=\frac1N\sum_n\mZ_{n,:,i}$; its entropy runs from 0 (one token) to $\ln261=5.565$; most concepts are near the top, about 110 at 0 (Fig. 7).
  9. Token-type concepts: position-specific, cls-only (text: one; bar chart: about 30), register-only (about 150), the latter about global scene properties (Fig. 8).
  10. Caveats stated by the paper: the depth taxonomy is not exhaustive; our own: evidence is visual and selected.
  • When you see $\va=\vz\mD$ → rows are tokens, atoms are rows of $\mD$, codes are non-negative and sparse.
  • When you see "importance for a linear probe" → $\mW'=\mD\mW\T$, then $\E[Z_i]W'_{ij}$; summing over $i$ must give the mean output.
  • When you see a UMAP or a 2-D PCA of atoms → treat it as an illustration; look for the cosine or spectrum statistic that backs it.
  • When you see a spectrum on a log axis → compare with the flat value $1/n$ and with a random baseline.
  • When you see a concept heat map → ask what it depends on, not only where it fires.
  • When you see an entropy of a footprint → translate it as $\ln$(effective number of tokens).
