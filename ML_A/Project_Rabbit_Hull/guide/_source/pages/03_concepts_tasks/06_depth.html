6. Depth uses monocular cues

Perturbing images in five controlled ways and looking at how the top depth concepts react sorts them into three families — projective geometry, shadows, local frequency transitions — with many concepts in between.

The problem

DINOv2 is trained without depth labels, yet linear probes predict depth well (the paper cites several works). What in its features supports this? The method: take the concepts that matter most for the depth probe, change the image so as to destroy one kind of depth cue at a time, and record how much each concept's activation drops. A concept's vector of five sensitivities is its profile.

Depth and monocular cue concepts §3 · p. 8 and App. C.3 · p. 35

Five manipulations: "local median blurring to suppress shadows, global blur to remove fine details, high-pass filtering to emphasize geometric patterns, soft edge enhancement to retain contours, and geometric warping to distort perspective cues". The profiles are projected with UMAP and give "three coherent clusters": projective geometry (perspective lines, structural convergence), shadow-based (soft lighting gradients), local frequency transitions (abrupt changes of detail or texture). "This taxonomy is not exhaustive: some concepts exhibit mixed sensitivity profiles."

The text and the figure disagree on two details

App. C.3 says that the projective cluster "shows sensitivity to warping and high-pass" and the shadow cluster "primary sensitivity to median filtering". In Fig. 6 the projective bars show warping at only about 0.1, and the shadow bars peak at blur, not at median. We follow the figure; the appendix wording looks like an error. The main text also says median blurring is used "to suppress shadows", yet it is the frequency family that responds to it, and the shadow family responds to global blur.

How to read this figure

Each panel is titled by a concept id; five bars on a 0–1 axis in the order of Fig. 6. Profiles of the three families appear (a single high bar at blur; high at the first two; high at the middle), but many panels have two or three bars of similar height, a few are nearly flat and high (all bars around 0.8–1, e.g. in row 4 and row 5), and a few respond only to warping. The panels are small and the labels hard to read. This is the evidence behind "many concepts exhibit mixed sensitivity profiles". The caption's "systematically organized" is not quantified: no count per family is given.

Link with classical cues. The appendix says the taxonomy "aligns with classical monocular depth cues from visual neuroscience": linear perspective and convergence (projective), shading and shadows, and blur / texture gradients (defocus and frequency). Names only: the paper does not test the link to human perception, and does not cite a particular source here.

Our reading

Perturbations are not clean: a high-pass filter also removes shading and a blur also removes edges, so a profile cannot identify a single cue; the families are best read as "kinds of image statistic". The "three clusters" are the authors' labelling of the UMAP. Page 6.
