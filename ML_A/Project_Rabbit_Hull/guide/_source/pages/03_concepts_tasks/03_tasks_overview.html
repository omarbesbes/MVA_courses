3. What each task uses

Classification draws on many concepts spread over the dictionary; segmentation and depth draw on few, compact groups; and the top-100 concepts of a task are more aligned with each other than random atoms, with a spectrum that decays faster.

The problem

Once every concept has an importance score per task (previous section), one can ask whether tasks share concepts, how many they need, and whether the concepts they need form a recognisable region. The three tasks are read off the labels of Fig. 2: image classification (ImageNet-1k), semantic segmentation (ADE20K) and monocular depth (NYU Depth v2). The paper does not describe the heads here beyond "linear probes"; the classifier works on [cls, mean of patches] (App. C.2).

Classification activates a wide and dispersed array of concepts, while segmentation and depth estimation draw on more compact and localized regions of the concept manifold. How to read this figure
  • Each dot is one atom of $\mD$; its position comes from UMAP (recall), which keeps neighbours close but distorts distances and areas. The same positions are used in all panels; only the colour changes (importance for the task).
  • What to look at: in the classification panel the important atoms are scattered over most of the map; in segmentation and depth they form compact patches.
  • What the authors conclude: functional regions; tasks reuse concepts non-uniformly.
  • What it does not show: UMAP clusters are not proof of low dimension, and the panels depend on the output dimension of each head (see below). The figure carries no number.
How to read this figure, panel by panel
  1. Left — histograms of importance (counts on a log axis). Classification scores run from about 1 to 4.3; segmentation from about 0.1 to 1.1; depth from 0 to about 0.25, with a sharp peak at 0. Order of magnitude reading from the plot, not exact. Depth has a spike of near-zero scores: most concepts are irrelevant for it. The caption says classification "utilizes a larger fraction of the dictionary", "likely reflecting the higher rank of the classification head". Note that the scales differ by a factor of 4 between tasks: scores are comparable within a task, not across tasks.
  2. Middle — cosine similarity of the top-100 concepts per task (kernel density), against all pairs. All pairs: a sharp peak at 0 (density about 9). Classification: peak near 0.01 with a right tail. Segmentation: peak shifted to about 0.07, tail to 0.6. Depth: bimodal, a mode near $-0.08$ and a shoulder at 0.1–0.25. Two tasks have atoms that point in similar directions (positive cosines); depth has also anti-aligned ones. Reference: for 100 random directions in $\R^{768}$ cosines have standard deviation $1/\sqrt{768}=0.036$, whose Gaussian density peak is $\approx11$; the paper's all-pairs peak (about 9) is a little lower, i.e. the real dictionary is a bit broader than random.
  3. Right — Gram eigenvalues of the 100 top atoms, normalised to sum to 1, log scale, first 50 indices. The tasks start at 0.11–0.16 and fall to about 0.003–0.004; the random subset starts at about 0.047 and falls to about 0.009. A flat spectrum (100 orthogonal directions) would be $1/100=0.01$ everywhere; one direction would give 1. So 0.11–0.16 means one direction carries 11–16% of the energy. Script check: 100 random unit vectors in $\R^{768}$ give a top value of about 0.018 and a minimum about 0.004 (Marchenko–Pastur), so even Gaussian directions are not exactly flat; the real random subset (0.047) is much steeper than that, because the dictionary is not isotropic (its own singular values decay, page 4).

What "low-dimensional functional subspace" means

The paper's claim: the 100 atoms most used by a task nearly lie in a subspace of small dimension. Quantitatively, if the normalised Gram eigenvalues are $\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\dots$ with $\sum\lambda_l=1$, a fast decay means that a few directions carry most of the energy of these atoms. "Functional" refers to the task that selects them. It is a statement about 100 selected atoms, not about the task's weight vectors.

Four scatter plots, each a PCA (recall) of its own 500 atoms: the axes are the two top principal directions of that subset, so shapes are compared, not positions. What to look at: scatter (classification), arc (segmentation), two groups (depth, echoing the bimodal cosine density of Fig. 3), blob (random). What it does not show: a 2-D PCA of 500 points of a 768-dimensional space can show an arc even if the variance in the other directions is large; the percentage of variance explained is not given. The bimodality of depth is later matched to the families of Fig. 6.

Caveats to keep in mind
  • The output dimension differs across probes. The classification head has one row per class, so more outputs, so more chances for a concept to score high on some output; the paper itself attributes the breadth to "the higher rank of the classification head". Comparing "how many concepts" across tasks therefore partly compares heads. How scores are merged over outputs is not stated (section 2).
  • Top-100 and top-500 are fixed cut-offs. Cosines and spectra are computed on a selection made by the importance score; a selection by any score tends to pick related atoms.
  • UMAP and PCA pictures are visual; the quantitative support is Fig. 3 middle and right.
  • The random baseline is not flat. A fair reference for "decays fast" is the random subset (0.047 to 0.009), not the flat line 0.01. Even the random subset's first eigenvalue is 4.7 times the flat value.
Our reading

The three panels agree with each other, but none compares with a task-independent selection of 100 atoms chosen to be correlated with each other (for instance neighbours of one atom). That control is the natural one to separate "task-specific" from "any selection of mutually similar atoms". See page 6.
