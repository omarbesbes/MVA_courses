1. The working hypothesis and how it is made operational

The paper assumes that a token activation is a combination of at most $k=8$ non-negative multiples of unit vectors chosen among $c=32{,}000$ almost-orthogonal atoms, and finds those atoms with an archetypal sparse autoencoder trained on 1.4M images.

The problem

A ViT has 768 neurons per token, but images contain far more than 768 things. Neurons are polysemantic: one neuron responds to several unrelated things. The paper's answer is to look for directions instead of neurons, and to ask that each activation uses only a few of them. This is the linear representation hypothesis (LRH), whose roots (superposition) are recalled on page 1.

Definition 1 — Linear Representation Hypothesis §2 · p. 5

A representation $\va\in\R^d$ satisfies the LRH if there exist a sparsity constant $k$, an overcomplete dictionary $\mD=(\vd_1,\dots,\vd_c)\in\R^{c\times d}$ and a coefficient vector $\vz\in\R^c$ such that $\va=\vz\mD$, under the conditions:

$$\text{(i) } c\gg d,\qquad \text{(ii) } \mu(\mD)\equiv\max_{i\ne j}\abs{\mD_i\mD_j\T}\le\eps,\ \ \norm{\mD_i}_2=1,\qquad \text{(iii) } \abs{\supp(\vz)}\le k\ll c .$$
$\va=\vz\mD$
row-vector convention: $\va$ and $\vz$ are rows, $\mD$ has one atom per row. So $\va=\sum_i z_i\mD_i$, a combination of atoms.
(i) $c\gg d$
overcompleteness: more atoms than dimensions ($c/d\approx41.7$ here). It allows one atom per concept rather than one per dimension. A consequence is that the atoms cannot be orthogonal and the decomposition $\va=\vz\mD$ is not unique (see the toy example below).
(ii) $\mu(\mD)\le\eps$
quasi-orthogonality: the coherence $\mu$ is the largest absolute cosine between two different atoms (unit norm). Small $\eps$ means that atoms interfere little, so a sparse code can be read back from $\va$. Recall: Gram matrix and coherence. The definition does not give a value of $\eps$.
(iii) $\abs{\supp(\vz)}\le k\ll c$
sparsity: at most $k$ non-zero codes. This is what makes the decomposition meaningful despite overcompleteness.

The paper motivates (ii) by compression: arranging $c$ directions with minimal coherence is the problem of spherical codes (Tammes, Thomson), formalised by Grassmannian frames (Welch bound and Grassmannian frames). Definition 1 does not require non-negative codes: the non-negativity $\mZ\ge0$ comes later, with the SAE.

A worked example by hand

Example — $d=2$, three atoms, one active code

Take $c=3$ unit atoms in $\R^2$: $\mD_1=(1,0)$, $\mD_2=(0,1)$, $\mD_3=(1,1)/\sqrt2$. The off-diagonal entries of $\mD\mD\T$ are $0,\ 0.7071,\ 0.7071$, so $\mu(\mD)=0.7071$. Overcomplete ($3>2$) but far from "quasi-orthogonal": a toy example cannot be both, since in the plane three directions have at least coherence $0.5$ (the Welch bound $\sqrt{(c-d)/(d(c-1))}=0.5$; for the real dictionary it is $0.0356$).

Take the code $\vz=(1.5,\,0,\,2)$, with support size $2\le k$. Then $$\va=\vz\mD=1.5\,(1,0)+2\,(0.7071,0.7071)=(2.9142,\ 1.4142).$$ Non-uniqueness: the same $\va$ is also $\vz'=(2.9142,\,1.4142,\,0)$, a code on atoms 1 and 2 only. Both have support size 2. Sparsity alone does not choose between them; the SAE's encoder and its objective do.

The optimisation problem

The factorisation problem §2 · p. 5

For a batch of $n$ images, concatenating all tokens gives $\mA\in\R^{nt\times d}$. Find codes $\mZ\in\R^{nt\times c}$ and a dictionary $\mD\in\R^{c\times d}$ with

$$\min_{\mZ,\mD}\ \norm{\mA-\mZ\mD}_F^2\quad\text{subject to}\quad \mZ\ge0,\ \ \norm{\mZ_i}_0\le k,\ \ \mD_i\in\conv(\mA).$$
$\norm{\mA-\mZ\mD}_F^2$
sum of squared reconstruction errors over all tokens: fidelity.
$\mZ\ge0$
entrywise: concepts only add. A concept is "present with intensity $z_i$" and never "negatively present".
$\norm{\mZ_i}_0\le k$
row $i$ of $\mZ$ (one token) has at most $k$ non-zero entries. $\norm{\cdot}_0$ counts non-zeros.
$\mD_i\in\conv(\mA)$
each atom is a convex combination of real activations. This is the archetypal constraint: atoms stay inside the data cloud, which makes the dictionary stable across training runs (the paper's reason for choosing it). In practice $\conv(\mA)$ is replaced by the hull of 128,000 k-means centroids $\mC$ and $\mD=\mS\mC$ with $\mS$ row-stochastic (non-negative rows summing to 1). Note that in Definition 1 the atoms are unit vectors; this problem does not state a normalisation. The paper does not say how the two are reconciled.
The exact configuration §2 · p. 5
768$d$: width of DINOv2-B
261$t$: 1 cls + 4 registers + 256 patches
32,000$c$: atoms

Codes come from a single-layer encoder followed by a BatchTopK projection; training uses Adam for 50 epochs. So the dictionary is fitted on $1.4\text{M}\times261\approx3.65\times10^8$ tokens.

What these numbers say: each token, a point of $\R^{768}$, is rebuilt from 8 atoms ($k/c=0.025\%$ of the dictionary), and $R^2>88\%$ means about 12% of the squared norm (after centring, as usual for $R^2$; the paper does not say) is left unexplained. The $R^2$ is the only fidelity number given.

What $\mZ$ and $\mD$ mean: statistics and geometry

Interpretation of $\mZ$ and $\mD$ §2 · p. 5

$\mZ$ encodes the statistical structure of the activation space: which concepts are active, how often, to what degree. $\mD$ encodes the geometric structure: the atomic directions that span the space. The result is a library of 32,000 concept atoms, "each interpretable as a linear probe on DINOv2 activations".

This split organises the paper: page 3 (this one) uses $\mZ$ and $\mD$ together through a probe; page 4 studies $\mZ$ (co-activation) and $\mD$ (inner products) separately; page 5 replaces the linear picture. "Each atom is a linear probe" means: the score of atom $i$ on a token is the inner product with $\mD_i$. Because the atoms are not orthogonal, this score is not the code $z_i$ the encoder produces.

One problem, five methods (App. B)

Dictionary learning with method-specific constraints App. B · p. 33

$(\mZ^\star,\mD^\star)=\argmin\norm{\mA-\mZ\mD}_F^2$ with, depending on the method, the constraints of the table.

MethodConstraint in the paperIn words
ACE — k-means$\mZ_i\in\{\ve_1,\dots,\ve_k\}$each token is assigned to one cluster; the atom is its centroid (one-hot code)
ICE — PCA$\mD\mD\T=\mI$orthonormal atoms; at most $d$ of them
CRAFT — NMF$\mZ\ge0,\ \mD\ge0$parts-based: both factors non-negative
AA — archetypal analysis$\mZ_i\in\simplex^{c},\ \mD_i\in\conv(\mA)$each token is a convex mix of archetypes that lie in the data hull
SAE$\mZ=\Psi_\theta(\mA),\ \norm{\mZ}_0\le K$codes come from an encoder $\Psi_\theta$ (TopK, JumpReLU, ReLU…); amortised, batched

Reading the table: moving down, constraints on $\mZ$ go from "one atom" (k-means) to "few atoms" (SAE), and constraints on $\mD$ from "orthonormal" to "inside the data". The paper's model combines the last two rows: an SAE encoder with archetypal atoms and non-negative sparse codes. The paper writes $\ve_1,\dots,\ve_k$ for the k-means row where one would expect the $K$ cluster indicators; read $k$ there as the number of clusters. In this appendix $\simplex^c$ is the simplex of $\R^c$ (simplex). Approaches trade fidelity $\norm{\mA-\mZ\mD}_F$ against sparsity $\norm{\mZ}_0$ along a Pareto frontier.

Our reading — what the definition leaves open

$\eps$ is never given a value, and Fig. 10 of the paper (page 4) shows inner products up to $\pm0.9$ in the trained dictionary. So condition (ii) is a motivation, not a property verified for this dictionary. See page 6.

Why can the same activation have several valid codes in the toy example, and what chooses among them in practice?

Answer

With $c>d$ the map $\vz\mapsto\vz\mD$ from $\R^c$ to $\R^d$ has a kernel, so many codes give the same $\va$. In the example $(1.5,0,2)$ and $(2.9142,1.4142,0)$ both have two non-zeros. In practice the trained encoder $\Psi_\theta$ returns one code per token (the $k$ largest after BatchTopK), so the choice is made by the architecture and the training objective.
