Before you start#

Part II of the paper has no theorem. It measures things: how often and how strongly concepts fire, how the atoms sit relative to each other, and what the tokens of one image look like. The tone is descriptive; the conclusion ("beyond the LRH") is a reading of those measurements, and we mark where it goes further than they do.

How to read this page#

Each section starts with a one-line summary, quotes the paper in a paper box with the page of the arXiv v3 PDF, and decodes it. Every figure of the paper is shown and followed by a "How to read this figure" box. Remarks of ours are in Our reading boxes and are developed on page 6. Numbers that are not in the paper (marked "our estimate" or "recomputed") come from checks/check_04.py.

The story of the page in seven steps#

  1. Occurrence. Most concepts fire rarely; three concepts about position fire on about a quarter of all tokens (Fig. 9 A–C).
  2. Co-activation. The Gram matrix Z⊤Z has a smooth spectrum, between two baselines (Fig. 9 right).
  3. Geometry of the atoms. Inner products have heavy tails, singular values decay fast, atoms are distributed over many coordinates (Fig. 10).
  4. Antipodal pairs, and usage versus geometry. Some atoms are almost opposite; co-activation explains little of the geometry (Fig. 11).
  5. The shape of an image. PCA maps of the patch tokens of one image are smooth and follow objects (Figs. 12, 13, 22).
  6. Position. A position basis that is high-rank in early layers and two-dimensional in late ones; smoothness survives its removal (Figs. 14, 15, 27).
  7. Interpolative geometry. A conjecture that tokens are mixtures of prototypes; page 5 turns it into the Minkowski Representation Hypothesis.

Two sizes need care. Z has one row per token, over all images: with 1.4M images that is about 3.65×108 rows (our estimate: 1.4×106×261). The matrix G is therefore a sum over more than 3×108 tokens, and the paper's "number of firings" counts tokens, not images.

1. How often and how strongly concepts fire#

For almost every concept, the rarer it is, the higher its energy when it fires (a triangular envelope in Fig. 9 A). Three concepts break the pattern: they fire on roughly a quarter of all tokens and encode position (left, right, bottom).

The problem#

The LRH says a token uses few concepts out of many. That is a statement about the support of the code. It says nothing about two other things one can measure directly on Z: how often each concept is on (its frequency), and how large it is when on (its energy). If the dictionary behaved like a pure sparse code, we would expect every concept to be rare. The paper looks for exceptions.

What the paper plots#

How to read this figure
  • Axes. Horizontal: firing count (the bulk is below about 2×106, the outliers are near 0.95–1.15×108). Vertical: conditional energy, from 0 to about 5.
  • The bulk. A triangle: at low counts the energies are spread from small to large values; as the count grows, the largest energies disappear. Frequent concepts are weak when they fire.
  • The three outliers. Energy about 1.2–1.3, count about 108: far right, at a modest height. Panels B and C show them on example images: they light up a side of the image (left, right, bottom) regardless of the content.
  • What the authors conclude. Concepts live in different energy regimes, and the code is a hybrid: sparse selective concepts plus a few universal positional ones.
  • What the figure does not show. The shape of the triangle is not explained (no model, no axis scale given for the energy of the typical token); and it says nothing about which concepts sit in the bulk.

How dense is "dense"? A back-of-envelope estimate#

Our estimate — from the numbers of the paper

The paper trains on 1.4M ImageNet images (t=261 tokens each) and reads outlier counts of about 0.95 to 1.15×108 off the figure. The following uses these two readings; the exact counts are not in the text.

  1. Tokens: 1.4×106×261≈3.65×108.a row of Z per token.
  2. An outlier firing 0.95–1.15×108 times is active on 26% to 31% of all tokens.ratio of the count to the number of tokens.
  3. A typical concept fires fewer than 2×106 times, i.e. on less than 0.55% of the tokens.the same ratio, using the right edge of the bulk.
  4. Total activations: k×3.65×108≈2.9×109 (if every token uses exactly its k=8 slots).BatchTopK keeps k per token on average, so this is an average count.
  5. Three outliers at 1.05×108 each take 3.15×108 activations: about 11% of all activations (range 10–12%).3.15×108/2.9×109.
  6. The mean concept fires 2.9×109/32,000≈9×104 times, so the three outliers fire about 1000 times more often than the average one.1.05×108/9.1×104≈1150.
Why an always-on feature is awkward for a sparse code

A code with k=8 active entries per token has a budget of 8 slots per token. A concept that is on in 29% of the tokens occupies one of those slots in 29% of the tokens; three such concepts occupy 3×0.287≈0.86 slots per token on average (our estimate; the three are probably not independent, since "left" and "right" cannot hold at once). So about one slot in nine is not spent on image content but on position. More basically, a feature that is almost always on carries little information per token about the token: it behaves like a bias or a slowly varying coordinate, which is what a dense low-rank component is. The paper's analogy is "sparse plus dense low-rank" representations.

Interactive figure (needs JavaScript).

What to look at: start with 0 dense concepts: all dots in the triangle. Add three and move their frequency: the number of slots they take per token and their share of all activations follow the arithmetic above. Increase k: the same dense concepts take a smaller share. The cloud is a toy (our generator, not the paper's data); only the arithmetic of the readout is general.

What the paper links to

The text connects the three outliers to two other results: positional information is linearly decodable up to intermediate layers (section 8, Fig. 14) and forms a 2-dimensional subspace at the penultimate layer. A 2-D position subspace fits with three dense concepts: "left" and "right" are two opposite ends of the horizontal coordinate and "bottom" is one end of the vertical one (see the antipodal section for why one signed coordinate needs two non-negative atoms).

Our reading — "dense" is a threshold, and the triangle is not explained

The paper does not give a definition of dense, nor the exact counts. The triangle is plausible from a norm budget (tokens have a roughly bounded norm, so a concept cannot be both frequent and large), but this is our intuition, not a result. Also, the measured quantity is per token over all token types: it does not say whether the three concepts fire on registers and cls. See page 6.

A concept fires on 0.3% of the tokens with mean code 3. Another fires on 30% with mean code 1.2. Which has the larger E[Zi​], and which the larger conditional energy?

Answer

E[Z]=frequency×conditional energy: 0.003×3=0.009 against 0.3×1.2=0.36. The dense one has 40 times the mean code, the rare one a 2.5 times larger conditional energy. That is why the paper plots conditional energy against the count: E[Z] would mix the two.

Reflex

When you see "mean activation" of a sparse code → ask whether zeros are included. E[Zi​] includes them, E[Zi​∣Zi​>0] does not.

2. Co-activation: the Gram matrix of the codes#

The matrix G=Z⊤Z counts, for each pair of concepts, how strongly they fire together. Its spectrum decays smoothly: no gap, no dominant mode. The paper reads this as "no block structure"; against its two baselines the evidence is weak.

The problem#

If concepts came in modules (all "dog" concepts together, all "texture" concepts together), tokens would use whole modules at once and the co-activation matrix would be block-diagonal, with a few large eigenvalues (one per module) and a gap after them. The question is whether G looks like that.

Concepts co-occurrence §5 · p. 11–12, Fig. 9 right · p. 11

"The Gram matrix of concept co-activation G=Z⊤Z∈Rc×c [...] has a spectrum that decays smoothly, with no gaps or dominant modes, providing little evidence for modular low-rank structure. As reference points, we create two baselines, one from random activations Z with same sparsity (random), and one directly shuffling the coactivation matrix G (shuffle). Relative to baselines, it lies between the steep decay of the random surrogate and the inflated tail of the shuffled one. Below both would imply redundancy, above both extreme diffuseness; its intermediate position instead reflects a high-dimensional, distributed regime where concepts co-activate without forming clear clusters."

What G is and what its spectrum can show#

  1. Gij​=∑n​Zn,i​Zn,j​ entry of Z⊤Z: sum over tokens n of the product of the codes of i and j. Non-zero only for tokens where both are on.
  2. Gii​=∑n​Zn,i2​ diagonal: total squared energy of concept i. Frequent and strong concepts have large diagonals.
  3. G⪰0, so its eigenvalues are ≥0 and ∑i​λi​=trG=∑i​Gii​ G=Z⊤Z is a Gram matrix; the trace is the sum of eigenvalues.
  4. If the concepts form m disjoint modules that never co-fire across modules, G is block-diagonal with m blocks, so its eigenvalues are the union of those of the blocks block-diagonal matrices decompose; a module whose members co-fire a lot gives one large eigenvalue.

So block structure shows up as a few large eigenvalues followed by a drop. A smooth decay with no drop means no such separation. Note what this cannot tell: dense positional concepts (section 1) have huge diagonal entries and co-fire with everything, which tends to create a few large eigenvalues; the paper does not discuss this.

The two baselines of Appendix E, formula by formula#

Baselines for the co-activation spectrum App. E · p. 37

Random baseline: Rij​=Uij​⋅1(Vij​<ρ) with Uij​,Vij​∼U(0,1), then R~=​R+R⊤​F​(R+R⊤)/2​⋅​Z⊤Z​F​, with diag(R~)=diag(Z⊤Z). Shuffled baseline: take the upper-triangular entries {Gij​:i<j}, permute them randomly, and put them back symmetrically, keeping the diagonal of G.

ρ
the density of G (fraction of non-zero entries); the paper says "identical sparsity ρ" without a formula.
Uij​, Vij​
U draws the value, V decides whether the entry exists: each entry is non-zero with probability ρ and then uniform in (0,1). Independent over pairs: no pair is special.
(R+R⊤)/2
symmetrisation, so that eigenvalues are real.
⋅ ​Z⊤Z​F​/​R+R⊤​F​
rescaling so that the total mass (Frobenius norm) matches. Since ​(R+R⊤)/2​F​=21​​R+R⊤​F​, the formula as written gives a Frobenius norm of 21​∥G∥F​ (checked in check_04.py); a global factor changes nothing on a normalised spectrum, so this is only a typo-level remark.
shuffle
keeps three things: the diagonal, the multiset of off-diagonal values, symmetry. Destroys: which pair gets which value.
What each baseline is a null for
  • Random: "same density and same total mass, but co-occurrences without any structure". Its spectrum has no outstanding modes.
  • Shuffled: "same values, scrambled pairs". Because ∑i​λi2​=∥G∥F2​ and ∑i​λi​=trG are the same for G and for the shuffle, the two spectra share total mass and total squared mass. If the original has structure, its energy is concentrated in fewer eigenvalues, so the shuffle (spread out) has the heavier tail. On a toy with 20 planted blocks (our simulation, check_04.py) the tenth eigenvalue is 0.59 of the first for G and 0.31 for its shuffle: structure gives a slower head and a lighter tail, which is the picture the paper describes.
How to read this figure
  • Axes. Horizontal: eigenvalue rank, 1 to 500. Vertical: normalised eigenvalue, log scale.
  • What to look at. No cliff in the original curve; it lies between the random curve (steeper) and the shuffled one (heavier tail).
  • Numbers. The three curves stay within a factor of about 1.3 of each other over the plotted range (our reading of the figure).
  • The authors' conclusion. Smooth and intermediate: co-activation is high-dimensional and distributed, without modules.
  • What it does not show. A test of the module hypothesis; only that the spectrum is not extreme in either direction.
Our reading — a factor 1.3 on a log axis is a weak signal

A log-scale plot makes small gaps look like gaps. The three curves differ by a factor of about 1.3, and no variability across random draws or subsamples is shown. "Between two baselines" is also what any matrix with partial structure would do. A better test is a statistic with a null distribution (for instance the top-eigenvalue share against many shuffles). See page 6.

Reflex

When you see "the spectrum lies between two baselines" → ask what each baseline preserves, and by how much the curves differ on a linear scale.

3. Geometry of the dictionary#

The 32,000 atoms are not spread like random vectors and not like a Grassmannian frame: inner products have heavy tails (up to about 0.9), singular values decay fast (the dictionary is anisotropic), and atoms are spread over many coordinates (Hoyer score about 0.22–0.25).

The problem and the reference points#

The LRH (Definition 1, page 3) asks for unit atoms with small coherence. "Small" needs a scale. The paper builds two reference dictionaries with the same c=32,000 and d=768.

Baselines for concept geometry App. F · p. 38

"Random vectors on sphere: we sample a Gaussian random matrix H∈Rc×d, Hij​∼N(0,1) i.i.d., and then we normalized each row to have L2 norm 1. [...] Grassmannian frame: [...] we numerically computed the Grassmannian frame of c atoms in d dimension. [...] we adapted the algorithm from recent work TAAP, [...] adapted it to CUDA [...] solving the frame once still takes 6hr on an A100 GPU. In the end, we reached maximal coherence of 0.065897."

Reminder — coherence, Welch bound, Grassmannian frames

Coherence of unit vectors: μ(D)=maxi=j​∣⟨Di​,Dj​⟩∣. For c>d unit vectors in Rd, the Welch bound says μ≥d(c−1)c−d​​; a Grassmannian frame is a set of c unit vectors minimising μ (it reaches the bound only for special (c,d)). more

Recomputed — the numbers behind the baselines
  1. Welch bound for c=32,000, d=768: (32000−768)/(768×31999)​≈0.0356. plug in; no frame can be below this.
  2. The computed Grassmannian frame has μ=0.0659: 1.85 times the Welch bound. 0.065897/0.03565. The bound is not tight for these sizes, so the numerical frame is only an approximation of the optimum (as the paper says).
  3. Random unit vectors: an inner product is approximately N(0,1/d), standard deviation 1/768​=0.0361. the inner product of two independent uniform points on the sphere has variance 1/d.
  4. There are c(c−1)/2≈5.1×108 pairs. The largest of P approximately Gaussian values is about 2lnP/d​=0.229. Gaussian tail: Pe−dμ2/2≈1. Matches the paper's "random within ±0.2" (a sample of 4000 atoms gives 0.196 in check_04.py).

(B) Singular values: tight frame, Marchenko–Pastur, anisotropy#

Reminder — tight frames and the spectrum of random matrices

D∈Rc×d with unit rows has ∑i≤d​σi2​=∥D∥F2​=c. If all singular values are equal, σi​=c/d​ and the rows form a tight frame (D⊤D=dc​I). For a random matrix with i.i.d. rows, the singular values fall in the Marchenko–Pastur band c/d​(1±d/c​). more

  1. Tight-frame level: c/d​=32000/768​=6.455. ∑σ2=c spread evenly over d values.
  2. Marchenko–Pastur band: 6.455×[1−0.155,1+0.155]=[5.45,7.45]. d/c​=0.155. A simulated random 32000×768 dictionary gives [5.47,7.45], matching the figure's "7.4→5.5".
  3. The Grassmannian frame is (almost) tight: flat at ≈6.5. minimal coherence goes with a nearly flat spectrum.
  4. The SAE: σ1​≈22.5, so σ12​≈506, i.e. 12 times the average c/d=41.7 and 1.6% of the total mass in one direction. 22.52/41.7=12.2 and 506/32000=1.6%.
Anisotropy

A set of atoms is anisotropic when their mass is not spread evenly over directions: some directions of Rd carry much more squared mass σ2 than others. Isotropic means all σi2​ equal to c/d. Here: a few directions hold much more mass, and the tail of singular values goes to almost 0, i.e. some directions of the space are practically unused by the dictionary.

Interactive figure (needs JavaScript).

What to look at: at 0 the atoms are uniform on the sphere and the singular values fill the Marchenko–Pastur band. Raise anisotropy: the first singular values grow above the band and the last fall below it, while ∑σ2 stays equal to c. Both are small-size versions (c=160, d=32) of the 32000×768 case.

Global anisotropy: two tentative explanations §5 · p. 12 and footnote

"One potential explanation is that the activations A themselves have anisotropic covariance, thus to reconstruct them A≈ZD with L2 error, more atoms are allocated to the more important and higher variance subspace." Footnote: "A speculative hypothesis is that the affine components of the last LayerNorm deforms the activation space into an ellipsoidal geometry, locally increasing curvature along some directions. In such regions, dictionary learning may require more atoms to adequately cover the manifold with low reconstruction error. While this remains to be formalized [...]"

  • First explanation. The atoms are in conv(A) (archetypal SAE) and the loss is ∥A−ZD∥F2​; high-variance directions of A matter more for the loss, so more atoms point there. This is checkable (compare the spectrum of D with the covariance spectrum of A); the paper does not do it.
  • Second explanation. After the last LayerNorm, LN(x)=γ⊙u+β with u centred and scaled to norm d​. If the entries of γ are non-zero, the output lies on an ellipsoid. The paper calls this speculative and unformalised.
Our reading — the two explanations are not exclusive, and the second is a real constraint

Both can hold: the ellipsoid shape is itself a source of anisotropic covariance. We test the ellipsoid claim in our experiments (page 6); it also bears on the Minkowski hypothesis of page 5.

(C–D) Hoyer scores: atoms are distributed#

Reminder — Hoyer sparsity

For x∈Rn, Hoyer(x)=n​−1n​−∥x∥1​/∥x∥2​​∈[0,1]: 1 for a one-hot vector, 0 for a constant one. more

For a Gaussian vector, ∥x∥1​/∥x∥2​≈2n/π​, so the score tends to 1−2/π​=0.202 (simulation at n=768: 0.210). An atom aligned with one neuron would score near 1; a vector with 77 equal non-zero entries (10% of 768) scores 0.71. The SAE atoms score 0.22–0.25: almost as spread as random vectors, slightly sparser. So each atom is a combination of many of the 768 coordinates: a distributed representation, not neuron-aligned.

Our reading — Hoyer depends on the basis

The score is measured in the coordinate basis of the residual stream, which the model has no reason to privilege except through LayerNorm's per-coordinate γ,β (and known high-norm coordinates in some ViTs). A score close to that of Gaussian vectors says the atoms are not axis-aligned; it does not say they are "as random as" Gaussian ones (they are not, by panels A and B).

Johnson–Lindenstrauss footnote §5 · p. 12

"The Johnson–Lindenstrauss (JL) lemma implies that p=exp(O(mε2)) vectors can be embedded into Rm such that all pairwise inner products are preserved up to a small distortion ε>0. This supports the feasibility of near-orthogonality even in regimes where p≫m."

Decoded: with m=d=768 dimensions one can place exponentially many (exp(O(768ε2))) unit vectors with all ∣⟨⋅,⋅⟩∣≤ε. Equivalently d≳ε−2lnc: with lnc=10.4, near-orthogonality at the 0.2 level needs a few hundred dimensions. So the LRH's "overcomplete but quasi-orthogonal" is feasible, and the paper's point is that the learned dictionary does not use this freedom (it prefers "structured redundancy"). more

What the paper concludes, and what to keep

The paper: the dictionary is "neither maximally incoherent nor uniformly distributed", with clusters, reduced effective rank, and groups of atoms tied to tasks (page 3). Keep apart: (A) is a statement about pairs (some are far above 0.066); (B) about the global spectrum; (C–D) about coordinates. None of them alone contradicts the LRH, since the LRH (Def. 1) only asks for small coherence ε to be some number; the paper's argument is that μ≈0.9 is not small.

Why must the singular values of a unit-row dictionary with c=32000, d=768 have mean square 41.7, whatever its geometry?

Answer

∑i=1d​σi2​=∥D∥F2​=∑j​∥Dj​∥2=c since rows are unit. Divide by d: c/d=41.7. A larger first singular value must be paid by smaller ones.

Reflex

When you see "the singular values decay" for a dictionary → compare with c/d​ and the Marchenko–Pastur band, not with a flat line at 1.
