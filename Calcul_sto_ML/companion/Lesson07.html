“How do smooth maps and changes of probability transform a semimartingale, and which part (drift or noise) changes?”

§7.1 Itô's formula

notes, pp. 60–62

The problem. If \(X\) is a semimartingale and \(f\) is smooth, is \(f(X)\) a semimartingale, and what are its drift and its martingale part? This is the chain rule of stochastic calculus. It is the tool that turns every computation of the course into algebra.

The intuition. Taylor: \(f(X_{t+h})-f(X_t)\approx f'(X_t)\Delta X+\frac12f''(X_t)(\Delta X)^2\). For a smooth path, \((\Delta X)^2=O(h^2)\) and the second term disappears. For a semimartingale, \((\Delta X)^2\approx\Delta\br{X}\), of order \(h\), and the terms add up to \(\frac12\int f''(X)\,\dd\br{X}\). Informally: \(\dd B\sim\sqrt{\dd t}\), so \((\dd B)^2=\dd t\).

Theorem 7.1 (Itô's formula)notes pp. 60–61

(1) If \(X\) is a real continuous semimartingale and \(f\in C^2(\R)\), then \(f(X)\) is a semimartingale and, a.s. for every \(t\), \[ f(X_t)=f(X_0)+\int_0^tf'(X_s)\,\dd X_s+\frac12\int_0^tf''(X_s)\,\dd\br{X}_s. \] (2) If \(X=(X^1,\dots,X^d)\) has continuous semimartingale components and \(F\in C^2(\R^d)\), then \[ \begin{aligned}F(X_t)=F(X_0)&+\sum_i\int_0^t\partial_iF(X_s)\,\dd X^i_s\\ &+\frac12\sum_{i,j}\int_0^t\partial^2_{ij}F(X_s)\,\dd\br{X^i,X^j}_s.\end{aligned} \]

In one sentenceChain rule plus half the second derivative times the bracket.
Proof (the notes' route)Taylor with integral remainder on each cell: \(f(X_{t_{i+1}})-f(X_{t_i})=f'(X_{t_i})\Delta_iX+I_i(\Delta_iX)^2\), with \(I_i=\int_0^1f''(X_{t_i}+u\Delta_iX)(1-u)\,\dd u\). Sum over \(i\). The first sum converges in probability to \(\int f'(X)\,\dd X\) (Prop. 6.18). Replace \(I_i\) by \(\frac12f''(X_{t_i})\): the error is at most \(\max_i|I_i-\frac12f''(X_{t_i})|\cdot\sum(\Delta X)^2\to0\), by uniform continuity of \(f''\) on the compact range of the path. The remaining sum tends to \(\frac12\int f''(X)\,\dd\br{X}\) (Prop. 5.44).
Where it failsIf \(f\) is only \(C^1\), or \(f''\) is not continuous, the formula needs modification. For \(f(x)=|x|\), Tanaka's formula adds a local time (Beyond the notes; see Lesson 6, E5). If \(X\) has jumps, extra jump terms appear.
Used againEverywhere: Lévy's characterization, exponential martingales, Girsanov, the linear SDE (Example 8.1), Tanaka's SDE (Example 8.16).

Remark 7.2 (notation): \(\sum_i\int\partial_iF(X)\,\dd X^i=\int\nabla F(X)^\top\,\dd X\).

Remark 7.3 (diffusions)notes p. 60

If \(X_t=x+\int_0^tb_s\,\dd s+\int_0^t\sigma_s\,\dd B_s\), with \(B\) a \(d\)-dimensional Brownian motion and \((\int\sigma\,\dd B)^i=\sum_j\int\sigma^{ij}\,\dd B^j\), then \(\br{X^i,X^j}_t=\sum_k\int_0^t\sigma^{ik}_s\sigma^{jk}_s\,\dd s\). With \(a_s=\sigma_s\sigma_s^\top\): \[ \begin{aligned}F(X_t)=F(X_0)&+\int_0^t\nabla F(X_s)^\top b_s\,\dd s\\ &+\int_0^t\nabla F(X_s)^\top\sigma_s\,\dd B_s\\ &+\frac12\int_0^t\tr\big(a_s\nabla^2F(X_s)\big)\,\dd s.\end{aligned} \]

Watch out: a missing ½ in Remark 7.3 (p. 60)

The last display of Remark 7.3 in the notes reads \(\cdots+\int_0^t\tr(a_s\nabla^2F(X_s))\,\dd s\), without the factor \(\frac12\), although the display just above it has \(\frac12\int\tr(\sigma\sigma^\top\nabla^2F)\,\dd s\). The correct formula has \(\frac12\), as above. Check: for \(F(x)=|x|^2\) and \(X=B\) in \(\R^d\), \(\E|B_t|^2=dt\) forces the drift term to be \(\frac12\tr(I\cdot2I)\,t=dt\). The opening phrase of the remark, “if \((X_t)\) is a diffusion, i.e. if \(\xi\) is bounded on \([0,1]\)”, is also garbled; the intended hypothesis is that \(b\) and \(\sigma\) are progressive and locally bounded.

Remark 7.4: if \(X\) takes values in an open set \(O\), \(f\in C^2(O)\) suffices. Example: \(\log X\) for a positive semimartingale, or \(1/|x|\) on \(\R^3\setminus\{0\}\) (Lesson 5, Lab 5.3).

Proposition 7.5 · Remark 7.6 (time-dependent Itô formula)notes p. 61

Finite-variation components contribute no second-order terms. In particular, for \(F(t,x)\) of class \(C^{1,2}\) and \(X\) as in Remark 7.3: \[ \begin{aligned}F(t,X_t)&=F(0,X_0)+\int_0^t\nabla F(s,X_s)^\top\sigma_s\,\dd B_s\\ &\quad+\int_0^t\big(\partial_sF+\nabla F^\top b_s\big)(s,X_s)\,\dd s\\ &\quad+\frac12\int_0^t\tr\big(a_s\nabla^2_xF(s,X_s)\big)\,\dd s.\end{aligned} \]

(Remark 7.6 is the displayed formula; the notes state it with the \(\frac12\), correctly.)

Beyond the notes: the generator, and why Langevin samples \(\pi\)

For a time-homogeneous diffusion \(\dd X=b(X)\,\dd t+\sigma(X)\,\dd B\), Remark 7.6 says that \(f(X_t)-\int_0^t\mathcal Lf(X_s)\,\dd s\) is a local martingale. Here \(\mathcal L=b\cdot\nabla+\frac12\tr(\sigma\sigma^\top\nabla^2)\) is the generator. For Brownian motion it is \(\frac12\Delta\), and this is Prop. 2.8 (iii) again. If \(u(t,x)\) solves \(\partial_tu=\mathcal Lu\), then \(u(T-t,X_t)\) is a local martingale: the Kolmogorov equation announced in the title of the notes' §8.3.

For the Langevin diffusion of the board, \(\dd X=-\nabla U\,\dd t+\sqrt2\,\dd B\), so \(\mathcal Lf=\Delta f-\nabla U\cdot\nabla f=e^{U}\nabla\cdot(e^{-U}\nabla f)\). Integrating by parts, \(\int\mathcal Lf\,e^{-U}\,\dd x=0\) for every test function \(f\). That makes \(\E_\pi[\mathcal Lf]=0\), which is exactly the statement that \(\pi\propto e^{-U}\) is invariant. This is the mechanism behind “\(\Law(X_t)\to\pi\)” in Lesson 1.

Example 7.7 (stochastic exponential)notes p. 62

(1) For a continuous semimartingale \(X\), \(\cE(X)_t=\exp\big(X_t-\frac12\br{X}_t\big)\) satisfies \(\cE(X)_t=\cE(X)_0+\int_0^t\cE(X)_s\,\dd X_s\). If \(X\) is a local martingale, so is \(\cE(X)\). (2) (corrected) If \(X=X_0+M+A\) with \(A=-\frac\lambda2\br{M}\), then \(e^{\lambda X}\) is a continuous local martingale.

Proof of (1). Itô for \(F(x,y)=e^{x-y/2}\), applied to \((X,\br{X})\): \(\partial_xF=F\), \(\partial_yF=-\frac12F\), \(\partial^2_{xx}F=F\), and \(\br{X}\) has finite variation. So \(\dd\cE=\cE\,\dd X-\frac12\cE\,\dd\br{X}+\frac12\cE\,\dd\br{X}=\cE\,\dd X\). (2): \(e^{\lambda X_t}=e^{\lambda X_0}\exp\big(\lambda M_t-\frac{\lambda^2}2\br{M}_t\big)=e^{\lambda X_0}\cE(\lambda M)_t\).

Watch out: Example 7.7 (2) as printed (p. 62)

The notes' hypothesis reads \(X_t=X_0+\lambda t+\int_0^t\frac{\lambda^2}2\,\dd\br{X}_s\). This does not make sense: the drift \(\lambda t\) has the wrong dimension, and with \(X_t=\lambda t\) (so \(\br{X}=0\)), \(e^{\lambda X_t}=e^{\lambda^2t}\) is deterministic and increasing, not a local martingale. The proof (“apply the previous point to \(\lambda X\), noting \(\cE(\lambda X_t)=e^{\lambda X_t-\frac{\lambda^2}2\br{X}_t}\)”) shows the intended statement: \(e^{\lambda X}\) is a local martingale exactly when the drift of \(X\) is \(-\frac\lambda2\br{X}\). Example: \(X_t=B_t-\frac\lambda2t\).

Lab 7.1 · Thm. 7.1, Rem. 7.6

Itô's formula, path by path: what if we forget ½f″?

Question: Itô's formula is an almost sure identity for each path. On a grid, how close is \(f(B_1)-f(0)\) to the discrete expansion \(\sum f'(B_{t_i})\Delta B+\sum(\partial_tf+\frac12f'')(B_{t_i})\Delta t\)? What if we use the ordinary chain rule, dropping \(\frac12f''\)? Four functions, including the time-dependent \(F(t,x)=e^{x-t/2}\), for which \(\partial_tF+\frac12\partial_x^2F=0\): the exponential martingale.

What the lab shows (default settings unless stated)
  • \(f(x)=x^3\): with the correction, the mean path-wise error is 0.0168 at \(2^{14}\) steps, and it decreases as the mesh refines (chart). Without it, the error stalls at 1.36, which is \(\E|3\int_0^1B_s\,\dd s|\approx3\sqrt{1/3}\sqrt{2/\pi}=1.38\).
  • \(f(x)=x^2\): the correction is exactly \(\int_0^1\dd s=1\) (measured 1.0000), and the error without it is 1. This is \(B_t^2=2\int B\,\dd B+t\) from Lesson 6.
  • \(F=e^{x-t/2}\): error 0.00474 with the correction. The \(\frac12f''\) term alone averages 0.4963 (exact \(\frac12\)), and it is exactly cancelled by \(\partial_tF\). So \(e^{B_t-t/2}=1+\int_0^te^{B_s-s/2}\,\dd B_s\), a martingale.

§7.2 Consequences of Itô's formula

notes, pp. 62–63

The problem. How do we recognize a Brownian motion? And is every continuous local martingale “a Brownian motion in disguise”?

Theorem 7.8 (Lévy's characterization)notes pp. 62–63

A \(d\)-dimensional continuous local martingale \(M\), null at 0, with \(\br{M^i,M^j}_t=\delta_{ij}t\), is a \(d\)-dimensional standard \((\F_t)\)-Brownian motion.

In one sentenceContinuity, the martingale property and the bracket \(t\) together force Gaussian independent increments.
ProofFix \(a\in\R^d\) and \(N=a^\top M\), so \(\br{N}_t=|a|^2t\). By Example 7.7, \(\cE(N)_t=\exp\big(a^\top M_t-\frac12|a|^2t\big)\) is a local martingale. It is a true martingale by Novikov (Prop. 7.19), since \(\br{N}_t\) is deterministic. Hence \(\E\big[e^{a^\top(M_t-M_s)}\mid\F_s\big]=e^{\frac12|a|^2(t-s)}\) for every \(a\). A conditional moment generating function that does not depend on the past identifies \(M_t-M_s\) as \(N(0,(t-s)I_d)\), independent of \(\F_s\).
Why each hypothesisContinuity: the compensated Poisson process has \(\br{\cdot}_t=\lambda t\) (predictable bracket), and it is not Brownian. The bracket: \(2B\) has bracket \(4t\). Local martingale: \(B_t+t\) has bracket \(t\), but it is not centered.
Used againExample 7.9, Girsanov (Rem. 7.18), Tanaka's SDE (Example 8.12), and correlated Brownian motions (Lesson 5, E8).
Watch out: two slips in the proof of Theorem 7.8 (pp. 62–63)

The notes write \(\cE(N_t)=\exp\big(N_t+\frac12\br{N}_t\big)=\exp\big(a^\top M_t+\frac12|a|^2t\big)\). The sign is wrong: this process has expectation \(e^{|a|^2t}\), not 1 (script errata_checks.py: 29.40 against \(e^{a^2t}=29.37\) for \(a=1.3\), \(t=2\)). The next display, \(\E[e^{a^\top M_t}\mid\F_s]=e^{a^\top M_s}e^{\frac12|a|^2(t-s)}\), is the correct consequence of the minus sign. Second, the notes justify the martingale property by “being bounded on every finite interval”, but \(\cE(N)\) is not bounded. The correct reason is Novikov's criterion, or dominated convergence with \(\E\sup_{s\le t}e^{a^\top M_s}\lt\infty\). The final sentence, “Standard Gaussian maximal estimates then show continuity of paths”, is unnecessary: \(M\) is continuous by hypothesis.

Example 7.9notes p. 63

\(\beta_t=\int_0^t\sgn(B_s)\,\dd B_s\) is a Brownian motion: \(\br{\beta}_t=\int_0^t\sgn(B_s)^2\,\dd s=t\) (Lesson 6, E5; Lab 6.1).

Theorem 7.10 (Dambis–Dubins–Schwarz)notes p. 63

If \(M\) is a continuous local martingale with \(M_0=0\) and \(\br{M}_\infty=\infty\), there is a Brownian motion \(W\) such that \(M_t=W_{\br{M}_t}\).

Why (not proved in the notes). Let \(T_u=\inf\{t:\br{M}_t\gt u\}\), the inverse clock, and \(W_u=M_{T_u}\). By optional stopping, \(W\) is a local martingale in the time-changed filtration \((\F_{T_u})\). Its bracket is \(\br{M}_{T_u}=u\). It is continuous, because \(M\) is constant on the intervals where \(\br{M}\) is flat. By Lévy, \(W\) is a Brownian motion. Meaning: every continuous local martingale is a Brownian motion run with a random, adapted clock. Its “speed” is \(\dd\br{M}/\dd t\) (for example \(\sigma_s^2\) for \(\int\sigma\,\dd B\)).

Theorem 7.11 (Knight, corrected)notes p. 63

If \(M=(M^1,\dots,M^d)\) is a continuous local martingale with \(M_0=0\), \(\br{M^i,M^j}=0\) for \(i\ne j\) and \(\br{M^i}_\infty=\infty\) for each \(i\), there is a \(d\)-dimensional Brownian motion \(W\) with \(M^i_t=W^i_{\br{M^i}_t}\).

The notes omit the hypothesis \(\br{M^i}_\infty=\infty\), without which the clocks may stop and the time changes are not defined for all \(u\). (Otherwise one needs an enlarged probability space.) The remarkable point is independence: orthogonal martingales, run on their own clocks, become independent Brownian motions, even though the clocks themselves may be dependent.

Lab 7.2 · Thm. 7.8, Ex. 7.9, Thm. 7.10

Every continuous martingale is a Brownian motion on its own clock

Question: let \(M_t=\int_0^t\sigma_s\,\dd B_s\) with a random volatility \(\sigma_s=0.5+|B_s|\). At a fixed time, \(M_1\) is a mixture of Gaussians with random variances, so it has heavy tails. Dambis–Dubins–Schwarz says that reading \(M\) at the random time where its clock \(\br{M}=\int\sigma^2\,\dd s\) reaches \(u\) gives exactly \(N(0,u)\). We simulate paths, stop each one when its clock hits \(u\) (with an exact interpolation inside the last step), and compare.

What the lab shows (default settings unless stated)
  • On its own clock: with \(u=0.5\), the KS distance to \(N(0,u)\) is 0.0055 (p-value 0.925), and the excess kurtosis is −0.067. The histogram matches the Gaussian density.
  • On the calendar clock: \(M_1\) has variance 1.364 and excess kurtosis 7.402, far from Gaussian. The reference code (20,000 independent paths) confirms: KS p-value 0.454 and kurtosis +0.010 on the clock, against +6.73 at time 1.
  • \(\sigma=\sgn(B)\) (Example 7.9): the clock is the calendar, and the KS distance is 0.0114. This is Lévy's characterization in action.
  • Why it matters in practice. Stochastic-volatility models, and the noise in stochastic gradient methods, have heavy-tailed increments in calendar time that become Gaussian in “intrinsic time”. The bracket is the right clock.

§7.3 Girsanov's theorem(s)

notes, pp. 63–67

The problem. Two SDEs that differ only by their drift, \(\dd X=\dd B\) and \(\dd X=b\,\dd t+\dd B\), produce laws on path space. How are these laws related? Can we compute expectations under one by reweighting samples from the other? The notes: Girsanov's theorems allow one to “compare local martingale or semimartingale laws under a change of measure”, and to “relate distributions of solutions of SDEs, and in particular derive useful tools for studying sampling and generative stochastic models” (p. 63). The setting is the canonical space \(\Omega=C(\R_+,\R^d)\) of Lesson 1.

The intuition. In one dimension and at one time: \(N(\theta,1)\) has density \(e^{\theta x-\theta^2/2}\) with respect to \(N(0,1)\). Shifting the mean is the same as reweighting by an exponential. On path space, shifting the drift by \(f\) is the same as reweighting by the exponential martingale \(\exp\big(\int f\,\dd B-\frac12\int f^2\,\dd s\big)\). The weight is \(\cE(L)\), with \(L=\int f\,\dd B\).

The density process. For a continuous local martingale \(L\), \(\cE(L)=\exp(L-\frac12\br{L})\) is a positive local martingale, hence a supermartingale (Thm. 5.20). It converges a.s. to \(\cE(L)_\infty\), with \(\E\cE(L)_\infty\le1\) (Cor. 4.16). A change of measure needs total mass 1.

Theorem 7.12notes p. 64

A nonnegative continuous supermartingale \(D\) with \(\E D_\infty=\E D_0=1\) is uniformly integrable, and is a martingale closed by \(D_\infty\).

Why. Fatou gives \(\E[D_\infty\mid\F_t]\le D_t\). Taking expectations, \(1=\E D_\infty\le\E D_t\le\E D_0=1\). So the nonnegative variable \(D_t-\E[D_\infty\mid\F_t]\) has mean 0, hence is 0: \(D_t=\E[D_\infty\mid\F_t]\), a closed martingale, hence UI (Thm. 4.25). Lab 4.6 shows the failure mode: the exponential martingale \(\cE(uB)\) on \([0,\infty)\) has \(\E\cE_\infty=0\).

Theorem 7.13 (Girsanov, infinite horizon)notes p. 64

If \(\E[\cE(L)_\infty]=1\), then \(\dd\Q=\cE(L)_\infty\,\dd\P\) is a probability measure (\(\Q\ll\P\)), and for every continuous \(\P\)-local martingale \(M\), the process \(M-\br{M,L}\) is a continuous \(\Q\)-local martingale.

Theorem 7.14 (Girsanov, finite horizon)notes p. 64

If \(\E[\cE(L)_T]=1\), then \(\dd\Q^L=\cE(L)_T\,\dd\P\) is a probability measure equivalent to \(\P\) on \(\F_T\), and \((M_t-\br{M,L}_t)_{t\le T}\) is a \(\Q^L\)-local martingale for every continuous \(\P\)-local martingale \(M\).

In one sentenceChanging the measure by \(\cE(L)\) keeps brackets and shifts drifts by \(\br{\cdot,L}\).
Proof idea (the notes give none). Bayes: with \(D_t=\cE(L)_t=\E[\dd\Q/\dd\P\mid\F_t]\), a process \(Y\) is a \(\Q\)-local martingale iff \(YD\) is a \(\P\)-local martingale. For \(Y=M-\br{M,L}\), since \(\dd D=D\,\dd L\): \(\dd(YD)=Y\,\dd D+D\,\dd M-D\,\dd\br{M,L}+\dd\br{M,D}\), and \(\dd\br{M,D}=D\,\dd\br{M,L}\) cancels the third term. What is left is a sum of stochastic integrals. Brackets do not change under an equivalent measure, since \(\sum(\Delta X)^2\to\br{X}\) in probability for both.
Used againCameron–Martin (Thm. 7.16), weak existence (Prop. 7.21), weak uniqueness for bounded drift (Example 8.13).
Remark 7.15 (an ODE without solution, corrected)notes pp. 64–65

The ODE \(x_t=\int_0^t\ind_{\{x_s\le0\}}\,\dd s\) has no solution on any interval \([0,T]\).

Why. Such an \(x\) would be non-decreasing and start at 0. If \(x\le0\) on some \([0,\varepsilon]\), then \(x=0\) there, but then \(x_\varepsilon=\int_0^\varepsilon1\,\dd s=\varepsilon\gt0\), a contradiction. So \(x_s\gt0\) for all small \(s\gt0\), hence for all \(s\gt0\) by monotonicity. But then \(x_t=\int_0^t\ind_{\{x_s\le0\}}\,\dd s=0\), again a contradiction. The drift \(\ind_{\{x\le0\}}\) is bounded but discontinuous, so neither Picard nor Peano applies.

Watch out: the equation printed in Remark 7.15 has a solution (pp. 64–65)

The notes consider \(x_t=t+\int_0^t\ind_{\{x_s\gt0\}}\,\dd s\) and argue that it has no solution. But \(x_t=2t\) solves it: \(t+\int_0^t\ind_{\{2s\gt0\}}\,\dd s=2t\) (script errata_checks.py). The notes' argument (“If \(\tau=\infty\), then \(x_t=0\) for all \(t\), absurd. If \(\tau\lt\infty\), … \(x_t=\int_\tau^t\ind\,\dd s=0\)”) fits the equation \(x_t=\int_0^t\ind_{\{x_s\le0\}}\,\dd s\), which is the corrected version above. The opening sentence (“the assumptions above are quite restrictive, and this is the first time we have used a Brownian motion…”) is garbled. The intended point, which Prop. 7.21 makes precise: adding Brownian noise restores existence for bounded measurable drifts. Lab 7.4 shows the Euler scheme for the ODE getting stuck at \(x=h\) (the step), while the SDE is well posed.

Theorem 7.16 (Cameron–Martin)notes p. 65

Let \(f\in L^2([0,T])\) be deterministic and \(L_t=\exp\big(\int_0^tf\,\dd B-\frac12\int_0^tf^2\,\dd s\big)\). For every bounded measurable \(\Phi:C([0,T],\R)\to\R\), \[ \E\Big[\Phi\Big(\big(B_t+\textstyle\int_0^tf(s)\,\dd s\big)_{t\le T}\Big)\Big]=\E\big[L_T\,\Phi\big((B_t)_{t\le T}\big)\big]. \]

In one sentenceThe law of Brownian motion shifted by a deterministic \(L^2\) path has density \(L_T\) with respect to Wiener measure.
Proof\(\br{\int f\,\dd B}_T=\int_0^Tf^2\lt\infty\) is deterministic, so Novikov gives \(\E L_T=1\). Under \(\Q^L\), \(\tilde B=B-\int f\) is a Brownian motion (Girsanov plus Lévy: Rem. 7.18). Hence \[ \begin{aligned}\E^{\P}\big[\Phi(B+\textstyle\int f)\big]&=\E^{\Q^L}\big[\Phi(\tilde B+\textstyle\int f)\big]\\ &=\E^{\Q^L}[\Phi(B)]=\E^{\P}[L_T\Phi(B)].\end{aligned} \]
How it is used: importance sampling on path spaceTo estimate a rare-event probability \(\E[\Phi(B)]\), simulate a drifted path \(B+\int f\) and weight it by \(\exp\big(-\int f\,\dd B-\frac12\int f^2\big)\), which is Theorem 7.16 read backwards. A good drift makes the rare event typical and the weights stable (Lab 7.3).
When it failsIf \(f\notin L^2\), for example \(f(s)=s^{-1/2}\) with \(\int_0^Tf^2=\infty\), the two laws are mutually singular (Beyond the notes: the Cameron–Martin space is exactly \(\{\int f:f\in L^2\}\)). There is no density, and reweighting is impossible.
Watch out: a slip in the proof of Theorem 7.16 (p. 65)

The notes write \(\E^{\P}[\Psi(B)]=\E^{\Q^L}[\Psi(\tilde B+\int f)]\). Since \(\tilde B+\int f=B\), the right side is \(\E^{\Q^L}[\Psi(B)]\), which is not \(\E^{\P}[\Psi(B)]\) unless \(f=0\). The intended identity is \(\E^{\P}[\Psi(B+\int f)]=\E^{\Q^L}[\Psi(\tilde B+\int f)]\): under \(\Q^L\), \(\tilde B\) has the law of \(B\) under \(\P\). The chain of equalities above is the corrected proof.

Remark 7.17: \(\Q^L(A)=\E[\ind_A\cE(L)_T]\) for \(A\in\F_t\), \(t\le T\). Semimartingales under \(\P\) remain semimartingales under \(\Q^L\), and \(\P\)-a.s. statements remain \(\Q^L\)-a.s. true, since \(\Q^L\ll\P\). Remark 7.18: if \(B\) is a \((\P,\F_t)\)-Brownian motion, then \(B-\br{L,B}\) is a \(\Q^L\)-local martingale with bracket \(t\). By Lévy it is a \(\Q^L\)-Brownian motion.

Proposition 7.19 (Novikov, Kazamaki)notes p. 66 (proof skipped)

Then (i) ⇒ (ii) ⇒ (iii): (i) Novikov: \(\E\big[e^{\frac12\br{L}_T}\big]\lt\infty\); (ii) Kazamaki: \((e^{L_t/2})_{t\le T}\) is uniformly integrable; (iii) \(\E[\cE(L)_T]=1\), so \(\cE(L)\) is a true martingale on \([0,T]\).

The notes' statement says “continuous martingale” and lists (ii) with “\(\E[e^{L_T/2}]\lt\infty\)”; the standard form is as above. How to use it: if \(\br{L}_T\) is bounded (bounded drift, as in Prop. 7.21) or deterministic (Cameron–Martin, Lévy), Novikov holds trivially. These conditions are sufficient, not necessary (E6). Proposition 7.21 (weak existence for bounded drift, sign corrected)notes p. 66

Let \(b:\R_+\times\R^d\to\R^d\) be measurable and bounded. There is a filtered probability space \((\Omega,\F,(\F_t),\Q)\) carrying a Brownian motion \(B\) and a continuous adapted \(X\) with \(X_t=x_0+B_t+\int_0^tb(s,X_s)\,\dd s\).

Proof (finite horizon \(T\)). Start with a Brownian motion \(X\) from \(x_0\) under \(\P\). Put \(L_t=+\int_0^tb(s,X_s)\cdot\dd X_s\). Then \(\br{L}_t=\int_0^t|b|^2\,\dd s\le t\sup|b|^2\), so Novikov gives \(\E\cE(L)_T=1\). Under \(\dd\Q^L=\cE(L)_T\,\dd\P\), Girsanov says \(X-x_0-\br{X,L}\) is a local martingale, with \(\br{X,L}_t=\int_0^tb(s,X_s)\,\dd s\). Its bracket is still \(tI\), so by Lévy, \(B:=X-x_0-\int_0^tb(s,X_s)\,\dd s\) is a \(\Q^L\)-Brownian motion.

Watch out: the sign of \(L\) in Proposition 7.21 (p. 66)

The notes set \(L_t=-\int_0^tb(s,X_s)\cdot\dd X_s\). Then \(\br{X,L}=-\int b\,\dd s\), and Girsanov makes \(X-x_0+\int b\,\dd s\) the \(\Q^L\)-Brownian motion: the solution of the SDE with drift \(-b\), not \(+b\). The correct choice is \(L=+\int b\cdot\dd X\). Evidence: for constant \(b=0.7\), the weights with \(+\) give \(\E_{\Q}[X_1-x_0]=0.7024\) and with \(-\) give \(-0.7002\) (script errata_checks.py, \(10^6\) samples). Lab 7.4 shows the same for a discontinuous drift.

Remark 7.22: extending to \([0,\infty)\) needs a consistent family of measures \(\Q_T\) and a Kolmogorov-type extension on the canonical space; the notes refer to Revuz and Yor, Chapter VIII.

Lab 7.4 · Rem. 7.15, Prop. 7.21

Noise restores existence: building an SDE solution by reweighting Brownian paths

Question: the ODE \(x'=\ind_{\{x\le0\}}\), \(x_0=0\), has no solution (Rem. 7.15, corrected). The SDE \(\dd X=\ind_{\{X\le0\}}\dd t+\varepsilon\,\dd B\) does, for every \(\varepsilon\gt0\) (Prop. 7.21). We compute \(\E[X_1]\) in two independent ways. (a) Euler–Maruyama simulation. (b) The proof of Prop. 7.21: simulate \(\varepsilon W\) (no drift) and reweight by \(\cE(L)_1\) with \(L=\int b(X)/\varepsilon\,\dd W\). We also try the notes' sign, and a direct simulation of the SDE with drift \(-\ind_{\{X\le0\}}\).

What the lab shows (default settings unless stated)
  • The construction works (\(\varepsilon=0.3\)): Euler–Maruyama gives \(\E X_1=\) 0.1956 ± 0.0019, and the reweighted Brownian paths give 0.1944 ± 0.0045. The time spent in \(\{X\le0\}\) averages 0.194, equal to \(\E X_1\) as the equation requires, since \(\E\int\varepsilon\,\dd B=0\).
  • The notes' sign builds the other SDE: the Euler simulation with drift \(-\ind_{\{X\le0\}}\) gives −0.8166 ± 0.0053, and the weights with the notes' sign give −0.8509 ± 0.4292. The two agree, but the second is extremely noisy. The reference code (100,000 paths, independent random numbers) gives −0.8101 and −0.8188.
  • Reweighting degenerates as \(\varepsilon\to0\): \(\br{L}_1\) can be as large as \(1/\varepsilon^2\). At \(\varepsilon=0.3\) the effective sample size is 0.190 of the paths, and at \(\varepsilon=0.1\) it is 0.017. There the estimate is 0.0638 ± 0.0040 against the Euler value 0.0753, a gap of about 3 nominal standard errors: the standard error itself is unreliable. With 100,000 paths the reference code gets 0.0744 against 0.0749. With the notes' sign at \(\varepsilon=0.1\), the weights cannot reach the target law at all (0.0041 against −0.9314). Girsanov is exact in law, but not always usable as a Monte Carlo method.
  • The ODE limit: the Euler scheme for \(\varepsilon=0\) ends at \(x(1)=\) 0.001, one step \(h\) above 0, where the drift switches off. As \(h\to0\) this tends to 0, and \(x\equiv0\) is not a solution. At \(\varepsilon=1\) the mean is 0.3786; at \(\varepsilon=0.1\), 0.0753. The SDE solutions shrink towards 0 as the noise vanishes.
Beyond the notes: Girsanov in generative modeling

For two SDEs with the same diffusion coefficient \(\sigma\) and drifts \(b_1,b_2\), Girsanov gives the relative entropy of their path laws on \([0,T]\): \(\mathrm{KL}(\P^1\|\P^2)=\frac12\E^{\P^1}\int_0^T|\sigma^{-1}(b_1-b_2)(X_s)|^2\,\dd s\). Score-based diffusion models train a network \(s_\theta\) to approximate \(\nabla\log p_t\). This identity turns the training loss, a weighted score-matching error, into a bound on the KL divergence between the generated law and the data law (Song et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2023). It is the board's Chapter 0 item (2) made quantitative. The same identity with \(b_2=-\nabla U\) controls the bias of Langevin samplers.

Exercises

E1 · Moments by Itô's formula Thm. 7.1

Use Itô's formula to compute \(\E[B_t^4]\) and \(\E[e^{\lambda B_t}]\) without using the Gaussian density.

Hint

Write \(f(B_t)=f(0)+\int f'(B)\,\dd B+\frac12\int f''(B)\,\dd s\), take expectations (the stochastic integral is an \(L^2\) martingale here), and solve the resulting ODE for \(m(t)=\E f(B_t)\).

Solution

\(B_t^4=4\int B^3\,\dd B+6\int B^2\,\dd s\), so \(\E B_t^4=6\int_0^ts\,\dd s=3t^2\). For \(e^{\lambda B}\): \(m'(t)=\frac{\lambda^2}2m(t)\), \(m(0)=1\), so \(m(t)=e^{\lambda^2t/2}\). The integrals \(\int B^3\,\dd B\) and \(\int e^{\lambda B}\,\dd B\) are true martingales because \(\E\int_0^tB^6\lt\infty\) and \(\E\int_0^te^{2\lambda B}\lt\infty\) (Remark 6.15).

E2 · Uniqueness of the stochastic exponential Example 7.7

Show that \(Z=\cE(X)\) is the only continuous semimartingale with \(Z_t=1+\int_0^tZ_s\,\dd X_s\) (for \(X_0=0\)).

Solution

\(\cE(X)\gt0\). Let \(Y=1/\cE(X)=\exp(-X+\frac12\br{X})\). Itô gives \(\dd Y=Y(-\dd X+\dd\br{X})\). If \(Z\) is another solution, the product rule gives

\[ \begin{aligned}\dd(ZY)&=Z\,\dd Y+Y\,\dd Z+\dd\br{Z,Y}\\ &=ZY(-\dd X+\dd\br{X})+ZY\,\dd X-ZY\,\dd\br{X}=0.\end{aligned} \]

So \(ZY\equiv1\) and \(Z=\cE(X)\).

E3 · Lévy's characterization with characteristic functions Thm. 7.8, alternative proof

For \(d=1\), apply Itô's formula to \(e^{iuM_t}\) (real and imaginary parts) to show \(\E[e^{iu(M_t-M_s)}\mid\F_s]=e^{-u^2(t-s)/2}\).

Solution

\(e^{iuM_t}=e^{iuM_s}+iu\int_s^te^{iuM_r}\,\dd M_r-\frac{u^2}2\int_s^te^{iuM_r}\,\dd r\). The integrand is bounded, so the stochastic integral is a true martingale with zero conditional mean. For \(A\in\F_s\), \(\phi(t)=\E[\ind_Ae^{iu(M_t-M_s)}]\) satisfies \(\phi(t)=\P(A)-\frac{u^2}2\int_s^t\phi(r)\,\dd r\), hence \(\phi(t)=\P(A)e^{-u^2(t-s)/2}\). This version needs no integrability argument: \(|e^{iuM}|=1\).

E4 · Time change of a Wiener integral Thm. 7.10

Let \(M_t=\int_0^t\sigma(s)\,\dd B_s\) with deterministic \(\sigma\) and \(\int_0^\infty\sigma^2=\infty\). Identify the clock and the Brownian motion of Dambis–Dubins–Schwarz.

Solution

\(\br{M}_t=A(t)=\int_0^t\sigma^2\) is deterministic and increasing. \(W_u=M_{A^{-1}(u)}\) is a Gaussian process with \(\Cov(W_u,W_v)=A(A^{-1}(u\wedge v))=u\wedge v\), so it is a Brownian motion, and \(M_t=W_{A(t)}\). Lab 7.2 with the “deterministic” volatility is exactly this case.

E5 · Hitting a level with drift, via Girsanov and reflection Thm. 7.16 + Lesson 2

Show that \(\P\big(\sup_{t\le1}(B_t+\theta t)\ge a\big)=\bar\Phi(a-\theta)+e^{2\theta a}\,\bar\Phi(a+\theta)\) for \(a\gt0\).

Hint

By Cameron–Martin, the probability is \(\E[e^{\theta B_1-\theta^2/2};S_1\ge a]\), where \(S_1=\sup_{t\le1}B_t\). Use the joint density of \((S_1,B_1)\) from Lesson 2, E2, or split according to \(B_1\ge a\) or \(B_1\lt a\) and use reflection on the second part.

Solution

On \(\{B_1\ge a\}\), \(S_1\ge a\) automatically, which gives \(\E[e^{\theta B_1-\theta^2/2};B_1\ge a]=\bar\Phi(a-\theta)\) by completing the square. On \(\{S_1\ge a,B_1\lt a\}\), reflection maps \(B_1\mapsto2a-B_1\), which preserves the law of paths that hit \(a\). So this part is

\[ \begin{aligned}\E\big[e^{\theta(2a-B_1)-\theta^2/2};B_1\gt a\big]&=e^{2\theta a}\E\big[e^{-\theta B_1-\theta^2/2};B_1\gt a\big]\\ &=e^{2\theta a}\bar\Phi(a+\theta).\end{aligned} \]

With \(\theta=0\) we recover \(2\bar\Phi(a)\).

E6 · Novikov is sufficient, not necessary Prop. 7.19, beyond the notes

Let \(L_t=\int_0^tB_s\,\dd B_s\). Using \(\E\exp\big(\lambda\int_0^TB_s^2\,\dd s\big)=\big(\cos(T\sqrt{2\lambda})\big)^{-1/2}\) for \(T\sqrt{2\lambda}\lt\pi/2\) (Cameron–Martin's formula), show that Novikov's condition holds only for \(T\lt\pi/2\).

Solution

Novikov requires \(\E e^{\frac12\br{L}_T}=\E e^{\frac12\int_0^TB^2}\lt\infty\). With \(\lambda=\frac12\), \(\sqrt{2\lambda}=1\), this is finite if and only if \(T\lt\pi/2\), since the expectation blows up as \(T\uparrow\pi/2\). Yet \(\cE(L)\) is a true martingale on every \([0,T]\). Under the new measure, \(B\) solves \(\dd B=B\,\dd t+\dd\tilde W\), a linear SDE that does not explode, and a non-explosion argument then gives \(\E\cE(L)_T=1\). A condition can fail while its conclusion holds.

E7 · Maximum likelihood for a drift, via Girsanov Thm. 7.14, beyond the notes (statistics of SDEs)

Observe a path of \(\dd X=-\theta X\,\dd t+\dd B\) on \([0,T]\), with \(X_0=0\). Show that the log-likelihood of \(\theta\) with respect to Wiener measure is \(\ell(\theta)=-\theta\int_0^TX\,\dd X-\frac{\theta^2}2\int_0^TX^2\,\dd t\), and that the maximum-likelihood estimator is \(\hat\theta=-\int_0^TX\,\dd X\big/\int_0^TX^2\,\dd t\).

Solution

By Girsanov (with the corrected sign of Prop. 7.21), the law of the solution has density \(\cE\big(\int b(X)\,\dd X\big)_T\) with respect to the law of \(X\) under Brownian motion, where \(b(x)=-\theta x\). That is, \(\exp\big(-\theta\int X\,\dd X-\frac{\theta^2}2\int X^2\,\dd t\big)\). Setting \(\ell'(\theta)=0\) gives \(\hat\theta\). By Itô, \(\int_0^TX\,\dd X=\frac12(X_T^2-T)\), so \(\hat\theta\) only needs \(X_T\) and \(\int X^2\). Check (reference code): 400 paths with \(\theta=2\), \(T=50\): mean \(\hat\theta=2.037\), standard deviation 0.299, against the asymptotic \(\sqrt{2\theta/T}=0.283\).

E8 · The Euler scheme for the ODE of Remark 7.15 Rem. 7.15, corrected

Show that the Euler scheme \(x_{k+1}=x_k+h\ind_{\{x_k\le0\}}\), \(x_0=0\), gives \(x_k=h\) for all \(k\ge1\), and explain why the limit \(h\to0\) is not a solution.

Solution

\(x_1=h\gt0\), and from then on the increment is 0. As \(h\to0\) the scheme converges uniformly to \(x\equiv0\). But \(x\equiv0\) gives \(\int_0^t\ind_{\{0\le0\}}\,\dd s=t\ne0\). The limit of the scheme does not solve the equation, which itself has no solution. Lab 7.4 gives \(x(1)=\) 0.001 for \(h=10^{-3}\). With noise the problem is well posed, and its solutions converge, as \(\varepsilon\to0\), to a generalized (Filippov) solution. This is how the zero-noise limit selects a solution (Beyond the notes).

Conclusion

  • Itô's formula. \(\dd f(X)=f'(X)\,\dd X+\frac12f''(X)\,\dd\br{X}\), and its multidimensional and time-dependent forms (Thm. 7.1, Rem. 7.6). It comes from Taylor's formula and the quadratic sums of Lesson 5. The second-order term is not optional (Lab 7.1). It links processes to PDEs through the generator, and it explains why Langevin dynamics samples \(e^{-U}\).
  • Exponentials. \(\cE(X)=e^{X-\br{X}/2}\) solves \(\dd Z=Z\,\dd X\) (Example 7.7). It is a local martingale and a supermartingale, and a true martingale under Novikov or Kazamaki (Prop. 7.19).
  • Recognizing Brownian motion. The bracket \(t\) plus continuity plus the local martingale property (Lévy). Every continuous local martingale is a time-changed Brownian motion (Dambis–Dubins–Schwarz; Lab 7.2).
  • Changing the measure. \(\dd\Q=\cE(L)\,\dd\P\) keeps brackets and shifts drifts by \(\br{\cdot,L}\) (Girsanov). Cameron–Martin is the deterministic-drift case and gives importance sampling on path space (Lab 7.3). With bounded drifts, reweighting Brownian paths gives weak solutions of SDEs whose ODE counterpart has none (Prop. 7.21, sign corrected; Lab 7.4). Weight degeneracy is the practical limit.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)

print("1) Ito's formula for f(x)=x^3 on [0,1] (2^14 steps, 500 paths): error with and without the 1/2 f'' term")
n = 2**14
dB = rng.standard_normal((500, n)) / np.sqrt(n)
B = np.concatenate([np.zeros((500, 1)), np.cumsum(dB, axis=1)], axis=1)

print("2) Dambis-Dubins-Schwarz: M = int (0.5+|B|) dB read at clock <M> = 0.5 is N(0, 0.5); at fixed time 1 it is not Gaussian")
M_, dt, u = 20000, 1e-3, 0.5
b = np.zeros(M_); x = np.zeros(M_); q = np.zeros(M_); stopped = np.full(M_, np.nan); x1 = None
for k in range(3000):
    sig = 0.5 + np.abs(b); d = np.sqrt(dt) * rng.standard_normal(M_); dq = sig**2 * dt
    hit = np.isnan(stopped) & (q + dq >= u)
    f = np.clip((u - q[hit]) / dq[hit], 0, 1)
    stopped[hit] = x[hit] + sig[hit] * (f * d[hit] + np.sqrt(f * (1 - f) * dt) * rng.standard_normal(hit.sum()))
    x += sig * d; b += d; q += dq
    if k == 999: x1 = x.copy()
    if not np.isnan(stopped).any() and x1 is not None: break

print("3) Cameron-Martin importance sampling for p = P(sup_{t<=1} B_t >= a), a = 3 (100 steps, exact bridge maxima)")
def bridge_max(x0, x1, h, U): return 0.5 * (x0 + x1 + np.sqrt((x1 - x0)**2 - 2 * h * np.log(U)))
a, nst, Mp = 3.0, 100, 20000
exact = 2 * stats.norm.sf(a)
for theta in [0.0, 3.0, 6.0]:
    h = 1 / nst; z = np.zeros(Mp); w = np.zeros(Mp); smax = np.zeros(Mp)
    for _ in range(nst):
        dw = np.sqrt(h) * rng.standard_normal(Mp); zn = z + dw + theta * h
        smax = np.maximum(smax, bridge_max(z, zn, h, rng.random(Mp))); z = zn; w += dw
    est = np.where(smax >= a, np.exp(-theta * w - 0.5 * theta**2), 0.0)
    print(f"   theta={theta}: estimate {est.mean():.3e} (relative s.e. {est.std()/np.sqrt(Mp)/max(est.mean(),1e-300):.3f});  exact {exact:.3e}")

print("4) Weak existence for dX = 1{X<=0} dt + eps dB (Prop. 7.21): Euler-Maruyama vs Girsanov reweighting of eps*W, 100,000 paths")
for eps in [1.0, 0.3, 0.1]:
    Mw, nn = 100000, 1000; h = 1 / nn
    x = np.zeros(Mw); xn = np.zeros(Mw); y = np.zeros(Mw); L = np.zeros(Mw); Q = np.zeros(Mw)
    for _ in range(nn):
        x += (x <= 0) * h + eps * np.sqrt(h) * rng.standard_normal(Mw)
        xn -= (xn <= 0) * h - eps * np.sqrt(h) * rng.standard_normal(Mw)
        dw = np.sqrt(h) * rng.standard_normal(Mw); bb = (y <= 0) / eps

    wplus, wminus = np.exp(L - 0.5 * Q), np.exp(-L - 0.5 * Q)
    ess = wplus.sum()**2 / np.sum(wplus**2) / Mw
    print(f"   eps={eps}: E X_1 Euler(+b) {x.mean():.4f} | weights(+) {np.mean(y*wplus):.4f} (ESS/n {ess:.3f}) | Euler(-b) {xn.mean():.4f} | weights(-, notes' sign) {np.mean(y*wminus):.4f}")

Cheat sheet

Itô's formula

\(\dd f(X)=f'(X)\dd X+\frac12f''(X)\dd\br{X}\). Several dimensions: \(+\frac12\sum\partial^2_{ij}F\,\dd\br{X^i,X^j}\). Diffusion: drift \(\nabla F^\top b+\frac12\tr(\sigma\sigma^\top\nabla^2F)\).

Generator

\(\mathcal L=b\cdot\nabla+\frac12\tr(a\nabla^2)\). \(f(X_t)-\int\mathcal Lf(X)\) is a local martingale. BM: \(\frac12\Delta\). Langevin: \(\Delta-\nabla U\cdot\nabla\), invariant law \(e^{-U}\).

Stochastic exponential

\(\cE(X)=e^{X-\br{X}/2}\), \(\dd\cE=\cE\,\dd X\), unique solution. A supermartingale if \(X\in\Mloc\).

Lévy

Continuous local martingale, \(\br{M^i,M^j}=\delta_{ij}t\) ⇒ BM. \(\int\sgn(B)\,\dd B\) is a BM.

DDS and Knight

\(M_t=W_{\br{M}_t}\) if \(\br{M}_\infty=\infty\). Orthogonal martingales on their clocks are independent BMs.

Girsanov

\(\dd\Q=\cE(L)_T\dd\P\), \(\E\cE(L)_T=1\) ⇒ \(M-\br{M,L}\) is a \(\Q\)-local martingale; brackets unchanged. BM \(B\) ⇒ \(B-\br{B,L}\) is a \(\Q\)-BM.

Cameron–Martin

\(\E\Phi(B+\int f)=\E[e^{\int f\dd B-\frac12\int f^2}\Phi(B)]\), \(f\in L^2\). Importance sampling: simulate with drift, weight by the inverse density.

Novikov and applications

\(\E e^{\br{L}_T/2}\lt\infty\) ⇒ \(\E\cE(L)_T=1\). Weak existence for bounded drift: \(L=+\int b\cdot\dd X\). KL of path laws \(=\frac12\E\int|\sigma^{-1}(b_1-b_2)|^2\).

Self-test

1. Write Itô's formula for \(f(B_t)\).

\(f(B_t)=f(0)+\int f'(B)\,\dd B+\frac12\int f''(B)\,\dd s\).

2. Where does the \(\frac12f''\) come from?

From the second-order Taylor term: \(\sum\frac12f''(X_{t_i})(\Delta X)^2\to\frac12\int f''\,\dd\br{X}\) (Prop. 5.44).

3. What is the error of the ordinary chain rule for \(B^3\)?

\(3\int_0^1B\,\dd s\), with mean absolute value about 1.38 (Lab 7.1: 1.36).

4. Compute \(\dd(e^{B_t-t/2})\).

\(e^{B_t-t/2}\,\dd B_t\): the drift terms \(-\frac12\) and \(+\frac12\) cancel.

5. Which factor is missing in the notes' last formula of Remark 7.3?

\(\frac12\) in front of \(\int\tr(a\nabla^2F)\,\dd s\).

6. When is \(e^{\lambda X}\) a local martingale, for \(X=X_0+M+A\)?

When \(A=-\frac\lambda2\br{M}\), because then \(e^{\lambda X}=e^{\lambda X_0}\cE(\lambda M)\).

7. State Lévy's characterization and give a counterexample for each hypothesis.

Continuous local martingale with bracket \(t\) ⇒ BM. Counterexamples: \(N_t-\lambda t\) (not continuous), \(2B\) (bracket \(4t\)), \(B+t\) (not a local martingale).

8. What sign error is in the notes' proof of Lévy's theorem?

\(\cE(N)=\exp(N+\frac12\br{N})\) should be \(\exp(N-\frac12\br{N})\).

9. State Dambis–Dubins–Schwarz.

\(M_t=W_{\br{M}_t}\) for some BM \(W\), if \(M\in\Mloc\), \(M_0=0\) and \(\br{M}_\infty=\infty\).

10. Why is \(M_1=\int_0^1(0.5+|B|)\,\dd B\) not Gaussian while \(M\) at clock time \(u\) is?

\(M_1\) mixes Gaussians with random variances \(\br{M}_1\). On its clock, the variance is fixed at \(u\) (Lab 7.2: kurtosis 7.402 against −0.067).

11. What does Knight's theorem add, and which hypothesis do the notes omit?

Orthogonal martingales become independent BMs on their own clocks. The notes omit \(\br{M^i}_\infty=\infty\).

12. When is \(\cE(L)\) a UI martingale on \([0,\infty)\)?

If and only if \(\E\cE(L)_\infty=1\) (Thm. 7.12). Novikov/Kazamaki with \(T=\infty\) suffice.

13. State Girsanov's theorem (finite horizon).

If \(\E\cE(L)_T=1\) and \(\dd\Q=\cE(L)_T\dd\P\), then every \(\P\)-local martingale \(M\) gives a \(\Q\)-local martingale \(M-\br{M,L}\) on \([0,T]\).

14. Does a change of measure change the bracket?

No: brackets are limits of quadratic sums in probability, and equivalent measures have the same null sets.

15. What is wrong with the equation in Remark 7.15, and what is the correct one?

\(x=t+\int\ind_{x\gt0}\) is solved by \(x=2t\). The intended equation is \(x=\int\ind_{x\le0}\), which has no solution.

16. State Cameron–Martin.

For deterministic \(f\in L^2\), \(B+\int f\) has density \(\exp(\int f\,\dd B-\frac12\int f^2)\) with respect to Wiener measure.

17. How does Cameron–Martin give an importance sampler, and what limits it?

Simulate a drifted path, weight by the inverse density. Too much drift makes the weights degenerate (Lab 7.3: relative error 0.216 at \(\theta=6\) against 0.018 at \(\theta=3\)).

18. Why is Novikov satisfied in Prop. 7.21?

\(\br{L}_T=\int|b|^2\le T\sup|b|^2\) is bounded.

19. What does the notes' sign \(L=-\int b\cdot\dd X\) produce?

A weak solution of the SDE with drift \(-b\).

20. Why is Girsanov relevant for diffusion models?

It expresses the KL divergence between path laws of SDEs with different drifts as \(\frac12\E\int|\sigma^{-1}\Delta b|^2\), which links score-matching losses to sampling error.

21. Give the maximum-likelihood estimator of \(\theta\) for \(\dd X=-\theta X\,\dd t+\dd B\).

\(\hat\theta=-\int X\,\dd X/\int X^2\,\dd t=\frac{T-X_T^2}{2\int_0^TX^2\,\dd t}\) (E7).
