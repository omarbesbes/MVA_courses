Measurability, Filtrations and Stopping Times

What do we know at time t, and which random times can be decided with that information?

§0 Where this lesson sits

The notes open the chapter by admitting that it is technical: “Here we gather some technical conditions and results which, while they may seem overly complicated at first sight, are important to consider if we want to construct and manipulate continuous-time processes” (p. 21). This page keeps asking what each condition buys.

What it reuses.

  • From Lesson 2: stopping times (Def. 2.11), the σ-field \(\F_\tau\) (Def. 2.13), the filtration \(\F_{t+}\) and the fact that Brownian motion does not feel the difference (Prop. 2.4, Cor. 2.9). Also the hitting time \(\tau_a\) (Example 2.12) and the dyadic rounding \(\tau_N\downarrow\tau\) from the strong Markov proof (Thm. 2.15).
  • From Lesson 1: modifications and indistinguishability (Def. 1.9, Def. 1.11). Completing the filtration is what lets a modification of an adapted process stay adapted.

What it prepares.

  • Lesson 4 needs right-continuous filtrations for càdlàg modifications of martingales (Thm. 4.11). Optional stopping uses \(\F_\sigma\subset\F_\tau\) (Prop. 3.17, Prop. 4.36) and \(\{\tau\lt\sigma\}\in\F_{\sigma\wedge\tau}\) (Cor. 4.31).
  • Lessons 5–6 need progressive measurability: integrands must be progressive (Prop. 5.17, Def. 6.4). Local martingales are defined through stopping times (Def. 5.18), and stopped integrals through Prop. 6.12.
  • Lesson 8: “the augmented filtration” of the driving Brownian motion (Def. 8.2) is the usual augmentation of §3.1. Strong and weak solutions differ exactly by the filtration they are adapted to (Rem. 8.6, Def. 8.14).

A question that comes back

“What exactly do we know at time \(t\), and which random times can we act on?” Lesson 2 met this question twice: information “just after” \(s\) (\(\F_{s+}\), Prop. 2.4), and restarting at a random time, which works for stopping times and fails for the last zero (Lesson 2, E8). Here the answer becomes a set of rules: usual conditions, progressive processes, \(\F_\tau\). Lesson 4 turns it into the question of when martingale identities survive at random times. Lab 3.2 previews that answer.

§3.1 Filtrations and usual conditions

notes, pp. 21–22

The problem. A filtration models information growing with time. Two technical defects make continuous-time arguments break. First, null sets may be missing, so an a.s. modification of an adapted process may fail to be adapted. Second, information may jump “just after” \(t\), so natural random times such as “the first time \(X\) enters an open set” are not stopping times.

Definition 3.1 (filtration)notes p. 21

A filtration on \((\Omega,\F,\P)\) is an increasing family \((\F_t)_{t\ge0}\) of sub-σ-fields of \(\F\): \(s\le t\Rightarrow\F_s\subset\F_t\).

Definition 3.2 (right-continuity)notes p. 21

(a) \(\F_{t+}=\bigcap_{s\gt t}\F_s\). (b) \((\F_t)\) is right-continuous if \(\F_t=\F_{t+}\) for every \(t\).

The notes' interpretation: \((\F_t)\) is right-continuous if the information “evolves smoothly with time and does not contain new information coming from an infinitesimal time change.” Proposition 3.3: \((\F_{t+})\) is a filtration and it is right-continuous (Exercise E1).

Example 3.4notes pp. 21–22

(1) A filtration that is not right-continuous. (2) For a Brownian motion and \(a\gt0\), compare \(\tau_a=\inf\{t\ge0:B_t=a\}\) and \(\sigma_a=\inf\{t\ge0:B_t\gt a\}\). \(\tau_a\) is a stopping time of the natural filtration \(\F^B_t=\sigma(B_s:s\le t)\). \(\sigma_a\) is only a stopping time of \((\F^B_{t+})\): \[ \{\sigma_a\le t\}=\bigcap_{\varepsilon\in\Qb_+}\Big\{\sup_{r\in[0,t+\varepsilon]\cap\Qb}B_r\gt a\Big\}\in\F^B_{t+}. \] It is a stopping time of the completed natural filtration, because \(\sigma_a=\tau_a\) a.s. (Exercise 3.7).

Watch out: three slips in Example 3.4 (notes p. 21)
  1. Part (1) reads “\(\F_t=\{\varnothing,\Omega\}\) while \(\F_0=\sigma(Z)\)”. That is not a filtration: it decreases. The intended example is the reverse: \(\F_0=\{\varnothing,\Omega\}\) and \(\F_t=\sigma(Z)\) for \(t\gt0\). Then \(\F_{0+}=\sigma(Z)\ne\F_0\). It is the natural filtration of \(X_t=tZ\): the path reveals \(Z\) at every \(t\gt0\), but not at \(t=0\).
  2. Part (2) first says “it is easy to verify that \(\sigma_a\) is an \((\F^B_t)\)-stopping time”, then that it is not. The displayed event is in \(\F^B_{t+}\), so the first claim should read “an \((\F^B_{t+})\)-stopping time”.
  3. The second displayed event, \(\{\sup_{[0,t]}B\gt a\}\cup\{\sup_{[0,t]}B=a\text{ and }\exists s\gt0:B_{t+s}\gt a\}\), is not \(\{\sigma_a\le t\}\): a crossing at a much later time \(t+s\) does not give \(\sigma_a\le t\). The second part should say “\(B_t=a\) and for every \(\varepsilon\gt0\) there is \(s\in(0,\varepsilon)\) with \(B_{t+s}\gt a\)”. It depends on the path after \(t\), which is why \(\sigma_a\) is not an \((\F^B_t)\)-stopping time. The notes also write “\(\sigma_a\) is complete for the completed natural filtration”, meaning it is a stopping time for it.

The notes conclude (p. 22): for large classes of processes including Brownian motion, the completed natural filtration is right-continuous. For Brownian motion this is Prop. 2.4 in disguise: every \(A\in\F_{s+}\) differs from an event of \(\F_s\) by a null set. For general processes it fails, so to show that first hitting times are stopping times “we must consider right-continuous and complete filtrations.”

Definition 3.5 (complete, completed filtration)notes p. 22

(a) \((\F_t)\) is complete if every negligible set belongs to \(\F_0\). (b) The completed filtration is \(\F^c_t=\sigma(\F_t\cup\cN)\), where \(\cN\) is the class of negligible sets.

Proposition 3.6 (usual augmentation)notes p. 22

(a) \((\F^c_t)\) is complete. (b) If \((\F_t)\) is right-continuous, \((\F^c_t)\) satisfies the usual conditions: it is complete and right-continuous. The usual augmentation of \((\F_t)\) is \((\F^c_{t+})\).

Key idea: what the usual conditions buy

Completeness: if \(X\) is adapted and \(Y\) is a modification of \(X\), then \(Y\) is adapted, since \(\{Y_t\in A\}\) differs from \(\{X_t\in A\}\) by a null set. An a.s. limit of adapted processes is adapted. So we can pass to continuous or càdlàg versions freely, as in Thm. 4.11 and Thm. 6.3. Right-continuity: “\(\{\tau\lt t\}\in\F_t\) for all \(t\)” is enough to be a stopping time (Exercise 3.8). Hitting times of open sets by right-continuous processes are stopping times (Thm. 3.20). Infima of stopping times are stopping times (Thm. 3.18). The notes: “In this course, if not specified otherwise, we will always consider filtered probability spaces satisfying the usual conditions” (p. 22).

Exercise 3.7notes p. 22

Using the Brownian example above, show that \(\sigma_a=\tau_a\) almost surely. Hints and a full solution: Exercise E3.

The mechanism: after \(\tau_a\), the path is \(a\) plus a fresh Brownian motion (strong Markov). A Brownian motion started at 0 takes positive values on every interval \([0,\varepsilon]\) (Blumenthal). So the path exceeds \(a\) immediately after touching it. Lab 3.1 measures the key ingredient: the probability that a Brownian path stays \(\le0\) on \([0,h]\) is 0, whatever \(h\).

Watch out: the notes' solution sketch of Exercise 3.7

It bounds \(\{\sigma_a\gt\tau_a\}\) by \(\{\exists t\ge0:\sup_{s\in[t,t+\varepsilon]}B_s=a\}\), with \(\varepsilon\) undefined, and invokes “the fact that Brownian motion crosses any level immediately after hitting it”, which is what has to be proved. The event to control is \(\{\exists\varepsilon\gt0: B\le a\text{ on }[\tau_a,\tau_a+\varepsilon]\}\). Its probability is 0 by the strong Markov property at \(\tau_a\) and Blumenthal's law (E3).

Lab 3.1 · Example 3.4, Exercise 3.7

Can a Brownian path stay below its starting point for a while?

Question: \(\sigma_a=\tau_a\) holds because a Brownian motion started at 0 cannot stay \(\le0\) on any interval \([0,h]\). On a grid of \(n\) points of \((0,h]\), the path values form a Gaussian random walk. The probability that it stays \(\le0\) has an exact formula, \(\binom{2n}{n}4^{-n}\approx1/\sqrt{\pi n}\) (Sparre Andersen's theorem, beyond the notes). We check it and watch it go to 0 as the grid refines.

What the lab shows (default settings unless stated)
  • With 16 grid points, \(\hat\P=\) 0.1441 against the exact 0.1399. With \(2^{11}=2048\) points, 0.0120 against 0.0125. The fitted log–log slope is −0.513 (theory \(-\frac12\)). Refining the grid can only add constraints, so the continuous-time probability is \(\lim_n\binom{2n}n4^{-n}=0\).
  • No dependence on \(h\). With \(h=10^{-6}\) instead of \(h=1\), the outputs are identical (0.0120 at the finest grid). With the same seed the paths are the same up to the factor \(\sqrt h\), which does not change signs. This is Brownian scaling: staying below 0 is just as impossible on a microsecond as on a year. It is the numerical face of Blumenthal's 0–1 law.
Exercise 3.8notes p. 22

Show that \(\tau\) is an \((\F_t)\)-stopping time if and only if \(\{\tau\lt t\}\in\F_t\) for every \(t\) [true when \((\F_t)\) is right-continuous; see below].

Watch out: Exercise 3.8 needs right-continuity

As stated, without right-continuity, Exercise 3.8 is false. The notes' own sketch proves only \(\{\tau\le s\}\in\F_{s+}\) for the converse. It then adds “in particular \(\{\tau\le s\}\in\F_s\) when the filtration is right-continuous”. In general, \(\{\tau\lt t\}\in\F_t\ \forall t\) characterizes \((\F_{t+})\)-stopping times, also called optional times. Counterexample: in the corrected Example 3.4 (1), take \(\tau=0\) on \(\{Z=1\}\) and \(\tau=1\) on \(\{Z=-1\}\). Then \(\{\tau\lt t\}\in\F_t\) for all \(t\), but \(\{\tau\le0\}=\{Z=1\}\notin\F_0\).

Exercise 3.9notes p. 22

Show that \(\tau\) is an \((\F_{t+})\)-stopping time if and only if \(\tau\wedge t\) is \(\F_t\)-measurable for every \(t\).

This one is correct as stated (Exercise E5). The version with \(\F_t\) in place of \(\F_{t+}\) is false: the same \(\tau\) as above has \(\tau\wedge t\) \(\F_t\)-measurable for every \(t\), yet it is not an \((\F_t)\)-stopping time.

§3.2 Measurability and stochastic processes

notes, pp. 23–24

The problem. The notes write: “Dealing with processes, one would like to know that if \((X_t)\) is a stochastic process, then \(X_\tau\ind_{\{\tau\lt\infty\}}\) is \(\F_\tau\)-measurable. This is not always the case” (p. 23). Similarly, \(\int_0^tH_s\,\dd s\) should be \(\F_t\)-measurable, which needs joint measurability in \((s,\omega)\) on \([0,t]\times\Omega\) with respect to \(\cB([0,t])\otimes\F_t\).

Definition 3.10notes p. 23

Let \((X_t)\) take values in \((E,\mathcal E)\). (a) \(X\) is measurable if \((t,\omega)\mapsto X_t(\omega)\) is \(\cB([0,\infty))\otimes\F/\mathcal E\)-measurable. (b) \(X\) is adapted if each \(X_t\) is \(\F_t/\mathcal E\)-measurable. (c) \(X\) is progressively measurable (progressive) if, for every \(t\gt0\), \((\omega,s)\in\Omega\times[0,t]\mapsto X_s(\omega)\) is \(\F_t\otimes\cB([0,t])/\mathcal E\)-measurable.

Progressive implies measurable and adapted. Progressive means that the whole past trajectory up to \(t\), as a function of \((\omega,s)\), is known at time \(t\) in a jointly measurable way.

Proposition 3.11notes p. 23

If \(X\) is right-continuous, it is measurable: \(X_t=\lim_nX_{\lceil2^nt\rceil/2^n}\) pointwise, and each approximation is measurable.

Proposition 3.12notes p. 23

If \(X\) is right-continuous and adapted, it is progressive.

Correct proof. Fix \(t\). For \(s\in[0,t]\) put \(X^{(n)}_0=X_0\) and \(X^{(n)}_s=X_{((k+1)2^{-n})\wedge t}\) for \(s\in(k2^{-n},(k+1)2^{-n}]\). Each \(X^{(n)}\) is a finite sum of terms \(\ind_{(\text{interval})}(s)\,X_{u}(\omega)\) with \(u\le t\), so it is \(\cB([0,t])\otimes\F_t\)-measurable. By right-continuity, \(X^{(n)}_s\to X_s\) for every \(s\), since the right endpoint decreases to \(s\). Pointwise limits of measurable maps are measurable.

Watch out: the notes' proof of Prop. 3.12 uses the wrong endpoint (p. 23)

The notes approximate by \(X^{(n)}_s=\sum_kX_{k/2^n}\ind_{(k/2^n,(k+1)/2^n]}(s)\), the value at the left endpoint. For a right-continuous process with jumps this converges to the left limit \(X_{s-}\), not to \(X_s\). Example: \(X_s=\ind\{s\ge1/2\}\) gives \(X^{(n)}_{1/2}=X_{1/2-2^{-n}}=0\ne X_{1/2}=1\). Left endpoints are the right choice for left-continuous processes. For right-continuous ones use the right endpoint, capped at \(t\) to stay \(\F_t\)-measurable. Prop. 3.11 uses \(\lceil2^nt\rceil/2^n\), which is correct.

Definition 3.13notes p. 23

\(A\in\cB(\R_+)\otimes\F\) is progressive if \((\omega,s)\mapsto\ind_A(s,\omega)\) is a progressive process.

Proposition 3.14notes p. 24

(1) The progressive sets form a σ-field, the progressive σ-field \(\cP\). (2) \(X\) is progressive if and only if \((t,\omega)\mapsto X_t(\omega)\) is \(\cP\)-measurable.

(1) is Exercise E7: “\(A\cap([0,t]\times\Omega)\in\cB([0,t])\otimes\F_t\) for all \(t\)” is stable under complements and countable unions. Lesson 6 builds stochastic integrals on the Hilbert space \(L^2\big([0,T]\times\Omega,\cP,\dd t\otimes\P\big)\) of progressive processes (Def. 6.4, Thm. 8.9).

Remark 3.15. A process can be measurable (even adapted) without being progressive. (Beyond the notes: every measurable adapted process has a progressive modification, by the Chung–Doob–Meyer theorem. With the usual conditions the distinction is therefore harmless for most purposes, but it matters for the definitions.)

§3.3 Stopping times and measurability

notes, pp. 24–26

The problem. We will constantly evaluate processes at stopping times (\(M_\tau\), \(B_{\tau+t}\)), compare two stopping times, and take limits of stopping times. We need to know what is measurable with respect to what.

Definition 3.16notes p. 24

For a stopping time \(\tau\), the notes define \[ \{A\in\F:\ A\cap\{\tau\lt t\}\in\F_t\ \ \forall t\ge0\}, \] calling it \(\F_{\tau-}\) (see the Watch out box: this set is \(\F_{\tau+}\)). The notes then list facts that are “repeatedly useful”:

  • \(\F_{\tau-}\subset\F_{\tau+}\);
  • if \((\F_t)\) is right-continuous, \(\F_\tau=\F_{\tau+}\);
  • if \(\tau\le t\) for a constant \(t\), then \(\F_\tau\subset\F_t\) and \(\F_{\tau+}\subset\F_{t+}\);
  • with \(\tilde\F_t=\F_{t+}\), \(\tilde\F_\tau=\F_{\tau+}\);
  • \(\tau\) is \(\F_\tau\)-measurable;
  • for \(A\in\F\), \(\tau^A=\tau\ind_A+\infty\ind_{A^c}\) is a stopping time if and only if \(A\in\F_\tau\).
Watch out: Definition 3.16 names the wrong σ-field (p. 24)

The set \(\{A: A\cap\{\tau\lt t\}\in\F_t\ \forall t\}\) is \(\F_{\tau+}\), the σ-field used in the strong Markov property. By Exercise 3.8 applied to \(\tau^A\), it is exactly the set of \(A\) with \(A\cap\{\tau\le t\}\in\F_{t+}\) for all \(t\). The standard strict past is different: \(\F_{\tau-}=\sigma\big(\F_0\cup\{A\cap\{t\lt\tau\}:A\in\F_t,\ t\ge0\}\big)\), the information strictly before \(\tau\). With the standard definitions, \(\F_{\tau-}\subset\F_\tau\subset\F_{\tau+}\), and the first bullet is then true. In the last bullet the notes write “for any \(A\in\F_\tau\), \(\tau^A\) is a stopping time if and only if \(A\in\F_\tau\)”, which is circular. It should start “for any \(A\in\F\)”.

Why \(\tau\) is \(\F_\tau\)-measurable (one line): \(\{\tau\le s\}\cap\{\tau\le t\}=\{\tau\le s\wedge t\}\in\F_{s\wedge t}\subset\F_t\).

Proposition 3.17notes pp. 24–25 (proof: exercise)

Let \(\sigma,\tau\) be stopping times. (a) If \(\sigma\le\tau\), then \(\F_\sigma\subset\F_\tau\) and \(\F_{\sigma+}\subset\F_{\tau+}\). (b) \(\sigma\wedge\tau\) and \(\sigma\vee\tau\) are stopping times, \(\F_{\sigma\wedge\tau}=\F_\sigma\cap\F_\tau\), and \(\{\sigma\lt\tau\},\{\sigma=\tau\},\{\sigma\le\tau\}\in\F_{\sigma\wedge\tau}\) (the notes print \(\{\sigma\lt t\}\) for the first). (c) \(\sigma+t\) is a stopping time for every constant \(t\ge0\).

Proofs: Exercise E6. The key identity for (a) is \(A\cap\{\tau\le t\}=(A\cap\{\sigma\le t\})\cap\{\tau\le t\}\) when \(\sigma\le\tau\). These facts drive optional stopping. For instance, the proof of Cor. 4.31 splits on \(\{\tau\lt\sigma\}\in\F_{\sigma\wedge\tau}\).

Lab 3.2 · Def. 2.11, preview of Lesson 4

Why stopping times? Martingale identities at six random times

Question: what goes wrong at a random time that is not a stopping time? For a bounded stopping time \(\tau\), optional stopping (Lesson 4, Thm. 4.30) gives \(\E[B_\tau]=0\) and \(\E[B_\tau^2-\tau]=0\): \(B\) and \(B^2-t\) are martingales (Example 4.3). We compute both quantities for six random times \(\tau\le1\), two of which are stopping times.

What the lab shows (default settings unless stated)
  • Stopping times (hitting time \(\tau_{0.5}\wedge1\); “stop at \(\frac12\) if \(B_{1/2}\gt0\), else at 1”): \(\E[B_\tau]=\) 0.006 and 0.010, and \(\E[B_\tau^2-\tau]=\) 0.005 and −0.006. All are zero within the Monte Carlo error (largest standard error 0.012).
  • Random times that look ahead break the identities. “Stop at \(\frac12\) if \(B_1\gt0\)”: \(\E[B_\tau]=\) −0.186 (exact \(-1/(2\sqrt{2\pi})=-0.1995\)). The time of the maximum: \(\E[B_\tau]=\) 0.782; this is \(\E[S_1]=\sqrt{2/\pi}\approx0.798\) minus a small grid bias. The last zero: \(\E[B_\tau^2-\tau]=\) −0.486, i.e. \(-\E[L]=-\frac12\). Stopping 0.1 before \(\tau_a\): \(\E[B_\tau]=\) −0.280.
  • A trap. For the “peek” time, \(\E[B_\tau^2-\tau]=\) −0.005 is zero anyway, by the symmetry \(B\mapsto-B\). One identity can hold by accident. Being a stopping time is what makes all of them hold at once.
Theorem 3.18notes p. 25

Let \((\tau_n)\) be a non-increasing sequence of stopping times and \(\tau=\inf_n\tau_n\). Then \(\tau\) is an \((\F_{t+})\)-stopping time [the notes say \((\F_t)\); true if \((\F_t)\) is right-continuous], and \(\F_{\tau+}=\bigcap_n\F_{\tau_n+}\). If \((\F_t)\) is right-continuous, \(\F_\tau=\bigcap_n\F_{\tau_n}\).

Why. \(\{\tau\lt t\}=\bigcup_n\{\tau_n\lt t\}\in\F_t\), and Exercise 3.8 (in its correct form) turns this into “\(\tau\) is an \((\F_{t+})\)-stopping time”. The notes write \(\bigcap_n\) instead of \(\bigcup_n\): an infimum is below \(t\) as soon as one term is. For the σ-fields: if \(A\in\bigcap_n\F_{\tau_n+}\), then \(A\cap\{\tau\lt t\}=\bigcup_n\big(A\cap\{\tau_n\lt t\}\big)\in\F_t\), so \(A\in\F_{\tau+}\). The reverse inclusion follows from \(\tau\le\tau_n\) and Prop. 3.17 (a).

Watch out: Theorem 3.18 as stated needs right-continuity

Counterexample (corrected Example 3.4 (1): \(\F_0\) trivial, \(\F_t=\sigma(Z)\) for \(t\gt0\), \(Z=\pm1\)). Let \(\tau_n=1/n\) on \(\{Z=1\}\) and \(\tau_n=1\) on \(\{Z=-1\}\). Each \(\tau_n\) is an \((\F_t)\)-stopping time, and they decrease. But \(\tau=\inf_n\tau_n\) equals 0 on \(\{Z=1\}\), and \(\{\tau\le0\}=\{Z=1\}\notin\F_0\). Under the usual conditions, which the course assumes, the statement is correct.

This is the result behind every “approximate \(\tau\) from above by dyadic stopping times” argument: Thm. 2.15, Thm. 4.29, Thm. 4.35.

Lab 3.3 · Thm. 3.18 and its uses

Round a stopping time up, not down

Question: proofs replace a stopping time \(\tau\) by the dyadic times \(\lceil2^n\tau\rceil/2^n\downarrow\tau\), which are again stopping times. Why not \(\lfloor2^n\tau\rfloor/2^n\uparrow\tau\)? We take \(\tau=\tau_a\wedge1\) on a fine grid (\(2^{12}\) steps) and compute \(\E[B]\) at both roundings for grid levels \(n=1,\dots,10\).

What the lab shows (default settings unless stated)
  • Rounded up: \(\E[B]=\) −0.0065 at \(n=4\) and −0.0090 at \(n=10\). Zero within the standard error (0.0101) at every level, because each rounded-up time is a bounded stopping time.
  • Rounded down: \(\E[B]=\) −0.1747 at \(n=4\) and −0.0235 at \(n=10\). Rounding down picks a time before \(\tau\), chosen because \(B\) was about to reach \(a\). That uses the future, and it biases \(\E[B]\) below 0. The bias vanishes only in the limit, by continuity.
  • The reference code (20,000 independent paths on a 1000-step grid) confirms the pattern: \(-0.289\), \(-0.162\), \(-0.081\), \(-0.044\) for \(n=2,4,6,8\) when rounding down, against \(|\E B|\le0.002\) when rounding up.
Theorem 3.19notes p. 25

If \((X_t)\) is \((\F_t)\)-progressive with values in \(\R^d\) and \(\tau\) is a stopping time, then \(X_\tau\ind_{\{\tau\lt\infty\}}\) is \(\F_\tau\)-measurable.

In one sentenceA progressive process stopped at a stopping time is known at that time.
ProofFix \(t\) and a Borel set \(A\). On \(\{\tau\le t\}\), \(X_\tau=X_{\tau\wedge t}\), so \(\{X_\tau\ind_{\{\tau\lt\infty\}}\in A\}\cap\{\tau\le t\}=\{X_{\tau\wedge t}\in A\}\cap\{\tau\le t\}\). The map \(\omega\mapsto(\omega,\tau(\omega)\wedge t)\) is \(\F_t/\F_t\otimes\cB([0,t])\)-measurable, since \(\tau\wedge t\) is \(\F_t\)-measurable. Composing with the progressive map \((\omega,s)\mapsto X_s(\omega)\) on \(\Omega\times[0,t]\) shows that \(X_{\tau\wedge t}\) is \(\F_t\)-measurable.
Why progressiveComposition needs joint measurability of \((\omega,s)\mapsto X_s(\omega)\) with respect to \(\F_t\otimes\cB([0,t])\). Adaptedness alone gives nothing about \(X\) at the random time \(\tau(\omega)\). This is the whole point of Definition 3.10 (c).
Used again\(B_\tau\) in the strong Markov property (Prop. 2.20), \(M_\tau\) in optional stopping (Thm. 4.29), stopped processes \(M^\tau\) (Rem. 5.25).
Theorem 3.20 (hitting times)notes p. 25 (admitted)

If \((\F_t)\) satisfies the usual conditions and \(X\) is progressive and right-continuous, then \(\tau_A=\inf\{t\ge0:X_t\in A\}\) is a stopping time whenever \(A\) is open or closed.

(Beyond the notes: under the usual conditions, the début theorem gives much more: \(\tau_A\) is a stopping time for every Borel set \(A\) and every progressive \(X\). Its proof uses capacity theory, which is why courses admit it.)

Theorem 3.21notes p. 26 (proof: exercise)

As printed: let \((\F_t)\) be a complete filtration, \(X\) right-continuous in \(\R^d\), \(A\in\cB(\R^d)\) and \(\tau_A=\inf\{t\ge0:X_t\in A\}\). (a) If \(A\) is open, \(\tau_A\) is a stopping time. (b) If \(A\) is closed, \(\tau_A\) is a stopping time.

Watch out: Theorem 3.21 is false as stated

“Complete” is not enough; the filtration must be right-continuous, and \(X\) must be adapted (the notes do not say so). Counterexample for (a): \(X_t=tZ\) with \(Z=\pm1\), its natural filtration completed, and \(A=(0,\infty)\) open. Then \(\tau_A=0\) on \(\{Z=1\}\) and \(\tau_A=\infty\) on \(\{Z=-1\}\), so \(\{\tau_A\le0\}=\{Z=1\}\). But \(\F_0\) contains only events of probability 0 or 1, and \(\P(Z=1)=\frac12\). The correct statements (Exercise E8):

  • (a′) \(A\) open and \(X\) right-continuous and adapted: \(\tau_A\) is an \((\F_{t+})\)-stopping time. It is a stopping time under right-continuity.
  • (b′) \(A\) closed and \(X\) continuous and adapted: \(\tau_A\) is a stopping time, with no condition on the filtration.

For closed \(A\) and merely right-continuous \(X\), one needs the usual conditions (Thm. 3.20).

Exercises

The chapter is full of “Exercise” proofs. Here they are, in the order of the notes, with the corrections discussed above.

E1 · Proposition 3.3 notes p. 21: “Proof. Exercise.”

Show that \((\F_{t+})\) is a filtration and is right-continuous.

Solution

For \(s\le t\): \(\F_{s+}=\bigcap_{u\gt s}\F_u\subset\bigcap_{u\gt t}\F_u=\F_{t+}\), since the second intersection is over fewer, larger σ-fields. Intersections of σ-fields are σ-fields. Right-continuity: \(\bigcap_{s\gt t}\F_{s+}=\bigcap_{s\gt t}\bigcap_{u\gt s}\F_u=\bigcap_{u\gt t}\F_u=\F_{t+}\).

E2 · Proposition 3.6 (a) notes p. 22: “Proof. Exercise.”

Show that \(\F^c_t=\sigma(\F_t\cup\cN)\) is complete. Show also that \(A\in\F^c_t\) if and only if \(A\) differs from some \(A'\in\F_t\) by a subset of a null set.

Solution

\(\cN\subset\F^c_0\subset\F^c_t\), so the filtration is complete by definition. For the description: the class \(\{A:\exists A'\in\F_t,\ A\triangle A'\subset N\in\cN\}\) contains \(\F_t\) and \(\cN\). It is a σ-field, because symmetric differences behave well under complements and countable unions. And it is contained in any σ-field containing \(\F_t\cup\cN\). (b) needs a little more care: one checks \(\bigcap_{s\gt t}\F^c_s=(\F_{t+})^c\), using the description above and a sequence \(s_n\downarrow t\).

E3 · Exercise 3.7: \(\sigma_a=\tau_a\) a.s. notes p. 22
Hint 1

\(\sigma_a\ge\tau_a\) always, by continuity. Write \(\{\sigma_a\gt\tau_a\}\) in terms of \(\tilde B_s=B_{\tau_a+s}-a\).

Hint 2

By the strong Markov property, \(\tilde B\) is a Brownian motion. What does Blumenthal's law say about \(\{\exists\varepsilon\gt0:\tilde B\le0\text{ on }[0,\varepsilon]\}\)?

Solution

If \(\sigma_a\gt\tau_a\), then \(B\le a\) on \([\tau_a,\sigma_a)\), so \(\tilde B\le0\) on \([0,\varepsilon]\) with \(\varepsilon=\sigma_a-\tau_a\gt0\). The event \(E=\{\exists\varepsilon\gt0:\tilde B_s\le0\ \forall s\le\varepsilon\}\) is in the germ σ-field of \(\tilde B\), and \(\tilde B\) is a Brownian motion (Thm. 2.15; \(\tau_a\lt\infty\) a.s.). By Blumenthal's law (Cor. 2.9) and the discussion after it, \(\P(E)=0\): a Brownian motion started at 0 takes positive values in every \([0,\varepsilon]\). Hence \(\P(\sigma_a\gt\tau_a)=0\). Lab 3.1 checks the quantitative version: \(\P(\text{stay}\le0\text{ at }n\text{ grid points})=\binom{2n}n4^{-n}\to0\).

E4 · Exercise 3.8 (corrected) notes p. 22

Show: (i) every stopping time satisfies \(\{\tau\lt t\}\in\F_t\) for all \(t\). (ii) \(\{\tau\lt t\}\in\F_t\ \forall t\) if and only if \(\tau\) is an \((\F_{t+})\)-stopping time. (iii) The two notions coincide when \((\F_t)\) is right-continuous.

Solution

(i) \(\{\tau\lt t\}=\bigcup_n\{\tau\le t-1/n\}\), with each term in \(\F_{t-1/n}\subset\F_t\). (ii) If \(\{\tau\lt u\}\in\F_u\) for all \(u\), then \(\{\tau\le s\}=\bigcap_n\{\tau\lt s+1/n\}\), and for each \(u\gt s\) the terms with \(s+1/n\lt u\) lie in \(\F_u\), so \(\{\tau\le s\}\in\bigcap_{u\gt s}\F_u=\F_{s+}\). Conversely, if \(\{\tau\le s\}\in\F_{s+}\) for all \(s\), then \(\{\tau\lt t\}=\bigcup_n\{\tau\le t-1/n\}\) and \(\F_{(t-1/n)+}\subset\F_t\). (iii) is immediate. The counterexample to the unconditional statement is in the Watch out box above.

E5 · Exercise 3.9 notes p. 22

Show that \(\tau\) is an \((\F_{t+})\)-stopping time if and only if \(\tau\wedge t\) is \(\F_t\)-measurable for every \(t\).

Solution

(⇒) For \(s\lt t\), \(\{\tau\wedge t\le s\}=\{\tau\le s\}\in\F_{s+}\subset\F_t\); for \(s\ge t\) it is \(\Omega\). (⇐) For \(t\gt s\), \(\{\tau\le s\}=\{\tau\wedge t\le s\}\in\F_t\). Intersecting over \(t\gt s\) gives \(\{\tau\le s\}\in\F_{s+}\). The \(\F_t\) version is false: in the corrected Example 3.4 (1), \(\tau=0\) on \(\{Z=1\}\) and \(1\) on \(\{Z=-1\}\). Then \(\tau\wedge t\) is \(\F_t\)-measurable for all \(t\), but \(\{\tau\le0\}\notin\F_0\).

E6 · Proposition 3.17 notes p. 25: “Proof. Exercise.”
Solution

(a) If \(A\in\F_\sigma\) and \(\sigma\le\tau\), then \(A\cap\{\tau\le t\}=\big(A\cap\{\sigma\le t\}\big)\cap\{\tau\le t\}\in\F_t\). The same with \(\F_{t+}\).

(b) \(\{\sigma\wedge\tau\le t\}=\{\sigma\le t\}\cup\{\tau\le t\}\) and \(\{\sigma\vee\tau\le t\}=\{\sigma\le t\}\cap\{\tau\le t\}\). By (a), \(\F_{\sigma\wedge\tau}\subset\F_\sigma\cap\F_\tau\). Conversely, if \(A\in\F_\sigma\cap\F_\tau\), then \(A\cap\{\sigma\wedge\tau\le t\}=(A\cap\{\sigma\le t\})\cup(A\cap\{\tau\le t\})\in\F_t\). For \(\{\sigma\lt\tau\}\):

\[ \begin{aligned}&\{\sigma\lt\tau\}\cap\{\sigma\wedge\tau\le t\}\\ &\quad=\bigcup_{r\in\Qb\cap[0,t]}\{\sigma\le r\lt\tau\}\ \cup\ \{\sigma\le t\lt\tau\}\in\F_t,\end{aligned} \]

so \(\{\sigma\lt\tau\}\in\F_{\sigma\wedge\tau}\). The other two events follow by symmetry and complements.

(c) \(\{\sigma+t\le u\}=\{\sigma\le u-t\}\in\F_{u-t}\subset\F_u\) for \(u\ge t\), and it is \(\varnothing\) for \(u\lt t\).

E7 · The progressive σ-field notes p. 24, Prop. 3.14 (1): “Left as an exercise.”
Solution

\(A\) is progressive iff \(A\cap([0,t]\times\Omega)\in\cB([0,t])\otimes\F_t\) for all \(t\). This contains \(\R_+\times\Omega\) and is stable under complements, since \((A^c)\cap([0,t]\times\Omega)=([0,t]\times\Omega)\setminus(A\cap[0,t]\times\Omega)\). It is stable under countable unions, since intersections distribute. So it is a σ-field.

E8 · Theorem 3.21, corrected notes p. 26: “Proof. Left as an exercise.”

(a′) \(A\) open, \(X\) right-continuous and adapted: show \(\{\tau_A\lt t\}\in\F_t\). (b′) \(A\) closed, \(X\) continuous and adapted: show \(\{\tau_A\le t\}\in\F_t\).

Solution

(a′) \(\{\tau_A\lt t\}=\bigcup_{s\in\Qb\cap[0,t)}\{X_s\in A\}\). If \(X_u\in A\) for some \(u\lt t\), then by right-continuity and openness \(X_s\in A\) for all \(s\) slightly larger than \(u\), including rational \(s\lt t\). So \(\tau_A\) is an \((\F_{t+})\)-stopping time (E4). (b′) Let \(d(x,A)\) be the distance to \(A\), a continuous function. Then \(\{\tau_A\le t\}=\big\{\inf_{s\in(\Qb\cap[0,t])\cup\{t\}}d(X_s,A)=0\big\}\). If the infimum is 0, a minimizing sequence \(s_k\) has a limit point \(s\le t\) with \(d(X_s,A)=0\), by continuity; \(A\) is closed, so \(X_s\in A\). The counterexample \(X_t=tZ\) shows (a′) cannot be upgraded to “stopping time” without right-continuity.

Conclusion

  • Usual conditions. Complete plus right-continuous (Prop. 3.6). Completeness makes modifications and a.s. limits adapted. Right-continuity turns “optional times” into stopping times, makes infima of stopping times stopping times, and makes hitting times of open sets stopping times. For Brownian motion the completed natural filtration already satisfies them.
  • Progressive processes. Joint measurability of the past (Def. 3.10), automatic for right-continuous adapted processes (Prop. 3.12, with the right endpoint). It makes \(X_\tau\) \(\F_\tau\)-measurable (Thm. 3.19), and it is the class of stochastic integrands (Lessons 5–6).
  • Stopping-time calculus. \(\F_\sigma\subset\F_\tau\) for \(\sigma\le\tau\), \(\F_{\sigma\wedge\tau}=\F_\sigma\cap\F_\tau\), and comparison events such as \(\{\sigma\lt\tau\}\in\F_{\sigma\wedge\tau}\) (Prop. 3.17). Approximation from above by dyadic stopping times (Thm. 3.18; Lab 3.3).
  • Why it matters. Stopping times are exactly the random times at which martingale identities survive (Lab 3.2). Lesson 4 turns this into the optional stopping theorems.
  • Errors in this chapter of the notes are more frequent than elsewhere (Examples 3.4, Exercise 3.8, Prop. 3.12's proof, Def. 3.16, Thm. 3.18, Thm. 3.21). All are fixed on this page with counterexamples. Under the usual conditions most of the statements become true.

Reference implementation (Python / NumPy)

Run as shown; the output is verbatim. It checks Lab 3.1 against the exact formula (scipy.special.comb) and repeats Labs 3.2–3.3 with independent random numbers.

"""Lesson 3 — reference checks (NumPy/SciPy): immediate crossing, stopping vs non-stopping times, dyadic rounding."""
import numpy as np
from scipy import special, stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)

print("1) Sparre Andersen: P(S_1<=0,...,S_n<=0) = C(2n,n)/4^n for a Gaussian random walk (any time scale)")
M = 200000
for n in [4, 16, 64, 256]:
    S = np.cumsum(rng.standard_normal((M, n)), axis=1)
    p = np.mean((S <= 0).all(axis=1))
    print(f"   n={n:4d}: simulated {p:.4f}  exact {special.comb(2*n, n)/4**n:.4f}  1/sqrt(pi n) = {1/np.sqrt(np.pi*n):.4f}")

print("2) E[B_tau] and E[B_tau^2 - tau] for random times tau <= 1 (grid of 1000 steps, 20000 paths)")
n, M, a = 1000, 20000, 0.5
B = np.concatenate([np.zeros((M, 1)), np.cumsum(rng.standard_normal((M, n)) / np.sqrt(n), axis=1)], axis=1)
t = np.arange(n + 1) / n
hit = np.where((B >= a).any(axis=1), (B >= a).argmax(axis=1), n)          # tau_a ^ 1 on the grid
half = np.where(B[:, n // 2] > 0, n // 2, n)                              # decision at time 1/2: stopping time
peek = np.where(B[:, n] > 0, n // 2, n)                                   # decision uses B_1: not a stopping time
argmax = B.argmax(axis=1)                                                 # time of the maximum: not a stopping time
rows = np.arange(M)
for name, idx in [("tau_a ^ 1", hit), ("1/2 if B_1/2>0 else 1", half), ("1/2 if B_1>0 else 1", peek), ("argmax", argmax)]:
    b = B[rows, idx]
    print(f"   {name:24s}: E[B_tau] = {b.mean():+.3f} (se {b.std()/np.sqrt(M):.3f}),  E[B_tau^2 - tau] = {np.mean(b**2 - t[idx]):+.3f}")
print(f"   exact E[B_tau] for the 'peek' time: -1/(2 sqrt(2 pi)) = {-0.5/np.sqrt(2*np.pi):.4f}; for argmax: E[S_1] = sqrt(2/pi) = {np.sqrt(2/np.pi):.4f} (minus grid bias)")

print("3) Rounding tau = tau_a ^ 1 to the dyadic grid: up (stopping time) vs down (anticipating)")
for k in [2, 4, 6, 8]:
    step = n / 2**k
    up = np.minimum(n, np.ceil(hit / step) * step).astype(int)
    dn = (np.floor(hit / step) * step).astype(int)
    print(f"   2^-{k}: E[B at rounded-up time] = {B[rows, up].mean():+.4f}   E[B at rounded-down time] = {B[rows, dn].mean():+.4f}")

Cheat sheet

Usual conditions

\(\cN\subset\F_0\) (complete) and \(\F_t=\F_{t+}\) (right-continuous). Usual augmentation: \((\F^c_{t+})\). Brownian filtration: already OK after completion.

Optional vs stopping

\(\{\tau\lt t\}\in\F_t\ \forall t\iff\tau\) is an \((\F_{t+})\)-stopping time \(\iff\tau\wedge t\in\F_t\ \forall t\). The same as a stopping time under right-continuity.

Measurability

Progressive ⇒ measurable and adapted. Right-continuous and adapted ⇒ progressive (approximate by right endpoints). Progressive ⇒ \(X_\tau\ind_{\{\tau\lt\infty\}}\in\F_\tau\).

\(\F_\tau\) rules

\(\sigma\le\tau\Rightarrow\F_\sigma\subset\F_\tau\). \(\F_{\sigma\wedge\tau}=\F_\sigma\cap\F_\tau\). \(\{\sigma\lt\tau\},\{\sigma\le\tau\}\in\F_{\sigma\wedge\tau}\). \(\tau\) is \(\F_\tau\)-measurable. \(\sigma+t\) is a stopping time.

Limits

Decreasing limits of stopping times are \((\F_{t+})\)-stopping times, and \(\F_{\tau+}=\bigcap\F_{\tau_n+}\). Round up: \(\lceil2^n\tau\rceil/2^n\) is a stopping time; rounding down is not.

Hitting times

Open \(A\) + right-continuous: optional time. Closed \(A\) + continuous: stopping time. Usual conditions: any Borel \(A\) (début theorem). \(\tau_a=\sigma_a\) a.s. for BM.

Self-test

1. Give a filtration that is not right-continuous.

\(\F_0\) trivial, \(\F_t=\sigma(Z)\) for \(t\gt0\): the natural filtration of \(X_t=tZ\). Then \(\F_{0+}=\sigma(Z)\).

2. What is wrong with the notes' Example 3.4 (1)?

“\(\F_t\) trivial while \(\F_0=\sigma(Z)\)” decreases, so it is not a filtration. Swap the roles.

3. Why is \(\sigma_a=\inf\{t:B_t\gt a\}\) not an \((\F^B_t)\)-stopping time?

Deciding \(\{\sigma_a\le t\}\) when \(B\) touches \(a\) exactly at \(t\) requires seeing the path just after \(t\).

4. Why is \(\sigma_a=\tau_a\) a.s.?

Strong Markov at \(\tau_a\) plus Blumenthal: a Brownian motion started at 0 is positive at times arbitrarily close to 0.

5. What does completeness of the filtration buy?

Modifications and a.s. limits of adapted processes are adapted.

6. What does right-continuity buy?

“\(\{\tau\lt t\}\in\F_t\)” suffices for a stopping time; hitting times of open sets and infima of stopping times are stopping times; \(\F_\tau=\F_{\tau+}\).

7. Is “\(\tau\wedge t\) is \(\F_t\)-measurable for all \(t\)” equivalent to “\(\tau\) is an \((\F_t)\)-stopping time”?

No, only to “\(\tau\) is an \((\F_{t+})\)-stopping time” (Exercise 3.9).

8. Define progressive measurability in words.

For every \(t\), the map \((\omega,s)\mapsto X_s(\omega)\) on \(\Omega\times[0,t]\) is jointly measurable for \(\F_t\otimes\cB([0,t])\).

9. Why must right-continuous processes be approximated by right endpoints?

Left endpoints converge to left limits \(X_{s-}\), which differ from \(X_s\) at jumps.

10. Why do we want \(X_\tau\) to be \(\F_\tau\)-measurable, and what guarantees it?

To condition on the state at a stopping time (strong Markov, optional stopping). Progressive measurability guarantees it (Thm. 3.19).

11. Show \(\F_\sigma\subset\F_\tau\) when \(\sigma\le\tau\).

\(A\cap\{\tau\le t\}=A\cap\{\sigma\le t\}\cap\{\tau\le t\}\).

12. Which σ-field does the notes' Definition 3.16 really define?

\(\F_{\tau+}\), not the strict past \(\F_{\tau-}\).

13. Is \(\inf_n\tau_n\) of decreasing stopping times a stopping time?

It is an \((\F_{t+})\)-stopping time; a stopping time under right-continuity. The counterexample uses \(\tau_n=1/n\) on \(\{Z=1\}\).

14. Why round a stopping time up, not down, in approximation arguments?

\(\lceil2^n\tau\rceil/2^n\) is a stopping time; \(\lfloor2^n\tau\rfloor/2^n\) anticipates. Lab 3.3: −0.1747 against −0.0065 at \(n=4\).

15. Is the hitting time of an open set by a right-continuous adapted process a stopping time?

An \((\F_{t+})\)-stopping time; a stopping time under right-continuity. Not with a merely complete filtration (\(X_t=tZ\)).

16. Is the hitting time of a closed set by a continuous adapted process a stopping time?

Yes, for any filtration: use the countable infimum of \(d(X_s,A)\).

17. Why does E[B at the time of the maximum on [0,1]] differ from 0?

That time is not a stopping time; the value is \(\E S_1=\sqrt{2/\pi}\approx0.80\). Lab 3.2: 0.782 on a grid.

18. What is \(\P(B\le0\text{ at }n\text{ equally spaced times in }(0,h])\)?

\(\binom{2n}n4^{-n}\approx1/\sqrt{\pi n}\), whatever \(h\) (Sparre Andersen).
