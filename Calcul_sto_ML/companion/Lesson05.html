Continuous Semi-Martingales

Which processes can serve as integrators, and how do we measure the roughness of a continuous martingale?

§0 Where this lesson sits

The notes announce the plan in one sentence: “Here we develop a general theory of stochastic integrals with respect to stochastic processes. We first consider processes with bounded variation, second martingale-like processes, and finally processes which are sums of the two” (p. 38). This lesson builds the three kinds of integrators. Lesson 6 builds the integral.

What it reuses.

  • From Lesson 2: quadratic variation of Brownian motion (Prop. 2.21) and the dichotomy “finite variation ⇔ zero quadratic variation” for continuous paths (Cor. 2.22). Theorem 5.23 generalizes the first, and Theorem 5.22 turns the second into a uniqueness theorem.
  • From Lesson 3: stopping times, stopped processes and progressive processes (Def. 3.10, Thm. 3.19). Local martingales are defined by stopping, and integrands must be progressive (Prop. 5.17).
  • From Lesson 4: stopped martingales are martingales (Cor. 4.32), Fatou and dominated convergence for conditional expectations, Doob's \(L^2\) inequality (Thm. 4.6), and uniform integrability (Thm. 4.24). The notes' sketch of Remark 5.15 even uses martingale convergence to compute a total variation.

What it prepares.

  • Lesson 6 defines \(\int H\,\dd M\) through the bracket: \(\br{H\cdot M,N}=H\cdot\br{M,N}\) (Thm. 6.6). It uses Kunita–Watanabe to make this well defined, and it defines \(\int H\,\dd X\) for semimartingales as a Stieltjes integral plus a martingale integral (Def. 6.16).
  • Lesson 7: Proposition 5.44 (weighted sums of squared increments converge to \(\int H\,\dd\br{X}\)) is exactly the second-order term in the proof of Itô's formula (Thm. 7.1). Lévy's characterization identifies Brownian motion by its bracket \(\br{M}_t=t\) (Thm. 7.8). Girsanov's drift is a cross-bracket \(\br{M,L}\) (Thm. 7.13).
  • Lesson 8: the solution of an SDE is a semimartingale whose bracket is \(\int\sigma\sigma^\top\,\dd s\).

A question that comes back

“Which random processes can we integrate against, and what intrinsic quantity measures their roughness?” Lesson 2 gave the Brownian answer: Brownian motion is too rough for Stieltjes integration (infinite variation), and its roughness is measured by \(\sum(\Delta B)^2\to t\). Here the answer is general. The integrators are continuous semimartingales: finite-variation part plus local-martingale part. The roughness is the bracket \(\br{M}\), which sees only the martingale part. Lesson 6 asks the question again for integrands (which \(H\)?), and Lesson 7 for functions (how does \(f(X)\) move?).

§5.1 Bounded variation functions and processes

notes, pp. 38–41

The problem. For a differentiable \(f\), \(\int g(s)f'(s)\,\dd s\) is ordinary calculus. Many integrators are not differentiable: jumps, staircases, Cantor-type functions. Yet “\(\int g\,\dd f\)” still makes sense whenever \(f\) moves by a finite total amount. The right notion is bounded variation, and the integral is against a measure built from \(f\).

Definition 5.1 (total variation)notes p. 38

\(f:I\to\R\) is of bounded variation (BV) on \([a,b]\subset I\) if \[ \TV([a,b],f)=\sup\Big\{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}|f(t_{i+1})-f(t_i)|\Big\}\lt\infty, \] where the supremum is over all \(n\in\N\) and all meshes \(a=t_0\lt\dots\lt t_n=b\).

Remark 5.2 in the notes reads “If \(I=[n,M]\), it is enough to check that \(\TV([n,M],f)\lt\infty\)”. This is garbled. The intended content: BV on an interval means BV on each compact subinterval, and for a compact interval one only has to check the whole interval, since \(\TV\) is additive over adjacent intervals.

Proposition 5.3 (Jordan decomposition)notes p. 38

A BV function is a difference of two non-decreasing functions: \(f=F_+-F_-\). One choice is \(F_+(t)=\TV([a,t],f)\) and \(F_-=F_+-f\). The decomposition is not unique (Remark 5.4): add the same non-decreasing function to both.

Why \(F_-\) is non-decreasing. For \(s\le t\), \(F_-(t)-F_-(s)=\TV([s,t],f)-(f(t)-f(s))\ge0\), since the total variation over \([s,t]\) is at least \(|f(t)-f(s)|\). The notes' proof inserts a superfluous \(\varepsilon\).

Theorem 5.5 (Stieltjes measure)notes pp. 38–39

If \(F\) is non-decreasing on a compact interval \(I\), there is a unique finite measure \(\dd F\) on \((I,\cB(I))\) with \(\dd F((s,t])=F(t)-F(s)\) for \(s\le t\) (and \(\dd F([s,t])=F(t)-F(s)\) when \(F\) is continuous).

In one sentenceA non-decreasing function is the distribution function of a measure.
Proof in the notes (a sampler)For continuous \(F\), let \(F^{-1}(u)=\inf\{t:F(t)\ge u\}\) be the generalized inverse, and \(U\) uniform on \(F(I)\). Then \(\P(F^{-1}(U)\le t)=\P(U\le F(t))\), so the law \(\pi\) of \(F^{-1}(U)\) satisfies \(\pi((s,t])=(F(t)-F(s))/|F(I)|\), and \(\dd F=|F(I)|\,\pi\). This is the inverse transform sampling used by every random-number library. Uniqueness is π–λ on intervals.
Watch outThe notes write \(\dd F([s,t])=F(t)-F(s)\). For a jump at \(s\) the closed interval would need \(F(t)-F(s-)\); the correct general statement uses half-open intervals \((s,t]\) and right-continuous \(F\). The notes only treat continuous \(F\) and say the general case “follows by the standard extension theorems”.
Used again\(\dd A_t(\omega)\) for every finite-variation path, and \(\dd\br{M}_t\) for brackets (non-decreasing, continuous).
Definition 5.6 (signed Stieltjes measure)notes p. 39

For \(f\) BV with \(f=F_+-F_-\), set \(\dd f=\dd F_+-\dd F_-\). It does not depend on the decomposition: both sides give \(f(t)-f(s)\) on intervals, and π–λ concludes.

Theorem 5.7 (Hahn–Jordan)notes p. 39

There are unique measures \(\dd f^+\), \(\dd f^-\) with \(\dd f=\dd f^+-\dd f^-\) and \(\dd f^+\perp\dd f^-\) (mutually singular: carried by disjoint sets). Theorem 5.8 is the same statement for any difference of finite measures.

Definitions 5.9–5.10 (total variation measure)notes p. 40

\(|\dd f|=\dd f^++\dd f^-\), and \(|\dd f|(I)\) is the total variation of \(f\). Likewise \(|\mu|=\mu^++\mu^-\) for a signed measure.

Exercise 5.11 asks to show that “\(f\) has density with respect to \(|\dd f|\) given by \(\ind_N-\ind_{N^c}\)”. It should read \(\dd f\) has density \(\ind_N-\ind_{N^c}\) with respect to \(|\dd f|\), where \(N\) carries \(\dd f^+\) (see E1).

Definition 5.12 (Stieltjes integral)notes p. 40

For bounded Borel \(g\) with \(\int|g|\,|\dd f|\lt\infty\): \(\int g\,\dd f=\int g\,\dd f^+-\int g\,\dd f^-\).

Proposition 5.13notes p. 40

For \(f\) BV, \(g\) bounded measurable and \([a,b]\subset I\): (1) \(\int_{[a,b]}g\,\dd f=\int_{[a,b]}g\,\dd f^+-\int_{[a,b]}g\,\dd f^-\); (2) \(\big|\int_{[a,b]}g\,\dd f\big|\le\int_{[a,b]}|g|\,|\dd f|\); (3) \(G(t)=\int_{[a,t]}g\,\dd f\) is BV with \(\dd G=g\,\dd f\), \(\dd G^+=g^+\dd f^++g^-\dd f^-\) and \(\dd G^-=g^-\dd f^++g^+\dd f^-\) (corrected); (4) if \(f\in C^1(I)\), then \(\dd f=f'(t)\,\dd t\) and \(|\dd f|=|f'(t)|\,\dd t\).

Watch out: Proposition 5.13 (3) as printed (p. 40)

The notes write \(\dd G^+=g\,\dd f^++g\,\dd f^-\) and \(\dd G^-=g\,\dd f^-+g\,\dd f^+\) “up to the obvious sign convention when \(g\) changes sign”. As printed, \(\dd G^+=\dd G^-=g\,|\dd f|\), which would force \(\dd G=0\). The positive part of \(g\,\dd f\) comes from \(g\ge0\) on the support of \(\dd f^+\) and from \(g\le0\) on the support of \(\dd f^-\). Hence the corrected formulas above; these are measures, since \(g^\pm\ge0\).

Watch out: Prop. 5.13 (4) does not extend to “continuous”

(4) needs \(f\in C^1\), or at least absolute continuity. A continuous BV function can have a Stieltjes measure with no density at all. The Cantor function \(F\) is continuous, non-decreasing, and \(F'=0\) almost everywhere. Yet \(\dd F\) is a probability measure, carried by the Cantor set, which has Lebesgue measure 0. So \(\int g\,\dd F\ne\int gF'\,\dd t=0\). Lab 5.1 samples this measure with the notes' own construction.

Lab 5.1 · Thm. 5.5 and Prop. 5.13 (4)

Proposition 5.14 (Riemann–Stieltjes sums, corrected)notes p. 41

Let \(f\) be BV and \(g\) continuous on \([a,b]\). For every sequence of meshes of \([a,b]\) whose size tends to 0, \(\sum_ig(t^n_i)\big(f(t^n_{i+1})-f(t^n_i)\big)\to\int_{(a,b]}g\,\dd f\).

Watch out: Proposition 5.14 needs continuity of g

The notes assume only “\(g\) measurable and bounded”. That fails even for smooth \(f\). With \(f(t)=t\) and \(g=\ind_{\Qb}\), \(\int g\,\dd f=\text{Leb}(\Qb\cap[a,b])=0\), yet every mesh with rational points has Riemann sums equal to \(b-a\). With a jump the evaluation point also matters. For \(f=g=\ind_{[1/2,1]}\) on meshes containing \(1/2\), left-point sums are 0 and right-point sums are 1 (script errata_checks.py). The proof for continuous \(g\), by uniform continuity:

\[ \begin{aligned}&\Big|\sum g(t_i)\Delta f-\int g\,\dd f\Big|\\ &\qquad\le\sup_{|s-u|\le\text{mesh}}|g(s)-g(u)|\cdot|\dd f|([a,b])\to0.\end{aligned} \]
Remark 5.15 (comparison with total variation)notes p. 41

\(|\dd f|([a,b])=\sup\big\{\sum_i|f(t_{i+1})-f(t_i)|\big\}\), the supremum over meshes of \([a,b]\): the total variation measure has mass equal to the total variation.

The notes' proof idea, spelled out (a martingale argument). Normalize so that \(|\dd f|\) is a probability \(\mu\) on \([0,1]\). Let \(h=\ind_N-\ind_{N^c}\) be the density of \(\dd f\) with respect to \(\mu\) (Exercise 5.11), so \(|h|=1\). Let \(\G_n\) be the σ-field generated by the dyadic intervals \(I_{n,k}\). Then \(h_n=\E_\mu[h\mid\G_n]\) equals \(\dd f(I_{n,k})/\mu(I_{n,k})\) on \(I_{n,k}\). \((h_n)\) is a bounded martingale, so it converges \(\mu\)-a.s. to \(\E[h\mid\G_\infty]=h\) (the dyadic intervals generate the Borel σ-field). By bounded convergence, \(\int|h_n|\,\dd\mu=\sum_k|\dd f(I_{n,k})|\to\int|h|\,\dd\mu=1\). The sums on the left are sums of \(|f(t_{i+1})-f(t_i)|\) (for continuous \(f\)), so they approach the total mass. This is Lesson 4 used to prove a measure-theory fact.

Lab 5.2 · Prop. 5.14, preview of Rem. 6.19

Does it matter where you evaluate the integrand?

Question: in \(\sum g(t_i)\Delta f\), does it matter whether \(g\) is evaluated at the left end \(t_i\) or the right end \(t_{i+1}\) of each cell? We take \(g=f\), compute both sums, and look at their difference. It is exactly \(\sum(\Delta f)^2\): the quadratic variation along the mesh. Three integrators: a smooth function, a Poisson path (finite variation, with jumps), and a Brownian path (continuous, infinite variation).

What the lab shows (default settings unless stated)
  • Smooth \(f\): right minus left is 0.0013 with \(2^{14}\) cells, and it tends to 0. Both sums converge to \(\int f\,\dd f=\frac12(f(1)^2-f(0)^2)=\frac12\), as for any continuous BV integrator.
  • Poisson path: the difference equals the number of jumps on every path (worst deviation 0.0000; mean 4.8880, prediction 4.8880). The left sum tends to \(0+1+\dots+(N-1)=\frac{N(N-1)}2\), the right sum to \(\frac{N(N+1)}2\): means 11.7900 and 16.6780 here. The exact expectations are \(\lambda^2/2=12.5\) and \((\lambda^2+2\lambda)/2=17.5\); the reference code gets 12.61 and 17.63.
  • Brownian path: the difference is 0.9993, close to \(t=1\) on every path (worst deviation 0.0345). Left sums average 0.0937 and right sums 1.0929; they converge to \(\frac{B_1^2-1}2\) and \(\frac{B_1^2+1}2\). This is the heart of Itô calculus: for Brownian integrators the evaluation point is part of the definition. Lesson 6 takes the left point (Prop. 6.18, Rem. 6.19).

§5.2 Stochastic processes with bounded variation

notes, p. 41

Definition 5.16 (finite-variation process)notes p. 41

\((A_t)\) is a bounded (finite) variation process if it is adapted and, for every \(\omega\), \(t\mapsto A_t(\omega)\) is BV on every compact interval. In this course \(A\) is also continuous, being the finite-variation part of a continuous semimartingale.

Proposition 5.17notes p. 41

If \(A\) is a finite-variation process and \(H\) is progressive with \(\int_0^t|H_s|\,|\dd A_s|\lt\infty\) for all \(t\), then \((H\cdot A)_t=\int_0^tH_s\,\dd A_s\) is a finite-variation process.

Why. Pathwise, this is Prop. 5.13 (3). The only issue is adaptedness. Split \(H=H^+-H^-\) and \(A=A^+-A^-\), so it suffices to treat \(H\ge0\) and \(A\) non-decreasing. By a monotone class argument, it suffices to take \(H_s(\omega)=\ind_{(u,v]}(s)\ind_C(\omega)\) with \(C\in\F_u\). Then \(\int_0^tH\,\dd A=\ind_C(A_{v\wedge t}-A_{u\wedge t})\), which is \(\F_t\)-measurable. Progressive measurability is exactly what makes the monotone class argument work on \([0,t]\times\Omega\) with \(\cB([0,t])\otimes\F_t\) (Def. 3.10).

How it is used. The finite-variation part of every stochastic integral against a semimartingale is this pathwise Stieltjes integral (Def. 6.16). Examples: \(\int_0^tH_s\,\dd s\), and \(\int_0^tH_s\,\dd\br{M}_s\).

§5.3 Continuous local martingales

notes, pp. 42–43

The problem. Stochastic integrals \(\int_0^tH_s\,\dd B_s\) with, say, continuous \(H\) are not always integrable: \(H\) may be huge on rare paths. They are still “martingales up to a sequence of stopping times”. Local martingales are this class, which is stable under stochastic integration and needs no integrability.

Definition 5.18 (local martingale)notes p. 42

\((M_t)\) is an \((\F_t)\)-local martingale if there are stopping times \(\tau_n\uparrow+\infty\) a.s. such that each stopped process \((M_{t\wedge\tau_n})_{t\ge0}\) is a true martingale. The sequence \((\tau_n)\) reduces \(M\). Notation: \(\Mloc\) is the class of continuous local martingales (the notes also write \(\mathcal M_{c,\mathrm{loc}}\)).

Intuition. Cut the process off before it gets dangerous (before \(|M|\) reaches \(n\)); what remains is a genuine martingale. Letting \(n\to\infty\) removes the cut. Every martingale identity can be applied to \(M^{\tau_n}\), and passing to the limit is where Fatou's lemma and dominated convergence come in.

Exercise 5.19 (p. 42) lists the basic stability properties: (1) continuous martingales are local martingales; (2) stopped local martingales are local martingales; (3) if \((\tau_n)\) reduces \(M\), then \(M^{\tau_n\wedge a}\) is a UI martingale; (4) \((\sigma_n\wedge\tau_n)\) also reduces \(M\) when \(\sigma_n\uparrow\infty\); (5) a local martingale of finite variation is constant, for continuous processes. See E2–E3.

Theorem 5.20notes p. 42

Let \(M\) be a continuous local martingale. (1) If \(M_t\ge0\) and \(M_0\in L^1\), then \(M\) is a supermartingale. (2) If \(\E\big[\sup_{s\le t}|M_s|\big]\lt\infty\) for every \(t\), then \(M\) is a true martingale.

In one sentenceNonnegative local martingales can only lose mass. Dominated ones are martingales.
Proof(1) \(\E[M_{t\wedge\tau_n}\mid\F_s]=M_{s\wedge\tau_n}\). Let \(n\to\infty\): the right side tends to \(M_s\), and conditional Fatou (valid because \(M\ge0\)) gives \(\E[M_t\mid\F_s]\le\liminf M_{s\wedge\tau_n}=M_s\). Taking \(s=0\) shows \(\E M_t\le\E M_0\lt\infty\). (2) The same, with conditional dominated convergence, dominated by \(\sup_{s\le t}|M_s|\).
Counterexample(Lab 5.3.) \(M_t=1/|W_t|\), with \(W\) a 3-d Brownian motion started at distance 1 from the origin, is a nonnegative continuous local martingale with \(\E M_t=\operatorname{erf}(1/\sqrt{2t})\downarrow0\). It is a strict supermartingale, not a martingale. Here \(\E\sup_{s\le t}M_s=\infty\), which is why (2) does not apply.
Used again(1) makes \(\cE(L)=e^{L-\br{L}/2}\) a supermartingale. Girsanov needs it to be a true martingale, which is the whole point of Novikov and Kazamaki (Thm. 7.12, Prop. 7.19).
Corollary 5.21notes p. 42

If \(M\) is a continuous local martingale with \(M_0\in L^1\), then \(\tau_n=\inf\{t:|M_t-M_0|=n\}\) reduces \(M\).

Why. \(M^{\tau_n}\) is a local martingale (stopping preserves the class) with \(|M^{\tau_n}|\le|M_0|+n\), integrable. By Theorem 5.20 (2) it is a true martingale. By continuity, \(\tau_n\uparrow\infty\). (The notes call \(M^{\tau_n}\) “bounded”, which is true when \(M_0\) is bounded.) Continuity is essential here: a jump could overshoot the level \(n\) by an unbounded amount.

Lab 5.3 · Def. 5.18, Thm. 5.20

A local martingale that is not a martingale

Question: Theorem 5.20 (1) says a nonnegative local martingale is a supermartingale. Can it lose mass? Beyond the notes: \(x\mapsto1/|x|\) is harmonic on \(\R^3\setminus\{0\}\). So by Itô's formula (Lesson 7), \(M_t=1/|W_t|\) is a continuous local martingale for a 3-d Brownian motion \(W\) started at \((1,0,0)\), reduced by the times \(\tau_n=\inf\{t:|W_t|\le1/n\}\). \(W_t\) is Gaussian, so \(\E M_t\) can be sampled exactly, and it has a closed form: \(\E M_t=\operatorname{erf}(1/\sqrt{2t})\).

What the lab shows (default settings unless stated)
  • Mass is lost. \(\hat\E M_1=\) 0.6813 against the exact 0.6827. \(\hat\E M_{10}=\) 0.2482 against 0.2482. A martingale would stay at \(M_0=1\). \(M_t\to0\) because \(W\) escapes to infinity in dimension 3; it is not uniformly integrable either.
  • Stopping restores the martingale property. Stopped when \(|W|\le1/4\), \(\hat\E[M_{1\wedge\tau_4}]=\) 1.000 ± 0.018: consistent with 1. The stopped process is bounded by 4, so it is a true martingale. The missing mass lives on the rare paths that come very close to the origin, where \(M\) is huge. Stopping removes the danger; letting \(n\to\infty\) brings it back only in the limit, and there the mass leaks away.
Theorem 5.22 (finite variation + continuous local martingale ⇒ constant)notes p. 43

A continuous local martingale of finite variation is constant: almost surely \(M_t=M_0\) for all \(t\).

In one sentenceA continuous martingale cannot move smoothly: if it moves at all, it has infinite variation.
ProofFirst, \(M\) bounded with \(|\dd M|([0,t])\) bounded and \(M_0=0\). Orthogonality of martingale increments gives \[ \begin{aligned}\E[M_t^2]&=\sum_i\E\big[(M_{t_{i+1}}-M_{t_i})^2\big]\\ &\le\E\Big[\max_i|\Delta_iM|\cdot|\dd M|([0,t])\Big]\to0\end{aligned} \] as the mesh goes to 0: by continuity \(\max_i|\Delta_iM|\to0\), and bounded convergence applies. So \(M_t=0\). In general, localize with \(\tau_n=\inf\{t:|M_t|+|\dd M|([0,t])=n\}\). This is the same argument as Cor. 2.22, in expectation.
Counterexample(Lab 5.4.) The compensated Poisson process \(N_t-\lambda t\) is a martingale of finite variation, and it is not constant. Its jumps break “\(\max_i|\Delta_iM|\to0\)”.
Used againUniqueness of the semimartingale decomposition (Rem. 5.41), uniqueness of the bracket (Thm. 5.23), uniqueness of the stochastic integral (Thm. 6.6).
Watch out

The notes' statement reads “Let \((M_t)\) be a local martingale of bounded variation”, without “continuous”, although the section is about continuous local martingales and the proof uses continuity. The proof also writes \(\E[M_t^2]\) where \(\E[(M_t-M_0)^2]\) is meant, i.e. it assumes \(M_0=0\).

Lab 5.4 · Thm. 5.22, Thm. 5.23

Why continuity matters: a martingale of finite variation

Question: Theorem 5.22 fails without continuity. How exactly, and what does the realized quadratic variation of a jump martingale converge to? \(X_t=N_t-\lambda t\), with \(N\) a Poisson process of rate \(\lambda\), is a martingale: independent increments with mean 0. Its paths have finite variation: slope \(-\lambda\) plus jumps of size 1.

What the lab shows (default settings unless stated)
  • A martingale: \(\hat\E X_T=\) 0.020 and \(\hat\E X_T^2=\) 10.187 against \(\lambda T=\) 10.000. So it is not constant.
  • Finite variation: the total variation on \([0,T]\) is \(N_T+\lambda T\), with mean 20.02. Theorem 5.22 would force a continuous martingale with this property to be constant.
  • Quadratic variation counts the jumps: on a grid of 4096 cells, \(\sum(\Delta X)^2\) is within 0.0471 of \(N_T\) on average (mean 10.019). For a continuous finite-variation path such as \(\lambda t\sin t\), the same sum is 2.4 × 10⁻² and tends to 0 as the grid refines. For jump martingales, \(\sum(\Delta X)^2\to N_T\) (the “optional” bracket \([X]\)), while \(X^2-\lambda t\) is also a martingale (the “predictable” bracket \(\br{X}_t=\lambda t\)). For continuous martingales the two coincide, which is why the notes can speak of the bracket (E9).

§5.4 Quadratic variation of continuous local martingales

notes, pp. 43–47

The problem. For Brownian motion, \(B_t^2-t\) is a martingale and \(\sum(\Delta B)^2\to t\) (Example 4.3, Prop. 2.21). Does every continuous local martingale have such an intrinsic clock?

Theorem 5.23 (bracket, quadratic variation)notes p. 43 (admitted)

For every continuous local martingale \(M\) there is a unique increasing, continuous, adapted process \(\br{M}\) with \(\br{M}_0=0\) such that \(M^2-\br{M}\) is a local martingale. Moreover, for every sequence of meshes of \([0,t]\) whose size tends to 0, \[ \sum_i\big(M_{t^n_{i+1}}-M_{t^n_i}\big)^2\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\P}\br{M}_t. \]

In one sentenceEvery continuous local martingale carries its own clock \(\br{M}\): the sum of its squared increments, and the compensator of \(M^2\).
Why uniqueIf \(A\) and \(A'\) both work, \(A-A'=(M^2-A')-(M^2-A)\) is a continuous local martingale of finite variation, so it is constant, equal to 0 (Thm. 5.22). Continuity of \(A\) is part of the requirement: for the compensated Poisson process both \(N_t\) and \(\lambda t\) compensate \(X^2\) (Lab 5.4).
Proof idea (admitted in the notes)For bounded \(M\), the discrete compensators along dyadic meshes (Remark 5.24: \(H_n=\sum_{k\lt n}\E[(\Delta M_k)^2\mid\F_k]\)), or equivalently \(\sum(\Delta M)^2\), form a Cauchy sequence in \(L^2\). Doob's inequality makes the convergence uniform in time and the limit continuous. Localize for the general case (Le Gall, Thm. 4.9; Revuz–Yor, Thm. IV.1.3).
Examples\(\br{B}_t=t\). \(\br{B^2-t}_t=4\int_0^tB_s^2\,\dd s\) (Example 6.23). \(\br{e^{B-t/2}}_t=\int_0^te^{2B_s-s}\,\dd s\). For \(M=\int H\,\dd B\), \(\br{M}=\int H^2\,\dd s\) (Lesson 6).

Remark 5.25: notation \(A^\tau_t=A_{t\wedge\tau}\) for stopped processes.

Theorem 5.26notes pp. 43–44

For a stopping time \(\tau\), \(M^\tau\) is a continuous local martingale and \(\br{M^\tau}=\br{M}^\tau\).

Why. \((M^\tau)^2-\br{M}^\tau=(M^2-\br{M})^\tau\) is a stopped local martingale, hence a local martingale. Uniqueness then identifies the bracket.

Definition 5.27notes p. 44

A martingale is square-integrable if \(\E M_t^2\lt\infty\) for every \(t\).

Theorem 5.28 (\(L^2\) martingales and brackets)notes p. 44

Let \(M\) be a continuous local martingale with \(M_0\in L^2\). Then \(\E\br{M}_t\lt\infty\) for all \(t\) if and only if \(M\) is a true square-integrable martingale. In that case \(M^2-\br{M}\) is a true martingale and \(\E\br{M}_t=\E M_t^2-\E M_0^2\).

Why. With a reducing sequence, \(\E M^2_{t\wedge\tau_n}=\E\br{M}_{t\wedge\tau_n}+\E M_0^2\). If \(M\) is square-integrable, Doob gives \(\E\sup_{s\le t}M_s^2\le4\E M_t^2\), and monotone convergence gives \(\E\br{M}_t\lt\infty\). Conversely, Fatou gives \(\E M_t^2\le\E\br{M}_t+\E M_0^2\). Also \(\E\sup_sM^2_{s\wedge\tau_n}\le4(\E\br{M}_t+\E M_0^2)\), so \(\sup_{s\le t}|M_s|\in L^2\), and Theorem 5.20 (2) makes \(M\) a true martingale. Meaning: the expected clock \(\E\br{M}_t\) is the variance budget of \(M\). This is the Itô isometry in embryo (Lesson 6).

Corollary 5.29notes p. 44

If \(M\in\Mloc\), \(M_0=0\) and \(\br{M}\equiv0\), then \(M\equiv0\).

Definition 5.30 · Proposition 5.31notes pp. 44–45

A martingale is bounded in \(L^2\) if \(\sup_t\E M_t^2\lt\infty\). For a continuous (local) martingale with \(M_0=0\): \(\E\br{M}_\infty\lt\infty\iff M\) is bounded in \(L^2\). In that case \(M_t\to M_\infty\) in \(L^2\), \(\E M_\infty^2=\E\br{M}_\infty=\sup_t\E M_t^2\), and \(M^2-\br{M}\) is uniformly integrable.

These \(L^2\)-bounded continuous martingales, with the norm \(\|M\|^2=\E\br{M}_\infty=\E M_\infty^2\), form the Hilbert space \(\Mtc\) on which Lesson 6 builds the integral (Thm. 6.3).

Exercise 5.32: \(\br{\lambda M}=\lambda^2\br{M}\) (E4). “The bracket acts as a norm square” (p. 45), which motivates polarization.

Definition 5.33 (cross-bracket)notes p. 45

For \(M,N\in\Mloc\): \[ \begin{aligned}\br{M,N}&=\tfrac12\big(\br{M+N}-\br{M}-\br{N}\big)\\ &=\tfrac14\big(\br{M+N}-\br{M-N}\big).\end{aligned} \] By Remark 5.34, \(\br{M,M}=\br{M}\).

Proposition 5.35notes pp. 45–46

(1) \(\br{M,N}\) is the unique continuous finite-variation process, null at 0, such that \(MN-\br{M,N}\) is a continuous local martingale. (2) \(\sum_i\Delta_iM\,\Delta_iN\to\br{M,N}_t\) in probability, along meshes whose size tends to 0. (3) \(\br{M^\tau,N^\tau}=\br{M,N}^\tau\).

Proposition 5.36: for \(L^2\)-bounded continuous martingales null at 0, \(\E\br{M,N}_\infty=\E[M_\infty N_\infty]\). Proposition 5.37: \((M,N)\mapsto\br{M,N}\) is bilinear and symmetric. Definition 5.38: \(M\) and \(N\) are orthogonal if \(\br{M,N}\equiv0\), equivalently if \(MN\) is a local martingale. Example: two independent Brownian motions are orthogonal. \(B\) and \(\rho B+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}W\) have \(\br{\cdot,\cdot}_t=\rho t\) (Lab 5.5).

Theorem 5.39 (Kunita–Watanabe inequality)notes pp. 46–47

For \(M,N\in\Mloc\) and progressive \(H,K\), almost surely, \[ \begin{aligned}&\int_0^\infty|H_tK_t|\,|\dd\br{M,N}_t|\\ &\qquad\le\Big(\int_0^\infty H_t^2\,\dd\br{M}_t\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\int_0^\infty K_t^2\,\dd\br{N}_t\Big)^{1/2}.\end{aligned} \]

In one sentenceCauchy–Schwarz for brackets, in the sense of measures: \(|\dd\br{M,N}|\le\sqrt{\dd\br{M}\,\dd\br{N}}\).
Proof ideaOn each interval, \(|\br{M,N}^t_s|\le(\br{M}^t_s\br{N}^t_s)^{1/2}\): take limits in Cauchy–Schwarz for \(\sum\Delta M\Delta N\). Sum over a partition and use Cauchy–Schwarz for sums. This gives the measure inequality on finite unions of intervals, extended to all Borel sets. Then approximate \(H,K\) by step functions.
Used againIt shows that \(N\mapsto\E\int H\,\dd\br{M,N}\) is a continuous linear form on \(\Mtc\). That is the key step in the Riesz-representation construction of \(H\cdot M\) (Thm. 6.6).

§5.5 Continuous semimartingales

notes, pp. 47–49

The problem. Interesting processes have both a trend and noise: \(X_t=x+\int_0^tb_s\,\dd s+\int_0^t\sigma_s\,\dd B_s\), or \(f(B_t)\), as Itô's formula will show. We need a class that contains both finite-variation processes and local martingales, is stable under smooth functions, and has a well-defined integral and bracket.

Definition 5.40 (continuous semimartingale)notes p. 47

\(X\) is a (continuous) semimartingale if \(X_t=X_0+M_t+A_t\), with \(M\in\Mloc\), \(A\) a continuous adapted finite-variation process, and \(M_0=A_0=0\).

Remark 5.41 (canonical decomposition)notes p. 47

The decomposition is unique up to indistinguishability: if \(M+A=M'+A'\), then \(M-M'=A'-A\) is a continuous local martingale of finite variation, null at 0, hence 0 (Thm. 5.22).

Key idea

Uniqueness of the decomposition is what makes “drift” and “noise” well defined. Girsanov's theorem changes the probability measure and moves part of the martingale into the drift (Thm. 7.13). Under each measure the decomposition is still unique, but it differs between measures. The bracket does not change, since it only depends on the paths.

Definition 5.42 (bracket of semimartingales)notes p. 47

If \(X=X_0+M+A\) and \(Y=Y_0+N+B\), then \(\br{X,Y}=\br{M,N}\), and \(\br{X}=\br{X,X}\). The finite-variation parts do not contribute.

Proposition 5.43notes p. 47 (proof: exercise)

For meshes of \([0,t]\) whose size tends to 0, \(\sum_i\Delta_iX\,\Delta_iY\xrightarrow{\P}\br{X,Y}_t\).

The proof is E5: expand \(\Delta X\Delta Y=\Delta M\Delta N+\Delta M\Delta B+\Delta A\Delta N+\Delta A\Delta B\). The cross terms vanish, being bounded by \(\max|\Delta(\text{continuous})|\times\TV(\text{finite variation})\). This is the dichotomy of Lab 2.5, now applied to semimartingales.

Proposition 5.44notes pp. 48–49

For a continuous adapted \(H\) and semimartingales \(X,Y\), along meshes of \([0,t]\) whose size tends to 0, \[ \sum_iH_{t^n_i}\,\Delta_iX\,\Delta_iY\xrightarrow{\P}\int_0^tH_s\,\dd\br{X,Y}_s. \]

Why. By polarization reduce to \(X=Y\), and drop the finite-variation part as in Prop. 5.43. For \(X=M\), compare three things: the weighted sum on a fine mesh \(m\), the sum on a coarse mesh \(n\) with \(H\) frozen on coarse cells, and the Stieltjes integral. Freezing \(H\) costs little by continuity of \(H\) (the term \(T_1\)). On each coarse cell the fine squared increments add up to the bracket increment (\(T_2\)). The coarse Riemann–Stieltjes sum converges to \(\int H\,\dd\br{M}\) (\(T_3\), Prop. 5.14 with continuous \(H\), as corrected above).

Watch out

The proof opens with “Note by interpolation that we only need to consider the case \(X=Y\)”; it should read “by polarization”. Its first displayed bound runs off the right margin of p. 48, so its last term is cut. The statement's “for any mesh” should be “for any sequence of meshes whose size tends to 0”.

Link

Proposition 5.44 is the engine of Itô's formula. In \(f(X_t)-f(X_0)=\sum f'(X_{t_i})\Delta X+\sum\frac12f''(\xi_i)(\Delta X)^2\), the second sum converges to \(\frac12\int f''(X_s)\,\dd\br{X}_s\) by exactly this proposition (Thm. 7.1). The first sum is a Riemann sum for the stochastic integral (Prop. 6.18).

E1 · Exercise 5.11 (corrected) notes p. 40

Let \(N\) be a Borel set with \(\dd f^-(N)=0\) and \(\dd f^+(N^c)=0\) (Hahn decomposition). Show that \(\dd f=(\ind_N-\ind_{N^c})\,|\dd f|\).

Solution

For a Borel \(A\): \(\int_A(\ind_N-\ind_{N^c})\,|\dd f|=|\dd f|(A\cap N)-|\dd f|(A\cap N^c)\). On \(N\), \(|\dd f|=\dd f^+\) because \(\dd f^-(N)=0\); on \(N^c\), \(|\dd f|=\dd f^-\). So the expression is \(\dd f^+(A)-\dd f^-(A)=\dd f(A)\). The density has absolute value 1: this is what Remark 5.15's martingale proof uses.

E2 · Exercise 5.19 (1)–(4) notes p. 42
Solution

(1) Take \(\tau_n=n\): a stopped continuous martingale is a martingale (Cor. 4.32). (2) If \((\tau_n)\) reduces \(M\), then \((M^\tau)^{\tau_n}=(M^{\tau_n})^\tau\) is a stopped martingale, hence a martingale. (3) \(M^{\tau_n\wedge a}_t=M^{\tau_n}_{t\wedge a}=\E[M^{\tau_n}_a\mid\F_t]\) is closed, hence UI (Thm. 4.25). E3 · Exercise 5.19 (5), and why continuity is needed notes p. 42

Show that if \(M,N\) are continuous local martingales, \(M+N\) has finite variation if and only if it is constant. Show that the claim fails for local martingales with jumps.

Solution

\(M+N\) is a continuous local martingale. If it has finite variation it is constant by Thm. 5.22; the converse is trivial. With jumps: \(M=N_t-\lambda t\) (compensated Poisson) and \(N\equiv0\). Then \(M\) has finite variation and is not constant (Lab 5.4).

E4 · Exercise 5.32 notes p. 45

Show that \(\br{\lambda M}=\lambda^2\br{M}\) for \(M\in\Mloc\) and \(\lambda\in\R\).

Solution

\((\lambda M)^2-\lambda^2\br{M}=\lambda^2(M^2-\br{M})\) is a continuous local martingale, and \(\lambda^2\br{M}\) is continuous, increasing, adapted and null at 0. Uniqueness in Thm. 5.23 gives the claim. Also, directly: \(\sum(\Delta\lambda M)^2=\lambda^2\sum(\Delta M)^2\).

E5 · Proposition 5.43 notes p. 47: “Proof. Exercise.”
Hint

Expand \(\Delta X\Delta Y\) into four terms. For the mixed terms, use \(|\sum\Delta M\Delta B|\le\max_i|\Delta_iM|\cdot|\dd B|([0,t])\).

Solution
\[ \begin{aligned}\sum\Delta X\Delta Y&=\sum\Delta M\Delta N+\sum\Delta M\Delta B\\ &\quad+\sum\Delta A\Delta N+\sum\Delta A\Delta B.\end{aligned} \]

The first term tends to \(\br{M,N}_t\) in probability (Prop. 5.35 (2)). Each other term is bounded by the maximal increment of a continuous process, which tends to 0 by uniform continuity on \([0,t]\), times the total variation of a finite-variation process, which is finite. So they tend to 0 almost surely.

E6 · Proposition 5.14, corrected notes p. 41

(a) Prove that Riemann–Stieltjes sums converge for continuous \(g\). (b) Show they may fail to converge to \(\int g\,\dd f\) for bounded measurable \(g\).

Solution

(a) \(\int_{(a,b]}g\,\dd f=\sum_i\int_{(t_i,t_{i+1}]}g\,\dd f\), so

\[ \begin{aligned}&\Big|\sum_ig(t_i)\Delta_if-\int g\,\dd f\Big|\\ &\quad\le\sum_i\int_{(t_i,t_{i+1}]}|g(t_i)-g(s)|\,|\dd f|(\dd s)\\ &\quad\le\omega_g(\text{mesh})\,|\dd f|((a,b]),\end{aligned} \]

where \(\omega_g\) is the modulus of continuity of \(g\). (b) \(f(t)=t\), \(g=\ind_\Qb\) on \([0,1]\): the integral is 0, and the sums on rational meshes equal 1. With a common jump, \(f=g=\ind_{[1/2,1]}\): the left-point sums are 0, the right-point sums are 1, and \(\int_{(0,1]}g\,\dd f=g(\tfrac12)\cdot1=1\).

E7 · The strict local martingale of Lab 5.3 Beyond the notes (supports Thm. 5.20)

For a 3-d Brownian motion \(W\) with \(W_0=x\), \(|x|=1\), show that \(\E[1/|W_t|]=\operatorname{erf}\big(1/\sqrt{2t}\big)\).

Hint

Newton's shell theorem: the potential \(\int\mu(\dd y)/|x-y|\) of a radially symmetric measure \(\mu\) equals \(\mu(B(0,|x|))/|x|+\int_{|y|\gt|x|}\mu(\dd y)/|y|\). Here \(\E[1/|W_t|]=\int\frac{\mu(\dd y)}{|x-y|}\), with \(\mu=N(0,tI_3)\) centered at 0.

Solution

Let \(R=|Z|\) with \(Z\sim N(0,I_3)\) (chi law with 3 degrees of freedom, density \(\sqrt{2/\pi}\,r^2e^{-r^2/2}\)), and \(a=1/\sqrt t\). The shell theorem gives \(\E[1/|W_t|]=\P(R\le a)+\frac1{\sqrt t}\E[R^{-1};R\gt a]\). Now \(\E[R^{-1};R\gt a]=\sqrt{2/\pi}\int_a^\infty re^{-r^2/2}\,\dd r=\sqrt{2/\pi}\,e^{-a^2/2}\), and \(\P(R\le a)=\operatorname{erf}(a/\sqrt2)-\sqrt{2/\pi}\,ae^{-a^2/2}\). With \(\sqrt t\,a=1\) the exponential terms cancel, leaving \(\operatorname{erf}(1/\sqrt{2t})\). Check: Lab 5.3 and the reference code (0.6825 against 0.6827 at \(t=1\)).

E8 · Correlated Brownian motions and Kunita–Watanabe uses Def. 5.33, Thm. 5.39

Let \(B,W\) be independent Brownian motions and \(Y=\rho B+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}W\). Compute \(\br{B,Y}\) and \(\br{Y}\), and check the Kunita–Watanabe bound.

Solution

By bilinearity, \(\br{B,Y}=\rho\br{B}+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\br{B,W}=\rho t\), since \(BW\) is a martingale, so \(\br{B,W}=0\). Also \(\br{Y}=\rho^2t+(1-\rho^2)t=t\): \(Y\) is a Brownian motion (Lévy, Lesson 7). Kunita–Watanabe: \(|\rho|t\le\sqrt{t\cdot t}\), with equality only if \(|\rho|=1\). Lab 5.5 measures \(\sum\Delta B\Delta Y\) within 0.0075 of \(\rho=0.6\).

E9 · Without continuity the bracket is not unique explains the continuity requirement in Thm. 5.23

Let \(X_t=N_t-\lambda t\). Show that both \(X_t^2-N_t\) and \(X_t^2-\lambda t\) are martingales.

Solution

Use independent increments: \(\E[(X_t-X_s)^2\mid\F_s]=\lambda(t-s)\), which gives \(\E[X_t^2-\lambda t\mid\F_s]=X_s^2-\lambda s\). Also \(N_t-\lambda t=X_t\) is a martingale, so \(X^2-N=(X^2-\lambda t)-X\) is a martingale too. Both \(N_t\) and \(\lambda t\) are increasing and compensate \(X^2\). Only \(\lambda t\) is continuous, and requiring continuity restores uniqueness. The realized sums \(\sum(\Delta X)^2\) converge to \(N_t\) (Lab 5.4), not to \(\lambda t\). For continuous martingales the two notions agree, which is why Lesson 5 can speak of the bracket.

Conclusion

  • Finite-variation integrators. A BV function defines a signed measure \(\dd f\), with Jordan and Hahn decompositions and total variation measure \(|\dd f|\). \(\int g\,\dd f\) is a Lebesgue integral, and Riemann–Stieltjes sums converge for continuous \(g\) (Prop. 5.14, corrected). \(\dd f\) need not have a density (Cantor, Lab 5.1).
  • Local martingales. Martingales after stopping (Def. 5.18). Nonnegative ones are supermartingales and can lose mass (Thm. 5.20, Lab 5.3). Dominated ones are martingales.
  • Rigidity. A continuous local martingale of finite variation is constant (Thm. 5.22). This gives uniqueness of brackets, of decompositions and, next lesson, of stochastic integrals. Jumps break it (Lab 5.4).
  • The bracket. \(\br{M}\): the unique continuous increasing compensator of \(M^2\), and the limit of \(\sum(\Delta M)^2\) (Thm. 5.23). \(\E\br{M}_t=\E M_t^2-\E M_0^2\) (Thm. 5.28). The cross-bracket comes by polarization, and Kunita–Watanabe is Cauchy–Schwarz for it (Thm. 5.39).
  • Semimartingales. \(X=X_0+M+A\), with a unique decomposition, bracket \(\br{X}=\br{M}\), and weighted quadratic sums converging to \(\int H\,\dd\br{X}\) (Prop. 5.44). These are the integrators of Lesson 6 and the objects of Itô's formula in Lesson 7.

Next: Lesson 6 builds \(\int H\,\dd M\): first as an isometry into the Hilbert space \(\Mtc\), then by localization, then for semimartingales.

Cheat sheet

BV functions

\(f=F_+-F_-\) (Jordan). \(\dd f=\dd f^+-\dd f^-\) (Hahn), \(|\dd f|=\dd f^++\dd f^-\), \(|\dd f|([a,b])=\TV\). \(C^1\): \(\dd f=f'\dd t\). Continuous ≠ absolutely continuous (Cantor).

Stieltjes integral

\(\int g\,\dd f=\int g\,\dd f^+-\int g\,\dd f^-\). Riemann–Stieltjes sums converge if \(g\) is continuous. Left or right point: same limit for continuous BV \(f\).

Local martingale

\(M^{\tau_n}\) is a martingale, \(\tau_n\uparrow\infty\). Nonnegative ⇒ supermartingale. \(\E\sup_{s\le t}|M_s|\lt\infty\) ⇒ martingale. Example of a strict one: \(1/|W|\) in 3-d.

Rigidity

Continuous local martingale + finite variation ⇒ constant. Fails with jumps (\(N_t-\lambda t\)).

Bracket

\(M^2-\br{M}\in\Mloc\), \(\br{M}\) continuous increasing. \(\sum(\Delta M)^2\to\br{M}_t\). \(\br{M^\tau}=\br{M}^\tau\). \(\E\br{M}_t=\E M_t^2-\E M_0^2\).

Cross-bracket

\(\br{M,N}=\frac14(\br{M+N}-\br{M-N})\). \(MN-\br{M,N}\in\Mloc\). Kunita–Watanabe: \(|\dd\br{M,N}|\le\sqrt{\dd\br{M}\dd\br{N}}\).

Semimartingale

\(X=X_0+M+A\), unique. \(\br{X,Y}=\br{M,N}\). \(\sum H_{t_i}\Delta X\Delta Y\to\int H\,\dd\br{X,Y}\).

Examples of brackets

\(\br{B}=t\). \(\br{B^2-t}=4\int B^2\). \(\br{e^{B-t/2}}=\int e^{2B-s}\dd s\). Correlated pair: \(\br{B,\rho B+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}W}=\rho t\).

Self-test

1. Give the Jordan decomposition of a BV function.

\(F_+(t)=\TV([a,t],f)\), \(F_-=F_+-f\); both non-decreasing.

2. How does the proof of Theorem 5.5 construct \(\dd F\)?

As \(|F(I)|\) times the law of \(F^{-1}(U)\), \(U\) uniform on \(F(I)\) (inverse transform sampling).

3. Is a continuous non-decreasing \(F\) always \(\int F'\)?

No: the Cantor function has \(F'=0\) a.e. but \(\dd F\ne0\) (Lab 5.1).

4. Why is \(\dd G^+=g\,\dd f^++g\,\dd f^-\) (as printed) wrong?

It would equal \(\dd G^-\), forcing \(\dd G=0\). The correct formula is \(\dd G^+=g^+\dd f^++g^-\dd f^-\).

5. What hypothesis does Prop. 5.14 really need?

\(g\) continuous. Counterexamples: \(g=\ind_\Qb\); a jump shared by \(f\) and \(g\).

6. Left-point minus right-point sums of \(\int f\,\dd f\): what is the limit for a Poisson path? For Brownian motion?

Minus the number of jumps; minus \(t\). In both cases it is minus the quadratic variation (Lab 5.2).

7. Define a local martingale and a reducing sequence.

\(\tau_n\uparrow\infty\) with each \(M^{\tau_n}\) a martingale.

8. Why is a nonnegative local martingale a supermartingale?

Conditional Fatou in \(\E[M_{t\wedge\tau_n}\mid\F_s]=M_{s\wedge\tau_n}\).

9. Give a local martingale that is not a martingale.

\(1/|W_t|\) for a 3-d Brownian motion from \(x\ne0\): \(\E M_t=\operatorname{erf}(|x|/\sqrt{2t})/|x|\) decreases (Lab 5.3).

10. Which reducing sequence always works for a continuous local martingale with \(M_0\in L^1\)?

\(\tau_n=\inf\{t:|M_t-M_0|=n\}\).

11. Why must a continuous martingale with finite variation be constant?

\(\E(M_t-M_0)^2=\E\sum(\Delta M)^2\le\E[\max|\Delta M|\cdot\TV]\to0\).

12. Give a non-constant martingale of finite variation.

\(N_t-\lambda t\) (compensated Poisson).

13. State the two characterizations of \(\br{M}\).

The unique continuous increasing adapted process null at 0 making \(M^2-\br{M}\) a local martingale; the limit in probability of \(\sum(\Delta M)^2\).

14. Why does uniqueness of \(\br{M}\) need continuity of the compensator?

For \(X=N-\lambda t\), both \(N_t\) and \(\lambda t\) compensate \(X^2\) (E9).

15. What is \(\E\br{M}_t\) for a square-integrable continuous martingale?

\(\E M_t^2-\E M_0^2\).

16. If \(\br{M}\equiv0\) and \(M_0=0\), what is \(M\)?

\(M\equiv0\) (Cor. 5.29).

17. How is \(\br{M,N}\) defined, and what does orthogonality mean?

By polarization. Orthogonal: \(\br{M,N}=0\), i.e. \(MN\) is a local martingale.

18. State Kunita–Watanabe in words.

Cauchy–Schwarz for bracket measures: \(\int|HK|\,|\dd\br{M,N}|\le\|H\|_{L^2(\dd\br{M})}\|K\|_{L^2(\dd\br{N})}\).

19. Why is the semimartingale decomposition unique?

The difference of two decompositions is a continuous local martingale of finite variation null at 0, hence 0.

20. What is \(\br{X}\) for \(X_t=t^2+B_t\)?

\(t\): the finite-variation part does not contribute.

21. What does Prop. 5.44 have to do with Itô's formula?

It turns \(\sum\frac12f''(X_{t_i})(\Delta X)^2\) into \(\frac12\int f''(X)\,\dd\br{X}\).
