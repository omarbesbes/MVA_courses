Lesson 1 · Chapter 1 of the notes · notes pp. 3–12

Brownian Motion and Its First Properties

What is Brownian motion, does it exist, and what does it mean to know the law of a random path?

§0 Where this lesson sits

This is the first lesson, so it reuses nothing from the course except standard probability: Gaussian vectors, the Borel–Cantelli lemma, the π–λ (monotone class) theorem and Markov's inequality. It builds the one object everything else is about. The notes put it this way: “We characterize the distribution of a stochastic process through its finite-dimensional distributions” (Remark 1.3, p. 3). Most of the lesson is about what that sentence buys and what it misses.

What this lesson prepares.

  • The definition of Brownian motion (Def. 1.7) and its Gaussian characterization (Prop. 1.13) are the input of the Markov property, Theorem 2.3. They also feed the computation of quadratic variation, Proposition 2.21.
  • The invariances of Prop. 1.16 come back twice in Lesson 2. Time inversion drives the 0–1 argument of Exercise 2.10, and scaling gives the self-similarity of hitting times in Example 2.16.
  • The Wiener space and the canonical process (§1.5) are where laws of continuous processes live. They are used in Theorem 2.1, where the notes say that “two processes with the same distribution give the same distribution on \(W^d\)” (p. 13). They carry Girsanov's theorem (§7.3, which works “on the canonical Wiener space seen in Chapter 1”, p. 63) and weak uniqueness of SDEs (Definition 8.11).
  • The recipe of the Lévy construction (Theorem 1.19) returns at least twice: bound consecutive differences, sum them with Borel–Cantelli, get a uniformly Cauchy sequence. It proves Kolmogorov's continuity criterion (Thm. 1.33), and completeness of the space of \(L^2\) martingales (Theorem 6.3).
  • The board's Chapter 0 (Langevin sampling) is the motivation for the last lessons: SDEs (§8.1) and their Markov semigroups (Definition 8.21).

A question that comes back

“What does it mean to know the law of a random path, and which properties of the path does the law see?” Here the answer starts. The law is the collection of finite-dimensional distributions (fidis). The fidis do not see continuity, so two processes can have the same law while only one of them has continuous paths (§1.2). Continuous processes fix this: their law is a measure on a space of continuous functions (§1.5). No earlier lesson asked this question. Later lessons keep asking it. Lesson 2 asks for the law of the future given the past. Lesson 7 asks how the law of a path changes when we change probability measure. Lesson 8 separates two notions of uniqueness for SDEs: same paths, or only the same law.

The first lecture opened with a “Chap 0. Motivation and illustration”. It lists three places where stochastic calculus enters machine learning:

  1. Sampling and Monte Carlo simulation. “Goal: sample from a target distribution \(\pi\)”, which is “typically known up to a multiplicative constant”. “One option is Markov chain Monte Carlo methods”, and the board names the Langevin algorithm. Setting: \(\pi \ll \Leb\) with \(\dd\pi/\dd\Leb \propto e^{-U}\) and \(U : \R^d \to \R\) of class \(C^1\). “We can therefore consider the Langevin diffusion, i.e., a solution of the SDE \[ \dd X_t = -\nabla U(X_t)\,\dd t + \sqrt{2}\,\dd B_t, \] \((B_t)_t\) is a \(d\)-dim Brownian motion.” Then: “We can show that \(\Law(X_t) \to \pi\) as \(t \to +\infty\) (in some sense).”
  2. Generative models, in particular diffusion models.
  3. Theoretical ML, for example the analysis of deep neural networks. “Consider a one hidden layer NN. Here we only need to fit the weights of the hidden layer.” Let \(\{(w^i_k)_{k\in\N} : i = 1,\dots,N\}\) be the sequence produced by SGD/GD for the weight with index \(i\). Define \(\tilde w^{i,N}_t = w^i_{\lfloor tN\rfloor}\) (\(\lfloor\cdot\rfloor\) is the floor function). Then “\(\{(\tilde w^{i,N}_t)_{t\ge 0} : N\in\N\}\) \(\to\) \((w^*_t)_{t\ge0}\) as \(N\to+\infty\), which is a solution of an SDE”.

Why the constant does not matter. Write \(\pi(x) = e^{-U(x)}/Z\) with \(Z = \int e^{-U}\). The unknown \(Z\) is exactly what makes direct sampling hard: you cannot even evaluate the density. The Langevin dynamics only uses \(\nabla U = -\nabla \log \pi\), and the gradient kills \(Z\). This is the first reason the course studies SDEs: an SDE whose drift involves only \(\nabla\log\pi\) can have \(\pi\) as its long-run law.

The algorithm. Time is continuous in the SDE but discrete on a computer. The simplest discretization replaces \(\dd t\) by a step \(\gamma\) and \(\dd B_t\) by an increment \(\sqrt{\gamma}\,\xi\) with \(\xi\sim N(0,I_d)\):

\[ X_{k+1} = X_k - \gamma\,\nabla U(X_k) + \sqrt{2\gamma}\;\xi_{k+1}. \]

This is the unadjusted Langevin algorithm (ULA). The board only names “the Langevin algorithm”; the formula is the Euler scheme applied to the board's SDE.

Beyond the notes

Why is \(\pi\) the limit? The density \(\rho_t\) of \(X_t\) solves the Fokker–Planck equation \(\partial_t\rho = \nabla\cdot(\rho\nabla U) + \Delta\rho = \nabla\cdot\big(\rho\,\nabla U + \nabla\rho\big)\). For \(\rho = e^{-U}/Z\) we have \(\nabla\rho = -\rho\nabla U\), so the bracket vanishes: \(\pi\) is stationary. This PDE side of Markov processes is announced in the title of the notes' §8.3 (“Kolmogorov equation”) but never written in the notes. The heat equation of Proposition 2.8 is its simplest case (\(U = 0\)).

Why the discretization is not exact. ULA does not keep \(\pi\) invariant: its stationary law is off by an amount that grows with \(\gamma\). If \(\nabla U\) grows faster than linearly, the chain can even blow up for large \(\gamma\) (Roberts and Tweedie, 1996). Lab 1.1 measures both effects. Non-asymptotic bounds for ULA are in Durmus and Moulines (2017), co-authored by the lecturer.

Diffusion models and mean-field limits. Score-based diffusion models (Song et al., 2021; Ho et al., 2020) noise data with an SDE and generate by running a time-reversed SDE whose drift involves \(\nabla\log p_t\). The board's point (3) is the mean-field limit of two-layer networks (Mei, Montanari and Nguyen, 2018; Chizat and Bach, 2018; Rotskoff and Vanden-Eijnden, 2018; Sirignano and Spiliopoulos, 2020). Full references are in the references.

Lab 1.1 · from the board (Chap. 0)

The Langevin algorithm: when does it sample π, and when does it fail?

Question: the board says \(\Law(X_t)\to\pi\) for the Langevin diffusion. What happens to the algorithm, which uses a step \(\gamma\)? We start many independent chains at \(X_0\), run \(K = T/\gamma\) steps, and compare the law of \(X_K\) with \(\pi\). Two targets: a Gaussian \(U(x) = x^2/2\), where everything is explicit, and a double well \(U(x) = 2(x^2-1)^2\), whose gradient grows like \(x^3\).

What the lab shows (default settings unless stated)
  • The step size biases the result. Gaussian target, \(\gamma = 0.5\): \(\E[X_K^2] =\) 1.369 ± 0.031, against 1.000 under \(\pi\). The measured value agrees with the exact stationary value of the chain, \(1/(1-\gamma/2) =\) 1.333. (Derivation: the chain is \(X_{k+1} = (1-\gamma)X_k + \sqrt{2\gamma}\,\xi\), whose stationary variance \(v\) solves \(v = (1-\gamma)^2 v + 2\gamma\).) With \(\gamma = 0.05\) the value is 1.019 ± 0.024 (theory 1.026).
  • Double well: the bias is roughly proportional to \(\gamma\). The target value is \(\E_\pi[X^2] =\) 0.852. With \(\gamma = 0.05\): 0.763 ± 0.010. With \(\gamma = 0.01\): 0.834 ± 0.008. The chains cross the barrier: the share with \(X_K\lt 0\) is 0.498 (target 0.5).
  • When it fails. Double well, \(\gamma = 0.1\): 0.917 of the chains diverge. Past \(|x|\approx 2\), one step \(-\gamma\nabla U(x) = -8\gamma x(x^2-1)\) overshoots to the other side with a larger modulus, and the next step overshoots again. The diffusion never does this; only the discretization does. At \(\gamma = 0.05\) only 0.002 of the chains diverge.

To reproduce: choose the target and step in the controls, keep \(T=20\), 4000 chains, \(X_0=1\), seed 5.

§1.1 Crash course on stochastic processes

notes, p. 3 · board IMG_3212 – IMG_3222

The problem. A stochastic process is a random function of time. To do probability with it we need its law. But the function is an element of a huge space, \(\Xb^T\) (all functions from \(T\) to \(\Xb\)), and it is not obvious which events we can assign probabilities to.

The intuition. We never observe a whole path, only finitely many values. So we agree that two processes have the same law when every finite “snapshot” \((X_{t_1},\dots,X_{t_n})\) has the same distribution.

Definition 1.1 (stochastic process)notes p. 3

All random variables live on one probability space \((\Omega,\F,\P)\). A family \((X_t)_{t\in T}\) is a stochastic process on the measurable space \((\Xb,\cX)\) if, for every \(t\in T\), \(X_t\) is a random variable, i.e. \(\omega\mapsto X_t(\omega)\) is \(\F/\cX\)-measurable.

Remark 1.2. Mostly \(T=\R_+\) and \(\Xb=\R\) or \(\R^d\) with the Borel σ-field. Remark 1.3. “We characterize the distribution of a stochastic process through its finite-dimensional distributions.”

Definition 1.4 (fidis)notes p. 3

For every finite \(I=\{t_1,\dots,t_n\}\subset T\), the finite-dimensional distribution (fidi) \(\nu_I^X\) is the law of \((X_{t_1},\dots,X_{t_n})\) on \((\Xb^I,\cX^{\otimes I})\). The distribution of \((X_t)\) is the collection \(\{\nu_I^X : I\subset T \text{ finite}\}\).

Corollary 1.5notes p. 3

Two processes \((X_t)_{t\in T}\), \((Y_t)_{t\in T}\) valued in \((\Xb,\cX)\) have the same distribution if and only if, for every \(n\) and all pairwise distinct \(t_1,\dots,t_n\in T\), \((X_{t_1},\dots,X_{t_n}) \eqd (Y_{t_1},\dots,Y_{t_n})\).

Watch out

The notes (p. 3) and the board (IMG_3220) both write “have the same distribution if”. By Definition 1.4 the statement is an equivalence, and it is stated as one in Prop. 1.28 (3). Pairwise distinct times suffice: a repeated time adds a coordinate that is a copy of another, and the order of the times is just a permutation of coordinates.

Definition 1.6 (independent processes)notes p. 3

\((X^1_t)_{t\in T^1}\) and \((X^2_t)_{t\in T^2}\) are independent if, for all finite families \(t^1_1,\dots,t^1_n\in T^1\) and \(t^2_1,\dots,t^2_m\in T^2\), the vectors \((X^1_{t^1_1},\dots,X^1_{t^1_n})\) and \((X^2_{t^2_1},\dots,X^2_{t^2_m})\) are independent.

From the whiteboard IMG_3214, IMG_3215, IMG_3216, IMG_3217, IMG_3221, IMG_3222

The road not taken. The board first notes that “a stochastic process defines a ‘random’ object \(\omega\in\Omega\mapsto(X_t(\omega))_t\in\Xb^T\)”, where \(\Xb^T\) is the set of functions from \(T\) to \(\Xb\). “We would like therefore that \(\omega\mapsto(X_t(\omega))_t\) is a random variable. However, we need a σ-field for \(\Xb^T\)!” Then: “We can always define such a σ-field (see notes)… Here, we do not follow this road to define the distribution of a stochastic process, we use one characterization that we take as a definition.”

That σ-field is the product σ-field of Definition 1.24. Proposition 1.28 shows the two roads agree: a probability on \((\Xb^T,\cX^{\otimes T})\) is determined by its fidis.

Independence on the board uses the same times for both processes: “\((X_t)\) and \((Y_t)\) are indep \(\iff\) \(\forall t_1,\dots,t_N\), \((X_{t_1},\dots,X_{t_N})\indep(Y_{t_1},\dots,Y_{t_N})\)”. This agrees with Definition 1.6 when \(T^1=T^2\). Given two finite families, take their union \(J\): independence of the \(J\)-vectors implies independence of any sub-vectors.

How it is used. Every “same law” statement in the course (reflection principle, Lévy's characterization, weak uniqueness of SDEs) is checked on fidis, or on a class of events that generates the σ-field.

Watch out: one-dimensional marginals are not enough

Let \(G\sim N(0,1)\) and \(X_t=\sqrt t\,G\). Each \(X_t\) is \(N(0,t)\), exactly like \(B_t\). But \(\Cov(X_s,X_t)=\sqrt{st}\ne s\wedge t\), and \(X\) moves in one fixed random direction: its increments are perfectly correlated with the past. Lab 1.2 (“counter-example”) measures a correlation 1.000 between \(X_{1/2}\) and \(X_1-X_{1/2}\), against 0.003 for time-inverted Brownian motion. The law of a process needs the joint laws.

§1.2 Definitions and first properties

notes, pp. 3–4 · board IMG_3223 – IMG_3231

The problem. Model a particle pushed by many tiny independent shocks. Its displacement over \([s,t]\) should be a sum of many small independent pieces, hence Gaussian (central limit theorem). Its variance should be proportional to \(t-s\), and displacements over disjoint intervals should be independent.

Definition 1.7 (standard one-dimensional Brownian motion)notes pp. 3–4

A real process \((B_t)_{t\ge0}\) is a (1D standard) Brownian motion if

  1. it is almost surely continuous;
  2. \(B_0=0\) a.s.;
  3. for every \(n\) and \(0\le t_1\lt\dots\lt t_n\), the variables \(B_{t_1}, B_{t_2}-B_{t_1},\dots,B_{t_n}-B_{t_{n-1}}\) are independent;
  4. for all \(0\le s\lt t\), \(B_t-B_s\sim N(0,t-s)\).
From the whiteboard IMG_3223, IMG_3225

The board lists the same axioms in a different order: (i) \(B_0=0\), (ii) Gaussian increments \(N(0,t-s)\), (iii) independent increments, (iv) “almost surely \(t\mapsto B_t(\omega)\) is continuous”. The board's modification theorem assumes “(i), (ii), (iii)”, which are the notes' (ii)–(iv). This page always uses the notes' numbering. The board also writes “\(X_t-X_s\)” in axiom (ii) where \(B_t-B_s\) is meant.

Why continuity is a separate axiom. Axioms (ii)–(iv) only talk about finitely many times at once: they constrain the fidis. Continuity involves uncountably many times. The fidis cannot see it, as the next definitions make precise.

Definition 1.9 (modification)notes p. 4

\((X_t)_{t\in T}\) and \((\tilde X_t)_{t\in T}\) are modifications of each other if \(\P(X_t=\tilde X_t)=1\) for every \(t\in T\).

Definition 1.11 (indistinguishable)notes p. 4

They are indistinguishable if \(\P(\forall t\in T,\ X_t=\tilde X_t)=1\).

Indistinguishable implies modification. The converse fails when \(T\) is uncountable, because we cannot intersect uncountably many almost-sure events. The notes add (p. 4): if both processes are a.s. continuous and are modifications of each other on \(\Qb_+\), they are indistinguishable. The reason: a countable intersection of events of probability 1 has probability 1, and two continuous functions that agree on a dense set agree everywhere.

Watch out: same law, different paths

Let \(U\) be uniform on \((0,1)\), \(X_t=0\) and \(Y_t=\ind\{t=U\}\). For each fixed \(t\), \(\P(X_t\ne Y_t)=\P(U=t)=0\), so \(Y\) is a modification of \(X\) and has the same fidis. But every path of \(Y\) has a jump at \(U\), and \(\P(\forall t,\ X_t=Y_t)=0\). The fidis cannot tell a continuous process from a discontinuous one. This is why Definition 1.7 imposes continuity as an axiom, and why §1.5 moves the law of a continuous process onto a space of continuous functions.

Theorem 1.10 (continuous, even Hölder, modification)notes p. 4 (with Remark 1.8)

If \((\tilde B_t)_{t\ge0}\) satisfies (ii)–(iv), there is a modification \((B_t)_{t\ge0}\) that is \((1/2-\varepsilon)\)-Hölder for every \(\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)\).

In one sentenceA process with Brownian fidis can be “repaired” into a continuous one, and the repair does not change its law.
Proof ideaKolmogorov's criterion (Thm. 1.33). Because \(\tilde B_t-\tilde B_s\sim N(0,|t-s|)\), \(\E|\tilde B_t-\tilde B_s|^p = c_p|t-s|^{p/2} = c_p|t-s|^{1+(p/2-1)}\). So \(\varepsilon=p/2-1\) and exponents \(\alpha\lt \varepsilon/p=1/2-1/p\) are allowed. Let \(p\to\infty\).
Why not \(\alpha\ge 1/2\)?If \(B\) were \(\alpha\)-Hölder with \(\alpha\gt 1/2\) on \([0,t]\), then \(\sum(\Delta B)^2\le C^2\sum|\Delta t|^{2\alpha}\to0\). That contradicts the quadratic variation \(\sum(\Delta B)^2\to t\) of Prop. 2.21. The borderline \(\alpha=1/2\) fails too (Lévy's modulus, Lab 1.5).
Used againTo assume paths are continuous “for free”, e.g. time inversion (Prop. 1.16 (b)), and càdlàg modifications of martingales (Theorem 4.11).

§1.3 Definition 1.7 naturally characterizes the distribution

notes, pp. 4–6 · board IMG_3232 – IMG_3254

The problem. Axioms (iii) and (iv) are stated in terms of increments. Is there a compact description of all the fidis? Yes: they are Gaussian, and the covariance is the single function \(s\wedge t\).

Proposition 1.13 (Gaussian characterization)notes pp. 4–5

\((B_t)_{t\ge0}\) is a 1D Brownian motion if and only if it is a.s. continuous and, for every \(n\) and every \(t_1\lt\dots\lt t_n\), the vector \((B_{t_1},\dots,B_{t_n})\) is Gaussian with mean zero and covariance \(\Sigma_{ij}=\Cov(B_{t_i},B_{t_j})=t_i\wedge t_j\).

Why it is true. Put \(t_0=0\) and \(G=(B_{t_1},B_{t_2}-B_{t_1},\dots,B_{t_n}-B_{t_{n-1}})\). By (iii)–(iv), \(G\) has independent Gaussian coordinates, so \(G\sim N_n(0,\operatorname{diag}(a_1,\dots,a_n))\) with \(a_\ell=t_\ell-t_{\ell-1}\). Summing increments is multiplication by the lower-triangular matrix of ones:

\[ M=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\ 1&1&\ddots&\vdots\\ \vdots&&\ddots&0\\ 1&\cdots&1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad MG=(B_{t_1},\dots,B_{t_n}). \]

A linear image of a Gaussian vector is Gaussian, and

\[ \begin{aligned}\Cov\big((MG)_i,(MG)_j\big)&=\E\Big[\Big(\sum_{\ell\le i}G_\ell\Big)\Big(\sum_{\ell\le j}G_\ell\Big)\Big]\\ &=\sum_{\ell=1}^{i\wedge j}a_\ell=t_{i\wedge j}=t_i\wedge t_j.\end{aligned} \]

Conversely, \(M\) is invertible, so \(G=M^{-1}(B_{t_1},\dots,B_{t_n})\) is Gaussian. Its covariance is diagonal (reverse the computation). For a Gaussian vector, uncorrelated coordinates are independent: that gives (iii) and (iv).

Definition 1.15 (Gaussian process)notes p. 5 (with Remark 1.14)

\((X_t)_{t\in T}\) is a Gaussian process with mean \(t\mapsto m(t)\) and covariance function \((s,t)\mapsto\gamma(s,t)\) if every vector \((X_{t_1},\dots,X_{t_n})\), for pairwise distinct times, is Gaussian with \(\E[X_{t_i}]=m(t_i)\) and \(\Cov(X_{t_i},X_{t_j})=\gamma(t_i,t_j)\).

Remark 1.14, restated: Brownian motion is exactly the continuous Gaussian process with mean \(0\) and covariance \(s\wedge t\). Board version (IMG_3239): “Corollary: A BM is a Gaussian process with mean \(t\mapsto0\) and covariance f[unction] \((s,t)\mapsto s\wedge t\).”

Watch out

The board (IMG_3237) writes the covariance function as \(\gamma:T\times T\to\R_+\). A covariance function can be negative. For example, \(X_t=G_1\cos t+G_2\sin t\) with \(G_1,G_2\) i.i.d. \(N(0,1)\) has \(\gamma(s,t)=\cos(t-s)\). What a covariance function must be is symmetric and positive semi-definite: \(\sum_{i,j}c_ic_j\gamma(t_i,t_j)\ge0\).

Proposition 1.16 (invariances)notes pp. 5–6

If \((B_t)_{t\ge0}\) is a Brownian motion, so are: (a) \((-B_t)_{t\ge0}\); (b) \(\tilde B_t=tB_{1/t}\) for \(t\gt 0\), \(\tilde B_0=0\) (time inversion); (c) \(\big(a^{-1/2}B_{at}\big)_{t\ge0}\) for every \(a\gt 0\) (scaling); (d) \(\big(B_T-B_{T-t}\big)_{t\in[0,T]}\) for every \(T\gt 0\) (time reversal).

In one sentenceBrownian motion has no preferred sign, scale or direction of time. Zooming in by a factor \(a\) in time and \(\sqrt a\) in space gives Brownian motion again.

The midpoint \(B_{(a+b)/2}\) is then Gaussian with mean \((B_a+B_b)/2\) (the straight-line value) and variance \((b-a)/4\). Key idea

Summable probabilities of “large” differences imply that, almost surely, only finitely many differences are large. Together with a summable threshold \(a_n\), this makes the path sequence Cauchy. The same pattern proves Kolmogorov's criterion (Thm. 1.33) and completeness of \(\Mtc\) (Thm. 6.3).

Why the 8th moment? With the \(p\)-th moment the bound is \(2^n\,\E|G|^p\,2^{-p(n/4+1)}\), which is summable if and only if \(p\gt 4\). So \(p=8\) is a comfortable choice; \(p=4\) would fail. Gaussian tails, \(\P(|G|\ge x)\le e^{-x^2/2}\), would work with any polynomial threshold.

§1.5 Canonical space and canonical process

notes, pp. 9–12

The problem. A continuous process is a random element of a space of continuous functions. To talk about “the law of Brownian motion” independently of how it was built, we need that space and a good σ-field on it.

Wiener space. \(W^d=\{w:\R_+\to\R^d \text{ continuous}\}\). The sup norm over \(\R_+\) is not suitable: it can be infinite, and even on bounded functions the resulting space is not separable. The notes use instead the metric of uniform convergence on compacts,

\[ d_W(w,\tilde w)=\sum_{n\ge0}2^{-n}\,\frac{\|w-\tilde w\|_{\infty,n}}{1+\|w-\tilde w\|_{\infty,n}}, \] \[ \|w-\tilde w\|_{\infty,n}=\sup_{[0,n]}\|w-\tilde w\|. \]

“It is an easy exercise that \(d_W\) is a distance. In addition, \((W^d,d_W)\) is a Polish space” (p. 9). Polish means complete, separable and metric. See Exercise E1.

Let \((B_t)_{t\ge0}\) be a continuous Brownian motion on \((\Omega,\F,\P)\). The map \(\omega\mapsto(B_t(\omega))_{t\ge0}\) is \(\F/\cW^d\)-measurable, where \(\cW^d\) is the Borel σ-field of \((W^d,d_W)\). Its law \(\mu_W(A)=\P\big((B_t)_{t\ge0}\in A\big)\), \(A\in\cW^d\), is called the Wiener measure.

Why it is true. The key lemma is \(\cW^d=\sigma(W_t:t\ge0)\), where \(W_t:w\mapsto w_t\) is the canonical (coordinate) process. Given the lemma, measurability of \(\omega\mapsto B_\cdot(\omega)\) reduces to measurability of each \(\omega\mapsto B_t(\omega)\), which holds by definition. Each \(W_t\) is continuous for \(d_W\), so \(\sigma(W_t)\subset\cW^d\). For the converse inclusion (Remark 1.22), fix \(w\) and put \(F(\tilde w)=d_W(w,\tilde w)\). By density of \(\Qb\) and continuity, \(\|w-\tilde w\|_{\infty,n}=\sup_{[0,n]\cap\Qb}\|w-\tilde w\|\), a countable supremum of coordinate functions. So \(F\) is \(\sigma(W_t)\)-measurable, every open ball \(F^{-1}([0,r))\) is in \(\sigma(W_t)\), and in a separable metric space every open set is a countable union of balls. Under \(\mu_W\), the canonical process is itself a Brownian motion.

Watch out: two slips in Remark 1.22 (p. 10)

The ball is written \(B(w,r)=F^{-1}((0,r))\). It should be \(F^{-1}([0,r))\): \(F(w)=0\), and the centre belongs to the ball. The last line reads “\(F=\sigma(W_t:t\ge0)\)” where \(\cW^d=\sigma(W_t:t\ge0)\) is meant.

Definition 1.23notes p. 10

A stochastic process indexed by \(T\) is a family \(\{Y_t\}_{t\in T}\) of random variables. The path associated with \(\omega\) is \(t\mapsto Y_t(\omega)\).

Definition 1.24 (canonical process, product σ-field)notes p. 10

Theorem 1.25notes p. 10

Any stochastic process on \(\Xb\) defines a random variable \(\omega\mapsto(Y_t(\omega))_t\) from \((\Omega,\F)\) to \((\Xb^T,\cX^{\otimes T})\). Conversely, under any probability \(\mu\) on \((\Xb^T,\cX^{\otimes T})\), the canonical process is a stochastic process whose fidis are the push-forwards \((X_{t_1},\dots,X_{t_n})_\#\mu\).

(Measurability of \(\omega\mapsto Y_\cdot(\omega)\) is checked on the generators \(\{X_t\in A\}\), whose preimages \(\{Y_t\in A\}\) are events.) Two natural questions follow in the notes. Do the fidis characterize \(\mu\)? And given compatible fidis, does such a \(\mu\) exist?

Definition 1.26 (cylinders)notes p. 11

It is rectangular if \(A_J=\prod_{j\in J}A_j\) with \(A_j\in\cX\). Notation: \(\cC_{\cX}\) for cylinders, \(\cC^R_{\cX}\) for rectangular cylinders.

Example 1.27. With \(\Xb=\R\) and \(T=\N\): \(B_1(0,1)\times\R\times\R\times\cdots\) and \([0,1]^d\times\R\times\cdots\) are rectangular cylinders.

Proposition 1.28notes p. 11

(1) \(\cX^{\otimes T}=\sigma(\cC_{\cX})=\sigma(\cC^R_{\cX})\). (2) Two probabilities on \((\Xb^T,\cX^{\otimes T})\) are equal if and only if they agree on \(\cC^R_{\cX}\). (3) Two processes have the same law if and only if all their fidis agree.

Why it is true. (1) Rectangular cylinders are measurable, and each generator \(\{X_t\in A\}\) is a rectangular cylinder. (2) and (3) use the π–λ theorem. Rectangular cylinders are stable under finite intersection (a π-system), and the class of sets where two probabilities agree is a λ-system (a Dynkin system). So agreement on the π-system extends to the generated σ-field. Remark 1.29: “a distribution on \((\Xb^T,\cX^{\otimes T})\) is characterized by its fidis”. The board's “road not taken” and the fidi definition coincide.

Theorem 1.33 (Kolmogorov's continuity criterion)notes p. 12 (admitted)

Let \((X_t)_{t\in I}\) take values in a complete metric space \((\Xb,d_\Xb)\), with \(I\subset\R\) an interval. Suppose there are \(p\ge1\), \(\varepsilon\gt 0\) and \(C\gt 0\) with

\[ \E\big[d_\Xb(X_t,X_s)^p\big]\le C\,|t-s|^{1+\varepsilon}\qquad(s,t\in I). \]

Then \(X\) has a modification \(\tilde X\) that is locally \(\alpha\)-Hölder for every \(\alpha\in(0,\varepsilon/p)\). That is, for every \([a,b]\subset I\) there is a random \(A=A(\omega,\alpha,a,b)\) with \(d_\Xb(\tilde X_t,\tilde X_s)\le A|s-t|^\alpha\) for \(s,t\in[a,b]\), and \(\E[A^p]\lt\infty\).

In one sentenceIncrements that are small on average, faster than linearly in \(|t-s|\), force a Hölder-continuous version.
Proof idea (Beyond the notes, since the notes admit it.) It is the §1.4 recipe. At level \(n\), the \(2^n\) dyadic increments exceed \(2^{-\alpha n}\) with probability at most \(2^n\cdot C2^{-n(1+\varepsilon)}2^{\alpha np}=C2^{-n(\varepsilon-\alpha p)}\), by Markov. This is summable if \(\alpha\lt\varepsilon/p\). By Borel–Cantelli, eventually all dyadic increments at level \(n\) are at most \(2^{-\alpha n}\). A chaining argument (write any two dyadic times as sums over levels) gives a Hölder bound on the dyadics, and the path extends by continuity. \(\tilde X\) is a modification because \(X_{t_k}\to X_t\) in probability along dyadics.
Counterexample (why \(\varepsilon\gt 0\))A Poisson process \(N\) with rate \(\lambda\) has \(\E|N_t-N_s|\approx\lambda|t-s|\) for small \(|t-s|\). That is exponent \(1+\varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon=0\), and \(N\) jumps, so no continuous modification exists. The margin \(\varepsilon\) is essential.
Used againThm. 1.10 (here); continuity of stochastic integrals as \(L^2\) limits; and continuity in the parameters of SDE solutions (Beyond the notes).

Application to Brownian motion. \(\E|B_t-B_s|^p=c_p|t-s|^{p/2}\) with \(c_p=\E|G|^p=2^{p/2}\Gamma\big(\frac{p+1}2\big)/\sqrt\pi\). So \(\varepsilon=p/2-1\), and exponents \(\alpha\lt\frac{p/2-1}{p}=\frac12-\frac1p\) are allowed. For \(p=2\) nothing follows (\(\varepsilon=0\)). For \(p=4,6,8\) we get \(\alpha\lt1/4,1/3,3/8\), and all \(\alpha\lt1/2\) as \(p\to\infty\). The Python reference code prints \(c_p\) with a Monte Carlo check.

How rough is a Brownian path? Hölder exponents in practice

Question: the theory says Brownian paths are \(\alpha\)-Hölder for every \(\alpha\lt1/2\) and not for \(\alpha\ge1/2\). On a path sampled at \(2^J\) points we compute \(\omega(h)=\max_k|B_{(k+1)h}-B_{kh}|\) for \(h=2^{-4},\dots,2^{-J}\), the largest increment at scale \(h\). Then we look at \(\omega(h)/h^\alpha\) as \(h\to0\).

What the lab shows (default settings unless stated)
  • \(\alpha=0.4\): \(\omega(h)/h^{0.4}\) goes from 1.64 → 1.35 between the coarsest and the finest scale. It stays bounded, as a 0.4-Hölder path must.
  • \(\alpha=0.5\) and \(0.6\): the ratio grows, 2.17 → 4.69 and 2.86 → 16.34. The growth at \(\alpha=1/2\) is slow, like \(\sqrt{2\ln(1/h)}\): at the finest scale, \(\omega(h)/\sqrt{2h\ln(1/h)}=\) 0.940. This is Lévy's modulus of continuity (Beyond the notes), whose ratio tends to 1.

E1 · The metric \(d_W\) notes p. 9: “It is an easy exercise that \(d_W\) is a distance. In addition, \((W^d,d_W)\) is a Polish space.”

Show that \(d_W\) is a distance on \(W^d\), and that \(d_W(w_k,w)\to0\) if and only if \(w_k\to w\) uniformly on every compact interval.

Hint 1

Put \(\psi(x)=x/(1+x)\). Show that \(\psi\) is increasing on \([0,\infty)\) and subadditive: \(\psi(x+y)\le\psi(x)+\psi(y)\).

Hint 2

Each \(\|\cdot\|_{\infty,n}\) satisfies the triangle inequality. The terms are bounded by \(2^{-n}\), so the tail of the series is uniformly small.

Solution

Distance. Symmetry is clear. If \(d_W(w,\tilde w)=0\), every term vanishes, so \(w=\tilde w\) on each \([0,n]\). For the triangle inequality, \(\psi\) is increasing and \(\psi(x+y)=\frac{x+y}{1+x+y}\le\frac{x}{1+x}+\frac{y}{1+y}\). Writing \(\|\cdot\|_n\) for \(\|\cdot\|_{\infty,n}\):

\[ \begin{aligned}\psi(\|w-v\|_n)&\le\psi\big(\|w-u\|_n+\|u-v\|_n\big)\\ &\le\psi(\|w-u\|_n)+\psi(\|u-v\|_n).\end{aligned} \]

Multiply by \(2^{-n}\) and sum.

Topology. If \(w_k\to w\) uniformly on compacts, each term tends to 0. By dominated convergence for series (terms \(\le2^{-n}\)), \(d_W(w_k,w)\to0\). Conversely, \(2^{-n}\psi(\|w_k-w\|_{\infty,n})\le d_W(w_k,w)\), and \(\psi\) is a homeomorphism \([0,\infty)\to[0,1)\), so each \(\|w_k-w\|_{\infty,n}\to0\).

Polish (sketch). Complete: a \(d_W\)-Cauchy sequence is uniformly Cauchy on each \([0,n]\), so it converges uniformly on compacts to a continuous function. Separable: piecewise-linear functions with rational nodes and rational values, constant after the last node, form a countable dense set. On each \([0,n]\), use uniform continuity to approximate.

E7 · Continuity is not an event of the product σ-field Beyond the notes (explains §1.5)

Show that every \(A\in\cX^{\otimes T}\) is determined by countably many coordinates. That is, there is a countable \(S\subset T\) such that \(x\in A\) and \(y|_S=x|_S\) imply \(y\in A\). Deduce that \(C([0,1])\notin\cB(\R)^{\otimes[0,1]}\).

Hint

The sets with this property form a σ-field (a countable union of countable sets is countable), and it contains every \(\{X_t\in A\}\).

Solution

Let \(\cA\) be the class of sets determined by countably many coordinates. It contains the generators, is stable under complement (same \(S\)) and under countable unions (take \(S=\bigcup_nS_n\)). So \(\cA\supset\cX^{\otimes T}\). If \(C([0,1])\) were in the product σ-field, it would be determined by a countable \(S\). Take \(x\equiv0\), which is continuous, and \(y=\ind\{t_0\}\) with \(t_0\notin S\). Then \(y|_S=x|_S\), so \(y\) would be continuous, which is false.

E8 · The Langevin algorithm on a Gaussian target board, Chap. 0 (IMG_3206–3208)

For \(U(x)=x^2/2\), ULA reads \(X_{k+1}=(1-\gamma)X_k+\sqrt{2\gamma}\,\xi_{k+1}\). Find its stationary law, and show it is \(\pi=N(0,1)\) only in the limit \(\gamma\to0\). For which \(\gamma\) does the chain converge at all?

Hint

A stationary Gaussian law \(N(0,v)\) must satisfy \(v=(1-\gamma)^2v+2\gamma\).

Solution

Solve: \(v=\frac{2\gamma}{1-(1-\gamma)^2}=\frac{2\gamma}{2\gamma-\gamma^2}=\frac{1}{1-\gamma/2}\). Iterating, \(X_k=(1-\gamma)^kX_0+\) (a Gaussian with variance tending to \(v\)). This converges if and only if \(|1-\gamma|\lt1\), i.e. \(0\lt\gamma\lt2\). The bias \(v-1=\frac{\gamma/2}{1-\gamma/2}\) is of order \(\gamma\). Numerical check: Lab 1.1 gives 1.369 ± 0.031 at \(\gamma=0.5\), against \(1/(1-\gamma/2)=\) 1.333. The reference code gives 3.983 at \(\gamma=1.5\), against 4.

Conclusion

  • What Brownian motion is. A continuous process started at 0 with independent Gaussian increments of variance equal to the elapsed time. Equivalently, a continuous centered Gaussian process with covariance \(s\wedge t\) (Prop. 1.13). One function \(s\wedge t\) encodes everything, and it explains the invariances (Prop. 1.16).
  • Why it exists. Sample it coarse-to-fine with Brownian-bridge midpoints. Borel–Cantelli makes the sequence uniformly Cauchy (Thm. 1.19). Two choices decide success: reuse old randomness, and use the right noise scale \(2^{-(n+2)/2}\).
  • What its law is. A law of a process is its family of fidis (Prop. 1.28), and it exists as soon as the fidis are compatible (Thm. 1.31). The fidis do not see path properties such as continuity, so for continuous processes we use the Wiener space \(W^d\) and the Wiener measure (Thm. 1.21).
  • How rough it is. Hölder of every order below 1/2 (Thm. 1.10), by Kolmogorov's criterion (Thm. 1.33). Lesson 2 shows why not 1/2: its quadratic variation is \(t\), and it has infinite length.
  • Why we care (board Chap. 0). Brownian motion drives the Langevin diffusion, whose law converges to a target known only up to a constant. Discretizing it gives a sampler with a step-size bias, and it can blow up (Lab 1.1).

"""Lesson 1 — reference implementations (NumPy) with checks against SciPy."""
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats, special

def levy_construction(levels, paths, rng, noise="correct"):
    """Levy's dyadic construction on [0,1] (notes Thm 1.19). Returns X_levels at k/2^levels, shape (paths, 2^levels+1).
    noise="correct": midpoint std 2^{-(n+2)/2} (board IMG_3259); noise="notes": 2^{-(n+1)/2} as printed on p. 7."""
    x = np.zeros((paths, 2)); x[:, 1] = rng.standard_normal(paths)
    for n in range(levels):
        s = 2.0 ** (-(n + 2) / 2) if noise == "correct" else 2.0 ** (-(n + 1) / 2)
        mid = 0.5 * (x[:, :-1] + x[:, 1:]) + s * rng.standard_normal((paths, x.shape[1] - 1))
        new = np.empty((paths, 2 * x.shape[1] - 1)); new[:, 0::2] = x; new[:, 1::2] = mid
        x = new
    return x

def brownian_paths(paths, n, T, rng):
    """Exact Brownian motion on the grid k T/n (independent Gaussian increments)."""
    dB = rng.standard_normal((paths, n)) * np.sqrt(T / n)
    return np.concatenate([np.zeros((paths, 1)), np.cumsum(dB, axis=1)], axis=1)

def ula(grad_U, x0, gamma, K, chains, rng):
    """Unadjusted Langevin algorithm = Euler scheme for dX = -grad U(X) dt + sqrt(2) dB (board, Chap. 0)."""
    x = np.full(chains, float(x0))
    for _ in range(K):
        x = x - gamma * grad_U(x) + np.sqrt(2 * gamma) * rng.standard_normal(chains)
    return x

Cheat sheet

Brownian motion

Continuous, \(B_0=0\), independent increments, \(B_t-B_s\sim N(0,t-s)\). Equivalently: continuous centered Gaussian process with \(\Cov(B_s,B_t)=s\wedge t\).

Invariances

\(-B\), \(tB_{1/t}\), \(a^{-1/2}B_{at}\), \(B_T-B_{T-t}\) are Brownian motions. Hard point for \(tB_{1/t}\): continuity at 0.

Law of a process

The fidis \(\{\nu_I\}\). They determine the law on \(\cX^{\otimes T}\) (π–λ), and compatible fidis define one (Kolmogorov, Polish). They do not see continuity.

Modification vs indistinguishable

\(\P(X_t=Y_t)=1\ \forall t\) vs \(\P(X_t=Y_t\ \forall t)=1\). The same for continuous processes. Modifications have the same law.

Lévy's construction

\(X_0=tG\); midpoints \(+\,2^{-(n+2)/2}G\) (bridge variance \(h/4\)). \(\P(\|X_{n+1}-X_n\|\ge2^{-n/4})\le105\cdot2^{-n-8}\). Uniform limit is BM.

Kolmogorov's criterion

\(\E d(X_t,X_s)^p\le C|t-s|^{1+\varepsilon}\Rightarrow\) Hölder \(\alpha\lt\varepsilon/p\). For BM, \(\alpha\lt\tfrac12-\tfrac1p\to\tfrac12\). Needs \(\varepsilon\gt0\) (Poisson fails).

Wiener space

\(W^d=C(\R_+,\R^d)\), \(d_W\) = local uniform metric, Polish. \(\cW^d=\sigma(W_t)\). \(\mu_W\) = law of BM on \(W^d\).

Langevin (board)

\(\dd X=-\nabla U\dd t+\sqrt2\dd B\), \(\pi\propto e^{-U}\). ULA: \(X_{k+1}=X_k-\gamma\nabla U+\sqrt{2\gamma}\xi\). Bias \(O(\gamma)\); may diverge if \(\nabla U\) grows faster than linearly.

Self-test

Try each question before opening the answer.

1. Why does Definition 1.7 need continuity as a separate axiom?

Axioms (ii)–(iv) only constrain finitely many times at once, i.e. the fidis. Continuity involves uncountably many times and is invisible to the fidis. \(0\) and \(\ind\{t=U\}\) have the same fidis, but only the first is continuous.

2. Give two processes whose one-time marginals agree but whose laws differ.

\(B_t\) and \(\sqrt t\,G\): both have \(X_t\sim N(0,t)\), but the covariances are \(s\wedge t\) and \(\sqrt{st}\).

3. Derive \(\Cov(B_s,B_t)=s\wedge t\) from the axioms.

For \(s\le t\): \(\E[B_sB_t]=\E[B_s(B_t-B_s)]+\E[B_s^2]=0+s\), by independence of \(B_s=B_s-B_0\) and \(B_t-B_s\).

4. Is \((B_{2t}-B_t)_{t\ge0}\) a Brownian motion?

It is continuous and centered Gaussian with \(\Var=2t+t-2t=t\). But for \(t\ge2s\), \(\Cov(B_{2s}-B_s,B_{2t}-B_t)=2s-2s-s+s=0\ne s\). So it is not a Brownian motion: correct marginals, wrong covariance.

5. Which part of the time-inversion proof is not a covariance computation, and how is it done?

Continuity at \(t=0\). The Gaussian computation gives (ii)–(iv), hence a continuous modification \(\bar B\). \(\bar B\) agrees with \(\tilde B\) on the countable set \(\Qb^*_+\), hence on \((0,\infty)\), so \(\tilde B_t\to\bar B_0=0\).

6. What does continuity of \(tB_{1/t}\) at 0 say about \(B\) at infinity?

\(B_s/s\to0\) as \(s\to\infty\) almost surely.

7. For which \(\alpha\) is a Brownian path \(\alpha\)-Hölder on \([0,1]\)?

Exactly for \(\alpha\lt1/2\), almost surely. \(\alpha\gt1/2\) contradicts quadratic variation \(t\). At \(\alpha=1/2\), Lévy's modulus \(\sqrt{2h\ln(1/h)}\) shows the ratio is unbounded (Lab 1.5: 2.17 → 4.69).

8. Why is the midpoint noise in Lévy's construction \(2^{-(n+2)/2}\)?

Given the endpoints of an interval of length \(h\), the Brownian midpoint is Gaussian with mean the average of the endpoints and variance \(h/4\). With \(h=2^{-n}\), the standard deviation is \(2^{-(n+2)/2}\).

9. What does the noise \(2^{-(n+1)/2}\) printed in the notes produce?

A continuous Gaussian process with covariance \(2(s\wedge t)-st\): time-1 value \(N(0,1)\), but \(\Var X(1/2)=3/4\). It is not a Brownian motion.

10. Why does the board's threshold \(a_n=2^{-n/2}\) fail?

\(\|X_{n+1}-X_n\|_\infty=2^{-(n+2)/2}\max_{k\le2^n}|G_k|\), and the maximum grows like \(\sqrt{2n\ln2}\). So \(\P(A_n)\to1\): 0.7747 already at \(n=5\).

11. Why an 8th moment in the Borel–Cantelli bound, and would the 4th do?

The union bound gives \(2^n\,\E|G|^p\,2^{-p(n/4+1)}\), summable if and only if \(p\gt4\). The 4th moment gives a constant term, which fails. The 8th is safe.

12. What is the difference between Theorem 1.31 and Theorem 1.33?

1.31 (extension) builds a law on \(\Xb^T\) from compatible fidis and says nothing about paths. 1.33 (continuity) upgrades a process to a Hölder-continuous modification, given moment bounds on increments.

13. Is \(C([0,1])\) a measurable subset of \((\R^{[0,1]},\cB(\R)^{\otimes[0,1]})\)?

No. Product-measurable sets depend on countably many coordinates (Exercise E7).

14. Why not use the sup norm on \(C(\R_+)\)?

It can be infinite, and even on bounded functions the space is not separable, which breaks measure theory on it. \(d_W\) metrizes uniform convergence on compacts and makes \(W^d\) Polish.

15. What does the key lemma \(\cW^d=\sigma(W_t:t\ge0)\) buy?

Measurability of \(\omega\mapsto B_\cdot(\omega)\) as a map into \(W^d\), hence a well-defined Wiener measure. Also, laws on \(W^d\) are determined by their fidis.

16. Why does a Poisson process have no continuous modification, although \(\E|N_t-N_s|\le\lambda|t-s|\)?

Kolmogorov needs an exponent \(1+\varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon\gt0\). The Poisson process only gives exponent 1, and its paths jump.

17. Are the coordinates of a \(d\)-dimensional Brownian motion independent? Why?

Yes. The joint fidis are Gaussian with block-diagonal covariance \((t_i\wedge t_k)\ind\{j=\ell\}\). For Gaussian vectors, uncorrelated blocks are independent.

18. If \(O\) is an orthogonal \(d\times d\) matrix, is \(OB\) a Brownian motion?

Yes: \(OB\) is continuous and Gaussian with increment covariance \(O(t-s)I_dO^\top=(t-s)I_d\).

19. Why does the Langevin dynamics not need the normalizing constant of \(\pi\)?

It only uses \(\nabla U=-\nabla\log\pi\), and \(\nabla\log(e^{-U}/Z)=-\nabla U\) does not depend on \(Z\).

20. What is the stationary variance of ULA for \(\pi=N(0,1)\), and when is ULA stable?

\(1/(1-\gamma/2)\), for \(0\lt\gamma\lt2\).

21. Why does ULA explode on the double well for large steps, while the diffusion does not?

The drift \(-8x(x^2-1)\) is cubic. For \(|x|\gtrsim2\), one Euler step overshoots to the other side with a larger modulus, and this repeats. The diffusion moves continuously and is pulled back. Lab 1.1: 0.917 of the chains diverge at \(\gamma=0.1\).

22. In the gluing formula of Corollary 1.20, why does the sum stop at \(\lfloor t\rfloor-1\)?

At time \(t\) the pieces \(0,\dots,\lfloor t\rfloor-1\) have been completely traversed (each contributes \(B^k_1\)). The current piece \(\lfloor t\rfloor\) contributes only its value at time \(t-\lfloor t\rfloor\).
