A question that comes back

“How can we integrate against a path of infinite variation, and what survives from ordinary calculus?” Lesson 2 said Stieltjes integration is impossible, and Lesson 5 showed where the evaluation point starts to matter (Lab 5.2). The answer here: integrate in \(L^2(\Omega)\), not path by path. Take left-point Riemann sums, which give martingales. Extend by the isometry \(\E(\int H\,\dd M)^2=\E\int H^2\,\dd\br{M}\), then localize. What survives: linearity, associativity, stopping, and \(\E\int H\,\dd M=0\). What changes: the product rule gains the term \(\br{X,Y}\) (Prop. 6.20). Lesson 7 asks the same question for the chain rule.

§6.1 \(L^2\)-martingales

notes, pp. 50–54

The problem. Define \(\int_0^tH_s\,\dd M_s\) when \(M\) is a continuous martingale, such as Brownian motion, whose paths have infinite variation.

The intuition. For a step integrand \(H=\sum_iH_i\ind_{(t_i,t_{i+1}]}\), with \(H_i\) known at time \(t_i\), the only sensible definition is \(\sum_iH_i(M_{t_{i+1}}-M_{t_i})\): you hold \(H_i\) units of \(M\) on \((t_i,t_{i+1}]\), and your gain is the sum. Because \(H_i\) is chosen before the increment, cross terms vanish in expectation, and \(\E(\text{gain})^2=\E\sum H_i^2(\Delta\br{M})_i\). This is an isometry, and isometries extend by continuity from a dense set to its closure. The rest is functional analysis.

Definition 6.1notes p. 50

\(\Mtc\) is the set of continuous martingales that are bounded in \(L^2\) and null at 0, modulo indistinguishability.

Watch out

The notes write “the set of bounded martingales bounded in \(L^2\)”. The first “bounded” must go: Brownian motion stopped at a bounded time, or \(\int_0^{t\wedge1}B\,\dd B\), are in \(\Mtc\) but are not bounded. The next sentence, Proposition 6.2, begins with a stray “For \(\lambda\in\R\)”. Its proof derives the stopping property from the polarization identity, which does not prove it. Stopping preserves \(\Mtc\) because \(\E M_{t\wedge\tau}^2\le\E\sup_sM_s^2\le4\sup_s\E M_s^2\) (Doob).

Proposition 6.2notes p. 50

\(\Mtc\) is stable under stopping. For \(M,N\in\Mtc\), \(\br{M,N}_\infty\in L^1\), and \((M,N)\mapsto\E[\br{M,N}_\infty]\) is well defined.

(Integrability: \(2|\br{M,N}_t|\le\br{M+N}_t+\br{M}_t+\br{N}_t\), and each has finite expectation by Prop. 5.31.)

Theorem 6.3 (\(\Mtc\) is a Hilbert space)notes p. 50

\(\br{M,N}_{\Mtc}=\E[\br{M,N}_\infty]=\E[M_\infty N_\infty]\) is a scalar product on \(\Mtc\), which makes it a Hilbert space.

In one sentenceContinuous \(L^2\)-bounded martingales form a Hilbert space, isometric to a closed subspace of \(L^2(\Omega)\) via \(M\mapsto M_\infty\).
PositivityIf \(\E\br{N}_\infty=0\), then \(\br{N}\equiv0\), so \(N\equiv0\) (Cor. 5.29).
Completeness (thread T6)If \((M^n)\) is Cauchy, then \((M^n_\infty)\) is Cauchy in \(L^2\), with limit \(M_\infty\). Put \(M_t=\E[M_\infty\mid\F_t]\). Doob gives \(\E\sup_t|M^n_t-M_t|^2\le4\E|M^n_\infty-M_\infty|^2\to0\). So a subsequence converges uniformly almost surely, and the limit is continuous: it is a continuous version of \(M\). This is the Lesson 1 recipe again: uniform Cauchy plus a subsequence gives continuity (Thm. 1.19).
Why it mattersCompleteness is what allows extension by continuity (Thm. 6.6) and the Riesz representation.

The notes' proof starts with “It is often lost that it is bilinear and positive”, a transcription slip for “It is clear that”.

Definition 6.4 (integrands)notes p. 51

For \(M\in\Mtc\), \(L^2(M)\) is the set of progressive processes \(H\) with \(\E\big[\int_0^\infty H_s^2\,\dd\br{M}_s\big]\lt\infty\), modulo equality \(\dd\br{M}\otimes\dd\P\)-a.e.

Watch out: “non-progressive” (p. 51)

The notes read “the set of non-progressive processes \((H_t)\) such that \((\omega,t)\mapsto H_t(\omega)\) is \(\F/\cB(\R_+)\)-measurable”. It must be progressive processes, i.e. measurable with respect to the progressive σ-field \(\cP\) (Prop. 3.14). This is the whole point: the integrand must not look into the future. Lab 6.2 shows what a look-ahead of any size does to \(\E\int H\,\dd B\).

Theorem 6.5notes p. 51

\(L^2(M)\) is a Hilbert space for \(\br{H,K}_{L^2(M)}=\E\int_0^\infty H_sK_s\,\dd\br{M}_s\). It is the space \(L^2(\R_+\times\Omega,\cP,\dd\br{M}\otimes\dd\P)\).

Theorem 6.6 (the stochastic integral)notes pp. 51–53

Let \(M\in\Mtc\). For every \(H\in L^2(M)\) there is a unique \(H\cdot M\in\Mtc\), written \((H\cdot M)_t=\int_0^tH_s\,\dd M_s\), such that for every \(N\in\Mtc\) \[ \br{H\cdot M,N}_t=\int_0^tH_s\,\dd\br{M,N}_s\quad(\forall t), \] equivalently \[ \E\big[(H\cdot M)_\infty N_\infty\big]=\E\Big[\int_0^\infty H_s\,\dd\br{M,N}_s\Big]. \] The map \(H\mapsto H\cdot M\) is a linear isometry from \(L^2(M)\) to \(\Mtc\): \[ \E\big[(H\cdot M)_\infty^2\big]=\E\Big[\int_0^\infty H_s^2\,\dd\br{M}_s\Big]\qquad\text{(Itô isometry)}. \]

Watch out: a missing expectation in Theorem 6.6 (p. 51)

The notes write \(\br{(H\cdot M),N}_{\Mtc}=\int_0^\infty H_s\,\dd\br{M,N}_s\). The left side is a number (the \(\Mtc\) inner product, \(\E\br{\cdot,\cdot}_\infty\)); the right side is a random variable. Either put \(\E\) on the right, or read both sides as bracket processes, as in the statement above. The proof uses both forms.

Why it is true: two constructions in the notes.

  1. Isometric extension. Let \(\mathcal E\) be the elementary processes \(H=\sum_iH_i\ind_{(t_i,t_{i+1}]}\), with \(H_i\) bounded and \(\F_{t_i}\)-measurable. Put \(I_t=\sum_iH_i(M_{t_{i+1}\wedge t}-M_{t_i\wedge t})\). For \(j\lt i\), \(\E[H_iH_j\Delta_iM\,\Delta_jM]=\E\big[H_jH_i\Delta_jM\,\E[\Delta_iM\mid\F_{t_i}]\big]=0\), and \(\E[(\Delta_iM)^2\mid\F_{t_i}]=\E[\Delta_i\br{M}\mid\F_{t_i}]\). Hence \[ \E I_\infty^2=\E\sum_iH_i^2\,\Delta_i\br{M}=\E\int_0^\infty H_s^2\,\dd\br{M}_s. \] Elementary processes are dense in \(L^2(M)\) (the notes assert this without proof; see E2). An isometry defined on a dense set extends uniquely to the closure.
  2. Riesz representation. By Kunita–Watanabe and Cauchy–Schwarz, \(|\E\int H\,\dd\br{M,N}|\le\|H\|_{L^2(M)}\|N\|_{\Mtc}\). So \(N\mapsto\E\int_0^\infty H\,\dd\br{M,N}\) is a continuous linear form on the Hilbert space \(\Mtc\), represented by a unique element \(H\cdot M\). Uniqueness: if \(I^1,I^2\) both work, take \(N=I^1-I^2\) to get \(\E\br{I^1-I^2}_\infty=0\).
Key idea

The stochastic integral is defined by what it does to brackets: \(\br{H\cdot M,N}=H\cdot\br{M,N}\). Everything else follows from this one line plus rigidity (Thm. 5.22): associativity, stopping, and \(\br{H\cdot M,K\cdot N}=\int HK\,\dd\br{M,N}\). In practice: \(\dd(H\cdot M)=H\,\dd M\), and \(\dd\br{H\cdot M}=H^2\,\dd\br{M}\).

Lab 6.1 · Thm. 6.6, Rem. 6.9, Rem. 6.15

Isometry, mean zero, and which stochastic integrals are Gaussian

Question: Theorem 6.6 says \(\E\int H\,\dd B=0\) and \(\E(\int H\,\dd B)^2=\E\int H^2\,\dd s\). Is the law of \(\int_0^1H\,\dd B\) Gaussian? We compute left-point sums for four integrands: deterministic \(H_s=s\) (a Wiener integral), \(H=B\), \(H=\sgn(B)\), and \(H=B^2\). We compare the empirical mean, second moment and law with the theory.

What the lab shows (default settings unless stated)
  • Isometry, \(H=B\): \(\hat\E I^2=\) 0.4774 ± 0.0125 and \(\hat\E\int H^2=\) 0.4913. On this 500-step grid both have the exact value 0.4990, and the continuous-time value is 1/2. The mean is −0.0092 ± 0.0049.
  • Not Gaussian for \(H=B\): \(\int_0^1B\,\dd B=\frac{B_1^2-1}2\), a shifted chi-square. KS distance to \(N(0,\tfrac12)\): 0.2115; skewness 2.806 (exact \(2\sqrt2\approx2.83\)).
  • Gaussian for deterministic \(H\): \(\int_0^1s\,\dd B_s\sim N(0,\frac13)\). KS distance 0.0073, variance 0.3277.
  • Gaussian, surprisingly, for \(H=\sgn(B)\): KS distance 0.0080, variance 0.9901. The integrand is random, yet \(\br{\int\sgn(B)\,\dd B}_t=t\), so the integral is a Brownian motion by Lévy's characterization (Example 7.9). The law of an integral depends on the whole process, not only on \(\E H^2\).
  • Heavy tails, \(H=B^2\): \(\hat\E I^2=\) 0.9271 ± 0.0559 against the exact 0.9970. The agreement is noisier because \(I\) has heavy tails (skewness 0.299, KS 0.1956).

Remark 6.7: \(\E X_\tau=\E X_0\) for a right-continuous martingale and a bounded stopping time: optional stopping (Thm. 4.30).

Proposition 6.8notes p. 53

For \(M,N\in\Mtc\), \(H\in L^2(M)\) and a bounded stopping time \(\tau\): \(\E[(H\cdot M)_\tau N_\tau]=\E\big[\int_0^\tau H_s\,\dd\br{M,N}_s\big]\).

Proof (the notes' first display is circular). \(C_t=(H\cdot M)_tN_t-\int_0^tH\,\dd\br{M,N}\) is a martingale by Thm. 6.6 and Prop. 5.35, and it is null at 0. Optional stopping at the bounded \(\tau\) gives \(\E C_\tau=0\).

Remark 6.9notes p. 53

The bracket characterization reads \(\br{\int_0^\cdot H_s\,\dd M_s,N}_t=\int_0^tH_s\,\dd\br{M,N}_s\). In particular, stochastic integrals in \(\Mtc\) have mean zero: \(\E\int_0^\infty H_s\,\dd M_s=0\).

(The notes open this remark with “Parameterization extends to the bracket”, apparently a slip for “The characterization extends…”.)

Lab 6.2 · Def. 6.4, Rem. 6.9

What if the integrand peeks into the future?

Question: the mean-zero property \(\E\int H\,\dd B=0\) rests on \(H\) being progressive, known before each increment. How much look-ahead does it take to break it? We compute \(\E\big[\sum_iH_{t_i}\Delta B_i\big]\) for \(H_t=B_{t+\delta}\) (capped at time 1), for several shifts \(\delta\), on a 1000-step grid.

What the lab shows (default settings unless stated)
  • Adapted integrands give 0: \(\delta=0\): 0.007; a lag \(\delta=-0.1\): 0.002.
  • Any look-ahead gives about 1: one grid step ahead (\(\delta=0.001\)): 1.007; \(\delta=0.1\): 1.012; \(H\equiv B_1\): 1.014. Largest standard error 0.014. The reason: \(\E[B_{t_i+\delta}\Delta_iB]=\min(\delta,\Delta t_i)\) per cell, and these add up to \(t=1\) as soon as \(\delta\) is at least one cell. There is no gradual degradation: the property is all or nothing.
  • This is why the notes' “non-progressive” (Def. 6.4) must be “progressive”. It is also why the left point is the only evaluation point that keeps the martingale property (Lab 6.3).
Proposition 6.10notes p. 54

(a) For \(A\in\F_0\): \((\ind_AH)\cdot M=\ind_A(H\cdot M)\). (b) For \(K\in L^2(N)\): \(\br{H\cdot M,K\cdot N}=\int_0^\cdot H_sK_s\,\dd\br{M,N}_s\). In particular \(\br{H\cdot M}=\int_0^\cdot H_s^2\,\dd\br{M}_s\).

(As printed, (a) reads \(\int_0^\cdot\ind_AH\,\dd\br{M,N}=\ind_A\int_0^\cdot H\,\dd\br{M,N}\), which is trivial. The intended statement is the one above.) For (b), using the characterization twice:

\[ \begin{aligned}\br{H\cdot M,K\cdot N}&=H\cdot\br{M,K\cdot N}\\ &=H\cdot(K\cdot\br{M,N})=(HK)\cdot\br{M,N}.\end{aligned} \]
Proposition 6.11 (associativity)notes p. 54

\((HK)\cdot M=H\cdot(K\cdot M)\), i.e. \(\int H\,\dd\big(\int K\,\dd M\big)=\int HK\,\dd M\).

Proposition 6.12 (stopping)notes p. 54

\((H\cdot M)^\tau=(H\ind_{[0,\tau]})\cdot M=H\cdot M^\tau\). In particular, \(M^\tau=\ind_{[0,\tau]}\cdot M\).

Both are proved the same way: compute \(\br{\cdot,N}\) of both sides, and conclude by uniqueness.

§6.2 Integration with respect to continuous local martingales

notes, pp. 55–56

The problem. The \(L^2\) theory needs \(\E\int H^2\,\dd\br{M}\lt\infty\) and \(M\in\Mtc\). Brownian motion on \([0,\infty)\) is not bounded in \(L^2\), and natural integrands such as \(H=e^{B^2}\) are not in \(L^2\). We want \(\int_0^tH\,\dd M\) whenever \(\int_0^tH^2\,\dd\br{M}\lt\infty\) path by path.

Definition 6.13notes p. 55

For \(M\in\Mloc\) with \(M_0=0\), \(L^2_{\mathrm{loc}}(M)\) is the set of progressive \(H\) with \(\int_0^tH_s^2\,\dd\br{M}_s\lt\infty\) a.s. for every \(t\).

Theorem 6.14 (local extension)notes pp. 55–56

Let \(M\in\Mloc\), \(M_0=0\). (a) For \(H\in L^2_{\mathrm{loc}}(M)\) there is a unique continuous local martingale \(H\cdot M\) with \(\br{H\cdot M,N}=\int H\,\dd\br{M,N}\) for every \(N\in\Mloc\). (b) \(\br{H\cdot M,K\cdot N}=\int HK\,\dd\br{M,N}\) for \(K\in L^2_{\mathrm{loc}}(N)\). (c) If \(M\in\Mtc\) and \(H\in L^2(M)\), this is the integral of Theorem 6.6.

In one sentenceStop before things get large, integrate in \(L^2\), and glue the pieces.
Proof idea\(\tau_n=\inf\{t:\br{M}_t+\int_0^tH^2\,\dd\br{M}\ge n\}\). Then \(M^{\tau_n}\in\Mtc\) (its bracket is at most \(n\)) and \(H\in L^2(M^{\tau_n})\), so \(H\cdot M^{\tau_n}\) exists. Compatibility: \((H\cdot M^{\tau_n})^{\tau_m}=H\cdot M^{\tau_m}\) for \(m\lt n\) (Prop. 6.12). Define \(H\cdot M=H\cdot M^{\tau_n}\) on \([0,\tau_n]\); it is well defined, continuous, and \(\tau_n\uparrow\infty\) makes it a local martingale.
What is lostMean zero and the isometry can fail for \(H\in L^2_{\mathrm{loc}}\setminus L^2\): the integral is only a local martingale (Lesson 5, Lab 5.3). Remark 6.15 gives the safe version: if \(\E\int_0^\tau H^2\,\dd\br{M}\lt\infty\), then \((H\cdot M)^\tau\in\Mtc\), \(\E\int_0^\tau H\,\dd M=0\) and \(\E(\int_0^\tau H\,\dd M)^2=\E\int_0^\tau H^2\,\dd\br{M}\).
Used againEvery stochastic integral in Lessons 7–8, including \(\int f'(X)\,\dd X\) in Itô's formula and \(\int\sigma(X)\,\dd B\) in SDEs.

§6.3 Integration with respect to continuous semimartingales

notes, pp. 56–59

The problem. Finally, integrate against \(X=X_0+M+V\): a Stieltjes integral for \(V\) plus a stochastic integral for \(M\). The integrands are locally bounded progressive processes: \(\sup_{[0,t]}|H_s|\lt\infty\) a.s. for every \(t\), for example continuous adapted \(H\). Such \(H\) are \(|\dd V|\)-integrable on compacts and belong to \(L^2_{\mathrm{loc}}(M)\).

Definition 6.16notes p. 56

For a semimartingale \(X=X_0+M+V\) and a locally bounded progressive \(H\): \(\int_0^tH_s\,\dd X_s=\int_0^tH_s\,\dd M_s+\int_0^tH_s\,\dd V_s\).

Proposition 6.17notes p. 57

(a) \(H\cdot(K\cdot X)=(HK)\cdot X\). (b) \((H\cdot X)^\tau=(H\ind_{[0,\tau]})\cdot X=H\cdot X^\tau\). (c) \((H,X)\mapsto H\cdot X\) is bilinear. (d) If \(V\equiv0\), \(H\cdot X\) is a local martingale. (e) For an elementary \(H=\sum_iH_{t_i}\ind_{(t_i,t_{i+1}]}\) with \(H_{t_i}\) \(\F_{t_i}\)-measurable: \(\int_0^tH\,\dd X=\sum_iH_{t_i}(X_{t_{i+1}\wedge t}-X_{t_i\wedge t})\).

(The notes sum from \(i=0\) to \(p\) in \(H\) and to \(p-1\) in the integral, a minor index slip.) Note that \(H\cdot X\) is again a semimartingale, with martingale part \(H\cdot M\) and finite-variation part \(H\cdot V\), and \(\br{H\cdot X}=\int H^2\,\dd\br{X}\).

Proposition 6.18 (left Riemann sums)notes pp. 57–58

For \(H\) continuous and adapted, and meshes of \([0,t]\) whose size tends to 0: \(\sum_iH_{t^n_i}\big(X_{t^n_{i+1}}-X_{t^n_i}\big)\xrightarrow{\P}\int_0^tH_s\,\dd X_s\).

Why. The Riemann sum is exactly \(\int_0^tK^n\,\dd X\) for the elementary process \(K^n\) that freezes \(H\) at the left point of each cell. \(K^n\to H\) pointwise by continuity. If \(H\) and \(\br{X}\) are bounded, the isometry gives \(\E(\int(K^n-H)\,\dd M)^2=\E\int(K^n-H)^2\,\dd\br{M}\to0\) by dominated convergence. The \(V\) part converges by dominated convergence for Stieltjes integrals. The general case follows by stopping at \(\tau_m=\inf\{u:\sup_{r\le u}|H_r|+\br{X}_u\ge m\}\) and bounding \(\P(\tau_m\le t)\).

Remark 6.19notes p. 58

Unlike Stieltjes integrals, \(\sum_iH_{t_{i+1}}\Delta_iX-\sum_iH_{t_i}\Delta_iX=\sum_i\Delta_iH\,\Delta_iX\) does not tend to 0. For \(H=X=B\) it tends to \(t\).

The difference is the realized covariation of \(H\) and \(X\), which converges to \(\br{H,X}_t\). It vanishes only when \(H\) or \(X\) has finite variation, which is the Stieltjes case.

Lab 6.3 · Prop. 6.18, Rem. 6.19

Left point, right point or midpoint: three different integrals

Question: for \(\int_0^1H(B_s)\,\dd B_s\), what do left-point, right-point and midpoint Riemann sums converge to? The left point gives the Itô integral. Its exact value, from Itô's formula (Lesson 7), is \(F(B_1)-\frac12\int_0^1H'(B_s)\,\dd s\), where \(F'=H\), \(F(0)=0\). The midpoint in time converges to the Stratonovich integral (beyond the notes), which obeys ordinary calculus: \(\int_0^1H(B)\circ\dd B=F(B_1)\). We measure all three on the same paths.

What the lab shows (default settings unless stated)
  • \(H=B\): left sums average −0.0054 and converge to \(\frac{B_1^2-1}2\): mean error 0.0045 at \(2^{14}\) steps. Right sums average 0.9940, and they converge to \(\frac{B_1^2+1}2\). Midpoint sums average 0.4943 and converge to \(\frac{B_1^2}2\), the ordinary-calculus answer: mean error 0.0030. Right minus left is 0.9994, against \(\br{B,B}_1=1\).
  • \(H=\sin B\): right minus left is 0.7888, against \(\hat\E\int\cos B_s\,\dd s=\) 0.7895. The Itô limit \(1-\cos B_1-\frac12\int\cos B\) is matched with error 0.0037.
  • Why the left point: only left-point sums are martingale transforms. Their mean is 0 at every mesh (here −0.0054 for \(H=B\)). That is the property the whole theory (isometry, localization, Girsanov) is built on, and it is what the Euler–Maruyama scheme reproduces when simulating SDEs. The Stratonovich convention is natural when ordinary chain rules are needed, for example for SDEs on manifolds, and it differs from Itô by \(\frac12\br{H,X}\).
Proposition 6.20 (integration by parts)notes p. 58

For continuous semimartingales \(X,Y\): \[ X_tY_t=X_0Y_0+\int_0^tX_s\,\dd Y_s+\int_0^tY_s\,\dd X_s+\br{X,Y}_t. \]

In one sentenceThe product rule of stochastic calculus has an extra term, the covariation.
ProofAn algebraic identity on any mesh: \(X_{t_{i+1}}Y_{t_{i+1}}-X_{t_i}Y_{t_i}=X_{t_i}\Delta_iY+Y_{t_i}\Delta_iX+\Delta_iX\,\Delta_iY\). Sum over \(i\). The left side telescopes to \(X_tY_t-X_0Y_0\). On the right, two left Riemann sums (Prop. 6.18) and a realized covariation (Prop. 5.43).
ConsequencesRemark 6.21: products of semimartingales are semimartingales. Remark 6.22: for \(M\in\Mloc\) with \(M_0=0\), \(M_t^2-\br{M}_t=2\int_0^tM_s\,\dd M_s\). This identifies the local martingale of Theorem 5.23; for Brownian motion, \(\int_0^tB\,\dd B=\frac{B_t^2-t}2\).
Used againIt is the proof of Itô's formula for polynomials, and by density for \(C^2\) functions (Thm. 7.1). It is also how \(\cE(L)\) is shown to satisfy \(\dd\cE=\cE\,\dd L\) (Example 7.7).
Example 6.23 (brackets of powers of \(B\))notes pp. 58–59

For \(m,n\ge1\): \(\br{B^m,B^n}_t=mn\int_0^tB_s^{m+n-2}\,\dd s\).

Watch out: the printed proof of Example 6.23 is wrong (pp. 58–59)

The statement is right, but the proof is garbled. It claims \(\br{B^m,B^n}_t=\br{B^m,B^{m+n}}_t\), which is false: for \(m=n=1\) the right side is \(\br{B,B^2}_t=2\int_0^tB_s\,\dd s\), not \(t\). It then writes \(\br{B^m,B^n}_t=\{m+n\}\,m\int_0^tB_s^{m+n-1}\,\dd s\), which has the wrong power and the wrong constant. It has stray “\(++\)” signs, and it jumps to the right answer. A correct proof by induction with Prop. 6.20 follows.

Correct proof. Claim: for every \(k\ge1\), \(B^k\) is a semimartingale with \[ B_t^k=k\int_0^tB_s^{k-1}\,\dd B_s+\frac{k(k-1)}2\int_0^tB_s^{k-2}\,\dd s. \tag{\(\ast\)} \] For \(k=1\) this is trivial. Assume \((\ast)\) for \(k\). Integration by parts with \(X=B^k\), \(Y=B\) gives \(B^{k+1}=\int B^k\,\dd B+\int B\,\dd B^k+\br{B^k,B}\). By \((\ast)\) and associativity (Prop. 6.11), \(\int B\,\dd B^k=k\int B^k\,\dd B+\frac{k(k-1)}2\int B^{k-1}\,\dd s\). By Prop. 6.10, \(\br{B^k,B}=k\int B^{k-1}\,\dd s\). Summing: \[ B^{k+1}_t=(k+1)\int_0^tB^k\,\dd B+\Big(\frac{k(k-1)}2+k\Big)\int_0^tB^{k-1}\,\dd s, \] which is \((\ast)\) for \(k+1\). Now \(\br{B^m,B^n}\) only sees the martingale parts \(m\int B^{m-1}\,\dd B\) and \(n\int B^{n-1}\,\dd B\), so by Prop. 6.10, \(\br{B^m,B^n}_t=mn\int_0^tB^{m+n-2}\,\dd s\). Formula \((\ast)\) is Itô's formula for \(f(x)=x^k\), obtained from the product rule alone.

Exercises

The notes leave the density of elementary processes and several proofs implicit. The exercises below fill them in and practise the rules.

E1 · \(\int_0^tB\,\dd B\) by hand Rem. 6.19, Rem. 6.22

Show that \(\sum_iB_{t_i}\Delta_iB=\frac12\big(B_t^2-\sum_i(\Delta_iB)^2\big)\). Deduce \(\int_0^tB\,\dd B=\frac12(B_t^2-t)\), its mean and its variance.

Hint

\(B_{t_{i+1}}^2-B_{t_i}^2=2B_{t_i}\Delta_iB+(\Delta_iB)^2\).

Solution

Sum the hint over \(i\): \(B_t^2=2\sum B_{t_i}\Delta B+\sum(\Delta B)^2\). The quadratic sums tend to \(t\) (Prop. 2.21), so the left sums tend to \(\frac12(B_t^2-t)\). The mean is \(\frac12(t-t)=0\). The variance is \(\frac14\Var(B_t^2)=\frac14\cdot2t^2=\frac{t^2}2\), which agrees with the isometry \(\E\int_0^tB_s^2\,\dd s=\frac{t^2}2\). The right-point sums give \(\frac12(B_t^2+t)\). Lab 6.3 measures both.

E2 · Elementary processes are dense in \(L^2(M)\) asserted without proof in Thm. 6.6
Hint

First take \(H\) bounded, continuous and adapted, and freeze it at the left points. Then reach general progressive \(H\) with the averages \(H^{(n)}_t=n\int_{(t-1/n)^+}^tH_s\,\dd s\), which are continuous and adapted, when \(\br{M}_t=t\). For general \(\br{M}\), change time.

Solution (sketch)

(i) If \(H\) is bounded, continuous and adapted, \(K^n=\sum_iH_{t_i}\ind_{(t_i,t_{i+1}]}\to H\) pointwise, and \(\E\int(K^n-H)^2\,\dd\br{M}\to0\) by dominated convergence (truncate time at \(T\) and use \(\E\br{M}_\infty\lt\infty\)). (ii) Bounded progressive \(H\) and \(\br{M}_t=t\): \(H^{(n)}\) is continuous and adapted (progressive measurability is used here), and \(H^{(n)}\to H\) \(\dd t\otimes\dd\P\)-a.e. by Lebesgue's differentiation theorem. (iii) Truncation \(H\ind_{|H|\le k}\to H\) in \(L^2(M)\). (iv) For a general \(\br{M}\), run (ii) in the time scale \(A_t=\br{M}_t+t\). The full argument is in Karatzas–Shreve, Lemmas 3.2.4–3.2.7, or Revuz–Yor, Lemma IV.2.2.

E3 · Wiener integrals are Gaussian Lab 6.1

For deterministic \(f\in L^2([0,t])\), show that \(\int_0^tf(s)\,\dd B_s\sim N\big(0,\int_0^tf^2\big)\). Compute the law of \(\int_0^1s\,\dd B_s\), and express it without a stochastic integral.

Solution

For a step function \(f\), the integral is a finite linear combination of independent Gaussian increments, hence Gaussian with variance \(\sum f_i^2\Delta t_i=\int f^2\). Step functions are dense in \(L^2\), and \(L^2\) limits of centered Gaussians are Gaussian, with the limiting variance given by the isometry. For \(f(s)=s\) on \([0,1]\): \(N(0,\frac13)\). By Prop. 6.20 with \(X_s=s\) (finite variation, \(\br{X,B}=0\)): \(\int_0^1s\,\dd B_s=B_1-\int_0^1B_s\,\dd s\). Check: Lab 6.1 gives variance 0.3277 and KS distance 0.0073.

E4 · Rules of the calculus Prop. 6.10–6.12, 6.17

Let \(X_t=\int_0^tB_s\,\dd B_s\). Compute \(\br{X}_t\), \(\br{X,B}_t\), \(\E X_t^2\) and \(\int_0^tB_s\,\dd X_s\).

Solution

\(\br{X}=\int B^2\,\dd s\), \(\br{X,B}=\int B\,\dd s\), \(\E X_t^2=\E\int_0^tB^2=t^2/2\). By associativity, \(\int_0^tB\,\dd X=\int_0^tB^2\,\dd B\). By Example 6.23 \((\ast)\) with \(k=3\), this equals \(\frac13B_t^3-\int_0^tB_s\,\dd s\).

E5 · \(\int\sgn(B)\,\dd B\) is a Brownian motion previews Lesson 7, Example 7.9

Let \(\beta_t=\int_0^t\sgn(B_s)\,\dd B_s\). Show that \(\br{\beta}_t=t\), and explain why \(\beta\) is not a function of the path of \(B\) through \(B_t\) alone.

Solution

\(\br{\beta}=\int\sgn(B)^2\,\dd s=\int\ind_{B_s\ne0}\,\dd s=t\), since the time spent at 0 has Lebesgue measure zero (Fubini: \(\E\int\ind_{B_s=0}\,\dd s=0\)). By Lévy's characterization (Lesson 7), \(\beta\) is a Brownian motion. Beyond the notes: Tanaka's formula says \(|B_t|=\beta_t+L^0_t\), where \(L^0\) is the local time at 0, so \(\beta\) depends on the whole path of \(|B|\). This is exactly the SDE of Example 8.12.

E6 · Proposition 6.12 via brackets notes p. 54
Solution

For \(N\in\Mtc\):

\[ \begin{aligned}\br{(H\cdot M)^\tau,N}_t&=\br{H\cdot M,N}_{t\wedge\tau}\\ &=\int_0^{t\wedge\tau}H\,\dd\br{M,N}\\ &=\int_0^tH\ind_{[0,\tau]}\,\dd\br{M,N}\\ &=\br{(H\ind_{[0,\tau]})\cdot M,N}_t.\end{aligned} \]

The two processes have the same bracket with every \(N\). Their difference therefore has zero bracket with itself, so it is 0 (Cor. 5.29). Also \(\int H\ind_{[0,\tau]}\,\dd\br{M,N}=\int H\,\dd\br{M^\tau,N}\), which gives \(H\cdot M^\tau\).

E7 · Local martingale integrals can have nonzero mean limits of Thm. 6.14, links to Lesson 5

Beyond the notes: for a 3-d Brownian motion from \(x\ne0\), Itô's formula gives \(\frac1{|W_t|}=\frac1{|x|}-\int_0^t\frac{W_s}{|W_s|^3}\cdot\dd W_s\). Show that the stochastic integral on the right has strictly positive mean.

Solution

Its mean is \(\frac1{|x|}-\E\frac1{|W_t|}=1-\operatorname{erf}(1/\sqrt{2t})\gt0\) for \(|x|=1\) (Lesson 5, E7). The integrand \(H=W/|W|^3\) is in \(L^2_{\mathrm{loc}}\) (the path avoids 0), but \(\E\int_0^tH^2\,\dd s=\infty\), so Remark 6.15 does not apply. The integral is a strict local martingale. At \(t=1\) its mean is \(1-0.6827=0.3173\) (Lab 5.3).

E8 · Midpoint sums and the Stratonovich correction Lab 6.3, beyond the notes

Show that \(\sum_iB_{(t_i+t_{i+1})/2}\,\Delta_iB\to\frac12B_t^2\).

Solution

Write \(B_{m_i}=B_{t_i}+(B_{m_i}-B_{t_i})\), with \(m_i\) the midpoint. The first part gives the Itô sum, which tends to \(\frac12(B_t^2-t)\). The second is \(\sum(B_{m_i}-B_{t_i})\big((B_{m_i}-B_{t_i})+(B_{t_{i+1}}-B_{m_i})\big)\). Its mean is \(\sum\Delta t_i/2=t/2\), and its variance tends to 0 (independent increments). Total: \(\frac12B_t^2\). Check: Lab 6.3, mean error 0.0030.

Conclusion

  • Hilbert-space construction. \(\Mtc\) is a Hilbert space (Thm. 6.3). \(H\mapsto H\cdot M\) is the unique isometry \(L^2(M)\to\Mtc\) that extends \(\sum H_i\Delta M\) on elementary processes. Equivalently, it is characterized by \(\br{H\cdot M,N}=H\cdot\br{M,N}\) (Thm. 6.6).
  • What survives. Linearity, associativity, stopping (Props. 6.11–6.12); mean zero and the Itô isometry in \(L^2\) (Rem. 6.9, Lab 6.1). These need progressive integrands; any look-ahead breaks them (Lab 6.2).
  • Localization. For \(H\in L^2_{\mathrm{loc}}(M)\) and \(M\in\Mloc\) (Thm. 6.14). The integral is only a local martingale, so means can be nonzero (E7).
  • Semimartingales. \(\int H\,\dd X=\int H\,\dd M+\int H\,\dd V\) (Def. 6.16). Left Riemann sums converge (Prop. 6.18). The evaluation point matters: right sums differ by \(\br{H,X}\), and midpoints give Stratonovich (Lab 6.3).
  • Product rule. \(\dd(XY)=X\,\dd Y+Y\,\dd X+\dd\br{X,Y}\) (Prop. 6.20). It already yields Itô's formula for powers (Example 6.23, corrected proof) and \(M^2-\br{M}=2\int M\,\dd M\).

Next: Lesson 7 gives the chain rule, Itô's formula, and its consequences: Lévy's characterization, time changes, and Girsanov's change of measure.

Reference implementation (Python / NumPy)

The Itô integral as left-point Riemann sums, with the checks of the labs. It uses scipy.stats.kstest and scipy.stats.skew. It was run as shown, and the output is verbatim.

"""Lesson 6 — reference implementation of the Ito integral by left Riemann sums, with checks (NumPy/SciPy)."""
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(6)

def ito_integral(H, dB):
    """Left-point Riemann sums sum_i H_{t_i} (B_{t_{i+1}} - B_{t_i}) along the rows (Prop. 6.18).
    H: array (paths, n) of integrand values at the left end of each cell (must be adapted); dB: increments."""
    return np.sum(H * dB, axis=1)

paths, n = 20000, 1000
dB = rng.standard_normal((paths, n)) / np.sqrt(n)
B = np.concatenate([np.zeros((paths, 1)), np.cumsum(dB, axis=1)], axis=1)
t = np.arange(n) / n

print("1) int_0^1 B dB: left sums vs the Ito formula value (B_1^2 - 1)/2 (Prop. 6.20 / Rem. 6.22)")
I = ito_integral(B[:, :-1], dB)
print(f"   mean |left sum - (B_1^2-1)/2| = {np.mean(np.abs(I - (B[:, -1]**2 - 1)/2)):.4f};  right-left gap mean = {np.mean(np.sum(dB**2, axis=1)):.4f} (-> t = 1)")

print("2) Ito isometry E[(int H dB)^2] = E[int H^2 ds], mean zero, Gaussianity (Thm 6.6, Rem 6.15)")
for name, H in [("H_s = s", np.broadcast_to(t, (paths, n))), ("H_s = B_s", B[:, :-1]), ("H_s = sign(B_s)", np.sign(B[:, :-1]))]:
    I = ito_integral(H, dB)
    iso = np.mean(np.sum(H**2, axis=1) / n)
    ks = stats.kstest(I / np.sqrt(iso), "norm")
    print(f"   {name:16s}: E I = {I.mean():+.4f}  E I^2 = {np.mean(I**2):.4f}  E int H^2 = {iso:.4f}  KS p-value vs N(0, E int H^2) = {ks.pvalue:.3g}  skew = {stats.skew(I):+.3f}")

print("3) Look-ahead integrands: E[sum_i B_{(t_i+delta)^1} dB_i]  (0 only for delta <= 0)")
for delta in [-0.1, 0.0, 0.001, 0.1]:
    k = int(round(delta * n))
    idx = np.clip(np.arange(n) + k, 0, n)
    I = np.sum(B[:, idx] * dB, axis=1)
    print(f"   delta = {delta:+.3f}: {I.mean():+.4f} (se {I.std()/np.sqrt(paths):.4f})")

print("4) Example 6.23: <B^m, B^n>_1 = mn int_0^1 B^(m+n-2) ds, realized covariation on 2^16 steps, 100 paths")
m_steps = 2**16
Bf = np.concatenate([np.zeros((100, 1)), np.cumsum(rng.standard_normal((100, m_steps)) / np.sqrt(m_steps), axis=1)], axis=1)
for m_, n_ in [(1, 1), (2, 1), (2, 2), (2, 3), (3, 3)]:
    real = np.sum(np.diff(Bf**m_, axis=1) * np.diff(Bf**n_, axis=1), axis=1)
    form = m_ * n_ * np.sum(Bf[:, :-1]**(m_ + n_ - 2), axis=1) / m_steps
    print(f"   (m,n)=({m_},{n_}): mean |realized - formula| / mean |formula| = {np.mean(np.abs(real - form)) / np.mean(np.abs(form)):.4f}")

Cheat sheet

Spaces

\(\Mtc\): continuous \(L^2\)-bounded martingales null at 0, norm \(\E\br{M}_\infty\). \(L^2(M)\): progressive \(H\) with \(\E\int H^2\,\dd\br{M}\lt\infty\).

Definition

Elementary: \(\sum H_i\Delta M\), \(H_i\in\F_{t_i}\). Extend by isometry, or characterize by \(\br{H\cdot M,N}=\int H\,\dd\br{M,N}\).

Isometry and mean

\(\E(\int H\,\dd M)^2=\E\int H^2\,\dd\br{M}\), \(\E\int H\,\dd M=0\) (in \(L^2(M)\)). Only local martingales in \(L^2_{\mathrm{loc}}\).

Rules

\(\br{H\cdot M,K\cdot N}=\int HK\,\dd\br{M,N}\). \((HK)\cdot M=H\cdot(K\cdot M)\). \((H\cdot M)^\tau=H\cdot M^\tau\).

Semimartingales

\(\int H\,\dd X=\int H\,\dd M+\int H\,\dd V\) for locally bounded progressive \(H\). \(\sum H_{t_i}\Delta X\to\int H\,\dd X\) for continuous adapted \(H\).

Evaluation point

Right − left \(\to\br{H,X}\). Midpoint → Stratonovich \(=\) Itô \(+\frac12\br{H,X}\).

Product rule

\(XY=X_0Y_0+\int X\,\dd Y+\int Y\,\dd X+\br{X,Y}\). \(\int_0^tB\,\dd B=\frac12(B_t^2-t)\).

Powers

\(\dd B^k=kB^{k-1}\dd B+\frac{k(k-1)}2B^{k-2}\dd t\). \(\br{B^m,B^n}=mn\int B^{m+n-2}\,\dd s\).

Self-test

1. Why can't \(\int H\,\dd B\) be defined path by path as a Stieltjes integral?

Brownian paths have infinite variation, so the Riemann sums depend on the evaluation point (Lab 5.2, Lab 6.3).

2. Define \(\Mtc\) and its norm.

Continuous \(L^2\)-bounded martingales null at 0; \(\|M\|^2=\E\br{M}_\infty=\E M_\infty^2\).

3. Which lemma makes \(\Mtc\) complete?

Doob's \(L^2\) inequality: \(L^2\) convergence of the terminal values gives uniform convergence of a subsequence, hence a continuous limit.

4. Why must integrands be progressive?

So that \(H_{t_i}\) is known before \(\Delta_iM\), which kills cross terms and gives mean zero. Any look-ahead gives \(\E\sum H\Delta B\approx t\) (Lab 6.2).

5. State the Itô isometry.

\(\E(\int_0^\infty H\,\dd M)^2=\E\int_0^\infty H^2\,\dd\br{M}\).

6. What characterizes \(H\cdot M\) uniquely?

\(\br{H\cdot M,N}=\int H\,\dd\br{M,N}\) for all \(N\in\Mtc\) (or \(\Mloc\)).

7. Compute \(\br{\int H\,\dd M}\).

\(\int H^2\,\dd\br{M}\).

8. Compute \(\int_0^tB\,\dd B\) and its variance.

\(\frac12(B_t^2-t)\); variance \(t^2/2\).

9. Is \(\int_0^1B\,\dd B\) Gaussian? And \(\int_0^1s\,\dd B_s\)?

No (shifted chi-square, skewness 2.806 in Lab 6.1). Yes: \(N(0,\frac13)\).

10. Is \(\int_0^1\sgn(B)\,\dd B\) Gaussian?

Yes: its bracket is \(t\), so it is a Brownian motion (Lévy).

11. What does localization buy, and what does it cost?

It allows \(H\in L^2_{\mathrm{loc}}\) and \(M\in\Mloc\). The price: the integral is only a local martingale, so the mean and the isometry may fail.

12. Which integrands are allowed against a semimartingale in §6.3?

Locally bounded progressive processes, e.g. continuous adapted ones.

13. Right-point minus left-point sums of \(\int H\,\dd X\) converge to what?

\(\br{H,X}_t\).

14. What do midpoint-in-time sums of \(\int B\,\dd B\) converge to?

\(\frac12B_t^2\) (Stratonovich).

15. State integration by parts.

\(X_tY_t=X_0Y_0+\int X\,\dd Y+\int Y\,\dd X+\br{X,Y}_t\).

16. Identify the local martingale \(M^2-\br{M}\).

\(2\int M\,\dd M\) (for \(M_0=0\)).

17. What is wrong with the notes' proof of Example 6.23?

It uses the false identity \(\br{B^m,B^n}=\br{B^m,B^{m+n}}\) and a wrong intermediate formula. The result is still right (Lab 6.4).

18. Give the semimartingale decomposition of \(B^3\).

\(3\int B^2\,\dd B+3\int B\,\dd s\).

19. Why does Euler–Maruyama correspond to Itô, not Stratonovich?

It evaluates the coefficients at the left end of each step, which is exactly the left-point Riemann sum.

20. Why is \(\E\int_0^tH\,\dd B\ne0\) possible for continuous adapted \(H\)?

If \(\E\int H^2=\infty\) the integral may be a strict local martingale, e.g. \(-\int W/|W|^3\cdot\dd W\) in 3-d (E7).
