A question that comes back

“Given drift and noise coefficients, is there a process, is it unique, and in which sense: same paths or same law?”

  • Lesson 1 asked the first version: does a process with prescribed laws exist? Constructions (Lévy), and uniqueness in law via fidis.
  • Lesson 5 asked for uniqueness of decompositions.
  • Lesson 7 constructed solutions by changing the measure (Prop. 7.21), and showed that an ODE with a bad drift can have no solution at all.

§8.1 SDEs: existence and pathwise uniqueness

notes, pp. 68–71

The problem. Many models are described by their local dynamics: a velocity field plus noise. In ML: Langevin dynamics for sampling, forward and backward diffusions in generative models, continuous-time limits of stochastic gradient descent (board, Chapter 0). The equation \(\dd X_t=b(t,X_t)\,\dd t+\sigma(t,X_t)\,\dd B_t\) means the integral equation below. We need conditions under which it defines a unique process.

Example 8.1 (linear SDE, Ornstein–Uhlenbeck)notes p. 68

For \(A\in\R^{d\times d}\), \(C\in\R^{d\times m}\) and an \(m\)-dimensional Brownian motion, the linear SDE \(\dd X_t=AX_t\,\dd t+C\,\dd B_t\), i.e. \(X_t=X_0+\int_0^tAX_s\,\dd s+\int_0^tC\,\dd B_s\), has the solution \[ X_t=e^{At}X_0+\int_0^te^{A(t-s)}C\,\dd B_s. \] Pathwise uniqueness holds: the difference of two solutions solves the linear ODE \(\frac{\dd}{\dd t}(X-Y)=A(X-Y)\) with zero initial value, hence vanishes.

Verification by Itô's formula (the notes say “we can easily verify”). Write \(X_t=e^{At}\big(X_0+\int_0^te^{-As}C\,\dd B_s\big)=e^{At}Z_t\). The product rule, with \(e^{At}\) of finite variation (no bracket term), gives \(\dd X=Ae^{At}Z\,\dd t+e^{At}e^{-At}C\,\dd B=AX\,\dd t+C\,\dd B\). Law: for deterministic \(X_0=x\), \(X_t\) is Gaussian (a Wiener integral, Lesson 6, E3) with mean \(e^{At}x\) and covariance \(\Sigma_t=\int_0^te^{As}CC^\top e^{A^\top s}\,\dd s\). If \(A\) is stable (eigenvalues with negative real part), \(\Sigma_t\to\Sigma_\infty\), the solution of the Lyapunov equation \(A\Sigma+\Sigma A^\top+CC^\top=0\) (E3). In one dimension, \(\dd X=-aX\,\dd t+c\,\dd B\) has stationary law \(N(0,c^2/2a)\). With \(c=\sqrt2\), this is the Langevin diffusion of the board for \(U(x)=ax^2/2\).

Lab 8.1 · Example 8.1

The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process: exact sampling vs the Euler scheme

Question: the linear SDE has an explicit Gaussian transition, so we can sample it exactly and compare with the Euler–Maruyama scheme \(X_{k+1}=X_k-aX_kh+c\sqrt h\,\xi_k\). What does the step \(h\) do to the long-run law? This is exactly the unadjusted Langevin algorithm of Lab 1.1 when \(c=\sqrt2\).

What the lab shows (default settings unless stated)
  • Exact sampling (\(a=1\), \(c=\sqrt2\)): \(\Var X_{10}=\) 1.0037, against the stationary \(c^2/2a=\) 1.0000. \(\E X_1=\) 0.7356, against \(x_0e^{-1}=0.7358\).
  • Euler inflates the variance to \(c^2/(2a-a^2h)\). For \(h=0.1\): 1.0364 against the formula 1.0526. For \(h=1\): 2.0309 against 2.0000. It is the same bias \(1/(1-h/2)\) as ULA in Lesson 1. For \(h\ge2/a\) the scheme diverges. At \(h=1.9\) it is still far from stationarity after \(K=5\) steps: measured 13.0592, exact after 5 steps 13.0264, stationary limit 20.0000.
  • Strong error on \([0,1]\) against a fine-grid reference driven by the same noise: 0.0471 for \(h=0.1\), 0.0044 for \(h=0.01\). That is roughly order 1 in \(h\), as expected for additive noise. (The strong error uses \(h\) capped at 0.25.) The library sdeint reproduces our Euler scheme to machine precision (reference code).
Definitions 8.2–8.3 (the equation, and its solutions)notes p. 68

Let \(b:\R_+\times\R^d\to\R^d\) and \(\sigma:\R_+\times\R^d\to\R^{d\times m}\) be measurable. Let \((\Omega,\F,\P)\) carry an \(m\)-dimensional Brownian motion with augmented filtration \((\F_t)\), and let \(X_0\) be \(\F_0\)-measurable. A solution of \(\cE(X_0,\Omega,(\F_t),\P,(B_t),b,\sigma)\) is a continuous \((\F_t)\)-adapted process with, almost surely for all \(t\), \[ X_t=X_0+\int_0^tb(s,X_s)\,\dd s+\int_0^t\sigma(s,X_s)\,\dd B_s. \] Componentwise: \(X^i_t=X^i_0+\int_0^tb^i(s,X_s)\,\dd s+\sum_{j=1}^m\int_0^t\sigma^{ij}(s,X_s)\,\dd B^j_s\).

(Definition 8.3 is the displayed notion of solution.)

Watch out: “strong solution” means two different things in the notes

Definition 8.3 calls any such process a “strong solution”. Definition 8.14, four pages later, defines a strong solution as one adapted to the filtration generated by the Brownian motion and \(X_0\). On this page, “solution” means Definition 8.3 and “strong solution” means Definition 8.14, which is the standard usage. Remark 8.15 admits the terminology is “a bit suspicious”.

Definition 8.4 (pathwise uniqueness)notes p. 68

Pathwise uniqueness holds for \(\cE_{X_0}(b,\sigma)\) if any two solutions on the same space, with the same Brownian motion and initial value, are indistinguishable.

Definition 8.5 (weak existence)notes p. 69

Weak existence holds if there is some filtered probability space with some \((\F_t)\)-Brownian motion and a process \(X\) solving the equation. The space and the noise are part of the answer.

Remark 8.6: “While it is intuitive to think that \((X_t)\) is adapted to the filtration generated by \((B_t)\), this is sometimes wrong. That is why we distinguish the two types of solutions.” Tanaka's equation (Example 8.12) is the standard counterexample.

Proposition 8.7 = Assumption (L) (Lipschitz and linear growth)notes p. 69

There are \(L,M\ge0\) such that, for all \(t\) (in the time interval considered) and \(x,y\): \[ \|b(t,x)-b(t,y)\|+\|\sigma(t,x)-\sigma(t,y)\|\le L\|x-y\|, \] \[ \|b(t,0)\|+\|\sigma(t,0)\|\le M. \]

Watch out

The notes label this “Proposition 8.7”, but it is an assumption: it has no conclusion. Its first sentence, “Let \(T\subset\R_+\) or \(T=[0,\infty)\)”, is garbled. Later statements refer to it as “the previous assumption”. Together the two conditions give linear growth, \(\|b(t,x)\|+\|\sigma(t,x)\|\le M+L\|x\|\le C(1+\|x\|)\).

Remark 8.8: under (L), the solution is even adapted to the Brownian filtration. The method is Picard iteration, “similarly to existence of solutions to ODEs… on an appropriate Banach space”, namely \(L^2_T=L^2([0,T]\times\Omega,\cP,\dd t\otimes\P)\), with \(\cP\) the progressive σ-field and inner product \(\br{Y,Z}_{L^2_T}=\int_0^T\E[\br{Y_t,Z_t}]\,\dd t\).

Theorem 8.9 (existence and pathwise uniqueness under (L))notes pp. 69–71

Assume (L) and \(\E\|X_0\|^2\lt\infty\), with \(X_0\) independent of \(B\). On a space carrying an \(m\)-dimensional Brownian motion with its augmented filtration, there is a solution \(X\) of \(\cE_{X_0}(b,\sigma)\) with \(X,\ b(\cdot,X),\ \sigma(\cdot,X)\in L^2_T\) for every \(T\). Any two solutions are indistinguishable (pathwise uniqueness).

In one sentenceLipschitz coefficients make the solution map a contraction after enough iterations, exactly as for ODEs, with the Itô isometry replacing the ordinary integral estimate.
Proof: the three steps of the notesDefine \(\psi(Y)_t=X_0+\int_0^tb(s,Y_s)\,\dd s+\int_0^t\sigma(s,Y_s)\,\dd B_s\). (1) \(\psi\) maps \(L^2_T\) to itself, by linear growth. (2) With \(Y^0=X_0\) and \(Y^{n+1}=\psi(Y^n)\): Cauchy–Schwarz on the \(\dd s\) integral, and the isometry on the \(\dd B\) integral, give \(u_{n+1}(t)\le K\int_0^tu_n(s)\,\dd s\) for \(u_n(t)=\E\|Y^{n+1}_t-Y^n_t\|^2\), with \(K=2L^2(T+1)\). By induction \(u_n(t)\le\frac{(Kt)^n}{n!}\sup u_0\), which is summable. So \((Y^n)\) is Cauchy in \(L^2_T\). (3) \(\psi\) is Lipschitz on \(L^2_T\), so the limit satisfies \(\psi(X)=X\).
UniquenessIf \(X,\tilde X\) are solutions, the same estimate applied to \(\psi^n(X)-\psi^n(\tilde X)=X-\tilde X\) gives \(\|X-\tilde X\|^2\le\frac{(KT)^n}{n!}\|X-\tilde X\|^2\to0\). One must first know that every solution lies in \(L^2_T\): the notes stop at \(\tau_R=\inf\{t:\|Y_t\|\ge R\}\), apply Gronwall's lemma (E2) to \(u^R(t)=\E\int_0^t\|Y^{\tau_R}_s\|^2\,\dd s\), and let \(R\to\infty\) with Fatou.
What fails without (L)Without Lipschitz: non-uniqueness, e.g. \(\dd x=2\sgn(x)\sqrt{|x|}\,\dd t\) from 0 has the solutions \(0\) and \(\pm t^2\). Without linear growth: explosion in finite time, e.g. \(\dd x=x^2\,\dd t\) blows up at \(1/x_0\). See Lab 8.3.
Watch out: hypotheses and constants in Theorem 8.9 (pp. 69–70)

The statement does not say \(\E\|X_0\|^2\lt\infty\), but the proof starts from “\(Y^0:=X_0\in L^2_T\)” and uses \(\E\|X_0\|^2\). The Picard estimate is written with the constant \(4L^2\tilde T\). Cauchy–Schwarz gives \(\tilde TL^2\) and the isometry gives \(L^2\), so the constant is \(2L^2(\tilde T+1)\). This is at most \(4L^2\tilde T\) only when \(\tilde T\ge1\). The conclusion is unaffected: any constant works, because \(C^n/n!\) is summable. Lab 8.2 shows this factorial decay.

Key idea

SDEs are solved by the same fixed-point argument as ODEs. The only new ingredient is the Itô isometry, which turns the noise term into a deterministic integral of second moments. On a computer, the fixed point of the discretized Picard map is exactly the Euler–Maruyama scheme (Lab 8.2): Picard converges to Euler, and Euler converges to the solution as \(h\to0\).

Lab 8.2 · Thm. 8.9

Watching Picard iteration converge

Question: the proof of Theorem 8.9 predicts \(\|Y^{n+1}-Y^n\|_{L^2_T}^2\le\frac{(KT)^n}{n!}\|Y^1-Y^0\|^2\). Is the decay really factorial, i.e. faster than geometric? We run Picard's iteration for the geometric Brownian motion \(\dd X=\mu X\,\dd t+\sigma X\,\dd B\) (Lipschitz, with explicit solution \(X_t=x_0e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t+\sigma B_t}\)), on fixed Brownian paths with 1000 steps, and measure the \(L^2_T\) distances by Monte Carlo.

What the lab shows (default settings unless stated)
  • Factorial decay: \(\|Y^1-Y^0\|=\) 1.37, \(\|Y^5-Y^4\|=\) 0.192, \(\|Y^{10}-Y^9\|=\) 8.99 × 10⁻⁴, \(\|Y^{15}-Y^{14}\|=\) 2.13 × 10⁻⁶. On the log scale the curve bends downward: the successive ratios shrink, which is the signature of \(1/n!\).
  • What Picard converges to: after 20 iterations, the distance to the Euler–Maruyama path computed on the same grid is 4.77 × 10⁻¹⁰, machine precision. The distance to the exact solution is 0.0783: the discretization error of the grid, which Picard cannot remove.

Remark 8.10: by construction the solution is adapted to the usual augmentation \((\F^B_t)\) of \(\sigma(X_0,B_s:s\le t)\): it is a strong solution.

Beyond the notes: Euler–Maruyama convergence rates

Under (L), the Euler–Maruyama scheme with step \(h\) satisfies \(\E\sup_{t\le T}|X^h_t-X_t|^2\le Ch\): strong order \(\frac12\). The weak error \(|\E f(X^h_T)-\E f(X_T)|\) is of order \(h\) for smooth \(f\). For additive noise (\(\sigma\) constant), the strong order improves to 1 (Lab 8.1). This is the numerical content of Lab 1.1: ULA is Euler–Maruyama for the Langevin SDE, and its bias of order \(\gamma\) is a weak error (Kloeden and Platen, 1992).

Lab 8.3 · what (L) protects against

Without Lipschitz: non-uniqueness. Without linear growth: explosion

Question: what happens when assumption (L) fails? (a) \(\dd X=2\sgn(X)\sqrt{|X|}\,\dd t+\varepsilon\,\dd B\) from 0. The drift is continuous but not Lipschitz at 0, and for \(\varepsilon=0\) the ODE has three solutions, \(0\) and \(\pm t^2\). (b) \(\dd X=X^2\,\dd t+\varepsilon\,\dd B\): the drift grows superlinearly, and the ODE explodes at \(t=1/x_0\).

What the lab shows (default settings unless stated)
  • Noise selects a solution at random (\(\varepsilon=0.01\)): a share 0.495 of the paths end positive and the rest negative, with \(\E|X_1|=\) 1.040, close to the value 1 of the ODE solutions \(\pm t^2\). The zero solution is never followed: noise pushes the path off 0, and the drift then carries it away. The Euler scheme for the ODE itself stays at \(x(1)=\) 0.000. For small noise the SDE is well posed, but the \(\varepsilon\to0\) limit is a random mixture of ODE solutions, which is the Peano phenomenon (Beyond the notes).
  • Explosion: from \(x_0=1.5\) (ODE blow-up at \(2/3\)), a share 1.000 of the paths exceed \(10^6\) before \(t=1\). From \(x_0=0.5\) (ODE blow-up at 2), only 0.001 do, even with \(\varepsilon=0.3\). Without linear growth, solutions may only exist up to a random explosion time, and Theorem 8.9 gives nothing.

§8.2 Weak uniqueness and strong solutions

notes, pp. 71–72

The problem. For sampling and generative modeling, what matters is usually the law of the solution (the law of \(X_T\), or of the path), not the particular path produced by a given Brownian motion. Can the law be unique while paths are not? Can a solution fail to be a function of the driving noise?

Definition 8.11 (weak uniqueness, uniqueness in law)notes p. 71

Weak uniqueness holds for \(\cE_{X_0}(b,\sigma)\) if any two solutions, possibly on different probability spaces with different Brownian motions, induce the same law on the Wiener space: \(\P(X\in A)=\tilde\P(\tilde X\in A)\) for all \(A\in\cW\).

(The notes write \(\cE_{X_0}(\sigma,b)\) here and \(\cE_{x_0}(1,b)\) in Example 8.13, with the arguments swapped relative to Definition 8.4. This page always writes \((b,\sigma)\).)

Example 8.12 (Tanaka's SDE: weak uniqueness without pathwise uniqueness)notes p. 71

Consider \(\dd X_t=\sgn(X_t)\,\dd B_t\), \(X_0=0\), with the convention \(\sgn(0)=-1\). Weak existence: start from a Brownian motion \(X\) and set \(\beta_t=\int_0^t\sgn(X_s)\,\dd X_s\). By Lévy, \(\beta\) is a Brownian motion (\(\br{\beta}_t=t\)), and \(\int_0^t\sgn(X_s)\,\dd\beta_s=\int_0^t\sgn(X_s)^2\,\dd X_s=X_t\). So \((X,\beta)\) is a solution. Weak uniqueness: every solution is a continuous local martingale with bracket \(\int\sgn(X)^2\,\dd s=t\), hence a Brownian motion by Lévy. The law is unique. No pathwise uniqueness: \(-X\) is another solution driven by the same \(\beta\), because \(\int_0^t\ind_{\{X_s=0\}}\,\dd\beta_s=0\).

Watch out: the convention for sign(0) matters

The notes do not fix \(\sgn(0)\). With \(\sgn(0)=0\), the process \(X\equiv0\) is also a solution (\(\dd X=\sgn(0)\,\dd B=0\)), and weak uniqueness fails too: both \(0\) and a Brownian motion solve the equation from 0. With \(\sgn(0)=\pm1\), all solutions have bracket \(t\), and Lévy gives uniqueness in law. The claim \(\int\ind_{\{X=0\}}\,\dd\beta=0\), left as an exercise in the notes, holds because its bracket is \(\int\ind_{\{X_s=0\}}\,\dd s=0\): Brownian motion spends zero Lebesgue time at 0 (E5).

Example 8.13 (bounded drift)notes p. 72 (exercise)

For measurable bounded \(b\) and \(\sigma=I\), \(\cE_{x_0}(b,I)\) has weak solutions (Girsanov, Prop. 7.21, with the sign corrected there), and weak uniqueness holds.

Why weak uniqueness (E6). If \(X\) solves \(\dd X=b(X)\,\dd t+\dd B\), then the reverse change of measure with density \(\cE(-\int b(X)\,\dd B)_T\) turns \(X-x_0\) into a Brownian motion. Hence the law of \(X\) is \(\cE\big(\int b(W)\cdot\dd W\big)_T\cdot\mu_W\), a formula that does not depend on which solution we started from. This covers drifts like \(\ind_{\{x\le0\}}\) (Lab 7.4), for which the ODE has no solution at all.

Definition 8.14 (strong solution)notes p. 72

A solution is strong if it is adapted to the augmented filtration generated by \(X_0\) and \(B\): the solution is a (non-anticipating) function of the initial value and the noise. Remark 8.15: other solutions are called weak.

Example 8.16notes p. 72

(1) Under (L), solutions are strong (Remark 8.10, and Yamada–Watanabe below). (2) The Tanaka solution is not strong: \(\F^\beta_t\subset\F^{|X|}_t\subsetneq\F^X_t\). The driving noise is a function of \(|X|\) only, so it cannot determine the sign of \(X\).

Why \(\beta\) is a function of \(|X|\). Let \(\varphi_\varepsilon(x)=\sqrt{\varepsilon+x^2}\), an even \(C^2\) function. Itô gives \(\varphi_\varepsilon(X_t)=\varphi_\varepsilon(X_0)+\int_0^t\varphi_\varepsilon'(X_s)\,\dd X_s+\frac12\int_0^t\varphi_\varepsilon''(X_s)\,\dd s\). Both \(\varphi_\varepsilon\) and \(\varphi_\varepsilon''\) are even, so \(\int\varphi_\varepsilon'(X)\,\dd X=\varphi_\varepsilon(|X_t|)-\varphi_\varepsilon(0)-\frac12\int\varphi_\varepsilon''(|X|)\,\dd s\) is \(\F^{|X|}\)-adapted. As \(\varepsilon\to0\), \(\varphi_\varepsilon'(x)=x/\sqrt{\varepsilon+x^2}\to\sgn(x)\) (for \(x\ne0\)), and the isometry gives \(\int\varphi'_\varepsilon(X)\,\dd X\to\beta\) in \(L^2\). So \(\beta\) is \(\F^{|X|}\)-adapted. If \(X\) were \(\F^\beta\)-adapted, then \(\F^X\subset\F^\beta\subset\F^{|X|}\), which is absurd: the sign of \(X_t\) is independent of \(|X_t|\).

Watch out

The notes write \(\varphi_\varepsilon(X_t)=\varepsilon+\int\cdots\). The constant is \(\varphi_\varepsilon(X_0)=\varphi_\varepsilon(0)=\sqrt\varepsilon\). They also write \(\F^B\) for the filtration of the driving Brownian motion, which in this example is \(\beta\).

Theorem 8.17 (Yamada–Watanabe, corrected)notes p. 72 (admitted)

Suppose pathwise uniqueness holds for \(\cE_x(b,\sigma)\). Then (1) weak uniqueness holds; (2) if moreover a (weak) solution exists, then on every filtered probability space carrying a Brownian motion there is a solution, and it is strong.

Watch out: Theorem 8.17 (2) needs weak existence

As printed, (2) asserts the existence of a strong solution from pathwise uniqueness alone. That is false. An equation with no solution at all has pathwise uniqueness vacuously, for example the ODE of Remark 7.15 viewed as an SDE with \(\sigma=0\). Yamada–Watanabe says: weak existence plus pathwise uniqueness implies strong existence and weak uniqueness. Tanaka's example shows that weak existence plus weak uniqueness does not imply strong existence.

Tanaka's SDE: two solutions, one noise, and a noise that forgets the sign

Question: we simulate a Brownian motion \(X\) and build \(\beta=\sum\sgn(X_{t_i})\Delta X\). We check three things. \(\beta\) is a Brownian motion. \(-X\) produces the same \(\beta\). And \(\beta\) can be rebuilt from \(|X|\) alone, via \(\tilde\beta=\varphi_\varepsilon(|X_t|)-\varphi_\varepsilon(0)-\frac12\int\varphi_\varepsilon''(|X|)\,\dd s\).

What the lab shows (default settings unless stated)

§8.3 Markov property and the Kolmogorov equation

notes, pp. 72–73

The problem. Brownian motion is Markov because its increments are independent (Thm. 2.3). Solutions of SDEs are driven by the same increments. Are they Markov, and do they have a semigroup? This is what makes sampling by simulation possible: to simulate the future, you only need the present state.

Definition 8.18 (Markov property, continued from Definition 2.2)notes p. 72

\(X\) is \((\F_t)\)-Markov if \(\E[g(X_t)\mid\F_s]=\E[g(X_t)\mid X_s]\) for all bounded measurable \(g\) and \(s\le t\) (Def. 2.2).

Theorem 8.19notes pp. 72–73

Under (L) and for \(X_0\in L^2\), the solution of \(\cE_{X_0}(b,\sigma)\) is \((\F_t)\)-Markov: \(\E[g(X_t)\mid\F_s]=P_{s,t}g(X_s)\), with \(P_{s,t}g(x)=\E\big[g\big(\psi_{s,t}(x,\tilde B)\big)\big]\).

In one sentenceRestart the equation at time \(s\): the future solves the same SDE, driven by fresh noise independent of the past, from the present value.
ProofFor \(u\ge0\), \(Y_u=X_{s+u}\) solves \(Y_u=X_s+\int_0^ub(s+r,Y_r)\,\dd r+\int_0^u\sigma(s+r,Y_r)\,\dd\tilde B_r\), with \(\tilde B_r=B_{s+r}-B_s\) independent of \(\F_s\) (Thm. 2.3). By pathwise uniqueness and strong existence, \(Y_{t-s}=\psi_{s,t}(X_s,\tilde B)\) for a measurable functional \(\psi_{s,t}\). Then “freeze” \(X_s\), which is \(\F_s\)-measurable, and integrate out \(\tilde B\), which is independent of \(\F_s\): \(\E[g(X_t)\mid\F_s]=P_{s,t}g(X_s)\). This is the same freezing lemma as for Brownian motion (Cor. 2.5).
What is usedUniqueness is essential: without it, “the” future is not a function of the present and the future noise. The existence of a measurable \(\psi_{s,t}\) is used without comment in the notes. It follows from the strong-solution construction, which builds \(X\) as a limit of explicit functionals of \((X_s,\tilde B)\).
Watch out

In the proof, “adapted to the usual augmentation generated by \((X_s,\tilde B_t:t\le s)\)” should read \((X_s,\tilde B_r:r\le t)\): the restarted solution uses the new noise up to time \(t\).

Theorem 8.20 (time-homogeneous case)notes p. 73 (proof left to the reader)

If \(b,\sigma\) do not depend on time, then \(\E[g(X^x_t)\mid\F_s]=\E[g(X^{y}_{t-s})]\big|_{y=X_s}=\psi^g_{t-s}(X_s)\): the transition only depends on the elapsed time.

Definition 8.21 (Markov semigroup)notes p. 73

\(P_tg(x)=\E[g(X^x_t)]\), where \(X^x\) solves \(\cE_x(b,\sigma)\). Remark 8.22: this is the Markov semigroup of \(\cE(b,\sigma)\).

Corollary 8.23 (semigroup property)notes p. 73

\(P_t\circ P_s=P_{t+s}\).

Why. \(P_{t+s}g(x)=\E\big[\E[g(X^x_{t+s})\mid\F_s]\big]=\E[P_tg(X^x_s)]=P_s(P_tg)(x)\), by Theorem 8.20. So \(P_{t+s}=P_sP_t\), and by symmetry of the argument \(P_tP_s\) as well. This is the Chapman–Kolmogorov equation of Lesson 2, now for any Lipschitz SDE. The notes end here (p. 73), although §8.3 is titled “Markov Property and Kolmogorov Equation”. The Kolmogorov equations are completed below, marked “Beyond the notes”.

Beyond the notes: the Kolmogorov equations (what the title of §8.3 announces)

Generator. For time-homogeneous (L) coefficients and \(g\in C^2_c\), Itô's formula (Remark 7.6) gives \(P_tg(x)=g(x)+\int_0^tP_s(\mathcal Lg)(x)\,\dd s\), with \(\mathcal L=b\cdot\nabla+\frac12\tr(\sigma\sigma^\top\nabla^2)\). So \(\frac{\dd}{\dd t}P_tg=P_t\mathcal Lg\).

Backward equation. \(u(t,x)=P_tg(x)\) solves \(\partial_tu=\mathcal Lu\), \(u(0,\cdot)=g\): the semigroup identity \(P_{t+h}=P_tP_h\), differentiated in \(h\) at 0. For Brownian motion this is the heat equation of Prop. 2.8. It is a “Feynman–Kac” link: PDE solutions are expectations over SDE paths, and Monte Carlo solves PDEs.

Forward (Fokker–Planck) equation. If \(X_t\) has density \(\rho_t\), then \(\partial_t\rho=\mathcal L^*\rho=-\nabla\cdot(b\rho)+\frac12\sum_{ij}\partial_{ij}\big((\sigma\sigma^\top)_{ij}\rho\big)\). A stationary law satisfies \(\mathcal L^*\pi=0\). For Langevin, \(b=-\nabla U\) and \(\sigma=\sqrt2I\), this gives \(\pi\propto e^{-U}\) (board, Chapter 0).

Time reversal and diffusion models. If \(X\) solves \(\dd X=b\,\dd t+\sigma\,\dd B\) on \([0,T]\), then \(\bar X_t=X_{T-t}\) solves an SDE with drift \(-b+\sigma\sigma^\top\nabla\log\rho_{T-t}\) (Haussmann and Pardoux, 1986). Score-based generative models learn \(\nabla\log\rho_t\) and run this reversed SDE from noise back to data. It is the board's point (2), built on exactly the objects of this lesson: an SDE, its Markov semigroup, and its Fokker–Planck equation.

E1 · The linear SDE (Example 8.1) notes p. 68: “We can easily verify by Itô's formula”

Show that \(X_t=e^{At}x+\int_0^te^{A(t-s)}C\,\dd B_s\) solves \(\dd X=AX\,\dd t+C\,\dd B\), and compute its mean and covariance.

Hint

Write \(X_t=e^{At}Z_t\) with \(Z_t=x+\int_0^te^{-As}C\,\dd B_s\), and use the product rule with a finite-variation factor.

Solution

\(\dd(e^{At}Z_t)=Ae^{At}Z_t\,\dd t+e^{At}\,\dd Z_t\) (no bracket, since \(e^{At}\) has finite variation) \(=AX_t\,\dd t+C\,\dd B_t\). Mean: \(e^{At}x\), since Wiener integrals have mean 0. Covariance, by the isometry: \(\Sigma_t=\int_0^te^{A(t-s)}CC^\top e^{A^\top(t-s)}\,\dd s\). In one dimension (\(A=-a\), \(C=c\)): \(\Var X_t=\frac{c^2}{2a}(1-e^{-2at})\). Lab 8.1 measures 1.0037 at \(t=10\), against 1.0000.

E2 · Gronwall's lemma used in the uniqueness part of Thm. 8.9

If \(u\) is bounded, measurable and nonnegative on \([0,T]\) with \(u(t)\le\alpha+\beta\int_0^tu(s)\,\dd s\), then \(u(t)\le\alpha e^{\beta t}\).

Solution

Iterate the inequality \(n\) times:

\[ \begin{aligned}u(t)&\le\alpha\sum_{k\lt n}\frac{(\beta t)^k}{k!}+\beta^n\int_{0\le s_n\le\dots\le s_1\le t}u(s_n)\,\dd s\\ &\le\alpha\sum_{k\lt n}\frac{(\beta t)^k}{k!}+\|u\|_\infty\frac{(\beta t)^n}{n!},\end{aligned} \]

and let \(n\to\infty\). With \(\alpha=0\) this is the uniqueness argument of Thm. 8.9. The iterated integral producing \(t^n/n!\) is the same mechanism as the Picard bound.

E3 · Stationary law of OU, and of its Euler scheme Example 8.1, Lab 8.1

(a) Show that if \(A\) is stable, \(\Sigma_\infty\) solves \(A\Sigma+\Sigma A^\top+CC^\top=0\). (b) In 1-D, find the stationary variance of \(X_{k+1}=(1-ah)X_k+c\sqrt h\,\xi_k\), and the condition on \(h\).

Solution

(a) \(\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\Sigma_t=A\Sigma_t+\Sigma_tA^\top+CC^\top\) (differentiate the integral). At the limit the derivative vanishes. (b) \(v=(1-ah)^2v+c^2h\), so \(v=\frac{c^2}{2a-a^2h}\), provided \(|1-ah|\lt1\), i.e. \(0\lt h\lt2/a\). Lab 8.1: 1.0364 against 1.0526 at \(h=0.1\). With \(a=1\), \(c=\sqrt2\) this is \(\frac1{1-h/2}\), the ULA bias of Lesson 1, E8.

E4 · Geometric Brownian motion Lab 8.2

Solve \(\dd X=\mu X\,\dd t+\sigma X\,\dd B\), \(X_0=x\gt0\), and explain why Theorem 8.9 applies.

Solution

The coefficients \(\mu x\) and \(\sigma x\) are Lipschitz with linear growth, so there is a unique strong solution. Guess \(X=x\,\cE(\mu t+\sigma B)=x\exp\big((\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}2)t+\sigma B_t\big)\). Itô for \(f(y)=e^y\) gives \(\dd X=X\big((\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}2)\dd t+\sigma\dd B\big)+\frac12X\sigma^2\dd t=\mu X\,\dd t+\sigma X\,\dd B\). By uniqueness, this is the solution. Note \(\E X_t=xe^{\mu t}\), while the median is \(xe^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t}\): the Itô correction is visible in the typical path.

E5 · \(\int\ind_{\{X=0\}}\,\dd\beta=0\) Example 8.12: “(exercise)”
Solution

\(N_t=\int_0^t\ind_{\{X_s=0\}}\,\dd\beta_s\) is a continuous local martingale with bracket \(\int_0^t\ind_{\{X_s=0\}}\,\dd s\), since \(\br{\beta}_t=t\). By Fubini, \(\E\int_0^t\ind_{\{X_s=0\}}\,\dd s=\int_0^t\P(X_s=0)\,\dd s=0\), because \(X\) is a Brownian motion. So the bracket vanishes a.s., hence \(N\equiv0\) (Cor. 5.29). Consequence: with \(\sgn(0)=-1\), \(\int\sgn(-X)\,\dd\beta=-\int\sgn(X)\,\dd\beta+2\int\ind_{\{X=0\}}\,\dd\beta=-X\). Lab 8.4 sees this identity, up to the first grid step.

E6 · Weak uniqueness for bounded drift Example 8.13: “Exercise”

Let \(b\) be bounded measurable. Show that any two solutions of \(\dd X=b(X)\,\dd t+\dd B\), \(X_0=x\), have the same law on \([0,T]\).

Hint

Given a solution \((X,B)\), change the measure with \(\cE(L)\), \(L=-\int b(X)\cdot\dd B\), so that \(X-x\) becomes a Brownian motion. Then express the original law through the new one.

Solution

Novikov holds (\(\br{L}_T\le T\sup|b|^2\)). Under \(\dd\Q=\cE(L)_T\,\dd\P\), \(W:=B+\int b(X)\,\dd s=X-x\) is a Brownian motion (Girsanov plus Lévy). Rewrite the density in terms of \(X\): \(\cE(L)_T^{-1}=\exp\big(\int b(X)\cdot\dd X-\frac12\int|b(X)|^2\dd s\big)\). So for bounded measurable \(F\), with \(W\) a Brownian motion:

\[ \begin{aligned}\E^\P[F(X)]&=\E^\Q\big[F(X)\cE(L)_T^{-1}\big]\\ &=\E\Big[F(x+W)\,e^{\int b(x+W)\cdot\dd W-\frac12\int|b(x+W)|^2\dd s}\Big].\end{aligned} \]

The right side does not depend on the solution: weak uniqueness. It is exactly the reweighting of Lab 7.4.

E7 · Theorem 8.20 notes p. 73: “left to the reader”
Solution

With time-independent coefficients, the restarted process \(Y_u=X_{s+u}\) solves \(\cE_{X_s}(b,\sigma)\), with the same \(b,\sigma\), driven by \(\tilde B\). By uniqueness in law, the law of \(Y_{t-s}\) given \(X_s=y\) is that of \(X^y_{t-s}\). Freezing gives \(\E[g(X_t)\mid\F_s]=\E[g(X^y_{t-s})]|_{y=X_s}=P_{t-s}g(X_s)\). The transition depends on \(t-s\) only because shifting time does not change the coefficients.

E8 · Non-uniqueness without Lipschitz Lab 8.3

Find all solutions of \(x'=2\sgn(x)\sqrt{|x|}\), \(x(0)=0\).

Solution

For any \(t_0\ge0\), \(x(t)=\pm(t-t_0)_+^2\) is a solution, and so is \(x\equiv0\): there are infinitely many. The drift is continuous (Peano gives existence) but not Lipschitz at 0 (\(\sqrt{|x|}\) has infinite slope), so the Picard argument fails. With small noise, Lab 8.3 finds \(\P(X_1\gt0)=\) 0.495 and \(\E|X_1|=\) 1.040. The noise leaves 0 immediately, so the zero-noise limit selects the branches \(\pm t^2\) (with \(t_0=0\)) with probability \(\frac12\) each.

Conclusion, and back to the board's Chapter 0

  • Existence and uniqueness. Lipschitz and linear-growth coefficients give a unique strong solution, by Picard iteration in \(L^2_T\) with the Itô isometry (Thm. 8.9). Without Lipschitz, uniqueness can fail; without linear growth, solutions can explode (Lab 8.3).
  • Two kinds of uniqueness, two kinds of solutions. Pathwise vs in law, strong vs weak (Def. 8.4, Def. 8.11, Def. 8.14). Tanaka's equation has uniqueness in law but no pathwise uniqueness and no strong solution (Example 8.12, Lab 8.4). Yamada–Watanabe: weak existence plus pathwise uniqueness gives a strong solution (Thm. 8.17, corrected). Girsanov gives weak existence and uniqueness for bounded drifts (Example 8.13).
  • Markov and semigroup. Solutions restart as solutions: they are Markov (Thm. 8.19), with semigroup \(P_tP_s=P_{t+s}\) (Cor. 8.23). Their generator is \(\mathcal L\), and \(u=P_tg\) solves the Kolmogorov backward equation (Beyond the notes; Lab 8.5).

Reference implementation (Python / NumPy)

"""Lesson 8 — reference checks: Euler-Maruyama against the sdeint library, OU exact law, Picard iteration,
Tanaka's SDE, non-Lipschitz selection and explosion, the Markov semigroup and the Kolmogorov equation."""
import numpy as np
import sdeint
from scipy import stats, integrate

rng = np.random.default_rng(8)

def euler_maruyama(b, sigma, x0, T, dW):
    """X_{k+1} = X_k + b(t_k, X_k) h + sigma(t_k, X_k) dW_k  (left-point / Ito convention), dW: (n, m) increments."""
    n = dW.shape[0]; h = T / n; x = np.array(x0, float); out = [x.copy()]
    for k in range(n):
        x = x + b(k * h, x) * h + sigma(k * h, x) @ dW[k]; out.append(x.copy())
    return np.array(out)

print("1) Our Euler-Maruyama vs sdeint.itoEuler on identical Brownian increments (2-d linear SDE of Example 8.1)")
A = np.array([[-1.0, 0.5], [-0.5, -1.0]]); C = np.array([[1.0, 0.0], [0.3, 0.8]])
T, n = 5.0, 5000
dW = rng.standard_normal((n, 2)) * np.sqrt(T / n)
ours = euler_maruyama(lambda t, x: A @ x, lambda t, x: C, [1.0, -1.0], T, dW)
lib = sdeint.itoEuler(lambda x, t: A @ x, lambda x, t: C, np.array([1.0, -1.0]), np.linspace(0, T, n + 1), dW=dW)
print(f"   max |ours - sdeint| over the path: {np.max(np.abs(ours - lib)):.2e}")

print("4) Tanaka's SDE dX = sign(X) dB: beta = int sign(X) dX is a Brownian motion; the same beta drives -X")

print("5) Beyond Lipschitz: Peano selection and explosion (2000 steps on [0,1], 5000 paths)")

Cheat sheet

SDE

\(X_t=X_0+\int b(s,X_s)\dd s+\int\sigma(s,X_s)\dd B_s\), continuous and adapted. Componentwise: \(\sum_j\int\sigma^{ij}\dd B^j\).

Linear SDE / OU

\(X_t=e^{At}X_0+\int e^{A(t-s)}C\dd B_s\), Gaussian. 1-D: stationary \(N(0,c^2/2a)\). Euler: \(c^2/(2a-a^2h)\), stable if \(h\lt2/a\).

Assumption (L) ⇒ Thm. 8.9

Lipschitz plus bounded at 0 ⇒ a unique strong solution in \(L^2_T\) (needs \(X_0\in L^2\)). Picard: \(\|Y^{n+1}-Y^n\|^2\le\frac{(KT)^n}{n!}\cdots\).

Uniqueness notions

Pathwise: indistinguishable on the same space. Weak: same law on \(W\). Strong solution: \(\F^{X_0,B}\)-adapted.

Yamada–Watanabe

Weak existence + pathwise uniqueness ⇒ weak uniqueness + strong existence on any space.

Examples

Tanaka \(\dd X=\sgn(X)\dd B\): weak yes, pathwise no, not strong. Bounded drift + \(\dd B\): all yes (Girsanov, Veretennikov). \(x'=2\sgn(x)\sqrt{|x|}\): non-unique. \(x'=x^2\): explodes.

Markov

\(\E[g(X_t)\mid\F_s]=P_{s,t}g(X_s)\). Homogeneous: \(P_tg(x)=\E g(X^x_t)\), \(P_tP_s=P_{t+s}\).

Kolmogorov (beyond the notes)

\(\partial_tu=\mathcal Lu\) for \(u=P_tg\). Fokker–Planck \(\partial_t\rho=\mathcal L^*\rho\). Langevin: \(\mathcal L^*e^{-U}=0\).

Self-test

1. Write the linear SDE's solution and its stationary law in 1-D.

\(X_t=e^{-at}x+c\int_0^te^{-a(t-s)}\dd B_s\); stationary law \(N(0,c^2/2a)\).

2. What is the stationary variance of the Euler scheme for OU, and when does it exist?

\(c^2/(2a-a^2h)\), for \(h\lt2/a\) (Lab 8.1: 2.0309 at \(h=1\), against 2).

3. State assumption (L), and say why the notes' “Proposition 8.7” is misnamed.

Lipschitz in \(x\), uniformly in \(t\), and bounded at \(x=0\). It is an assumption, not a result.

4. Which hypothesis on \(X_0\) does Theorem 8.9 need but not state?

\(\E\|X_0\|^2\lt\infty\) (and \(X_0\) independent of \(B\)).

5. Which two tools replace the ODE estimates in Picard's proof?

Cauchy–Schwarz for \(\int\dd s\), and the Itô isometry for \(\int\dd B\).

6. Why does the Picard sequence converge faster than geometrically?

The iterated integrals give \((Kt)^n/n!\) (Lab 8.2: 2.13 × 10⁻⁶ after 15 iterations).

7. What does the discretized Picard iteration converge to?

The Euler–Maruyama path on the same grid (distance 4.77 × 10⁻¹⁰ in Lab 8.2).

8. Define pathwise uniqueness and weak uniqueness.

Pathwise: two solutions on the same space with the same BM are indistinguishable. Weak: all solutions have the same law on path space.

9. Define a strong solution.

A solution adapted to the augmented filtration of \((X_0,B)\).

10. Why does Tanaka's SDE have weak uniqueness?

Every solution is a continuous local martingale with bracket \(t\), hence a BM (Lévy).

11. Why does it lack pathwise uniqueness?

\(-X\) solves it with the same \(\beta\), because \(\int\ind_{X=0}\,\dd\beta=0\).

12. Why is the Tanaka solution not strong?

\(\beta\) is a function of \(|X|\), so \(\F^\beta\subset\F^{|X|}\), which does not contain the sign of \(X\).

13. What goes wrong with \(\sgn(0)=0\)?

\(X\equiv0\) becomes a solution, so weak uniqueness fails too.

14. What does Yamada–Watanabe need that the notes omit?

Weak existence, in part (2).

15. Why do bounded drifts give weak existence and uniqueness?

Girsanov with Novikov (bounded \(\br{L}\)) builds the solution from a BM and gives an explicit formula for its law.

16. Give an SDE whose solutions explode.

\(\dd X=X^2\dd t+\varepsilon\dd B\) from \(x_0\gt0\): the ODE blows up at \(1/x_0\) (Lab 8.3).

17. Why is the solution of a Lipschitz SDE Markov?

Restarted at \(s\), it solves the same equation driven by fresh noise independent of \(\F_s\), and uniqueness makes the future a function of \((X_s,\tilde B)\).

18. Prove \(P_tP_s=P_{t+s}\).

Condition on \(\F_s\) and use Thm. 8.20: \(P_{t+s}g(x)=\E[P_tg(X^x_s)]\).

19. What equation does \(u(t,x)=P_tg(x)\) solve?

\(\partial_tu=\mathcal Lu\), with \(\mathcal L=b\cdot\nabla+\frac12\tr(\sigma\sigma^\top\nabla^2)\) (Kolmogorov backward; beyond the notes).

20. Why is \(\pi\propto e^{-U}\) stationary for the Langevin diffusion?

\(\mathcal L^*\pi=\nabla\cdot(\pi\nabla U+\nabla\pi)=0\), since \(\nabla\pi=-\pi\nabla U\).

21. How do diffusion models use this lesson?

They run the time reversal of a noising SDE, whose drift involves \(\nabla\log\rho_t\). Its law is controlled by the Markov semigroup and the Fokker–Planck equation, and by Girsanov for training errors.
