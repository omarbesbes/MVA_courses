Function Properties of the Brownian Motion: Markov and Quadratic Variation

What does the past tell us about the future of a Brownian path, and how rough is that path?

A question that comes back

“Given what we know now, what is the law of the future, and can ‘now’ be a random time?” Lesson 1 asked this in its simplest form: increments over disjoint intervals are independent (Def. 1.7 (iii)). This lesson turns the question into the Markov property at a fixed time (Thm. 2.3), into information “just after” \(s\) (Prop. 2.4), and into the strong Markov property at random times (Thm. 2.15). Lesson 4 asks it for martingales at random times: optional stopping. Lesson 8 asks it for solutions of SDEs (Thm. 8.19).

Opening result and setting

notes, p. 13

Theorem 2.1notes p. 13

If \((X_t)_{t\ge0}\) and \((Y_t)_{t\ge0}\) are almost surely continuous processes in \(\R^d\) with the same distribution (same fidis), their distributions on \(W^d\) are equal.

Why it is true. By the key lemma of Thm. 1.21, the Borel σ-field of \(W^d\) is generated by the coordinates, so the cylinders form a generating π-system. On cylinders the two laws are given by the fidis, which agree. The π–λ theorem finishes. (If the processes are only a.s. continuous, replace them by indistinguishable continuous versions; this does not change their laws.) This is the formal version of “Brownian motion has a unique law”: the Wiener measure \(\mu_W\) does not depend on the construction.

Setting of the chapter. \(B\) is a 1D standard Brownian motion and \((\F_t)\) its completed natural filtration,

\[ \F_t=\sigma(B_s:s\le t)\vee\cN, \]

where \(\cN\) is the class of negligible sets. Completing means adding all null sets to \(\F_0\), so that a.s. equalities never break measurability. Definition 3.5 makes this systematic.

§2.1 Markov property

notes, pp. 13–18

The problem. For prediction, filtering or sampling, we want the conditional law of the future given the past. In general this depends on the whole past path. A Markov process is one where it depends only on the present value. Brownian motion is the basic example, because its future increments are independent of the past.

Definition 2.2 (Markov property)notes p. 13

Let \((\G_t)\) be a filtration, i.e. \(\G_s\subset\G_t\) for \(s\le t\). An \(\R^d\)-valued process \((X_t)\) has the Markov property with respect to \((\G_t)\) if, for all \(s\le t\), the conditional law of \(X_t\) given \(\G_s\) equals the conditional law of \(X_t\) given \(X_s\). In other words, for every bounded measurable \(f:\R^d\to\R\), \[ \E[f(X_t)\mid\G_s]=\E[f(X_t)\mid X_s]. \]

Theorem 2.3 (simple Markov property)notes p. 13

For every \(s\ge0\), the process \((B_{t+s}-B_s)_{t\ge0}\) is a Brownian motion independent of \(\F_s\).

In one sentenceRestart the clock at any fixed time: what follows is a fresh Brownian motion that knows nothing of the past.
Proof ideaThat \(W_t=B_{t+s}-B_s\) is a Brownian motion is a direct check of Def. 1.7. For independence, by Thm. 1.32 it suffices to show that \((B_{s_1},\dots,B_{s_p})\) and \((W_{t_1},\dots,W_{t_m})\) are independent, for \(s_i\le s\). Both are functions of the increment vector \((B_{s_1},B_{s_2}-B_{s_1},\dots,B_s-B_{s_p},B_{s+t_1}-B_s,\dots)\), whose coordinates are independent by (iii). Null sets do not affect independence, so the completion is harmless.
Used againEverywhere: the martingale property of \(B\), \(B^2-t\) and \(e^{uB-u^2t/2}\) (Example 4.3), the semigroup property (below), and the proof of the strong Markov property (Thm. 2.15).

A subtler question: information “just after” \(s\). Define \(\F_{s+}=\bigcap_{u\gt s}\F_u\). This is what is known at every time strictly after \(s\), however close. It can contain events such as “\(B\) goes above \(B_s\) immediately after \(s\)”, which are not decided by time \(s\). Does knowing \(\F_{s+}\) instead of \(\F_s\) help predict the future?

Proposition 2.4notes pp. 13–14

For every \(s\ge0\), \((B_t-B_s)_{t\ge s}\) is independent of \(\F_{s+}=\bigcap_{u\gt s}\F_u\). Therefore \(B\) satisfies the Markov property with respect to \((\F_{t+})_{t\ge0}\).

Why it is true. We must show, for \(A\in\F_{s+}\), \(s\le t_1\lt\dots\lt t_m\) and bounded continuous \(\varphi_i\), the identity (1):

\[ \begin{aligned}&\E\Big[\ind_A\prod_{i=1}^m\varphi_i(B_{t_i}-B_s)\Big]\\ &\qquad=\P(A)\,\E\Big[\prod_{i=1}^m\varphi_i(B_{t_i}-B_s)\Big].\end{aligned} \]

Take \(t_1=s\) (so \(\varphi_1(B_{t_1}-B_s)=\varphi_1(0)\)) and pick \(u\in(s,t_2)\). Then \(A\in\F_u\), and by Theorem 2.3 applied at time \(u\), the increments \((B_t-B_u)_{t\ge u}\) are independent of \(\F_u\). Write \(B_{t_i}-B_s=(B_{t_i}-B_u)+(B_u-B_s)\) and condition on \(\F_u\):

\[ \E\Big[\ind_A\prod_{i}\varphi_i(B_{t_i}-B_s)\Big]=\varphi_1(0)\,\E\big[\ind_A\,\psi(B_u-B_s)\big], \] \[ \text{where}\quad \psi(b)=\E\Big[\prod_{i\ge2}\varphi_i(B_{t_i}-B_u+b)\Big]. \]

Now let \(u\downarrow s\). Paths are continuous, so \(B_u-B_s\to0\), and \(\psi\) is continuous (dominated convergence). Also \(\psi\) at \(u\) converges to the same expression with \(B_{t_i}-B_s\) (again by continuity), and everything is bounded. Dominated convergence gives \(\varphi_1(0)\,\E[\ind_A]\,\psi_s(0)\), which is the right side of (1). The class of bounded continuous test functions is enough to identify laws.

Key idea

Continuity of the paths lets us push the conditioning time \(u\) down to \(s\). Right-continuity of the filtration comes “for free” for Brownian motion because its paths are continuous and its increments independent. For general processes this fails, and Lesson 3 imposes it as the “usual conditions” (§3.1).

Watch out: garbled lines in the proof (notes p. 13)

The notes write “since \((B_t-B_s)_{t\ge s}\) is independent of \(\F_u\) and \(B_u-B_s\) is \(\F_u\)-measurable”. It is \((B_t-B_u)_{t\ge u}\) that is independent of \(\F_u\): the increments of \(B\) over \([s,u]\) are \(\F_u\)-measurable. The next display contains a stray symbol: “\(\E[\ind_A\psi(B_u-B_s)\rho_1]=\E[\ind_A\psi(B_u-B_s)]\rho(0)\)”, where \(\rho_1\) and \(\rho(0)\) should both be \(\varphi_1(0)\). Finally, “\(\psi\) is continuous… by equal integrability” should read “by dominated convergence”.

Corollary 2.5notes p. 14

\(B\) is Markov with respect to \((\F_{t+})\). For bounded measurable \(\varphi\) and \(s\le t\): \[ \E[\varphi(B_t)\mid\F_{s+}]=P_{t-s}\varphi(B_s),\qquad P_u\varphi(x)=\E[\varphi(x+B_u)]. \]

Why. \(B_t=(B_t-B_s)+B_s\), with the first term independent of \(\F_{s+}\) and the second \(\F_{s+}\)-measurable. The “freezing lemma” (condition on the measurable part, integrate out the independent part) gives \(\psi(B_s)\) with \(\psi(b)=\E[\varphi(b+B_t-B_s)]=P_{t-s}\varphi(b)\). Since \(\sigma(B_s)\subset\F_{s+}\), the tower property gives the same value for \(\E[\varphi(B_t)\mid B_s]\).

Definition 2.6 (Markov kernel)notes p. 14

\(Q:\Xb\times\cX\to[0,1]\) is a Markov kernel if (a) for each \(x\), \(A\mapsto Q(x,A)\) is a probability, and (b) for each \(A\), \(x\mapsto Q(x,A)\) is measurable.

Definition 2.7 (heat semigroup)notes pp. 14–15

The Markov semigroup of \(B\) is the family of kernels \(\tilde P_t(x,A)=\P(x+B_t\in A)\), also written \(P_t\). As operators on bounded measurable functions, \(P_t\varphi(x)=\int\varphi(y)\,p_t(x,y)\,\dd y\) with the Gaussian density \(p_t(x,y)=(2\pi t)^{-1/2}e^{-(y-x)^2/(2t)}\). They satisfy the semigroup property \(P_{t+s}=P_tP_s\).

Proof of the semigroup property (notes p. 15). Condition on \(\F_s\) and use Theorem 2.3:

\[ \begin{aligned}P_{t+s}\varphi(x)&=\E[\varphi(x+B_{t+s}-B_s+B_s)]\\ &=\E\big[\E[\varphi(x+B_{t+s}-B_s+B_s)\mid\F_s]\big]\\ &=\E[P_t\varphi(x+B_s)]=P_s(P_t\varphi)(x).\end{aligned} \]

In terms of densities this is the Chapman–Kolmogorov equation \(p_{t+s}(x,z)=\int p_s(x,y)p_t(y,z)\,\dd y\): a sum of independent Gaussians with variances \(s\) and \(t\) is Gaussian with variance \(s+t\).

Proposition 2.8 (Feller properties, generator, heat equation)notes p. 15

Let \(C_0(\R)\) be the continuous functions vanishing at \(\pm\infty\), and \(C^2_c(\R)\) the \(C^2\) functions with compact support.

  1. If \(\varphi\) is continuous and bounded, so is \(P_t\varphi\).
  2. If \(\varphi\in C_0(\R)\), then \(P_t\varphi\in C_0(\R)\) and \(\lim_{t\to0^+}\|P_t\varphi-\varphi\|_\infty=0\).
  3. If \(\varphi\in C^2_c(\R)\), then \(\dfrac{P_t\varphi(x)-\varphi(x)}{t}\to\dfrac12\varphi''(x)\) as \(t\downarrow0\), for every \(x\).
  4. If \(\varphi\in C^2_c(\R)\), then \(u_t(x)=P_t\varphi(x)\) solves the heat equation \(\partial_tu_t(x)=\frac12\partial_x^2u_t(x)\) on \((0,\infty)\times\R\).

Proofs. (i) is dominated convergence. For (ii), write \(P_t\varphi(x)-\varphi(x)=\int\psi(z)\big(\varphi(x+\sqrt t\,z)-\varphi(x)\big)\dd z\), with \(\psi\) the \(N(0,1)\) density. Choose \(k_\varepsilon\) so the Gaussian tail beyond \(\pm k_\varepsilon\) has mass \(\le\varepsilon/(4\|\varphi\|_\infty)\). Split into the tail (at most \(\varepsilon/2\)) and the compact part, where uniform continuity of \(\varphi\) makes the difference small once \(\sqrt t\,k_\varepsilon\) is small.

Watch out

In the proof of (ii) the notes write “Showing that \(\lim_{x\to\infty}\varphi(x)=0\) is easy and left to the reader”. That is the hypothesis. What must be shown is \(\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}P_t\varphi(x)=0\), i.e. \(P_t\varphi\in C_0\) (Exercise E5). The notes do not prove (iii) and (iv); here are the proofs.

(iii), beyond the notes' text but standard. Taylor's formula with integral remainder: \(\varphi(x+h)=\varphi(x)+h\varphi'(x)+h^2\int_0^1(1-u)\varphi''(x+uh)\,\dd u\). Put \(h=\sqrt t\,Z\) and take expectations: \(\E Z=0\), \(\E Z^2=1\), and \(\varphi''\) is bounded and continuous. So

\[ \begin{aligned}\frac{P_t\varphi(x)-\varphi(x)}{t}&=\E\Big[Z^2\int_0^1(1-u)\varphi''(x+u\sqrt tZ)\,\dd u\Big]\\ &\xrightarrow[t\to0]{}\frac12\varphi''(x).\end{aligned} \]

The operator \(\tfrac12\frac{\dd^2}{\dd x^2}\) is the generator of Brownian motion. (iv) The Gaussian density satisfies \(\partial_tp_t=\frac12\partial_x^2p_t\) (direct differentiation), and for \(t\gt0\) we may differentiate under the integral. Alternatively, (iii) and the semigroup property give \(\frac{u_{t+h}-u_t}{h}=P_t\frac{P_h\varphi-\varphi}{h}\to P_t(\frac12\varphi'')=\frac12\partial_x^2u_t\).

Link

This is the first link between a stochastic process and a PDE: averages of \(\varphi\) along Brownian paths solve the heat equation. Itô's formula (Thm. 7.1, Remark 7.6) generalizes the generator to \(b\cdot\nabla+\frac12\tr(\sigma\sigma^\top\nabla^2)\) for SDEs. Lesson 8 defines their semigroups (Def. 8.21). The Langevin diffusion of the board's Chapter 0 (Lesson 1) has generator \(\Delta-\nabla U\cdot\nabla\).

How fast, and which hypotheses matter? For three test functions we compute \(P_t\varphi\) exactly: truncated Gaussian moments for the bump, closed forms for the indicator and the cosine. We then check the semigroup identity with an independent 96-point Gauss–Hermite quadrature, and the generator at a point.

  • Strong continuity needs continuity. Bump: \(\|P_t\varphi-\varphi\|_\infty=\) 0.029 at \(t=10^{-2}\) and 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ at \(10^{-4}\). Indicator of \([-\frac12,\frac12]\): 0.5 at both times. At the jump, \(P_t\varphi=\frac12\) for every \(t\), so the sup-distance never goes to 0. The cosine is not in \(C_0\) but is uniformly continuous, and still converges (5.0 × 10⁻⁵ at \(10^{-4}\)). \(C_0\) is a convenient class, not a necessary one.
Corollary 2.9 (Blumenthal's 0–1 law)notes p. 16

For every \(A\in\F_{0+}\), \(\P(A)\in\{0,1\}\).

Why. By Prop. 2.4 with \(s=0\), \((B_t-B_0)_{t\ge0}=(B_t)_{t\ge0}\) is independent of \(\F_{0+}\). So \(\F_\infty=\sigma(B_t:t\ge0)\vee\cN\) is independent of \(\F_{0+}\). But \(\F_{0+}\subset\F_\infty\), so every \(A\in\F_{0+}\) is independent of itself: \(\P(A)=\P(A)^2\).

Why it is surprising, and why it is useful. \(\F_{0+}\) is the “germ” σ-field: events decided by the path on \([0,\varepsilon]\) for every \(\varepsilon\gt0\). The law says Brownian motion has no random initial behaviour: any such event is certain or impossible. For example, \(A=\{\forall\varepsilon\gt0,\ \exists t\le\varepsilon:\ B_t\gt0\}\) is in \(\F_{0+}\), and \(\P(A)\ge\limsup_{\varepsilon\to0}\P(B_\varepsilon\gt0)=1/2\), so \(\P(A)=1\). By symmetry \(B\) also takes negative values in every \([0,\varepsilon]\). So Brownian motion crosses 0 infinitely often immediately after time 0, and by the strong Markov property the same happens after hitting any level. This fact justifies Exercise 3.7.

Exercise 2.10notes p. 16

Show that almost surely \(\liminf_{t o\infty}B_t/\sqrt t=-\infty\). Deduce \(\limsup_{t o\infty}B_t/\sqrt t=+\infty\), \(\limsup_{t o0}B_t/\sqrt t=+\infty\) and \(\liminf_{t o0}B_t/\sqrt t=-\infty\). Hints and a full solution: Exercise E1.

Lab 2.2 · Exercise 2.10

How long until B_t/√t exceeds a level l? (almost surely, eventually)

Question: Exercise 2.10 says \(\limsup_{t\to\infty}B_t/\sqrt t=+\infty\) almost surely. So for every level \(l\), \(B_t/\sqrt t\) exceeds \(l\) at arbitrarily large times. §2.1 (continued) Stopping times and the strong Markov property

notes, pp. 16–18

The problem. Many natural times are random: the first time a price hits a barrier, the first time a sampler enters a region. We want to restart the process at such a time. That only makes sense for random times that do not look into the future.

Definition 2.11 (stopping time)notes p. 16

A random variable \(\tau\) with values in \(\R_+\cup\{+\infty\}\) is a stopping time with respect to \((\F_t)\) if \(\{\tau\le t\}\in\F_t\) for every \(t\).

Read it as: at every time \(t\), you can tell from the information available whether the event has already happened.

Example 2.12 (hitting time)notes p. 16

For \(a\in\R\), \(\tau_a=\inf\{t\ge0:B_t=a\}\) (with \(\inf\varnothing=+\infty\)) is a stopping time, since \[ \{\tau_a\le t\}=\Big\{\inf_{r\in[0,t]\cap\Qb}|B_r-a|=0\Big\}\in\F_t. \]

Watch out: an error in Example 2.12 (notes p. 16)

The notes write \(\sup_{r\in[0,t]\cap\Qb}|B_r-a|=0\). That event says \(B_r=a\) for every rational \(r\le t\), which is impossible for \(a\ne0\) since \(B_0=0\). The correct event uses the infimum. By continuity, \(B\) reaches \(a\) on the compact \([0,t]\) if and only if the distance \(|B_r-a|\) gets arbitrarily small on the dense set \([0,t]\cap\Qb\). A countable infimum of \(\F_t\)-measurable variables is \(\F_t\)-measurable.

The notes add that \(S_t=\sup_{r\le t}|B_r|\) is adapted, for the same reason (supremum over rationals by continuity). Two cautions.

  • A few lines later, Theorem 2.17 redefines \(S_t=\sup_{r\le t}B_r\), without absolute value. From here on this page uses \(S_t=\sup_{r\le t}B_r\).
  • Not every random time is a stopping time. The last zero before time 1, \(L=\sup\{t\le1:B_t=0\}\), is not: \(\{L\le t\}\) requires knowing that \(B\) never returns to 0 on \((t,1]\), which is future information (see Exercise E8).
Definition 2.13 (σ-field at a stopping time)notes p. 17

\(\F_\tau=\{A\in\F_\infty:\ A\cap\{\tau\le t\}\in\F_t\ \ \forall t\}\), where \(\F_\infty=\sigma(\F_t:t\ge0)\). It is a σ-field: the information available at time \(\tau\).

Watch out

The notes write \(\F_\tau=\{A\cap\{\tau\le t\}\in\F_t,\ \forall t\}\) and define \(\F_\infty\) without using it. The condition “\(A\in\F_\infty\)” is part of the definition. Theorem 2.15 uses the analogous \(\F_{\tau+}\) with \(\F_{t+}\).

Remark 2.14. \(\{\tau\le t\}\in\F_\tau\) for all \(t\), and \(\{\tau\lt\infty\}\in\F_\tau\). If \(Z\) is bounded and \(\F_\tau\)-measurable, then \(Z\ind_{\{\tau\le N\}}\) is \(\F_N\)-measurable. Lesson 3 collects many such facts (Def. 3.16, Prop. 3.17).

Theorem 2.15 (strong Markov property)notes p. 17

Let \(\tau\) be an \((\F_t)\)-stopping time. On \(\{\tau\lt\infty\}\), the process \((B_{t+\tau}-B_\tau)_{t\ge0}\) is a Brownian motion independent of \(\F_{\tau+}=\{A\in\F_\infty: A\cap\{\tau\le t\}\in\F_{t+}\ \forall t\}\). Precisely, for \(A\in\F_{\tau+}\), \(0\le t_1\lt\dots\lt t_m\) and bounded continuous \(\varphi_i\), identity (2) holds: \[ \begin{aligned}&\E\Big[\ind_A\ind_{\{\tau\lt\infty\}}\prod_{i=1}^m\varphi_i(B_{t_i+\tau}-B_\tau)\Big]\\ &\qquad=\P(A\cap\{\tau\lt\infty\})\,\E\Big[\prod_{i=1}^m\varphi_i(B_{t_i})\Big].\end{aligned} \]

In one sentenceRestarting at a stopping time also gives a fresh, independent Brownian motion.
Proof ideaDiscretize the stopping time from above: \(\tau_N=(\lfloor2^N\tau\rfloor+1)/2^N\). It takes values in the dyadic grid, is a stopping time, and \(\tau_N\downarrow\tau\). On \(\{\tau_N=(k+1)/2^N\}\) the process restarts at a fixed time, where Theorem 2.3 applies. Sum over \(k\), then let \(N\to\infty\) using continuity of paths and dominated convergence. (Thread T2: discretize dyadically, then pass to the limit.)
CounterexampleFor the last zero \(L\) before time 1 (not a stopping time), \(B_{L+t}-B_L\) does not return to 0 before \(1-L\). A Brownian motion started at 0 returns to 0 immediately (Blumenthal, Cor. 2.9). So the restarted process is not a Brownian motion.
Used againHitting-time process (Example 2.16), reflection principle (Thm. 2.17), Exercise 3.7. Lesson 8 proves the SDE Markov property in the same restart-and-freeze way (Thm. 8.19).

Example 2.16 (the hitting-time process)notes p. 18

For \(a\ge0\) let \(\tau_a=\inf\{t\ge0:B_t=a\}\). By the strong Markov property, \((\tau_a)_{a\ge0}\) has independent increments: for \(0\le a_1\lt\dots\lt a_m\), \((\tau_{a_1},\tau_{a_2}-\tau_{a_1},\dots,\tau_{a_m}-\tau_{a_{m-1}})\) has independent components. Moreover \(\tau_b-\tau_a\eqd\tau_{b-a}\) for \(a\le b\), since on \(\{\tau_a\lt\infty\}\) \[ \tau_b=\tau_a+\inf\{s\ge0:B_{s+\tau_a}-B_{\tau_a}=b-a\}. \] For every \(c\gt0\), \((\tau_{ca})_{a\ge0}\eqd(c^2\tau_a)_{a\ge0}\). Together with monotonicity, this makes \((\tau_a)_{a\ge0}\) a stable subordinator of index \(1/2\).

Why the scaling. \(\tilde B_t=c^{-1}B_{c^2t}\) is a Brownian motion (Prop. 1.16 (c) with \(a=c^2\)), and \(\inf\{t:B_t=ca\}=c^2\inf\{s:\tilde B_s=a\}\). Space scales like the square root of time, so hitting a level \(c\) times farther takes \(c^2\) times longer. The law of \(\tau_a\) follows from the reflection principle below: \(\P(\tau_a\le t)=\P(S_t\ge a)=2\P(B_t\ge a)=\P(a^2/B_1^2\le t)\). So

\[ \tau_a\eqd\frac{a^2}{G^2}\quad(G\sim N(0,1)),\qquad f_{\tau_a}(t)=\frac{a}{\sqrt{2\pi t^3}}\,e^{-a^2/(2t)}. \]

This is the Lévy distribution. Its tail is \(\P(\tau_a\gt t)\sim a\sqrt{2/(\pi t)}\), so \(\E[\tau_a]=\infty\): Brownian motion reaches every level (\(\tau_a\lt\infty\) a.s.), but the expected waiting time is infinite. “Subordinator” means an increasing process with independent stationary increments. “Stable of index 1/2” refers to the scaling \(c^2=c^{1/(1/2)}\) and to the Laplace transform \(\E[e^{-\lambda\tau_a}]=e^{-a\sqrt{2\lambda}}\), derived in Example 4.33.

Theorem 2.17 (reflection principle)notes p. 18

Let \(S_t=\sup_{r\in[0,t]}B_r\). For every \(a\ge0\), \(b\le a\) and \(t\gt0\): \[ \P(S_t\ge a,\ B_t\le b)=\P(B_t\ge 2a-b). \] Consequently \(S_t\eqd|B_t|\).

Watch out

The notes state it “for every \(b\le a\)” without \(a\ge0\). For \(a\lt0\) the identity fails: \(S_t\ge0\gt a\) always, so the left side is \(\P(B_t\le b)\ne\P(B_t\ge2a-b)\). The proof is left to the TD sheet (“This is easy as an exercise”); it is Exercise E2 below.

Proof sketch. Let \(\tau=\tau_a\) and \(\tilde B_s=B_{\tau+s}-a\) on \(\{\tau\lt\infty\}\). This is a Brownian motion independent of \(\F_{\tau+}\ni\{\tau\le t\}\) (Thm. 2.15), so \(\tilde B\eqd-\tilde B\) jointly with \(\tau\). Hence \(\P(\tau\le t,\ \tilde B_{t-\tau}\le b-a)=\P(\tau\le t,\ \tilde B_{t-\tau}\ge a-b)\), i.e. \(\P(S_t\ge a,B_t\le b)=\P(\tau\le t,B_t\ge2a-b)\). Since \(2a-b\ge a\), the event \(\{B_t\ge2a-b\}\) already implies \(\tau\le t\). For the consequence, take \(b=a\):

\[ \begin{aligned}\P(S_t\ge a)&=\P(S_t\ge a,B_t\le a)+\P(B_t\gt a)\\ &=2\P(B_t\ge a)=\P(|B_t|\ge a).\end{aligned} \]

Remark 2.18. “The result does not imply that \((S_t)_{t\ge0}\) as a process has the same distribution as \((|B_t|)_{t\ge0}\). This is very wrong (why?). But the two processes have the same time marginals.” Why: \(S\) is nondecreasing, while \(|B|\) returns to 0 infinitely often. For instance \(\P(S_1\lt S_{1/2})=0\) but \(\P(|B_1|\lt|B_{1/2}|)\gt0\). §2.2 Regularity of the paths

notes, pp. 19–20

The problem. Lesson 1 showed Brownian paths are continuous but rough (Hölder only below 1/2). How rough, exactly? Ordinary calculus works with functions of finite variation, for which \(\int f\,\dd g\) is a Stieltjes integral. Is a Brownian path one of those?

Proposition 2.21 (quadratic variation)notes p. 19

Fix \(t\gt0\) and meshes \(0=t^n_0\lt\dots\lt t^n_{k_n}=t\) with \(\max_k(t^n_k-t^n_{k-1})\to0\). Then \[ \sum_{k=1}^{k_n}\big(B_{t^n_k}-B_{t^n_{k-1}}\big)^2\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{L^2}t. \] For dyadic meshes \(t^n_k=tk2^{-n}\), the convergence also holds almost surely.

In one sentenceSquared increments add up to elapsed time, deterministically in the limit.
ProofPut \(Y^n_k=(\Delta_kB)^2-\Delta_kt\), centered and independent over \(k\). \(\Var((\Delta B)^2)=\E[(\Delta B)^4]-(\Delta t)^2=3(\Delta t)^2-(\Delta t)^2=2(\Delta t)^2\), so \(\Var(\sum_kY^n_k)=2\sum_k(\Delta_kt)^2\le2t\max_k\Delta_kt\to0\). The notes bound the variance by \(3\sum(\Delta t)^2\), which is also valid. For dyadic meshes (take \(t=1\)), Chebyshev gives \(\P(|Y^n|\ge1/n)\le2n^22^{-n}\), which is summable, and Borel–Cantelli gives a.s. convergence.
Why it mattersEach \((\Delta B)^2\) is random, of size \(\Delta t\), but their fluctuations cancel: a law of large numbers across the mesh. This is the source of the \(\frac12f''\) term in Itô's formula, \(\dd(B_t^2)=2B_t\,\dd B_t+\dd t\) (Thm. 7.1), and of the bracket \(\br{M}\) (Thm. 5.23).
Beyond the notes: quadratic variation is a limit, not a supremum

The limit is along a fixed sequence of meshes. If the partition may depend on the path, one can make the sums blow up: \(\sup_{\text{partitions}}\sum|\Delta B|^2=+\infty\) almost surely (Lévy). So the “2-variation” of a Brownian path is infinite, even though its quadratic variation along refining dyadic meshes is \(t\). For deterministic meshes that are not nested, a.s. convergence holds if the mesh size is \(o(1/\ln n)\) (Dudley, 1973).

Corollary 2.22 (infinite variation)notes pp. 19–20

Almost surely, Brownian motion has infinite total variation on every interval \([a,b]\): \(\|B\|_{\TV,[a,b]}=\sup\sum_k|B_{t_k}-B_{t_{k-1}}|=+\infty\).

Why. If \(\|B\|_{\TV,[a,b]}\lt\infty\), then along the dyadic meshes \(\sum(\Delta B)^2\le\max_k|\Delta_kB|\cdot\|B\|_{\TV,[a,b]}\to0\), because the maximal increment tends to 0 by uniform continuity. That contradicts Prop. 2.21. The same argument shows that any continuous function with nonzero quadratic variation has infinite variation, and conversely that continuous functions of finite variation have zero quadratic variation.

Key idea

Finite variation and nonzero quadratic variation exclude each other for continuous paths. Brownian motion is on the “nonzero quadratic variation” side, so \(\int H_s\,\dd B_s\) cannot be defined path by path as a Stieltjes integral. Lesson 5 separates processes into finite-variation parts and martingale parts (semimartingales). Lesson 6 builds the integral for the martingale part through an \(L^2\) isometry instead of path by path (Thm. 6.6).

Positive probability. By the reverse Fatou lemma, \(\P(\limsup_nA_n)=\lim_N\P(\bigcup_{n\ge N}A_n)\ge\limsup_n\P(A_n)\). Here \(\P(B_n\le-l\sqrt n)=\P(G\le-l)\) for every \(n\), so \(\P(A^l)\ge\P(G\le-l)\gt0\).

Conclusion. \(\P(\bigcap_{l\in\N}A^l)=1\), so \(\liminf_nB_n/\sqrt n=-\infty\), and a fortiori \(\liminf_{t\to\infty}B_t/\sqrt t=-\infty\). Applying this to \(-B\) gives \(\limsup=+\infty\). For \(t\to0\): \(W_{1/s}/\sqrt{1/s}=B_s/\sqrt s\), so \(\limsup_{t\to0}W_t/\sqrt t=\limsup_{s\to\infty}B_s/\sqrt s=+\infty\). \(W\) is a Brownian motion and this is a statement about its law, so it holds for \(B\) too. Symmetry gives the \(\liminf\).

Consequence. \(B\) is not differentiable at 0: \(B_t/t=(B_t/\sqrt t)/\sqrt t\) oscillates between \(\pm\infty\). By the Markov property the same holds at any fixed time.

E7 · p-variations along dyadic meshes extends Prop. 2.21

Let \(V_p^n=\sum_{k=1}^{2^n}|B_{k2^{-n}}-B_{(k-1)2^{-n}}|^p\). Compute \(\E V^n_p\) and show that \(V_p^n\to0\) a.s. for \(p\gt2\) and \(V^n_p\to\infty\) for \(p\lt2\).

Solution

\(\E V^n_p=2^n\,c_p\,2^{-np/2}=c_p\,2^{n(1-p/2)}\) with \(c_p=\E|G|^p\). Also \(\Var V^n_p=2^n(c_{2p}-c_p^2)2^{-np}\), so \(V^n_p/\E V^n_p\to1\) in \(L^2\), and a.s. by Chebyshev and Borel–Cantelli (the relative variance is \(O(2^{-n})\)). So \(V^n_p\to0\) for \(p\gt2\), \(\to1\) for \(p=2\) and \(\to\infty\) for \(p\lt2\). For \(p=1\): \(\E V^n_1=\sqrt{2/\pi}\,2^{n/2}\), as in Lab 2.5 (measured 144.4 against 144.4 at \(n=15\)). The exponent 2 is the threshold: Brownian motion is exactly “2-rough”.

E8 · The last zero is not a stopping time shows why Thm. 2.15 needs stopping times

Let \(L=\sup\{t\le1:B_t=0\}\). Show that \(L\) is not an \((\F_t)\)-stopping time.

Hint

Suppose it is, and apply the strong Markov property at \(L\) together with Blumenthal's law.

Solution

If \(L\) were a stopping time, then \(\tilde B_t=B_{L+t}-B_L=B_{L+t}\) would be a Brownian motion. By Blumenthal's law a Brownian motion started at 0 vanishes at times arbitrarily close to 0 (it changes sign infinitely often). So \(B_{L+t}=0\) for some \(t\in(0,1-L)\) on \(\{L\lt1\}\), an event of probability 1. That contradicts the definition of \(L\). (Beyond the notes: \(\P(L\le t)=\frac2\pi\arcsin\sqrt t\), Lévy's arcsine law.)

Conclusion

  • Markov at fixed times. After any fixed time \(s\), \(B_{s+\cdot}-B_s\) is a fresh Brownian motion independent of \(\F_s\) (Thm. 2.3), and even of \(\F_{s+}\) (Prop. 2.4). The conditional law of the future is described by the heat semigroup: \(\E[\varphi(B_t)\mid\F_s]=P_{t-s}\varphi(B_s)\).
  • Semigroup and PDE. \(P_{t+s}=P_tP_s\) (Chapman–Kolmogorov). The generator is \(\frac12\partial^2\), and \(u_t=P_t\varphi\) solves the heat equation (Prop. 2.8). This is the model for every Markov diffusion in the course.
  • 0–1 laws. \(\F_{0+}\) is trivial (Blumenthal): Brownian motion immediately oscillates around its starting point, and \(B_t/\sqrt t\) is unbounded both as \(t\to0\) and as \(t\to\infty\) (Exercise 2.10).
  • Strong Markov and its fruits. Restarting at stopping times gives the independence and scaling of hitting times (a stable subordinator of index 1/2, with infinite mean) and the reflection principle \(S_t\eqd|B_t|\). These equalities concern one-time marginals, not processes (Rem. 2.18).
  • Roughness. Quadratic variation \(t\), total variation \(\infty\) (Prop. 2.21, Cor. 2.22). Stochastic integrals cannot be pathwise Stieltjes integrals, and Itô calculus will carry an extra \(\frac12f''\,\dd t\).

    """Exact maximum of a Brownian bridge from x to y over a time step h (reflection principle)."""
    u = rng.random(np.shape(x))

Cheat sheet

Markov property

\(B_{s+\cdot}-B_s\) is a BM independent of \(\F_{s+}\). \(\E[\varphi(B_t)\mid\F_s]=P_{t-s}\varphi(B_s)\).

Heat semigroup

\(P_t\varphi(x)=\E\varphi(x+B_t)\). \(P_{t+s}=P_tP_s\). Generator \(\frac12\varphi''\). \(u_t=P_t\varphi\) solves \(\partial_tu=\frac12\partial^2_xu\).

Blumenthal

\(\F_{0+}\) is trivial. BM crosses 0 infinitely often near 0. \(\limsup B_t/\sqrt t=+\infty\) at \(0\) and at \(\infty\).

Stopping time

\(\{\tau\le t\}\in\F_t\). Hitting time of \(a\): use \(\inf_{r\in\Qb\cap[0,t]}|B_r-a|=0\). The last zero is not a stopping time.

Strong Markov

\(B_{\tau+\cdot}-B_\tau\) is a BM independent of \(\F_{\tau+}\). Proof: \(\tau_N=(\lfloor2^N\tau\rfloor+1)2^{-N}\downarrow\tau\).

Hitting times

\(\tau_a\eqd a^2/G^2\), density \(\frac{a}{\sqrt{2\pi t^3}}e^{-a^2/2t}\), \(\E\tau_a=\infty\), \(\tau_{ca}\eqd c^2\tau_a\), independent increments.

Reflection

\(\P(S_t\ge a,B_t\le b)=\P(B_t\ge2a-b)\) for \(a\ge0\), \(b\le a\). \(S_t\eqd|B_t|\) (marginals only).

Variation

\(\sum(\Delta B)^2\to t\) (\(L^2\); a.s. dyadic), \(\Var=2\sum(\Delta t)^2\). \(\TV=\infty\). \(p\)-variation along dyadics: \(0\) if \(p\gt2\), \(\infty\) if \(p\lt2\).

Self-test

1. State the simple Markov property of Brownian motion.

For every \(s\), \((B_{t+s}-B_s)_{t\ge0}\) is a Brownian motion independent of \(\F_s\) (even of \(\F_{s+}\)).

2. What does \(\F_{s+}\) contain that \(\F_s\) may not?

Events decided by the path on \([0,s+\varepsilon]\) for every \(\varepsilon\gt0\). An example is “\(B\) exceeds \(B_s\) immediately after \(s\)”. For Brownian motion such events carry no extra predictive power.

3. Which property of the paths does the proof of Prop. 2.4 use?

Continuity: it lets the conditioning time \(u\) go down to \(s\) while \(B_u-B_s\to0\).

4. Compute \(\E[B_3^2\mid\F_1]\).

\(P_2(x^2)(B_1)=B_1^2+2\).

5. Why is \(P_{t+s}=P_tP_s\) “just” a fact about Gaussians?

It says \(N(0,s)*N(0,t)=N(0,s+t)\) (Chapman–Kolmogorov for Gaussian kernels).

6. What is the generator of Brownian motion, and on which functions did we compute it?

\(\frac12\frac{\dd^2}{\dd x^2}\), as the limit of \((P_t\varphi-\varphi)/t\), for \(\varphi\in C^2_c\). Lab 2.1 measures −2.9991 against \(-3\) at \(t=10^{-4}\).

7. Why does \(\|P_t\varphi-\varphi\|_\infty\to0\) fail for \(\varphi=\ind_{[-1/2,1/2]}\)?

At the jump points \(P_t\varphi=\frac12\) for every \(t\gt0\), while \(\varphi=1\) there. Strong continuity needs (uniform) continuity of \(\varphi\).

8. State Blumenthal's 0–1 law and one consequence.

\(\F_{0+}\) is trivial. Consequence: \(\inf\{t\gt0:B_t\gt0\}=0\) a.s.; the path takes both signs on every \([0,\varepsilon]\).

9. Why is \(\{\tau_a\le t\}=\{\sup_{r\in[0,t]\cap\Qb}|B_r-a|=0\}\) wrong?

The sup event says \(B=a\) on all rationals of \([0,t]\), impossible for \(a\ne0\). It should be the infimum.

10. Why is the last zero before 1 not a stopping time?

Deciding \(\{L\le t\}\) requires knowing the future after \(t\). If it were one, strong Markov plus Blumenthal would force another zero after \(L\).

11. In the proof of the strong Markov property, why approximate \(\tau\) from above rather than below?

\(\tau_N=(\lfloor2^N\tau\rfloor+1)2^{-N}\ge\tau\) is still a stopping time: \(\{\tau_N\le t\}\) is decided by time \(t\). Rounding down would anticipate the future. Continuity then gives \(B_{\tau_N+\cdot}\to B_{\tau+\cdot}\).

12. What is the law of \(\tau_a\)? Is its mean finite?

\(\tau_a\eqd a^2/G^2\) (Lévy law), median \(a^2/\Phi^{-1}(3/4)^2\approx2.198a^2\). \(\E\tau_a=\infty\).

13. Why \(\tau_{ca}\eqd c^2\tau_a\)?

Brownian scaling: \(c^{-1}B_{c^2t}\) is a Brownian motion, and hitting \(ca\) for \(B\) at time \(t\) means hitting \(a\) for the scaled motion at time \(t/c^2\).

14. State the reflection principle, with its hypotheses.

For \(a\ge0\), \(b\le a\): \(\P(S_t\ge a,B_t\le b)=\P(B_t\ge2a-b)\).

15. \(S_t\eqd|B_t|\) for each \(t\). Is \((S_t)\eqd(|B_t|)\) as processes?

No. \(S\) is nondecreasing and \(|B|\) is not: \(\P(|B_1|\lt|B_{1/2}|)\approx0.352\) against \(\P(S_1\lt S_{1/2})=0\). Lab 2.4: correlations 0.814 against 0.454.

16. Why does a simulated grid maximum underestimate \(S_1\), and how do you fix it?

The path can peak between grid points. Sample the exact bridge maximum on each step, \(\frac12\big(x+y+\sqrt{(y-x)^2-2h\ln U}\big)\).

17. Compute \(\Var\big(\sum_k(\Delta_kB)^2\big)\).

\(2\sum_k(\Delta_kt)^2\), since \(\Var(G^2)=2\).

18. Why does nonzero quadratic variation imply infinite variation for a continuous path?

\(\sum(\Delta X)^2\le\max|\Delta X|\cdot\TV\to0\) if \(\TV\lt\infty\), by uniform continuity.

19. What is the quadratic variation of \(B_t+\sin(2\pi t)\)?

\(t\). The smooth part has zero quadratic variation, and the cross terms vanish (Lab 2.5: 1.0007).

20. Is \(\sup\) over all partitions of \(\sum(\Delta B)^2\) equal to \(t\)?

No: it is \(+\infty\) a.s. (Lévy). Quadratic variation is a limit along fixed meshes.
