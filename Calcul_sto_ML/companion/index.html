Study companion · Alain Oliviero-Durmus

Stochastic calculus in machine learning: sampling and generative modeling

Eight lesson pages, one per chapter of the notes, with interactive labs, worked exercises and self-tests.

What the course is about

Sampling algorithms and diffusion-based generative models are stochastic differential equations (SDEs) run on a computer. This course builds, starting from the definition of Brownian motion, the calculus needed to write such equations, to know when they have a solution, and to compute with them.

The first board of the course, “Chap 0. Motivation and illustration” (photos IMG_3205 2 – IMG_3211 2), names three places where this calculus is used in machine learning.

  1. Sampling and Monte Carlo. The goal is to sample from a law \(\pi\) whose density is proportional to \(e^{-U}\), known only up to a multiplicative constant. The Langevin diffusion \(\dd X_t=-\nabla U(X_t)\,\dd t+\sqrt2\,\dd B_t\) has \(\pi\) as its long-run law: \(\Law(X_t)\to\pi\) as \(t\to\infty\). Discretizing it in time gives the Langevin algorithm (Lab 1.1, Lab 8.5).
  2. Generative models, in particular diffusion models. They turn data into noise with an SDE and learn to run that SDE backwards in time (Lesson 8, Beyond the notes).
  3. Theory of deep learning. Train a network with one hidden layer of \(N\) neurons by gradient descent, with iterates \(w^i_k\). Speed up time as \(\tilde w^{i,N}_t=w^i_{\lfloor tN\rfloor}\). As \(N\to\infty\), these processes converge to the solution of an SDE (Lesson 1, board Chapter 0).

Giving a meaning to \(\dd X_t=b(X_t)\,\dd t+\sigma(X_t)\,\dd B_t\) takes the eight chapters of the notes, one per lesson here. We need a Brownian motion \(B\) (Lesson 1), its law, Markov property and roughness (Lesson 2), and a precise model of information (Lesson 3). Then come martingales (Lesson 4), the processes one can integrate against (Lesson 5), the stochastic integral (Lesson 6), the chain rule and changes of probability (Lesson 7), and finally existence and uniqueness of solutions (Lesson 8).

The notes stop at the semigroup property of SDE solutions (Corollary 8.23, p. 73). The machine-learning uses from the board, the Kolmogorov and Fokker–Planck equations, and the time reversal behind diffusion models are not in the notes. These pages add them, always inside a “Beyond the notes” box.

How the course is built

Three movements

1 · The object (Lessons 1–2)

Construct Brownian motion and put its law on the space of continuous paths. Then read off what the law implies. Independent increments give the Markov property. Gaussian scaling gives the roughness: quadratic variation \(t\), infinite total variation.

2 · Information and fair games (Lessons 3–5)

Filtrations say what is known at time \(t\). Stopping times are the random times one can act on. Martingales are processes whose best forecast is their present value. Local martingales and the bracket \(\br{M}\) extend the tools to processes that are fair only up to random times.

3 · The calculus (Lessons 6–8)

Define \(\int H\,\dd M\) in \(L^2\), by an isometry, rather than path by path. Derive the chain rule (Itô's formula) and the rule for changing the probability (Girsanov). Then solve SDEs and study their Markov semigroups.

One method runs through all three movements. Prove the statement on a finite grid, control the error, and let the grid shrink. It gives Lévy's construction, quadratic variation, the strong Markov property, optional stopping, the bracket, the stochastic integral, Itô's formula and Picard's iteration. It is thread T2 in the map of threads.

T1 · Laws on path space

What is “the law” of a random path, and when do two constructions give the same one?

L1: fidis; Wiener measure → L2: same law on W^d; Markov kernels → L7: Girsanov: a new law on path space → L8: weak uniqueness = equal laws

T2 · Discretize dyadically, then pass to the limit

Prove it on a grid, control the error, let the mesh go to 0.

L1: Lévy’s dyadic construction → L2: QV on dyadic meshes; τ_N ↓ τ → L3: progressive via dyadic approximations → L4: Doob and optional stopping on dyadic skeletons → L5: bracket as a limit of sums → L6: elementary integrands; Riemann sums → L7: Taylor on meshes = Itô → L8: Picard iterates

T3 · Quadratic variation: the roughness that drives Itô calculus

Paths have infinite variation but finite quadratic variation; everything follows.

L1: Hölder exponents below 1/2 → L2: Σ(ΔB)² → t; infinite variation → L4: B² − t is a martingale → L5: the bracket ⟨M⟩ → L6: Itô isometry → L7: ½ f″ d⟨X⟩; Lévy’s characterization → L8: σσᵀ in the dynamics

T4 · Information, stopping and localization

What is known at time t, and how do we cut a process at a random time?

L2: F_{s+}, Blumenthal, stopping times → L3: usual conditions; F_τ; hitting times → L4: optional stopping; uniform integrability → L5: local martingales → L6: integrals built by localization → L7: Novikov and Kazamaki criteria → L8: strong vs weak solutions (which filtration?)

T5 · Markov property, semigroups and PDEs

The future depends on the past only through the present; averages solve equations.

L1: independent Gaussian increments → L2: Markov, strong Markov, heat semigroup → L4: Laplace transform of hitting times → L7: generator b·∇ + ½Tr(a∇²) → L8: SDE solutions are Markov; P_tP_s = P_{t+s}

T6 · Completeness and L² geometry

Build objects as limits: Cauchy sequences, isometries, fixed points.

L1: Cauchy in C([0,1]) + Borel–Cantelli → L2: L² convergence of quadratic sums → L4: UI, Doob Lᵖ, closed martingales → L5: E⟨M⟩∞ = E M∞² → L6: M₂,c is a Hilbert space; Riesz → L8: contraction in L²_T; Gronwall

T7 · Toward sampling and generative modeling

Stochastic dynamics that sample a target law, and how to change the law of paths.

L1: Langevin diffusion (board, Chap. 0) → L2: the heat semigroup smooths → L4: exponential martingales → L7: Girsanov = importance sampling of paths → L8: OU and Langevin as SDEs; Markov semigroup

Synthesis of the course

The lessons at a glance

For each lesson: the question it answers, the objects it works with, its methods, and the one choice on which everything else depends.

Lesson and questionWhat it manipulatesMethodsThe choice that decides
1 · Brownian motion
What is Brownian motion, does it exist, and what is the law of a random path?		 Random functions \(t\mapsto B_t\); fidis; Gaussian vectors with covariance \(s\wedge t\); modifications and indistinguishable versions; the Wiener space \(\Wd=C(\R_+,\R^d)\). Gaussian characterization (Prop. 1.13); dyadic construction with Borel–Cantelli and a uniform Cauchy limit (Thm. 1.19); uniqueness of laws by monotone class (Prop. 1.28); Kolmogorov's extension theorem and continuity criterion (Thm. 1.31, 1.33). Where the law lives. The fidis do not see continuity, so the law is put on the space of continuous paths. In the construction: keep the old values and add midpoint noise of size \(2^{-(n+2)/2}\).
2 · Markov and quadratic variation
What does the past say about the future, and how rough is the path?		 The restarted path \(B_{s+\cdot}-B_s\); the σ-fields \(\F_s\) and \(\F_{s+}\); the heat semigroup \(P_t\); stopping times; the running maximum; sums of squared increments. Independent increments and freezing (Cor. 2.5); time inversion for Blumenthal's 0–1 law (Cor. 2.9); dyadic stopping times \(\tau_N\downarrow\tau\) (Thm. 2.15); reflection at \(\tau_a\) (Thm. 2.17); the variance of quadratic sums (Prop. 2.21). Restart only at stopping times: at the last zero before time 1 the restart fails (Lesson 2, E8). Measure roughness with squared increments: \(\sum(\Delta B)^2\to t\) while \(\sum|\Delta B|\to\infty\).
3 · Filtrations and stopping times
What do we know at time \(t\), and which random times can we decide?		 Filtrations \(\F_t\), \(\F_{t+}\) and their completion; adapted, measurable and progressive processes; stopping and optional times; \(\F_\tau\); hitting times \(\tau_A\). Countable reductions through path regularity; approximation of stopping times from above (Thm. 3.18); the usual augmentation (Prop. 3.6). The usual conditions. Right-continuity turns optional times into stopping times (Ex. 3.8) and makes hitting times of open sets stopping times (Thm. 3.20). With completeness alone, Thm. 3.21 fails (\(X_t=tZ\)).
4 · Martingales
When do martingale identities survive limits and stopping times?		 Martingales, sub- and supermartingales in continuous time; running maxima; upcrossings; uniformly integrable (UI) families; closed martingales; stopped processes. Discrete-time results on dyadic skeletons, then right-continuity; Doob's inequalities (Thm. 4.6, 4.9); upcrossings and convergence (Thm. 4.12, 4.15); Vitali (Thm. 4.13); optional stopping (Thm. 4.29, 4.30); exponential martingales (Ex. 4.33). Bounded stopping times, or uniform integrability. Without them, \(\E[B_{\tau_a}]=a\ne0\) (Lab 4.3), and \(e^{B_t-t/2}\to0\) almost surely although its mean is 1 (Lab 4.6).
5 · Semimartingales
Which processes are integrators, and how rough is a continuous martingale?		 Functions of bounded variation and their Stieltjes measures; finite-variation processes; local martingales and reducing sequences; the brackets \(\br{M}\) and \(\br{M,N}\); semimartingales \(X=X_0+M+A\). Jordan and Hahn decompositions (Prop. 5.3, Thm. 5.7); localization by stopping times; \(L^2\) estimates for sums of squared increments (Thm. 5.23); polarization; the Kunita–Watanabe inequality (Thm. 5.39). Continuity. A continuous local martingale of finite variation is constant (Thm. 5.22). This makes the decomposition \(X=X_0+M+A\) unique and the bracket well defined. With jumps it fails: \(N_t-t\) for a Poisson process \(N\) (Lab 5.4).
6 · Stochastic integrals
How do we integrate against a path of infinite variation?		 The Hilbert space \(\Mtc\); the integrands \(L^2(M)\) and \(L^2_{\mathrm{loc}}(M)\); elementary processes; the integral \(H\cdot M\); Riemann sums. Riesz representation through \(\br{H\cdot M,N}=H\cdot\br{M,N}\) (Thm. 6.6), which amounts to the isometry \(\E(H\cdot M)_\infty^2=\E\int H^2\,\dd\br{M}\); localization (Thm. 6.14); convergence of left Riemann sums (Prop. 6.18). Progressive integrands, evaluated at the left end point. Then integrals are (local) martingales, and \(\E\int H\,\dd M=0\) in the \(L^2\) case. Other points give other limits: for \(\int_0^1B\,\dd B\), right-point sums exceed left-point sums by about 1 (Lab 6.3).
7 · Itô and Girsanov
How do smooth maps and changes of probability transform semimartingales?		 \(f(X_t)\) for \(f\in C^2\); stochastic exponentials \(\cE(L)=\exp(L-\frac12\br{L})\); probabilities \(\dd\Q=\cE(L)_T\,\dd\P\); time changes by \(\br{M}\). Taylor expansion on meshes and bracket limits (Thm. 7.1); exponential martingales and characteristic functions (Lévy, Thm. 7.8); the inverse bracket as a clock (Thm. 7.10); change of measure (Thm. 7.13, 7.14); Novikov and Kazamaki (Prop. 7.19). Keep the second-order term \(\frac12f''\,\dd\br{X}\): \(\dd\br{X}\) is of order \(\dd t\), not \(\dd t^2\). For Girsanov: check that \(\cE(L)\) is a true martingale (Novikov); otherwise \(\Q\) has total mass below 1.
8 · SDEs
When does an SDE have a solution, is it unique, and in which sense?		 Coefficients \(b,\sigma\); solutions of \(\cE_{X_0}(b,\sigma)\); the space \(L^2_T\); pathwise and weak uniqueness; strong and weak solutions; the Markov semigroup \(P_t\). Picard iteration with Doob, the Itô isometry and Gronwall (Thm. 8.9); Lévy's characterization (Ex. 8.12); Girsanov for bounded drifts (Ex. 8.13); Yamada–Watanabe (Thm. 8.17); restarting with fresh noise (Thm. 8.19). Lipschitz and linear-growth coefficients, assumption (L): under it, everything holds. Beyond it, one must say which sense of solution is meant. Tanaka's SDE has weak solutions and uniqueness in law, but no strong solution. Without Lipschitz, uniqueness can fail; without linear growth, solutions can explode (Lab 8.3).

The question each lesson keeps asking

Each lesson page opens with “A question that comes back”: the one question the lesson keeps asking, and how earlier lessons asked it. Here are the eight, with links to where each page discusses it.

  1. Lesson 1: What does it mean to know the law of a random path, and which properties of the path does the law see?
  2. Lesson 2: Given what we know now, what is the law of the future, and can “now” be a random time?
  3. Lesson 3: What exactly do we know at time \(t\), and which random times can we act on?
  4. Lesson 4: An identity holds at every fixed time. Does it survive at random times, and in the limit \(t\to\infty\)?
  5. Lesson 5: Which random processes can we integrate against, and what intrinsic quantity measures their roughness?
  6. Lesson 6: How can we integrate against a path of infinite variation, and what survives from ordinary calculus?
  7. Lesson 7: How do smooth maps and changes of probability transform a semimartingale, and which part, drift or noise, changes?
  8. Lesson 8: Given drift and noise coefficients, is there a process, is it unique, and in which sense: same paths or same law?

Read together, the eight questions are three questions, asked with more generality each time.

  • Existence. Does the object exist, and on which space? Brownian motion on \(\Wd\) (Lesson 1), the integral in \(\Mtc\) (Lesson 6), solutions of SDEs (Lesson 8).
  • Law and information. What is known at time \(t\), and what is the law of what comes next? Fidis (Lesson 1), the Markov property (Lesson 2), filtrations (Lesson 3), uniqueness in law and semigroups (Lesson 8).
  • Stability. Which identities survive limits, random times and changes of probability? The strong Markov property (Lesson 2), optional stopping and uniform integrability (Lesson 4), localization (Lesson 5), Novikov's criterion (Lesson 7).
Key idea

Continuous paths, right-continuous complete filtrations, localization by stopping times and \(L^2\) isometries are what make limits safe. Quadratic variation is the price of roughness. It shows up as the extra term \(\frac12f''\,\dd\br{X}\) in Itô's formula, and as \(\sigma\sigma^\top\) in the generator of an SDE.

Corrections to the notes

The notes are a typed transcription of handwritten notes, and they contain slips. Every problem found was checked against the page image at 200–300 dpi, since text extraction garbles formulas. Each lesson page flags its problems where they occur, in a “Watch out” box with the evidence: the page image, a counterexample, a direct computation, or a numerical check. This section lists them all, chapter by chapter, with links.

The ones that change a statement

Most slips are typographical. These are not: as printed, the statement is false, or it proves something else.

The printed scale gives a continuous process with covariance \(2(s\wedge t)-st\), not a Brownian motion. Evidence: the board (IMG_3259), the notes' own Case 4, the exact covariance, Lab 1.3, and a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (\(p\approx10^{-53}\)).

How the lesson pages work

All eight pages have the same structure, so you always know where to look.

  1. §0 Where this lesson sits. What the lesson reuses and what it prepares, with links to the exact statements; the thread map with this lesson's stations; and “A question that comes back”.
  2. The sections of the notes, numbered as in the notes and with their pages. Each topic follows the same order: the problem, the intuition, the formulation, why it is true, when it fails, how it is used. Important results come with a one-sentence summary, the idea of the proof, a counterexample when an assumption matters, and where the result is used again.
  3. Labs inside the sections. Each lab asks one question, opens with the notes' own settings, and ends with “What the lab shows”, whose numbers are measured by the code at the default settings. Every lab has a seed, so its results are reproducible.
  4. Exercises: every exercise of the notes, and every step the notes leave to the reader, with progressive hints and a worked solution, all collapsed.
  5. End matter: conclusion, references, Python reference code with its printed output, cheat sheet, self-test, and glossary in English and French.

Five kinds of boxes, used sparingly:

Key idea

The one thing to remember from a section.

Watch out

A trap, a notation conflict, or an error in the notes, with the evidence.

Link

A connection to another lesson.

From the whiteboard

Material from the photos of the first lecture, with the file names (Lesson 1 only).

Beyond the notes

Anything that is in neither the notes nor the board. Everything outside these boxes is course content.

Links. Every statement has an anchor built from its number: Theorem 4.29 is Lesson04.html#n4-29, §2.1 is Lesson02.html#s2-1, Lab 7.3 is Lesson07.html#lab7-3. Keyboard. Controls are reachable with Tab; on a chart, the arrow keys move the crosshair, and a screen reader announces the values. Themes. The switch in the top bar toggles light and dark; the choice is remembered.

Glossary language. The glossaries give each term in English and French. The request left the second language open (“<SECOND LANGUAGE, OR DELETE>”). French was chosen because the notes refer to a “TD sheet” (travaux dirigés), the term French universities use for exercise classes.
