Continuous-Time Martingales and Submartingales

When do martingale identities survive limits in time and random (stopping) times?

§0 Where this lesson sits

A question that comes back

“An identity holds at every fixed time. Does it survive at random times, and in the limit \(t\to\infty\)?” Lesson 2 asked it for the Markov property (restart at a stopping time), and Lesson 3 for which random times are allowed. Here the identity is \(\E[M_t\mid\F_s]=M_s\). The answer is optional stopping for bounded stopping times, or for uniformly integrable martingales. Lab 4.3 exhibits the classic failure: \(\E[B_{\tau_a}]=a\ne0=\E[B_0]\). Lesson 7 asks the same question for \(\E[\cE(L)_t]=1\) as \(t\to\infty\).

§4.1 Definitions

notes, pp. 27–28

The problem. We want a notion of “fair game” in continuous time: a process whose best forecast of the future, given the present information, is its current value. It is the stochastic analogue of a constant function. Its variants, submartingales and supermartingales, are analogues of monotone functions.

Definition 4.1 (martingale, submartingale)notes p. 27

A real process \((M_t)_{t\ge0}\), adapted to \((\F_t)\), is an \((\F_t)\)-martingale (resp. submartingale) if \(\E|M_t|\lt\infty\) for every \(t\) and \(\E[M_t\mid\F_s]=M_s\) (resp. \(\ge M_s\)) for all \(s\le t\). A supermartingale satisfies \(\le\).

Remark 4.2 (notes). Multi-dimensional versions are componentwise. If the filtration is not implicit, adaptedness must be required.

Example 4.3 (Brownian martingales)notes p. 27

\(B\) is a Brownian motion with filtration \((\F_t)\) if \(B_0=0\), \(B\) is adapted, and for every \(s\), \((B_{t+s}-B_s)_{t\ge0}\) is a Brownian motion independent of \(\F_s\). This holds for the natural filtration \(\F^B\) and for its usual augmentation. Then the following are \((\F_t)\)-martingales: (a) \(B_t\); (b) \(B_t^2-t\); (c) \(\exp(uB_t-u^2t/2)\) for every \(u\in\R\).

Why. In all three, write \(B_t=(B_t-B_s)+B_s\), with the increment independent of \(\F_s\) and \(B_s\) known.

\[ \begin{aligned}\E[B_t^2-t\mid\F_s]&=\E[(B_t-B_s)^2]+2B_s\E[B_t-B_s]+B_s^2-t\\ &=(t-s)+B_s^2-t=B_s^2-s,\end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned}\E\big[e^{uB_t-u^2t/2}\mid\F_s\big]&=e^{uB_s-u^2t/2}\,\E\big[e^{u(B_t-B_s)}\big]\\ &=e^{uB_s-u^2t/2}e^{u^2(t-s)/2}=e^{uB_s-u^2s/2}.\end{aligned} \]
Key idea

The three martingales come from the Gaussian increments: mean zero gives (a), variance \(t-s\) gives (b), and the moment generating function \(e^{u^2(t-s)/2}\) gives (c). The subtracted terms \(t\) and \(u^2t/2\) are the compensators that make \(B^2\) and \(e^{uB}\) “fair”. Lesson 5 generalizes (b): \(M^2-\br{M}\) is a local martingale (Thm. 5.23). Lesson 7 generalizes (c): the stochastic exponential \(\cE(M)=\exp(M-\frac12\br{M})\) (Example 7.7).

Beyond the notes: the ML reading of (c)

\(e^{uB_t-u^2t/2}\) is the likelihood ratio (Radon–Nikodym density) between Brownian motion with drift \(u\) and Brownian motion without drift, on \([0,t]\). Its mean is 1, as for any density ratio. It is the simplest case of Girsanov's theorem (Thm. 7.16), and the reason importance-sampling weights on path space are martingales. Lab 4.6 shows the catch: they degenerate as \(t\) grows.

Proposition 4.4notes p. 28

If \((M_t)\) is a martingale, then \(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\E|M_s|\le\E|M_t|\lt\infty\).

Why. Conditional Jensen: \(\E[|M_t|\mid\F_s]\ge|\E[M_t\mid\F_s]|=|M_s|\). So \(|M|\) is a submartingale, and the expectations of a submartingale increase. Exercise 4.5 (E1) asks for the same statement for nonnegative submartingales.

§4.2 Martingales and continuity

notes, pp. 28–29

The problem. Discrete-time martingale theory (Doob's inequalities, optional stopping, convergence) is proved on finite or countable index sets. In continuous time, suprema over \([0,t]\) are uncountable, and even measurability is at stake. The strategy, announced by the notes (p. 28): prove everything on dyadic skeletons \(\{q2^{-n}\}\), then pass to the limit using right-continuity of paths. (Thread T2.)

Theorem 4.7 (Doob's \(L^p\) inequality, discrete time)notes p. 28

If \((\tilde M_n)\) is a \((\G_n)\)-martingale and \(p\gt1\), then \(\E\big[\sup_{0\le q\le n}|\tilde M_q|^p\big]\le\big(\frac p{p-1}\big)^p\E|\tilde M_n|^p\).

Theorem 4.6 (Doob's \(L^p\) inequality)notes p. 28

Let \((M_t)\) be a right-continuous \((\F_t)\)-martingale and \(p\gt1\). Then \[ \E\Big[\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|M_s|^p\Big]\le\Big(\frac p{p-1}\Big)^p\E|M_t|^p. \] For \(p=2\): \(\E[\sup_{s\le t}M_s^2]\le4\,\E[M_t^2]\).

In one sentenceThe whole path of a martingale up to time \(t\) is controlled, in \(L^p\), by its final value.
Proof ideaApply Thm. 4.7 to the discrete martingale \(M^{(n)}_q=M_{tq2^{-n}}\), \(q=0,\dots,2^n\). By right-continuity, \(\sup_q|M_{tq2^{-n}}|\uparrow\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|M_s|\) (the dyadic points of \([0,t]\) are dense, and \(t\) itself is included). Conclude with monotone convergence.
Why right-continuity mattersWithout it, the dyadic supremum can miss the true supremum. Example: a modification of Brownian motion that equals \(+10\) at one random time \(U\) (as in Lesson 1, §1.2) is still a martingale in the sense of Def. 4.1. But its supremum is at least 10.
Used againCompleteness of \(\Mtc\) (Thm. 6.3), the \(L^2\) theory of brackets (Thm. 5.28), and \(L^p\) convergence (Cor. 4.18).
Watch out

Theorem 4.6 in the notes omits “right-continuous”, but its proof uses it (“Since \((M_t)\) is right-continuous, almost surely \(\sup|M_{tq/2^n}|\uparrow\sup|M_s|\)”). Under the usual conditions every martingale with right-continuous \(t\mapsto\E M_t\) has a càdlàg modification (Thm. 4.11). So one can always work with that version, but the hypothesis belongs in the statement.

Theorem 4.8 (stopped submartingales, discrete)notes p. 28

If \((\tilde M_n)\) is a \((\G_n)\)-submartingale and \(T\) a \((\G_n)\)-stopping time, then \((\tilde M_{n\wedge T})_n\) is a \((\G_n)\)-submartingale.

Theorem 4.9 (Doob's maximal inequality)notes p. 29

If \((M_t)\) is a right-continuous submartingale, then for every \(\lambda\gt0\) and \(T\): \(\lambda\,\P\big(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}M_t\gt\lambda\big)\le3\,\E|M_T|\).

Theorem 4.10 (discrete maximal inequality)notes p. 29

If \((\tilde M_n)\) is a discrete-time submartingale, then \(\lambda\,\P\big(\sup_{q\le N}\tilde M_q\gt\lambda\big)\le3\,\E|\tilde M_N|\).

Watch out: constant, proof placement, and a false two-sided version (notes p. 29)
  • The constant 3 is not needed for the one-sided bound. The classical Doob inequality is \(\lambda\P(\sup_{t\le T}M_t\ge\lambda)\le\E[M_T^+]\le\E|M_T|\). The proof splits \(\{\sup M\ge\lambda\}\) at the first passage time and uses optional stopping. Lab 4.1 shows how loose \(3\E|M_T|\) is: for Brownian motion at \(\lambda=2\), \(3\E|B_1|=\) 2.3929, while \(\E[B_1^+]=\) 0.4048 and the true value of \(\lambda\P(\sup B\ge\lambda)\) is 0.0910.
  • The proofs are swapped. Theorem 4.9 gets the one line “extension of the discrete case”. The proof printed under Theorem 4.10 is actually the continuous-time extension (dyadic skeletons and right-continuity).
  • That proof silently switches to \(|M|\), claiming \(\lambda\P(\sup_{s\le t}|M_s|\gt\lambda)\le3\E|M_t|\). This is false for submartingales. Take the deterministic increasing function \(M_s=-5+5s\) on \([0,1]\). It is a submartingale with \(M_1=0\), so \(3\E|M_1|=0\), yet \(\sup|M_s|=5\). The correct two-sided bound for a right-continuous submartingale is \(\lambda\P(\sup_{s\le t}|M_s|\ge\lambda)\le\E|M_0|+2\E|M_t|\). For martingales, \(\E|M_0|\le\E|M_t|\) turns this into \(3\E|M_t|\), and applying the one-sided bound to the submartingale \(|M|\) even gives \(\E|M_t|\).
Lab 4.1 · Thm. 4.6 and Thm. 4.9

How tight are Doob's inequalities?

Question: Doob bounds the probability that a martingale ever exceeds \(\lambda\) on \([0,1]\) by \(\E[M_1^+]/\lambda\). It bounds the second moment of its running maximum by \(4\E[M_1^2]\). How close are these bounds to the truth? We test three martingales from Example 4.3, simulated on a grid. For Brownian motion the reflection principle gives the exact left side: \(\lambda\P(\sup_{s\le1}B_s\ge\lambda)=2\lambda\bar\Phi(\lambda)\).

What the lab shows (default settings unless stated)
  • Maximal inequality, Brownian motion, \(\lambda=2\): simulated \(\lambda\hat\P=\) 0.0860. It sits slightly below the exact 0.0910 because the grid misses peaks (Lab 2.4). The Doob bound is \(\E[B_1^+]=\) 0.4048, and the notes' \(3\E|B_1|=\) 2.3929. The chart shows the bound is closest near \(\lambda\approx0.7\)–\(1\).
  • Exponential martingale \(e^{B_t-t/2}\) (heavy right tail): \(\lambda\hat\P=\) 0.6452 against \(\E[M_1^+]=\E M_1=\) 1.0017. Here the bound is much tighter.
  • \(L^2\) inequality: \(\hat\E[\sup_{s\le1}B_s^2]=\) 1.781 against \(4\E B_1^2=\) 3.988; for \(e^{B_t-t/2}\), 6.491 against 10.322. The factor 4 is sharp in general over all martingales, not for these examples.
Theorem 4.11 (càdlàg modification)notes p. 29

If \((\F_t)\) is right-continuous (and complete), \((M_t)\) is a submartingale and \(t\mapsto\E[M_t]\) is right-continuous, then \(M\) has a càdlàg modification (right-continuous with left limits) which is again a submartingale.

So under the usual conditions every martingale has a càdlàg version: its mean is constant, hence right-continuous. From now on martingales are taken càdlàg, and all the continuous-time theorems apply. (Proof, beyond the notes: the upcrossing inequality on the countable set of dyadics shows that \(t\mapsto M_t\) has left and right limits along the dyadics, almost surely. Define \(\tilde M_t=\lim_{q\downarrow t,\,q\in D}M_q\); right-continuity of the filtration keeps it adapted, and right-continuity of the mean makes it a modification.)

§4.3 Convergence of martingales and optional stopping

notes, pp. 29–37

The problem. Two questions. (1) Does \(M_t\) have a limit as \(t\to\infty\), and in which sense? (2) Is \(\E[M_\tau]=\E[M_0]\) at a random time \(\tau\)? Both are answered by the same tool, uniform integrability, and both are proved from discrete time by dyadic approximation.

Upcrossings and almost sure convergence

Intuition. A real sequence converges in \([-\infty,\infty]\) if and only if, for every rational pair \(a\lt b\), it crosses from below \(a\) to above \(b\) only finitely many times. For a submartingale, the expected number of such upcrossings is controlled by how far above \(a\) it can end up.

Theorem 4.12 (upcrossing inequality, corrected)notes p. 29

Let \(a\lt b\) and \((\tilde M_q)_{q\in\N}\) be a submartingale. Let \(U_N[a,b]\) be the number of upcrossings of \([a,b]\) completed by time \(N\): the number of disjoint time intervals \([s,u]\subset[0,N]\) with \(\tilde M_s\le a\) and \(\tilde M_u\ge b\). Then \[ \E\big[U_N[a,b]\big]\le\frac{\E[(\tilde M_N-a)^+]}{b-a}, \] so that \(\E\big[U_\infty[a,b]\big]\le\sup_q\E[(\tilde M_q-a)^+]/(b-a)\).

Watch out: the notes' definition of an upcrossing (p. 29)

The notes define “\(N_{a,b}=\sum_q\ind_{[a,b]}(\tilde M_q)\), the number of upcrossings of \([a,b]\)”. That sum counts the visits of \([a,b]\), which can be huge without any crossing: a process sitting inside \([a,b]\) has infinitely many visits and no upcrossing. Lab 4.2 measures about 562.9 grid visits of \([0,0.5]\) for a Brownian path, against 0.720 upcrossings. The notes also require \(a\gt0\), which is unnecessary.

Proof idea (standard, beyond the notes' text). Consider the strategy “buy when \(\tilde M\le a\), sell when \(\tilde M\ge b\)”. Its gain is at least \((b-a)U_N-(\tilde M_N-a)^-\). It is a martingale transform \(\sum H_k\Delta\tilde M_k\) with \(H_k\in\{0,1\}\) predictable, so its expectation is at most \(\E[(\tilde M_N-a)^+]\) minus the gain of the complementary strategy, which is \(\ge0\) for a submartingale. The inequality follows.

Lab 4.2 · Thm. 4.12

Upcrossings: counting correctly, and Doob's bound

Question: how many times does a Brownian path on \([0,1]\) cross \([a,b]\) upwards, and how does the count compare with Doob's bound \(\E[(B_1-a)^+]/(b-a)\)? We also compute the notes' quantity \(\sum_q\ind_{[a,b]}(B_q)\) to see why it is not an upcrossing count.

What the lab shows (default settings unless stated)
  • For \([0,0.5]\) and 2000 grid steps: mean 0.720 upcrossings against the bound 0.824 (ratio 0.873). The notes' “\(N_{a,b}\)” averages 562.9: it counts grid points in \([a,b]\) and grows with the resolution.
  • Narrow band \([0,0.1]\): 3.203 upcrossings (bound 4.121). With 10,000 steps, 3.564 (bound 4.079). For a continuous-time Brownian path the number of upcrossings of a fixed band is finite, but it grows like \(1/(b-a)\) as the band shrinks. That is another face of infinite variation.
Theorem 4.15 (submartingale convergence)notes p. 30

If \((M_t)\) is a right-continuous submartingale with \(\sup_{t\ge0}\E[M_t^+]\lt\infty\), there is \(M_\infty\in L^1\) with \(M_t\to M_\infty\) almost surely.

Why. On each dyadic skeleton \(\{q2^{-n}\}\), Theorem 4.12 bounds the expected number of upcrossings of \([a,b]\) uniformly in \(n\). So along the countable set \(D\) of dyadics there are finitely many upcrossings of every rational band, almost surely. By right-continuity the same holds along all of \(\R_+\), so \(\lim_tM_t\) exists in \([-\infty,\infty]\). Fatou's lemma gives \(\E M_\infty^+\le\liminf\E M_t^+\lt\infty\). Since \(\E M_t\) increases, \(\E M_t^-=\E M_t^+-\E M_t\le\sup_s\E M_s^+-\E M_0\), and Fatou again gives \(M_\infty\in L^1\).

Corollary 4.16 (supermartingale convergence, corrected)notes p. 30

If \((M_t)\) is a right-continuous supermartingale with \(\sup_t\E[M_t^-]\lt\infty\), in particular if \(M\ge0\), then \(M_t\to M_\infty\) a.s., \(M_\infty\in L^1\), and for nonnegative \(M\), \(\E[M_\infty]\le\E[M_0]\).

Watch out: Corollary 4.16 is false as printed

The notes state it for any right-continuous supermartingale. Counterexample: the deterministic \(M_t=-t\) is a supermartingale, and it tends to \(-\infty\). The proof (“apply the previous result to \(-M\)”) needs \(\sup_t\E[(-M_t)^+]=\sup_t\E[M_t^-]\lt\infty\), which holds for nonnegative supermartingales, the case used later (Thm. 5.20, Thm. 7.12). For \(M\ge0\), Fatou gives \(\E M_\infty\le\liminf\E M_t\le\E M_0\).

Uniform integrability: the condition that makes limits and expectations commute

Definition 4.19 (uniform integrability)notes p. 31

A family \(\{X_i:i\in I\}\) is uniformly integrable (UI) if \(\lim_{A\to\infty}\sup_{i\in I}\E\big[|X_i|\ind_{\{|X_i|\ge A\}}\big]=0\).

Uniform integrability means no mass escapes to infinity: the tails are small uniformly over the family. It is weaker than domination by one integrable variable, and stronger than boundedness in \(L^1\). Examples: a family bounded in \(L^p\) for some \(p\gt1\) is UI (Remark 4.14), since \(\E[|X|\ind_{|X|\ge A}]\le\E|X|^p/A^{p-1}\). The family \(\{n\ind_{[0,1/n]}\}\) on \([0,1]\) is bounded in \(L^1\) but not UI.

Theorem 4.13 (= Theorem 4.20; Vitali)notes pp. 29, 31

If \(X_i\to X\) in probability and \(\{X_i\}\) is UI, then \(X_i\to X\) in \(L^1\).

The notes state this twice (Thm. 4.13 and Thm. 4.20, with Exercise 4.39 giving a proof). A clean proof is Exercise E4. The two properties used repeatedly are Exercise 4.21 (UI implies uniform absolute continuity: \(\P(A)\le\delta\Rightarrow\sup_i\E[|X_i|\ind_A]\le\varepsilon\)) and Exercise 4.22 (conditional expectations of a UI family, over any family of σ-fields, form a UI family). See E2 and E3.

(Anchors for Exercises 4.21 and 4.22; the notes repeat them as Exercises 4.40 and 4.41.)

Definition 4.17 (= Definition 4.23; closed martingale)notes pp. 30–31

A (sub)martingale is closed if there is \(M_\infty\in L^1\) with \(M_t=\E[M_\infty\mid\F_t]\) (resp. \(\le\)) for all \(t\).

“Closed” means the martingale is the running forecast of one terminal quantity. The notes repeat this as Definition 4.23.

Corollary 4.18notes pp. 30–31

If a martingale satisfies \(\sup_t\E|M_t|^p\lt\infty\) for some \(p\gt1\), it converges almost surely and in \(L^p\).

Why. Theorem 4.15 gives a.s. convergence, and Fatou gives \(M_\infty\in L^p\). Doob (Thm. 4.6) gives \(\sup_t|M_t|\in L^p\), a dominating variable for \(|M_t-M_\infty|^p\). The notes state it for “(sub-)martingales”. The domination step uses Doob's inequality, which needs a martingale or a nonnegative submartingale.

Theorem 4.24notes pp. 31–32

A uniformly integrable (sub)martingale converges almost surely and in \(L^1\) to some \(M_\infty\), and it is closed by \(M_\infty\).

Theorem 4.25notes p. 32

A closed martingale is uniformly integrable.

Why (4.24). UI implies \(L^1\)-bounded, hence a.s. convergence (Thm. 4.15), hence \(L^1\) convergence (Thm. 4.13). For \(A\in\F_t\) and \(T\ge t\), \(\E[\ind_AM_t]=\E[\ind_AM_T]\to\E[\ind_AM_\infty]\), so \(M_t=\E[M_\infty\mid\F_t]\). (4.25) is Exercise 4.22 applied to the single variable \(M_\infty\).

Key idea: three equivalent descriptions

For a càdlàg martingale: UI ⇔ converges in \(L^1\) ⇔ closed, i.e. \(M_t=\E[M_\infty\mid\F_t]\). This is Theorem 4.38, stated in discrete time; the continuous case follows from Theorems 4.24–4.25. A martingale that converges only almost surely can lose mass at infinity, as the next lab shows.

Lab 4.6 · Ex. 4.3 (c), Thm. 4.24

A martingale with mean 1 that tends to 0: why uniform integrability matters

Question: \(E_t=\exp(uB_t-u^2t/2)\) is a martingale with \(\E E_t=1\) for all \(t\) (Example 4.3 (c)). It is nonnegative, so it converges a.s. (Cor. 4.16), and in fact \(E_t\to0\), because \(B_t/t\to0\) (Lesson 1). So \(\E[\lim E_t]=0\ne1=\lim\E E_t\): the martingale is not UI and not closed. What does this look like in a simulation?

What the lab shows (default settings unless stated)
  • The sample mean collapses. With 20,000 paths and \(u=1\): 0.997 at \(t=1\), 0.951 at \(t=10\), and 0.337 at \(t=30\), where the exact mean is 1.
  • The mass hides in rare paths. At \(t=30\), 0.969 of the paths have \(E_{30}\lt0.01\), while the largest 1% of the paths carry 0.997 of the total. The exact probability is \(\P(E_{30}\lt0.01)=0.971\) (reference code).
  • Why this matters for ML. \(E_t\) is a likelihood ratio on path space, i.e. an importance-sampling weight. When the weights are not UI, Monte Carlo averages are dominated by a handful of samples and systematically underestimate. This is weight degeneracy, and in Lesson 7 it becomes the question of when \(\E[\cE(L)_T]=1\) (Novikov).
Theorem 4.26 (backward martingales, corrected)notes p. 32

Let \((\F_{-n})_{n\ge0}\) be a decreasing family: \(\F_{-(n+1)}\subset\F_{-n}\). Let \((M_{-n})\) be adapted and integrable with \(\E[M_{-n}\mid\F_{-(n+1)}]\ge M_{-(n+1)}\) (a backward submartingale). If \(\inf_n\E[M_{-n}]\gt-\infty\), equivalently \(\sup_n\E|M_{-n}|\lt\infty\), then \(M_{-n}\) converges a.s. and in \(L^1\) as \(n\to\infty\). For a backward martingale the limit is \(M_{-\infty}=\E[M_0\mid\F_{-\infty}]\), with \(\F_{-\infty}=\bigcap_n\F_{-n}\).

Watch out: Theorem 4.26 is printed backwards (p. 32)

The notes write the inclusion as \(\F_{-n}\subset\F_{-(n+1)}\) and the submartingale property as \(\E[M_{-(n+1)}\mid\F_{-n}]\ge M_{-n}\). Proof ideaRound up: \(\sigma_n=\lceil2^n\sigma\rceil/2^n\downarrow\sigma\) and \(\tau_n=\lceil2^n\tau\rceil/2^n\downarrow\tau\) are stopping times of the dyadic skeleton (Lab 3.3). Example 4.33 (Laplace transform of \(\tau_a\))notes p. 34

Let \(a\gt0\), \(\theta\gt0\) and \(M_t=\exp\big(\theta B_{t\wedge\tau_a}-\frac{\theta^2}2(t\wedge\tau_a)\big)\). Then \(0\le M_t\le e^{\theta a}\), so \(M\) is a bounded, hence UI, martingale (Cor. 4.32). It converges to \(M_\infty=\exp(\theta a-\frac{\theta^2}2\tau_a)\) (using \(\tau_a\lt\infty\) a.s.). Optional stopping gives \(\E M_\infty=\E M_0=1\), i.e. \[ \E\big[e^{-\theta^2\tau_a/2}\big]=e^{-\theta a}\quad(\theta\gt0), \] equivalently \(\E\big[e^{-\lambda\tau_a}\big]=e^{-a\sqrt{2\lambda}}\) for \(\lambda\ge0\). By symmetry, \(\E[e^{-\theta^2\tau_a/2}]=e^{-|\theta|a}\) for all \(\theta\in\R\).

In one sentenceA bounded exponential martingale stopped at the hitting time reveals the Laplace transform of that time.
Why \(\theta\gt0\) mattersBefore \(\tau_a\), \(B\le a\), so \(e^{\theta B}\le e^{\theta a}\) only when \(\theta\gt0\). For \(\theta\lt0\) the stopped exponential is not bounded, and the same computation would give the wrong answer \(e^{-\theta a}\gt1\).

Example 4.34 (Cauchy law from a planar Brownian motion)notes p. 35

Theorem 4.35 (optional stopping, closed case)notes p. 35

Exercises

E1 · Exercise 4.5 notes p. 28

Show that Proposition 4.4 holds for nonnegative submartingales: \(\sup_{s\le t}\E|M_s|\le\E|M_t|\).

Solution

For \(M\ge0\), \(|M_s|=M_s\le\E[M_t\mid\F_s]\) by the submartingale property, so \(\E|M_s|\le\E M_t=\E|M_t|\). Without nonnegativity it can fail: \(M_s=-5+5s\) has \(\E|M_0|=5\gt\E|M_1|=0\).

E2 · Exercises 4.21 and 4.40: UI implies uniform absolute continuity notes pp. 31, 36

Show: if \(\{X_i\}\) is UI, then \(\sup_i\E|X_i|\lt\infty\) and for every \(\varepsilon\gt0\) there is \(\delta\gt0\) with \(\P(A)\le\delta\Rightarrow\sup_i\E[\ind_A|X_i|]\le\varepsilon\). Conversely these two properties imply UI (this is Thm. 7.20).

Hint

Split \(|X_i|=|X_i|\ind_{\{|X_i|\ge M\}}+|X_i|\ind_{\{|X_i|\lt M\}}\).

Solution

Pick \(M\) with \(\sup_i\E[|X_i|\ind_{|X_i|\ge M}]\le\varepsilon/2\). Then \(\E[\ind_A|X_i|]\le\varepsilon/2+M\P(A)\le\varepsilon\) for \(\P(A)\le\delta=\varepsilon/(2M)\). Taking \(A=\Omega\) gives \(\sup_i\E|X_i|\le\varepsilon/2+M\). Conversely, by Markov, \(\P(|X_i|\ge A)\le\sup_j\E|X_j|/A\le\delta\) for \(A\) large, uniformly in \(i\). Apply uniform absolute continuity with the event \(\{|X_i|\ge A\}\).

E3 · Exercises 4.22 and 4.41: conditional expectations of a UI family notes pp. 31, 36–37

Show that \(\{\E[X_i\mid\G_j]:i\in I,j\in J\}\) is UI when \(\{X_i\}\) is UI and \(\{\G_j\}\) is any family of sub-σ-fields.

Solution

Let \(Y=\E[X_i\mid\G_j]\) and \(M=\sup_i\E|X_i|\). By Jensen, \(|Y|\le\E[|X_i|\mid\G_j]\), and \(\{|Y|\ge R\}\in\G_j\), so \(\E[|Y|\ind_{|Y|\ge R}]\le\E[|X_i|\ind_{|Y|\ge R}]\). By Markov, \(\P(|Y|\ge R)\le\E|Y|/R\le M/R\). Given \(\varepsilon\), take \(\delta\) from E2 and \(R=M/\delta\). Then \(\E[|Y|\ind_{|Y|\ge R}]\le\varepsilon\) for all \(i,j\). Consequence: every closed martingale \(M_t=\E[M_\infty\mid\F_t]\) is UI (Thm. 4.25), even \(M_\sigma\) at stopping times (used in Thm. 4.35).

E4 · Theorems 4.13 and 4.20, Exercise 4.39: Vitali's theorem notes pp. 29, 31, 36

Show that \(X_i\to X\) in probability and \(\{X_i\}\) UI imply \(X\in L^1\) and \(X_i\to X\) in \(L^1\).

Solution

A subsequence converges a.s., and Fatou gives \(\E|X|\le\liminf\E|X_{i_k}|\lt\infty\). So \(\{X_i-X\}\) is UI (a sum of two UI families). Fix \(\varepsilon\) and \(\delta\) from E2 for this family. Then \(\E|X_i-X|\le\varepsilon+\E\big[|X_i-X|\ind_{\{|X_i-X|\gt\varepsilon\}}\big]\le2\varepsilon\) as soon as \(\P(|X_i-X|\gt\varepsilon)\le\delta\), which holds for \(i\) large. Without UI: \(X_n=n\ind_{[0,1/n]}\to0\) in probability, but \(\E X_n=1\). This is the same escape of mass as in Labs 4.3 and 4.6.

E5 · Theorem 4.26 (backward martingales), corrected notes p. 32: “Left as an exercise.”

Prove that a backward martingale \(M_{-n}=\E[M_0\mid\F_{-n}]\), with \(\F_{-(n+1)}\subset\F_{-n}\), converges a.s. and in \(L^1\) to \(\E[M_0\mid\F_{-\infty}]\).

Hint

For fixed \(N\), \((M_{-N},M_{-N+1},\dots,M_0)\) is an ordinary martingale indexed by \(0,\dots,N\). Apply the upcrossing inequality to it: the bound involves only \(\E[(M_0-a)^+]\), uniformly in \(N\).

Solution

The number of upcrossings of \([a,b]\) by \((M_{-N},\dots,M_0)\), read forward, is at most \(\E[(M_0-a)^+]/(b-a)\) in expectation, uniformly in \(N\). Letting \(N\to\infty\), a.s. there are finitely many upcrossings of each rational band, so \(M_{-n}\) converges a.s. in \([-\infty,\infty]\). The family \(\{\E[M_0\mid\F_{-n}]\}\) is UI (E3), so the limit is finite and the convergence holds in \(L^1\) (E4). The limit \(L\) is \(\F_{-m}\)-measurable for every \(m\), hence \(\F_{-\infty}\)-measurable. For \(A\in\F_{-\infty}\), \(\E[\ind_AM_{-n}]=\E[\ind_AM_0]\) for all \(n\), and in the limit \(\E[\ind_AL]=\E[\ind_AM_0]\). So \(L=\E[M_0\mid\F_{-\infty}]\).

Conclusion

  • From discrete to continuous. Every continuous-time result is proved on dyadic skeletons and transferred by right-continuity. Under the usual conditions martingales have càdlàg versions (Thm. 4.11), so this is always possible.
  • Doob's inequalities. \(\lambda\P(\sup M\ge\lambda)\le\E M_T^+\) and \(\E\sup|M|^p\le(\frac p{p-1})^p\E|M_T|^p\) (Thm. 4.6, Thm. 4.9). The path is controlled by its endpoint, and these inequalities power the \(L^2\) theory of Lessons 5–6.
  • Convergence. Upcrossings bound the oscillations, which gives a.s. convergence (Thm. 4.15). Uniform integrability is exactly what upgrades a.s. convergence to \(L^1\) and makes the martingale closed (Thm. 4.24, Thm. 4.38). Nonnegative martingales converge a.s. but may lose mass (Lab 4.6).
  • Optional stopping. \(\E[M_\tau\mid\F_\sigma]=M_\sigma\) for bounded stopping times, or for UI martingales (Thm. 4.29, Thm. 4.30, Thm. 4.35). This computes exit probabilities, expected exit times and Laplace transforms (Example 4.33), and even produces the Cauchy law (Example 4.34). It fails without UI (\(\E B_{\tau_a}=a\)).
  • Errors in the notes (Thm. 4.6 missing right-continuity; Thm. 4.9/4.10 proofs swapped and a false two-sided claim; Thm. 4.12 upcrossing definition; Cor. 4.16; Thm. 4.26 reversed; Cor. 4.31 hypothesis; Example 4.34 display; misplaced proof of Thm. 4.35) are corrected above with counterexamples.

print("2) Upcrossings of [0, 0.5] by B on [0,1] (grid 2000): mean count vs Doob bound E[(B_1-a)^+]/(b-a)")
def upcross(path, a, b):
    n_up, below = 0, False
    for v in path:
        if not below and v <= a: below = True
        elif below and v >= b: n_up += 1; below = False
    return n_up
P = np.cumsum(rng.standard_normal((2000, 2000)) / np.sqrt(2000), axis=1)
P = np.concatenate([np.zeros((2000, 1)), P], axis=1)
counts = np.array([upcross(p, 0.0, 0.5) for p in P])
print(f"   mean {counts.mean():.3f}   bound {np.mean(np.maximum(P[:, -1], 0))/0.5:.3f}   (exact bound E[B_1^+]/0.5 = {2/np.sqrt(2*np.pi):.3f})")

print("3) Exit of (-b, a) = (-2, 1): P(hit a first) = b/(a+b) = 2/3, E[tau] = ab = 2 (vectorized, dt=1e-3, bridge-corrected)")
a, b, dt, M = 1.0, 2.0, 1e-3, 20000
x = np.zeros(M); alive = np.ones(M, bool); hitA = np.zeros(M, bool); tau = np.zeros(M); t = 0.0
while alive.any():
    t += dt
    idx = np.flatnonzero(alive)
    y = x[idx] + np.sqrt(dt) * rng.standard_normal(idx.size)
    pa = np.where(y >= a, 1.0, np.exp(-2 * np.clip(a - x[idx], 0, None) * np.clip(a - y, 0, None) / dt))
    pb = np.where(y <= -b, 1.0, np.exp(-2 * np.clip(x[idx] + b, 0, None) * np.clip(y + b, 0, None) / dt))
    ca, cb = rng.random(idx.size) < pa, rng.random(idx.size) < pb
    done = ca | cb
    hitA[idx[ca & ~cb]] = True
    both = ca & cb
    hitA[idx[both]] = rng.random(both.sum()) < 0.5
    tau[idx[done]] = t
    alive[idx[done]] = False
    x[idx] = y
print(f"   P(hit a first) = {hitA.mean():.4f} (se {hitA.std()/np.sqrt(M):.4f})   E[tau] = {tau.mean():.3f}")

print("4) Laplace transform of tau_a (Example 4.33): E[exp(-lam tau_a)] = exp(-a sqrt(2 lam)); tau_a ~ scipy.stats.levy(scale=a^2)")
for a_, lam in [(1.0, 1.0), (0.5, 2.0), (2.0, 0.5)]:

    val = integrate.quad(lambda s: np.exp(-lam * s) * lev.pdf(s), 0, np.inf, limit=200)[0]

print(f"   sample means of 4 blocks of 50000: {[round(float(v), 2) for v in XT.reshape(4, -1).mean(axis=1)]}  (no law of large numbers)")

print("6) Exponential martingale E_t = exp(B_t - t/2): E[E_t] = 1 but P(E_t < 0.01) -> 1")
for tt in [1, 10, 30]:

    Et = np.exp(np.sqrt(tt) * rng.standard_normal(20000) - tt / 2)
    print(f"   t={tt:2d}: P(E_t<0.01) exact {exact:.3f} simulated {np.mean(Et < 0.01):.3f};  sample mean of 20000 paths {Et.mean():.3f}")

Cheat sheet

Brownian martingales

\(B_t\), \(B_t^2-t\), \(e^{uB_t-u^2t/2}\). Compensators come from the mean, variance and MGF of Gaussian increments.

Doob

\(\lambda\P(\sup_{t\le T}M_t\ge\lambda)\le\E M_T^+\) (submart.). \(\E\sup_{t\le T}|M_t|^p\le(\frac p{p-1})^p\E|M_T|^p\) (right-continuous martingale).

Upcrossings

\(\E U_N[a,b]\le\E(M_N-a)^+/(b-a)\). Finitely many upcrossings of all rational bands ⇒ convergence.

Convergence

\(\sup\E M_t^+\lt\infty\) ⇒ a.s. limit in \(L^1\). Nonnegative supermart. ⇒ a.s. limit, \(\E M_\infty\le\E M_0\). \(L^p\)-bounded (\(p\gt1\)) ⇒ \(L^p\) limit.

UI

\(\sup_i\E[|X_i|;|X_i|\ge A]\to0\). UI ⇔ \(L^1\)-bounded + uniformly abs. continuous. UI + prob. ⇒ \(L^1\). \(\{\E[X\mid\G_j]\}\) is UI.

Closed martingales

UI ⇔ \(L^1\) convergence ⇔ \(M_t=\E[M_\infty\mid\F_t]\). \(e^{uB-u^2t/2}\) is not UI.

Optional stopping

\(\E[M_\tau\mid\F_\sigma]=M_\sigma\) for \(\sigma\le\tau\) bounded, or \(M\) UI. Stopped càdlàg martingales are martingales.

Computations

Exit of \((-b,a)\): \(\P(a)=\frac b{a+b}\), \(\E\tau=ab\). \(\E e^{-\lambda\tau_a}=e^{-a\sqrt{2\lambda}}\). \(X_T\) (planar BM at \(\{y=1\}\)) is Cauchy.

Self-test

1. Show that \(B_t^2-t\) is a martingale.

\(\E[B_t^2\mid\F_s]=B_s^2+(t-s)\) by expanding \((B_s+(B_t-B_s))^2\).

2. Why is \(|M|\) a submartingale when \(M\) is a martingale?

Conditional Jensen with the convex function \(|\cdot|\).

3. What does Doob's \(L^2\) inequality say, and which hypothesis on paths is needed?

\(\E\sup_{s\le t}M_s^2\le4\E M_t^2\), for right-continuous (càdlàg) martingales.

4. Is \(\lambda\P(\sup_{s\le t}|M_s|\ge\lambda)\le3\E|M_t|\) true for submartingales?

No: \(M_s=-5+5s\). It is true for martingales, and even with constant 1.

5. What is an upcrossing, and what does the notes' \(\sum_q\ind_{[a,b]}(M_q)\) count instead?

A passage from \(\le a\) to \(\ge b\). The sum counts visits of \([a,b]\).

6. Why does a nonnegative supermartingale converge a.s.?

\(-M\) is a submartingale with \(\sup\E(-M)^+=0\lt\infty\); apply Thm. 4.15.

7. Give a supermartingale that does not converge.

\(M_t=-t\).

8. Define uniform integrability and give a family bounded in \(L^1\) that is not UI.

\(\sup_i\E[|X_i|;|X_i|\ge A]\to0\). \(n\ind_{[0,1/n]}\).

9. Why is a family bounded in \(L^2\) uniformly integrable?

\(\E[|X|;|X|\ge A]\le\E X^2/A\).

10. State the three equivalent properties of a UI martingale.

UI; convergence in \(L^1\); closed by its limit.

11. Is \(e^{B_t-t/2}\) UI? What is its a.s. limit?

No. It tends to 0 a.s. while its mean stays 1. Lab 4.6: sample mean 0.337 at \(t=30\).

12. Which way do the σ-fields go for a backward martingale, and why does it converge?

They decrease as \(n\to\infty\) (\(\F_{-(n+1)}\subset\F_{-n}\)). The martingale is \(\E[M_0\mid\F_{-n}]\), automatically UI.

13. State optional stopping for bounded stopping times.

For \(\sigma\le\tau\le T\) and a càdlàg martingale, \(\E[M_\tau\mid\F_\sigma]=M_\sigma\).

14. Why does \(\E B_{\tau_a}=a\) not contradict optional stopping?

\(\tau_a\) is unbounded and \(B^{\tau_a}\) is not UI; the negative mass escapes (Lab 4.3).

15. Compute \(\P(\text{exit }(-b,a)\text{ at }a)\) and \(\E\tau\).

\(b/(a+b)\) and \(ab\).

16. Why must \(\theta\gt0\) in Example 4.33?

So that the stopped exponential martingale is bounded by \(e^{\theta a}\).

17. What is \(\E[e^{-\lambda\tau_a}]\), and what does its behaviour at \(\lambda=0\) say?

\(e^{-a\sqrt{2\lambda}}\); the infinite slope at 0 reflects \(\E\tau_a=\infty\).

18. Why is \(X_T\) Cauchy in Example 4.34?

\(X_T\eqd\sqrt TG\), so its characteristic function is \(\E e^{-\theta^2T/2}=e^{-|\theta|}\).

19. Why is a stopped càdlàg martingale a martingale?

Cor. 4.31 with the bounded times \(\tau\wedge t\) and \(s\): \(\E[M_{\tau\wedge t}\mid\F_s]=M_{\tau\wedge s}\).

20. In the proof of continuous optional stopping, why round the stopping times up?

\(\lceil2^n\sigma\rceil/2^n\) is a stopping time of the dyadic skeleton and decreases to \(\sigma\); right-continuity then passes to the limit (Lab 3.3).
