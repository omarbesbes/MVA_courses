7. Exercises

Exercise 1 — The neighbourhood rule by hand ★

The drafted code has target probability $0.05$. Its nearest codes, closest first, have target probabilities $0.08$, $0.06$, $0.10$, $0.04$. With $k=4$ and $\delta=0.2$: (a) which neighbours are counted? (b) What is the total variation between the distorted target and the target? (c) What is the acceptance probability if the drafter proposed the code with certainty? And if it proposed it with probability $0.1$, for the exact rule and for the relaxed rule?

Solution

(a) Add neighbours while their total stays below $\delta$: $0.08$, then $0.14$; the third would give $0.24\ge0.2$, so stop. Two neighbours are counted (the fourth is not examined: the set grows outward and stops at the first excess).

(b) The mass moved onto the drafted code: $0.14$.

(c) The set has mass $0.05+0.14=0.19$. Drafter certain: exact $0.05$, relaxed $0.19$. Drafter at $0.1$: exact $\min(1,0.05/0.1)=0.5$; relaxed $\min(1,0.19/0.1)=1$. In the second case the drafted code is always kept, and is produced more often than the target wants: $0.1$ against $0.05$ from this route alone.

Exercise 2 — A tolerance on robot actions ★★

An action dimension is cut into 256 bins. Suppose the drafted bin is uniformly distributed among the 41 bins within 20 of the target's choice. (a) What fraction of drafted tokens passes the exact test? A tolerance of $r=5$? Of $r=9$? (b) An action has 7 such tokens, drafted independently. What is the probability that the whole action is accepted, in the three cases? (c) With $r=9$, how large can the error be as a fraction of the range of the dimension, and why might a long task tolerate less than a short one?

Solution

(a) Exact: $\frac{1}{41}=2.4\%$. $r=5$: $\frac{11}{41}=27\%$. $r=9$: $\frac{19}{41}=46\%$.

(b) $\big(\tfrac1{41}\big)^7\approx5\times10^{-12}$; $\big(\tfrac{11}{41}\big)^7\approx10^{-4}$; $\big(\tfrac{19}{41}\big)^7\approx0.5\%$. Whole actions are essentially never accepted; the gain comes from prefixes, which is why the measured speed-ups stay modest (1.2× to 1.4×).

(c) $\frac{9}{256}=3.5\%$ of the range on each dimension, at each step. Errors on successive actions add up along a trajectory, so a task with more steps accumulates more drift before it ends: the paper finds $r=9$ acceptable on three suites and $r=5$ the limit on the long-horizon suite.

Exercise 3 — Tokens per second, or seconds per answer ★★

With $\ga=5$ and $c=0.06$, the exact rule accepts $2.5$ drafted tokens per round on average and a relaxed rule $3.6$. The answers have 420 tokens on average with the exact rule and 510 with the relaxed rule. Compute the speed-up over the target alone (a) of the exact rule, (b) of the relaxed rule in tokens per second, (c) of the relaxed rule per answer. (d) Up to which length would the relaxed rule be the faster of the two per answer?

Solution

(a) $\frac{2.5+1}{1+0.3}=2.69$. (b) $\frac{3.6+1}{1.3}=3.54$: 31% more than the exact rule. (c) $3.54\times\frac{420}{510}=2.91$: 8% more than the exact rule. (d) The two are equal when the length ratio is $\frac{4.6}{3.5}=1.31$, that is 552 tokens. A paper that reports (b) and a user who experiences (c) are describing the same system.

Exercise 4 — Retention, and the noise floor ★★

On an accuracy benchmark of 200 questions the target scores 80%, the drafter 60% and a lossy method 78%. On a refusal benchmark the target refuses 90% of harmful requests, the drafter 20%, the lossy method 70%. (a) Compute the retention rate PRR on each. (b) The exact method, on the same 200 questions, scores 78.5%. What do you conclude about the accuracy result?

Hint

The standard error of a proportion $x$ measured on $n$ examples is $\sqrt{x(1-x)/n}$.

Solution

(a) Accuracy: $\frac{78-60}{80-60}=0.90$. Refusals: $\frac{70-20}{90-20}=0.71$. The same rule keeps 90% of what the target adds in accuracy and 71% of what it adds in refusals: the pattern of §4.

(b) The standard error is $\sqrt{0.8\times0.2/200}=2.8$ points. The exact method, which should score 80%, is at 78.5%; the lossy method is at 78%. Both are within one standard error of the target and of each other. On this benchmark alone the accuracy cost of the lossy rule cannot be told from zero, nor from five points. The refusal result, 20 points below the target, is far outside the noise.

Exercise 5 — The option of the Transformers library ★★

The option accepts a drafted token with probability $\min\{1,\,1-w+w\,p(x)/q(x)\}$. (a) Show that this is the exact test of page 1 against the mixture $\pi=w\,p+(1-w)\,q$. (b) On example A of page 1, $p=(0.6,0.3,0.1)$ and $q=(0.3,0.5,0.2)$, with $w=0.7$: compute $\pi$, the acceptance probability, and $\TV(\pi,p)$. (c) Check the trade-off theorem of page 3, §2.

Solution

(a) $\dfrac{\pi(x)}{q(x)}=\dfrac{w\,p(x)+(1-w)\,q(x)}{q(x)}=w\,\dfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}+1-w$. If the residual is also computed from $\pi$, the output follows $\pi$ exactly.

(b) $\pi=0.7\,p+0.3\,q=(0.51,\,0.36,\,0.13)$. Acceptance: $\sum\min(\pi,q)=0.3+0.36+0.13=0.79$, against $0.70$ for the exact rule. $\TV(\pi,p)=0.3\times\TV(q,p)=0.3\times0.3=0.09$.

(c) Rejection $+$ bias $=0.21+0.09=0.30=\TV(p,q)$: equality. The mixture is on the front: each point of acceptance gained is paid by exactly one point of total variation. The theorem says no rule does better at one position; it says nothing about which tokens the 9% fall on.

Exercise 6 — A leak that exactness does not close ★★

A service streams each round of speculative decoding as one network packet. (a) Why does an observer of the encrypted traffic learn something about the text, even if the method is exact? (b) The service now sends exactly 4 tokens every 100 ms, waiting if fewer are ready. What is lost, and what remains visible?

Solution

(a) A packet carries between 1 and $\ga+1$ tokens, depending on how many drafts were accepted in that round. Encryption hides the content and not the size; the sequence of sizes is a signature of where the drafter and the target agree, which depends on the text. Exactness concerns the distribution of the text, not the schedule on which it is produced.

(b) Lost: latency. Tokens ready early wait for their slot, and the service can never emit faster than 40 tokens per second, whatever the acceptance. Still visible: the total length of the answer, and the moments when fewer than 4 tokens were ready, if the service then sends a shorter packet instead of waiting. Padding every packet to a fixed size closes that too, at a cost in bandwidth.
