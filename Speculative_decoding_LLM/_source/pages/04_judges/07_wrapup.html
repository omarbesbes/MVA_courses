8. What to remember

Summary
  • The exact test measures agreement with the target. Text written by a stronger model or by a human is rejected after two or three tokens: most rejections are not mistakes.
  • A judge is a second opinion applied where the exact test rejects: “is this different token harmless?” The answer is read from the target's hidden state after the drafted token (Judge Decoding, AutoJudge, SelfJudge) or from its entropy and its behaviour on the following tokens (FLy).
  • The methods differ by where the labels come from: hand annotation, counterfactual search on a task with checkable answers, the target's own likelihood, or no labels.
  • Reported gains, always against the exact rule on the same pair: between 1.1× and 2× in speed, two to three times the tokens per target call in the best case (a very large target, a strong drafter, long drafts), for a loss of zero to two points on the tasks the judge was built for.
  • The output can differ from the target's only through the judge's false accepts, and these compound over an answer.
  • Judges are task-specific (six points lost on code for a judge trained without code), need a notion of correct answer, and safety degrades before accuracy.

Reflexes

  • A speed-up “over the target” for a judge method → find the exact rule with the same drafter in the same table; the judge's own gain is the ratio of the two.
  • “Tokens accepted per round” → not speed: longer drafts cost drafter time, and a cheaper drafter can win with fewer tokens.
  • A judge → ask what it was trained on, and test it on something else.
  • “Accuracy preserved” → on which tasks? Is there an open-ended one, a hard one, a safety one?
  • A comparison at equal draft length → check that the exact baseline was given its own best draft length.
  • A “judge” that reads only $p$ and $q$ at the position → it is a rule of page 3.

What comes next

The judges of this page still look at one token at a time. But what we care about in an answer is rarely a token: it is a step of reasoning, a line of code, a sentence. Page 5 moves the unit of speculation up: the small model writes a whole step, and something — a reward model, the large model itself — scores the step. At that scale another change becomes possible: for the steps that are accepted, the large model may not need to read the tokens at all.

Glossary

mismatch
A drafted token that differs from the target's own choice at that position.
judge / verifier head
A small classifier that decides whether a mismatch may be accepted.
important / critical token
A mismatch whose acceptance changes the outcome (the final answer, the tests passed).
counterfactual rollout
Regenerating the rest of an answer after swapping one token, to see what changes.
false accept
A harmful mismatch that the judge lets through.
recovery ratio, PRR
Score of the accelerated system relative to the target's (and, for PRR, to the drafter's).
out-of-domain
A task unlike those the judge was trained on.
loose / loosely speculative decoding
Another name for lossy verification that accepts semantically acceptable tokens.
