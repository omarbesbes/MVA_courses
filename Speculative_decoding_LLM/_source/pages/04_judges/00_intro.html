What you will be able to do after this page
  1. Give the evidence that the exact test rejects correct text: what happens when a stronger model, or a human, writes the draft.
  2. Describe the judge: what it reads (a hidden state of the target), how small it is, and how its verdict is combined with the exact test.
  3. Explain the three ways of obtaining labels for a judge: by hand (Judge Decoding), by counterfactual search (AutoJudge), by the target's own likelihood (SelfJudge).
  4. Run FLy, the training-free rule, by hand on a draft: entropy gate, then deferred window.
  5. Quote, with their settings, what each method reports — and compare with the exact rule on the same pair of models.
  6. Explain why judges are task-specific, why a small false-accept rate matters over a long answer, and what was found about safety.

0. Before you start

How to read this page

You need page 1 (the exact rule, and its greedy form: accept if the drafted token is the target's argmax). Page 3 helps for the contrast but is not required; the one idea to bring from it is that at temperature 0 a relaxed rule is defined by which non-matching tokens it lets through (§4 there).

All the methods of this page work at temperature 0 or close to it, and all share the same skeleton: the exact test first; where it rejects, a second opinion. Method cards have the same four rows as on page 3 (what it tries, how it decides, what it achieves, what it costs). The numbers are the authors', in the authors' setting; when a paper reports both the exact rule and its own method on the same models, we give both.

The story of the page in six steps

The exact test measures agreement, not correctness; a judge is a cheap way to ask the large model whether a different token is still fine; everything depends on where the judge's training labels come from, and on the tokens it gets wrong.

  1. The observation (§1). Give the large model a draft written by a better model, or by a human: it still rejects after two or three tokens. Rejection is not a sign of error.
  2. The judge (§2). After the large model has read a drafted token, its own hidden state says whether something went wrong. A linear classifier on that state, trained on a few hundred annotated answers, triples the number of accepted tokens (Judge Decoding).
  3. Labels without annotators (§3). Swap each disagreement into the large model's answer, let it finish, and see whether the final answer changes (AutoJudge).
  4. Labels without answers (§4). Ask the large model's likelihood whether the rest of its own answer still fits after the swap (SelfJudge).
  5. No training at all (§5). Accept a disagreement only where the large model was unsure, and only if it then goes along with the draft for the next few tokens (FLy).
  6. The bill (§6). Judges are learned on a task and do not travel well; one harmful token accepted is enough to spoil an answer; and safety degrades before accuracy does.

Notation

SymbolMeaning
mismatchA position where the drafted token differs from the target's own choice (its argmax, given the same prefix). The exact rule rejects there.
harmless / harmful mismatchA mismatch whose acceptance leaves the quality of the answer unchanged / degrades it. Each method has its own operational definition; the papers also say unimportant / important, or acceptable / critical.
$\ga$Draft length (the papers write $M$, $K$ or “window”). With a judge it is much longer than on page 1: 20 to 60 tokens.

$e_i$The target's last-layer hidden state at the position of drafted token $i$, i.e. after reading that token.
judgeA classifier $e_i\mapsto$ probability that the drafted token is acceptable.
