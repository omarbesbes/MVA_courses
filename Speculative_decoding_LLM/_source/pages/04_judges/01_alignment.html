1. Agreement is not correctness

The exact test accepts a token when the large model would have written that very token. Feed it text written by a stronger model, or by a careful human, and it stops after two or three tokens: it measures alignment with one model's habits, not quality.

The experiment that started the family

If rejections were signs of mistakes, a better draft would be rejected less. The authors of Judge Decoding tested exactly that. They took Llama-3.1-405B as the target and replaced the usual small drafter by GPT-4o, a far stronger writer than any small drafter: GPT-4o writes complete answers, and one counts how many tokens the target accepts, with the greedy exact test, before the first rejection.

In the paper Bachmann et al. (Judge Decoding), ICLR 2025, §3.2 and Figures 2–3

“Counter-intuitively, we find that the target model does not reward the higher quality of tokens, accepting only roughly two before encountering the first rejection.” The same count for human-written Wikipedia summaries, used as answers to “What can you tell me about …?”: 3.1 tokens. And with the roles reversed on GSM8K — the 405B model drafting for the 8B model — 6.3 tokens, against 6.6 the usual way round: “acceptance rates do not improve with the quality of the responses.”

The exact test, then, is not a quality filter that happens to be strict. It answers a different question. A sentence can be opened in a hundred acceptable ways; the target has one favourite; anything else is “wrong” for the test. The paper states the consequence as a research question: “Can we adapt verification to assess token quality rather than alignment?”

A second observation of the same paper explains why simply drafting more does not help. With the exact test, the number of accepted tokens saturates as the draft gets longer: about 3.5 tokens for drafts of 5, about 11 for drafts of 50 (Llama-3.1-8B drafting for 405B). This is the $\frac{1}{1-\al}$ ceiling of page 1: a draft is cut at its first disagreement, however good the rest. It is the reason, say the authors, “why prior work is limited largely to $M\le7$”.

Pitfall — what this experiment does and does not show

It shows that exact matching is a poor proxy for quality: excellent text is rejected. It does not show that everything the exact test rejects is fine. A small drafter's disagreements are a mixture: many harmless rewordings, and some real mistakes — a wrong digit, a wrong variable. The whole difficulty of this page is to tell them apart token by token, before seeing the rest of the answer.

The common skeleton: a second opinion where the exact test rejects

All the methods of this page keep the loop of page 1 and change one step. The target still reads the whole draft in one pass. Then, at each position:

Algorithm — verification with a second opinion (greedy decoding)
  1. If the drafted token is the target's own choice: accept. the exact test; nothing is lost here
  2. Else (a mismatch): ask the second opinion — a trained judge (§2–§4) or a rule (§5).
  3. If it says “harmless”: accept the drafted token and go on. the only place where the output can differ from the target's
  4. If it says “harmful”: reject; the target writes its own token; the rest of the draft is discarded.

Two consequences follow from the structure alone. The second opinion can only add acceptances: with a judge that always says “harmful”, the method is exact speculative decoding. And every difference between the output and the target's own text comes from the judge's false positives: harmful mismatches it let through. The cost of the method is entirely there.

Why does the judge read the target's hidden state at the drafted token, and not the distribution $p_i$ that the target computed for that position?

Answer

$p_i$ was computed before the target saw the drafted token: it says what the target expected, which is what the exact test already uses. The hidden state at the drafted token is computed after reading it, with the whole context: it encodes the target's reaction to that token. The authors of Judge Decoding illustrate it: forced to start with “The capital of France is Berlin”, the model continues “… just kidding, it's actually Paris”. Something in its state after “Berlin” already knows.
