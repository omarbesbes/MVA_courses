8. Exercises

Exercise 1 — Which lever? ★

For each idea, say which term of the speed-up law it acts on ($\al$, $c$, or the number of tokens obtained from a call of the target at fixed $\al$ and $c$): (a) distilling the drafter on the target's outputs; (b) replacing a 1B drafter by one layer fed with the target's hidden state; (c) looking the continuation up in the prompt; (d) proposing three candidates for the first drafted token; (e) stopping the draft when the drafter's entropy is high; (f) a block drafter that writes 16 tokens in one pass; (g) block verification.

Solution

(a) $\al$. (b) Both: $c$ falls (one layer), and $\al$ rises (the head sees what the target computed). (c) $c$, down to zero; $\al$ is whatever the text allows. (d) The use of the call: more positions scored, more chances that one path survives. (e) The use of the call, and the drafter's wasted time: fewer tokens drafted after a rejection. (f) $c$: the cost of a draft no longer grows with its length. (g) The use of the call: the same draft and the same pass yield more kept tokens.

Exercise 2 — A lookup or a head? ★★

Use the law of page 1. A lookup drafter costs nothing ($c=0$); a head costs $c=0.05$. (a) On open-ended conversation the lookup has $\al=0.3$ and the head $\al=0.8$. Compare the best speed-ups (for the head, try $\ga=8$). (b) On a file-editing task where most of the output is copied, both reach $\al=0.9$. Compare at $\ga=16$. (c) In the heavy-load limit of §6, what is the speed-up of the lookup of (a) with $\ga=4$?

Hint

With $c=0$ the speed-up is the number of tokens per round, which grows with $\ga$ towards $\frac1{1-\al}$. Heavy load: $\frac{\tau}{\ga c+\ga+1}$.

Solution

(a) Lookup: at most $\frac1{1-0.3}=1.43$. Head, $\ga=8$: $\frac{1-0.8^{9}}{0.2\times(1+0.4)}=\frac{0.866}{0.28}=3.09$. The head wins by a factor of two.

(b) Lookup: $\frac{1-0.9^{17}}{0.1}=8.33$. Head: $\frac{8.33}{1+0.05\times16}=4.63$. The lookup wins: at equal acceptance, the free drafter can afford long drafts. This is the regime of prompt lookup, SuffixDecoding and “speculative edits” (§3, §6).

(c) $\tau=\frac{1-0.3^{5}}{0.7}=1.43$, and $\frac{1.43}{0+4+1}=0.29$: more than three times slower than plain decoding. A free drafter is not free under load: the target pays for every position it verifies. This is what SPEED-Bench measures for n-gram lookups at batch 32 (below 1×).

Exercise 3 — The best tree with four nodes ★★

Greedy decoding. At every position the target's choice is the drafter's first candidate with probability $0.6$ and its second candidate with probability $0.2$. You may score four drafted tokens. Compare (a) a chain of four first choices; (b) two candidates at the first position, and two after the first choice; (c) the best tree. Give the expected number of drafted tokens kept in each case.

Hint

The value of a node is the product of the probabilities along its path; the expected number of kept tokens is the sum of the values (§4).

Solution

Values: first choice at position 1: $0.6$; second choice at position 1: $0.2$; first choices at positions 2, 3, 4 along the main chain: $0.36$, $0.216$, $0.1296$; second choice at position 2 after the first choice: $0.6\times0.2=0.12$.

(a) $0.6+0.36+0.216+0.1296=1.306$. (b) $0.6+0.2+0.36+0.12=1.28$. (c) The four largest values are $0.6$, $0.36$, $0.216$ and $0.2$: a chain of three first choices plus one alternative for the first token, $1.376$. With four nodes the gain over a chain is 5%. Alternatives are worth adding where the main chain has become unlikely: the fourth token of the chain (value $0.13$) is worth less than a second guess at the first (value $0.2$).

Exercise 5 — Block verification: the order matters ★★

A draft of two tokens has the ratios $(\rho_1,\rho_2)$ between target and drafter. Compute the probability that both tokens are kept, for the token-by-token rule and for block verification, when (a) $(\rho_1,\rho_2)=(\tfrac12,2)$; (b) $(\rho_1,\rho_2)=(2,\tfrac12)$. Comment.

Solution

Token by token: $\min(1,\rho_1)\min(1,\rho_2)=\frac12$ in both cases. Block: (a) $w_1=\frac12$, $w_2=\min(1,\frac12\times2)=1$. (b) $w_1=\min(1,2)=1$, $w_2=\min(1,1\times\frac12)=\frac12$.

A deficit can be repaid by a later surplus; a surplus cannot be saved for a later deficit. The reason is in the first token. In (b) the target wants the first token twice as often as the drafter proposes it. Keeping it every time it is drafted supplies half of what is needed; the other half comes from the residual, after the rejection of other drafts, and those outputs continue with tokens drawn from the target. They already supply half of the sequences the target wants, so the drafted continuation may only be kept half of the time. In both cases the sequence as a whole has ratio $\rho_1\rho_2=1$, yet only (a) can be kept every time.

Exercise 6 — Where speculation stops paying ★★★

In the toy model of §6, find the batch size $B^\star$ at which the speed-up equals 1. Compute it for $\al=0.7$, $c=0.05$, $\kappa=0.005$ with $\ga=3$, then $\ga=8$.

Hint

Write $x=\kappa B$ and solve $\tau(1+x)=\ga c\,(1+x)+1+(\ga+1)x$.

Solution

$x\,(\tau-\ga c-\ga-1)=1+\ga c-\tau$, hence $$B^\star=\frac1\kappa\cdot\frac{\tau-1-\ga c}{\ga+1+\ga c-\tau}.$$ The numerator is positive exactly when speculation pays at batch 1; the denominator is always positive because $\tau\le\ga+1$.

$\ga=3$: $\tau=\frac{1-0.7^4}{0.3}=2.53$, so $B^\star=\frac{1}{0.005}\cdot\frac{1.38}{1.62}\approx171$. $\ga=8$: $\tau=\frac{1-0.7^9}{0.3}=3.20$, so $B^\star=\frac{1}{0.005}\cdot\frac{1.80}{6.20}\approx58$. The longer draft yields more tokens per round (3.20 against 2.53) and stops paying three times sooner: under load, drafts must shorten.
