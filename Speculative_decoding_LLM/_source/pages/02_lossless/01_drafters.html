1. A separate small model, and how to align it

The original design: an independent small language model drafts. Its acceptance rate is whatever its distribution happens to share with the target's; distillation raises it; its latency decides whether that helps.

Off-the-shelf pairs (a 1B model drafting for a 70B model of the same family, for example) reach token acceptance rates between roughly 0.35 and 0.8 depending on the pair, the task and the temperature. Two things can be improved: the overlap, and the cost.

DistillSpec — distil the drafter toward the target

Zhou et al. · ICLR 2024 · arXiv 2310.08461

losslesstrains the drafter2023

What it tries
Raise the acceptance rate at the source. Since acceptance is $1-\TV(p,q)$, train $q$ to be close to $p$.
How
Knowledge distillation of the drafter from the target, with two choices that matter: train on text generated by the drafter itself (“on-policy”), and pick the divergence (forward or reverse Kullback–Leibler, Jensen–Shannon, total variation) per task and decoding strategy.
What it achieves
“$10-45\%$ speedups over standard SD on a range of benchmarks” (T5 and GPT-like models, batch size 1).
What it costs
A training run per target and per task family. The drafter's cost $c$ is unchanged.

Latency matters as much as accuracy. A study of more than 350 configurations (Yan, Agarwal, Venkataraman, NAACL 2025) finds that throughput is governed by the drafter's latency, which depends on its depth more than on its parameter count, and that a drafter's accuracy on the task says little about its acceptance rate: a pruned drafter of 5 wide layers (0.8B parameters) gives about twice the throughput of a 24-layer drafter of 1.3B parameters. The authors of EAGLE (§2) make the same diagnosis from the other side: distillation “slightly improved the speedup ratio”, because “the bottleneck for speculative sampling performance lies in the high overhead of the draft model”. The next family attacks that overhead.

2. Let the target draft for itself: heads

Instead of a second model, attach a very small network to the target and feed it the target's own hidden state. The drafter then knows almost everything the target knows about the context, and costs one layer.

The target's last hidden state already contains what it is about to say. Medusa (ICML 2024) adds a few extra output heads to it: head $k$ predicts the token $k$ positions ahead, all from the same state, in one pass. The predictions are independent of one another, so they degrade quickly with $k$. EAGLE makes the drafting autoregressive again, but in the cheap place:

The EAGLE line

Li, Wei, Zhang, Zhang · EAGLE (ICML 2024, arXiv 2401.15077), EAGLE-2 (EMNLP 2024, arXiv 2406.16858), EAGLE-3 (NeurIPS 2025, arXiv 2503.01840)

losslesstrains a head per target2024–2025

What it tries
A drafter with the target's knowledge and the price of one layer.
How
EAGLE: a one-layer “autoregression head” predicts the target's next hidden state (second-to-top layer) from the previous states and tokens; the target's own output layer turns each predicted state into tokens. Drafts are a small tree. EAGLE-2: the tree's shape is chosen at each round from the drafter's confidence. EAGLE-3: predicts tokens directly, with a training procedure that imitates the test-time conditions.
What it achieves
Authors' tables, Vicuna-13B, greedy decoding, batch 1, MT-Bench: 3.07× (EAGLE), 4.26× (EAGLE-2), 5.58× (EAGLE-3), against 2.07× for Medusa and 1.93× for a separate small drafter in the same tables. EAGLE-3 on a 70B model: 4.12× on average.
What it costs
A head must be trained for each target model (one to two days of GPU time in the first paper). The numbers are batch-1 numbers measured by the authors; others measure less, and the gain shrinks under load (§6).

Two variations on “the target drafts for itself” avoid training: early exit (use the target's first layers as the drafter: Draft & Verify, Kangaroo, LayerSkip; reported gains 1.3× to 2.2×), and the multi-token-prediction heads that some recent models ship with.

Pitfall — Medusa's headline number is not a lossless number

Medusa's headline “2.3–2.8×” belongs to Medusa-2, which fine-tunes the target itself: the model is no longer the original one. And for sampling the paper proposes typical acceptance, the lossy rule of page 3, §6. With greedy decoding, measurements by other groups give Medusa 1.4× to 2.1×. Speed tables that put Medusa next to exact methods mix two kinds of results.

A head on the target reaches $\al=0.8$ at a cost $c=0.03$; a separate small model reaches the same $\al=0.8$ at $c=0.15$. With $\ga=5$, what are the two speed-ups?

Answer

Tokens per round: $\frac{1-0.8^6}{0.2}=3.69$ in both cases. Head: $\frac{3.69}{1+0.15}=3.21$. Small model: $\frac{3.69}{1+0.75}=2.11$. Same acceptance, half as much again in speed: this is the “overhead of the draft model” that the head removes.

3. Drafts without a model, and drafts in one pass

A draft can be looked up — in the prompt, in past outputs, in a corpus — at almost no cost. And since 2026 a draft can be produced for a whole block of tokens in a single pass, which changes the economics of drafting.

Lookups

When the output repeats text that already exists (editing code, quoting a document, an agent re-emitting earlier results), the best drafter is a search: find the last few generated tokens in a reference and propose what followed. The proposal has no probabilities, so the test is the one of page 1, Exercise 6; it is exact.

  • Prompt lookup decoding (a code repository by A. Saxena, not a paper): match the last tokens against the prompt; its author reports “2x-4x” on input-grounded tasks such as summarisation or editing.
  • REST (NAACL 2024): retrieve continuations from a large datastore of code or conversations; 1.62× to 2.36× in the paper.
  • SuffixDecoding (NeurIPS 2025): a suffix tree over the prompt and the previous outputs of the same service; up to 5.3× on repetitive agentic workloads in the paper.

On general benchmarks these give 1.2× to 2.7×. They are the variants most widely shipped (every major serving engine has an n-gram or lookup drafter), and they combine with neural drafters.

The 2026 turn: parallel block drafters

An autoregressive drafter, however small, costs $\ga$ sequential steps. In 2026 the leading exact methods replaced it with a drafter that produces a whole block of tokens in one pass, conditioned on the target's hidden states — built like a small discrete diffusion model. The acceptance per token is lower than with a careful autoregressive head, but the cost of a 16-token draft is one step, so long drafts become affordable.

DFlash and its successors

DFlash · ICML 2026 · arXiv 2602.06036 — JetSpec · 2026 · arXiv 2606.18394

losslesstrains a block drafter per target2026

What it tries
Remove the sequential cost of drafting: all the draft tokens of a round in a single forward pass.
How
A small block-diffusion drafter conditioned on the target's features fills a block of masked positions at once; the target verifies as usual. Successors restore some dependence between the tokens of the block and build trees over the block's predictions.
What it achieves
DFlash's table (Qwen3-4B, greedy, one GPU): 4.91× on average with blocks of 16, from 2.85× on chat to 6.09× on mathematics, against 1.81× to 2.08× for EAGLE-3 re-measured in the same table (two sizes of draft tree). JetSpec's table (Qwen3-8B): 9.64× on a mathematics benchmark and 4.58× on chat, against 6.12× and 2.72× for DFlash.
What it costs
A trained drafter per target. Records are on mathematics- and code-heavy suites at batch size 1, where the text is most predictable.

Note the spread inside one table: 2.85× on open-ended chat, 6.09× on mathematics, same method, same model. The acceptance rate depends on how predictable the text is, and no drafter changes that.
