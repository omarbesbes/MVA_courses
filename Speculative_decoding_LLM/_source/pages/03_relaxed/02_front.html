2. The exchange rate: one unit of acceptance costs one unit of bias

For any rule, acceptance gained beyond the exact rule is paid at least one-for-one in total variation: rejection probability + bias ≥ $\TV(p,q)$. The rules that reach equality form a straight line, the Pareto front; lenience is on it, thresholds are not.

The theorem

The exact rule rejects with probability $\TV(p,q)$ and has no bias. The opposite extreme, “accept everything”, never rejects and outputs $q$: its bias is $\TV(p,q)$. In both cases rejection + bias $=\TV(p,q)$. This is not a coincidence, and no rule can do better.

Result — the trade-off at one position

For every rule $(b,r)$, with acceptance $A$ and output distribution $\text{out}$:

$$\underbrace{(1-A)}_{\text{rejection probability}}+\underbrace{\TV(\text{out},p)}_{\text{bias}}\ \ge\ \TV(p,q),\qquad\text{that is}\qquad A\ \le\ 1-\TV(p,q)+\TV(\text{out},p).$$

Equality holds if and only if (i) the rule wastes no acceptance: $A=1-\TV(q,\text{out})$, the most any rule with this output can accept; and (ii) the output lies between the draft and the target on every token: $\min(p(v),q(v))\le\text{out}(v)\le\max(p(v),q(v))$.

Idea of the proof

Two facts you already know. First, page 1: if the output has distribution $\text{out}$ and the draft has distribution $q$, they cannot coincide more often than $1-\TV(q,\text{out})$; so the rule rejects with probability at least $\TV(q,\text{out})$. Second, total variation is a distance: going from $p$ to $q$ through $\text{out}$ is at least as long as going straight. Rejection pays for the leg from $q$ to $\text{out}$, bias is the leg from $\text{out}$ to $p$.

Full proof
  1. $$A=\P(\text{the draft is kept})\ \le\ \P(\text{output}=\text{draft})\ \le\ 1-\TV(q,\text{out}).$$When the draft is kept, the output is the draft. The draft has distribution $q$ and the output has distribution $\text{out}$: by the optimality result of page 1, §3 (applied with $\text{out}$ in the role of the target), two such tokens are equal with probability at most $1-\TV(q,\text{out})$.
  2. $$\TV(p,q)\ \le\ \TV(p,\text{out})+\TV(\text{out},q).$$Triangle inequality: for every token, $\abs{p(v)-q(v)}\le\abs{p(v)-\text{out}(v)}+\abs{\text{out}(v)-q(v)}$; sum over $v$ and halve.
  3. $$\TV(p,q)\ \le\ \TV(p,\text{out})+(1-A).$$Step 1 says $\TV(\text{out},q)\le1-A$; substitute in step 2.

Equality in step 3 needs equality in steps 1 and 2. Step 1 is condition (i). Step 2 is an equality exactly when $\abs{p(v)-q(v)}=\abs{p(v)-\text{out}(v)}+\abs{\text{out}(v)-q(v)}$ for every token, that is when $\text{out}(v)$ is between $p(v)$ and $q(v)$: condition (ii).∎

The proof suggests a second way to describe a lossy rule, often clearer than the pair $(b,r)$: name the distribution $\pi$ you are willing to output instead of $p$, then run the exact rule of page 1 with $\pi$ as the target. Accept $x$ with probability $\min\big(1,\frac{\pi(x)}{q(x)}\big)$, and after a rejection draw from $\pos{\pi-q}$ renormalised. By page 1 the output is exactly $\pi$ and the acceptance is $1-\TV(q,\pi)$, the best possible for that output: condition (i) holds by construction. All the freedom is in the choice of the surrogate target $\pi$, and the theorem says: take it between $q$ and $p$.

In the paper Narasimhan et al., “Faster Cascades via Speculative Decoding”, ICLR 2025, §4.1

“we use a user-specified target distribution $\pi=\mathbb{T}(q,p)\in\Delta_{\mathscr{V}}$ […] We accept a draft token $x_{t}$ when $q(x_{t})\leq\pi(x_{t})$ and reject it otherwise with probability $1-\frac{\pi(x_{t})}{q(x_{t})}$. Upon rejection, we re-sample from the residual distribution $\operatorname{norm}\left(\max\{0,\pi(\cdot)-q(\cdot)\}\right)$.”

This paper (the subject of §8) uses the same letters as this guide: $q$ is the small model, $p$ the large one. $\mathbb T$ is any recipe that builds a target from the two distributions. We will use this description again and again: for each method, ask what is its $\pi$?

In the paper Yin, Chen, Huang, Wang, NeurIPS 2024, Theorems 4 and 5

The paper writes $p$ for the draft and $q$ for the target (the reverse of this guide), $b$ for the acceptance probability and $\mathcal P$ for the replacement distribution. For a fixed $b$ it defines the smallest achievable bias, $\text{Loss}^*_{TV}(b):=\min_{\mathcal P}d_{TV}[\mathbb P^{\mathcal A},q]$, computes it in closed form (Theorem 4), and proves (Theorem 5): “For any Algorithm $\mathcal A$ in the class […] that satisfies $\min\{1,\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\}\le b(x)\le1,\ \forall x\in\mathcal V$. Then $\ \mathbb P^{\mathcal A}(\text{reject})+\text{Loss}^*_{TV}(b)=d_{TV}[p,q]$.” It concludes that “the pareto front is a straight line connecting $(0,d_{TV}[p,q])$ and $(d_{TV}[p,q],0)$”.

$\mathbb P^{\mathcal A}(\text{reject})$
Our rejection probability $1-A$.
$\text{Loss}^*_{TV}(b)$
The bias $\TV(\text{out},p)$ of the rule that uses acceptance $b$ and the best possible replacement distribution for it. Theorem 4 of the paper describes these best replacements: nothing on the tokens that acceptance already over-supplies, and no more than the remaining gap on the others — which is our condition (ii).
$\min\{1,q(x)/p(x)\}\le b(x)$
In our letters, $b(x)\ge\min(1,p(x)/q(x))$: the rule accepts at least as much as the exact rule, on every token. The paper proves the equality for these rules; our statement adds that no rule of any kind can be on the other side of the line.
Pareto front
The set of pairs (bias, rejection probability) that cannot be improved in one coordinate without losing in the other. Here it is the segment from “exact speculative decoding” (bias $0$, rejection $\TV(p,q)$) to “always accept the draft” (bias $\TV(p,q)$, rejection $0$).

The theorem has a plain reading. Total variation is a strict accountant: every extra draft you accept, beyond what the exact rule accepts, moves the output away from the target by at least the same amount. There is no clever test that gains $0.2$ of acceptance for $0.05$ of total variation. What a rule can do is (a) not waste — sit on the line rather than below it — and (b) choose which tokens receive the extra mass, something total variation does not see.

What total variation does not see

Two rules on the front, tuned to the same acceptance, have the same bias. They are not the same rule. In example A, tune both to $A=0.8$:

Lenience multiplies the acceptance probability of every over-proposed token by the same factor $1/\lambda$; the mixture adds the same amount $1-w$ to each. So lenience keeps a relative cap: no token comes out more than $1/\lambda$ times more often than the target wants, and a token the target forbids ($p(v)=0$) is never written. The mixture has no such cap: a token with $p(v)=0$ that the drafter proposes is accepted with probability $1-w$. Same total variation, very different behaviour on rare tokens — and rare tokens are where the mistakes are (a wrong digit, a wrong name). This is why the story does not end with the theorem: the later sections are all attempts to spend the bias on tokens that do not matter.

A rule keeps every drafted token ($b\equiv1$). Where is it in the plane, and is it on the front?

Answer

Its acceptance is 1 and its output is $q$, so its bias is $\TV(p,q)$: the point $(\TV(p,q),\,1)$, the right end of the front. It satisfies (i) trivially and (ii) because nothing is ever written after a rejection. It is “optimal” only in the accountant's sense: it is also the small model alone, with the large model paid for and ignored.
