What you will be able to do after this page
  1. Describe any token-level lossy rule in two equivalent ways — the pair (acceptance probability, replacement distribution), or the surrogate target it really samples from — and compute its acceptance and its bias on a small vocabulary.
  2. State and prove the exchange rate: rejection probability + bias ≥ $\TV(p,q)$, and say which rules reach equality.
  3. Explain lenience, its cap $p(x)/\lambda$, what its authors did and did not measure.
  4. Read the deterministic rules used with greedy decoding — top-$k$, margins, BiLD's rollback, typical acceptance — as “sets of acceptable tokens”, and compare those sets.
  5. Explain what mentored decoding and CACTUS optimise, and why a guarantee in Kullback–Leibler divergence protects rare tokens better than one in total variation.
  6. Explain the rules that decide per position which model to trust: fuzzy speculative decoding and speculative cascades, and the mixture targets.
  7. Summarise what the independent evaluations of 2026 found, and why the family leads naturally to the judges of page 4.

0. Before you start

How to read this page

You need page 1: the accept / resample rule, the overlap $\sum_v\min(p(v),q(v))=1-\TV(p,q)$, and the fact that no exact rule accepts more (§3 there). Page 2 is not required.

§1 and §2 are the theory: short, and worth doing with a pencil, because every later section is read through them. §3 to §9 are the methods, in the order in which the ideas appeared. Each method opens with a card:

The numbers reported by the authors, with the models, the task and the baseline. They are the authors' numbers, in the authors' setting.
What it costs
What is lost or required, including what later papers found.

Then come the statement of the paper (quoted and decoded), a toy example, and what was measured. §10 is different: it reports the head-to-head evaluations published in 2026, where the same rules are compared by people who did not invent them.

Pitfall — do not rank methods by their own papers' numbers

A speed-up of 2× in one paper and 1.5× in another say nothing about which method is better: the models, the tasks, the hardware, the temperature and the baseline differ, and so does the meaning of “speed-up” (over the target alone, over exact speculative decoding, at matched quality…). In this guide numbers from different papers are never put side by side in a chart. When a comparison exists inside one paper or one benchmark, we give it and say so.

The story of the page in seven steps

Accepting more than the exact rule moves the output away from the target, one for one in total variation; the whole question is on which tokens the difference falls.

  1. A language for lossy rules (§1). A rule is how often you accept and what you write otherwise. That fixes the output distribution, hence an acceptance and a bias.
  2. The exchange rate (§2). Rejection probability + bias ≥ $\TV(p,q)$. The best rules trade one for the other at par; many trade worse. A rule is best described by the surrogate target $\pi$ it samples from.
  3. Lenience (§3). The first relaxed rule for sampling: at par, with a cap on every token's probability, and published without any measurement of quality.
  4. Sets of acceptable tokens (§4–§6). With greedy decoding, relaxing means accepting tokens that are not the target's first choice: its top $k$, those within a margin of the best, those above a probability threshold that adapts to the entropy (typical acceptance).
  5. Guarantees that protect rare tokens (§7). Mentored decoding and CACTUS maximise acceptance under a bound on a Kullback–Leibler divergence: the output is the draft distribution clipped into a band around the target.
  6. Choosing whom to trust, position by position (§8–§9). Fuzzy speculative decoding takes the draft whenever the two distributions are close; speculative cascades take it when the small model looks at least as reliable; mixtures blend the two.
  7. The reality check (§10). Independent evaluations in 2026: the conservative rules keep accuracy and gain little, the aggressive ones gain acceptance and lose accuracy, especially with weak drafters and hard tasks. A formula on $p$ and $q$ does not know which tokens matter.

Notation

The notation of page 1, plus:

SymbolMeaning
$b(x)$Probability of accepting the drafted token $x$ (a number in $[0,1]$; $0$ or $1$ for a deterministic rule).
$r$Replacement distribution: what the output is drawn from after a rejection.
$A=\sum_vq(v)b(v)$Acceptance of the rule at this position (the exact rule has $A=1-\TV(p,q)$).
$\text{out}$, $\pi$Distribution of the output token. $\pi$ is used when the rule is defined by the distribution it aims at (the surrogate target).
bias$\TV(\text{out},p)$, unless another divergence is named.
$\lambda$Lenience factor in $(0,1]$ ($l$ in the original paper; $\lambda=1$ is exact).
$\KL(a\,\|\,b)$Kullback–Leibler divergence $\sum_va(v)\log\frac{a(v)}{b(v)}$ (natural logarithm).
$\Hent(p)$Entropy $-\sum_vp(v)\log p(v)$, in nats.

Papers that swap the letters. Yin et al. (§2), the mentored decoding post and CACTUS (§7) write $p$ for the draft and $q$ for the target. Everything below is translated to the convention of this guide: $p$ target, $q$ draft.

Colour code. As on page 1, plus amber for what only a relaxed rule accepts: accepted by the exact rule accepted by the relaxed rule only rejected.
