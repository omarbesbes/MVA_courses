7. Guarantees that protect rare tokens: mentored decoding and CACTUS

Fix a budget in Kullback–Leibler divergence and accept as much as the budget allows. The solution keeps the output inside a band around the target, from above and from below (mentored decoding), or lifts only the drafted token as far as the budget permits (CACTUS).

Why change the yardstick

§2 ended on a blind spot: total variation counts how much probability moves, not where it lands. A Kullback–Leibler divergence does look at where. $\KL(p\,\|\,\pi)=\sum_vp(v)\log\frac{p(v)}{\pi(v)}$ becomes large when the output $\pi$ starves a token the target wants ($\pi(v)\ll p(v)$): it protects the target's tokens from disappearing. In the other direction, $\KL(\pi\,\|\,p)=\sum_v\pi(v)\log\frac{\pi(v)}{p(v)}$ becomes large when the output inflates a token the target finds unlikely ($\pi(v)\gg p(v)$): it protects against rare tokens becoming frequent. Mentored decoding constrains the first, CACTUS the second.

Mentored decoding

Mentored decoding

V. Tran-Thien · blog post “An Optimal Lossy Variant of Speculative Decoding”, September 2023, with a written proof and code · post (not peer-reviewed)

lossytraining-freetoken level2023

What it tries
Solve the problem exactly: among all rules $(b,r)$, maximise the probability of accepting the draft subject to $\KL(p\,\|\,\text{out})\le D$ at each position.
How it decides
The optimum is a lenient test, $\min\big(1,\frac{p(x)}{\alpha\,q(x)}\big)$, with a factor $\alpha$ found for each position by binary search so that the divergence equals $D$, and a flattened residual proportional to $\pos{p/\beta-q}$. Positions where $\KL(p\,\|\,q)\le D$ accept the draft outright.
What it achieves
A guarantee at every position, for every token: $\frac{p(v)}{\beta}\le\text{out}(v)\le\frac{p(v)}{\alpha}$, and $\KL(p\,\|\,\text{out})\le D$. One small experiment (T5-large with T5-small, 1000 translation examples, one V100), reported as a chart: more tokens per second than exact speculative decoding; BLEU not significantly different for the smallest $D$, lower as $D$ grows.
What it costs
A search per position. The author asks for “extensive experiments”; later comparisons (below) mostly evaluate a simplified version, which is plain lenience.
In the post Tran-Thien 2023; the post writes $p$ for the draft and $q$ for the target

“we want to find the values of $(r_i)_{i \in V}$ or $(s_i)_{i \in V}$ that maximally increase the probability to accept $i$ while maintaining the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the resulting distribution and the target distribution under a certain constant $D$.” The solution: “$r_i = \min(1, \frac{q_i}{\alpha p_i})$ for a certain $\alpha$; $s_i = \frac{1}{1-\sum_jp_jr_j}\max(0, \frac{q_i}{\beta}-p_i)$ for a certain $\beta$”.

$r_i$, $s_i$
Our acceptance probability $b$ and replacement distribution $r$ (the letters of the post are the other way round, like its $p$ and $q$).
the constraint
In our letters $\KL(p\,\|\,\text{out})\le D$: the divergence from the target to the output, the one that punishes starving the target's tokens.
$\alpha$ and $\beta$
Two numbers, $\alpha\le1\le\beta$, tied to each other by the fact that what is rejected must equal what is refilled: $\sum_v\pos{q(v)-p(v)/\alpha}=\sum_v\pos{p(v)/\beta-q(v)}$. Choosing $D$ fixes both.

Put the acceptance and the residual together and the output has a striking form: it is the drafter's distribution clipped into a band around the target,

$$\text{out}(v)=\min\Big(\max\Big(q(v),\ \frac{p(v)}{\beta}\Big),\ \frac{p(v)}{\alpha}\Big).$$

Where the drafter is within the band, the output is the drafter's probability; where it over-proposes, the output is capped at $p/\alpha$ (lenience's cap); where it under-proposes, the output is raised to $p/\beta$. The lower edge is precisely the guarantee that lenience lacks (“there is no guarantee on the minimum probability”).

Both rules are on the front of §2. Lenience refills the two starved tokens in proportion to their gaps, and leaves the first one at $0.25$ for a target of $0.4$. Mentored decoding sends almost all the refill to that token, so that no token is below $p/1.4$. Total variation cannot tell the two apart; the Kullback–Leibler divergence can.

What later papers did with it. Narasimhan et al. (§8) prove that their framework contains mentored decoding (the surrogate target is the clipped distribution above) and compare the full rule with its simplification $\beta=1$: on their translation and summarisation tasks, “setting $\beta=1$ provides trade-offs comparable to or better than using a tuned value of $\beta$”, perhaps, they say, because they measure BLEU and ROUGE and not the divergence that the rule optimises. The 2026 benchmarks of §10 also use the $\beta=1$ version with one global factor — which is the lenience of §3 under another name. Keep this in mind when you read “mentored decoding” in a table.

CACTUS

Cactus — constrained acceptance speculative sampling

Y. Hao, L. Mou · ICLR 2026 · arXiv 2604.04987

lossytraining-freetoken level2026

What it tries
Replace the heuristic of typical acceptance by an optimisation with a hard constraint: accept the drafted token as often as possible while the distribution sampled from stays within a divergence $\delta$ of the verifier's.
How it decides
For the drafted token $x$, lift its target probability to $\gamma^*=\min\big(p(x)+\sqrt{2\delta\,p(x)(1-p(x))},\,1\big)$, scale the other tokens down, and run the exact test against that: accept with probability $\min\big(1,\frac{\gamma^*}{q(x)}\big)$. Recommended $\delta=1$.
What it achieves
Qwen3-8B and 14B verifiers with a 0.6B drafter, sampling (temperature 0.6, top-$p$ 0.95, top-$k$ 20), drafts of 20 tokens, $\delta=1$: rejected tokens down by 30% to 50% relative to exact speculative sampling; tokens accepted per round 5.44 → 7.61 (GSM8K, 8B). Scores: GSM8K 84.46 → 86.43 (8B) and 91.89 → 92.87 (14B); IFEval 84.10 → 84.84 and 84.47 → 86.32; GPQA 42.93 → 39.90 (8B) but 40.91 → 45.46 (14B). The paper reports “nearly 1.9× speedup over the verifier alone” for the 14B pair with drafts of 10.
What it costs
The bound is per drafted token; for the distribution actually sampled the paper proves a bound through a function with no closed form. With $\delta=1$ the lift is large for rare tokens (example below). Independent 2026 benchmarks class it among the aggressive rules (§10).

The construction is the surrogate-target idea of §2, with a twist: the surrogate depends on which token was drafted. Given the drafted token $x$, CACTUS asks for the distribution $h$ that gives $x$ as much probability as possible under the constraint $\KL(h\,\|\,p)\le\delta$ (the paper's theory allows any $f$-divergence and its experiments use this one). The answer raises $h(x)$ to $\gamma^*$ and shrinks all the other tokens by the same factor; the paper then notes that the exact test toward $h$ is the best way to sample it (its Observation 1, our condition (i)).

The numbers of the card deserve a careful look. On GSM8K and IFEval the scores do not move outside the noise. On GPQA they move in both directions, by more than the run-to-run standard deviation the paper reports for the verifier alone (0.8 to 1.8 points): down three points with one verifier, up four and a half with the other. This is the usual picture for a relaxed rule on a hard benchmark: the effect is not small, and it is not reliably of one sign.

Mentored decoding accepts the draft outright at every position where $\KL(p\,\|\,q)\le D$. What is the output distribution at such a position, and which rule of §8 does this resemble?

Answer

The output is $q$ itself: accepting every draft means sampling from the drafter, which is allowed because $q$ already satisfies the constraint. At those positions the rule is “the two distributions are close enough, take the draft”: exactly fuzzy speculative decoding (§8), which makes this test with a Jensen–Shannon divergence. The difference is at the other positions: mentored decoding still relaxes as far as the budget allows; fuzzy speculative decoding falls back to the target.
