1. Two questions that sort every method

Ask of any method: what is the unit that gets accepted or rejected, and is the large model still run on the accepted units? The first answer says how coarse the judgment is; the second says whether compute is saved or only time.

Why leave the token

Token-level speculation has a ceiling that is easy to state with the law of page 1. The speed-up is $\frac{1-\al^{\ga+1}}{(1-\al)(\ga c+1)}$, and whatever the draft length and however cheap the drafter, it is below $\frac{1}{1-\al}$: a draft is cut at its first rejected token, and the chance that $\ga$ tokens in a row all pass falls like $\al^\ga$.

In the paper Fu et al., “Scaling Speculative Decoding with Lookahead Reasoning”, NeurIPS 2025, abstract and §2

“Token-level speculative decoding (SD) helps, but its benefit is capped, because the chance that an entire $\gamma$-token guess is correct falls exponentially as $\gamma$ grows. This means allocating more compute for longer token drafts faces an algorithmic ceiling – making the speedup modest and hardware-agnostic.”

With $\al=0.8$ the ceiling is 5; in practice, with a real drafter cost, two to three. Pages 3 and 4 raised $\al$ by accepting more tokens. This page takes another road. A reasoning model writes its solution as a sequence of steps — a line of algebra, a case of an analysis, a sentence of a plan. Two different wordings of the same step differ in most of their tokens, so token-level acceptance is low; yet as steps they are interchangeable. If the unit of acceptance is the step, a whole step can be accepted at once, however its tokens are spelled.

Speculation or cascade? The second question

Changing the unit raises a question that did not arise on the previous pages. To accept a drafted token, the large model must have computed its distribution at that position: it reads the draft, in parallel. To accept a drafted step, one can use something else entirely — a reward model, a learned router — and the large model need not look at the step at all. Then the method is no longer a speculative decoder but a cascade: a system that lets the small model answer and calls the large one only when a rule says so.

In the paper Narasimhan et al., “Faster Cascades via Speculative Decoding”, ICLR 2025, abstract

“cascades offer better cost-quality trade-offs, often even outperforming the large model, while theoretically, speculative decoding offers a guarantee of quality-neutrality”.

The two mechanisms buy different things:

Speculative side: the large model reads everything

The large model processes every drafted unit, in parallel, to verify or to compare. Latency goes down (fewer sequential passes of the large model). Compute does not: it goes up, as on page 1. The output can stay close to the large model's own.

Cascade side: the large model is skipped

On accepted units the large model is not run (or only later, cheaply, to catch up). Compute goes down, and usually latency with it. But nothing ties the accepted text to the large model: its quality is that of the small model, filtered by the judge.

Reflex

When a paper reports “× fewer FLOPs” → it is on the cascade side; ask for the wall-clock time and for what guarantees quality. When it reports “× faster” with the large model verifying → it is on the speculative side; ask how much extra compute it spends.

A method lets a small model write a paragraph, then asks the large model “is this paragraph correct? answer yes or no”, and keeps it if the answer is yes. Which side is it on?

Answer

Between the two, and closer to the speculative side for the cost: the large model does read every drafted token (to answer, it must process the paragraph), so its compute on input is paid; only the token-by-token generation of the paragraph is saved, which is where the latency is. For quality it is on the cascade side: the kept paragraph is the small model's, vouched for by a one-token opinion. This is SpecReason (§3).
