0. How to read this guide

For whom. You know what a language model, a token and a probability distribution are. Nothing about speculative decoding is assumed. The mathematics needed is sums of probabilities; every symbol is explained where it appears.

In which order. Pages 1, 3, 4 and 5 are the course. Pages 2 and 6 are maps: what exists on the exact side, and how all this is used. With one hour: page 1, §3 (the rule and its proof), page 3, §2 (the trade-off), page 3, §10 (what independent evaluations found), and page 6, §5 (how to choose).

What the pages are made of.

  • Each section opens with one sentence that states its result. Read those alone for a first pass.
  • Each method has a card with four lines: what it tries, how it decides, what it achieves, what it costs.
  • Boxes titled “In the paper” quote the source, then a list decodes the quotation term by term.
  • Small examples can be checked by hand. Interactive figures let you move the quantities; each has a line “What to look at”.
  • Proofs, hints and solutions are folded; the buttons at the top open them all.
Pitfall — the numbers

A speed-up or a score is the number reported by a paper, in that paper's setting: its models, its tasks, its hardware, its baseline. Two numbers from two papers are not comparable, and this guide never ranks methods by them. When a later study measured several methods under the same conditions, the guide says so and gives that result separately. The state of the art described is that of November 2026.

1. The idea in five minutes

Writing is sequential and slow; reading is parallel and cheap. Let something cheap write, and let the large model read. An exact test keeps the output distribution unchanged; a lenient test keeps more of the draft and changes it.

  1. The asymmetry. A large model writes one token per pass, and each pass takes about the same time whether it scores one position or twenty. Scoring a text that already exists is therefore much cheaper than writing it. (page 1, §1)
  2. Draft, then verify. A drafter (a small model, a few extra layers, a lookup) proposes the next $\ga$ tokens. The large model, the target, scores them all in one pass: at each position it now knows its own distribution $p$, to compare with the drafter's $q$.
  3. The exact rule. Keep a drafted token $x$ with probability $\min\!\big(1,\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\big)$; at the first rejection, draw a replacement from the normalised positive part of $p-q$, and start a new round. The output is then distributed exactly as if the target had written alone. (page 1, §3, with the proof)
  4. The law of the speed-up. If a drafted token is accepted with probability $\al$ and one drafter call costs $c$ target calls, the speed-up is $\dfrac{1-\al^{\ga+1}}{(1-\al)(\ga c+1)}$. And $\al$ cannot exceed the overlap of the two distributions, $1-\TV(p,q)$. (page 1, §5)
  5. The exact frontier. Better drafters, trees of candidates, smarter verification: 3× to 5× for one request at a time in the papers of 2024–2026, 1.1× to 2× on a busy server. (page 2)
  6. The lossy step. To keep more of the draft than the overlap allows, change the test. The output distribution then moves away from the target's, by at least as much as the acceptance gained. (page 3, §2)

What to look at. One position, three possible tokens. The target (blue) prefers “Paris”; the drafter (orange) prefers “the”. At $\lambda=1$ the test is the exact rule: the draft is accepted 70% of the time and what comes out (amber) is the blue distribution. Lower $\lambda$: the test becomes lenient, the acceptance rises, more tokens come out of each call of the target, and the amber bars slide from the blue ones toward the orange ones. Read the third line: rejection plus distance stays equal to 0.30, the distance between the two models. Each point of acceptance was bought with a point of distance. No rule can do better at one position; the methods of this guide differ in where they let the distance fall.

That last sentence is the plan of the lossy pages:

  • Rules that read only the two distributions (page 3): lenience, accept sets, bounded divergences, mixtures. Cheap and general; blind to which tokens matter.
  • Judges (page 4): a classifier, trained or not, decides whether a mismatch is harmless. Longer accepted drafts; no guarantee, and a judge is only as good as its training.
  • Other units, other contracts (page 5): accept a whole reasoning step, or let the small model write and call the large one only where it matters. Some of these save latency, others save compute, and the second kind leaves the small model's text in the answer.
  • Other outputs (page 6, §1): images, speech and robot actions, where many tokens are nearly equivalent.
