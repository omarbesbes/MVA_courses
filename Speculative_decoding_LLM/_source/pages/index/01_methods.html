3. The lossy methods in one table

More than twenty approaches, four families. For each: what it tries, how it decides, what it achieves as reported by its authors in their own setting, and its main cost. The links lead to the full cards.

Read the rows, do not compare the “achieves” column from one row to the next: the models, tasks, hardware and baselines differ. Where an independent study exists, the last column says what it found.

4. Ten things the guide establishes

  1. The exact rule keeps the target's distribution, by a proof of three lines, and no exact rule rejects less. Its acceptance is the overlap $1-\TV(p,q)$.
  2. Exact methods progressed through the drafter, not the test: from about 2× to about 5× at batch 1 between 2023 and 2026.
  3. Those gains shrink under load, because verification spends capacity that is idle only on a quiet server.
  4. A lossy rule is an exact test against another distribution. Naming that distribution is the first step in understanding any method.
  5. At one position, a point of acceptance costs at least a point of total variation. Good rules pay exactly that; none pays less.
  6. What the total variation costs in quality depends on which tokens it falls on. Rules that cap how often a token can be over-produced hold up best in independent comparisons.
  7. Judges accept much longer drafts. They carry no guarantee, do not transfer across tasks, and their errors compound over a long answer.
  8. “Speculative” covers two different contracts. If the large model still processes what is accepted, latency is saved. If it does not, compute is saved and the small model's text is in the answer.
  9. Accepted tokens are not speed: a relaxed rule can lengthen the answers by more than it speeds up the rounds. Safety behaviour degrades before accuracy does.
  10. Exactness has a value of its own: an exact method inherits every evaluation of the target. A lossy method starts them again.

5. Notation and conventions

symbolmeaning
$p(x)$the target model's probability of the next token $x$ (the large model whose output is wanted)
$q(x)$the drafter's probability of $x$ (several papers swap the two letters; the guide always converts)
$\ga$the number of tokens drafted before each verification
$\al$the acceptance rate: the probability that a drafted token is kept
$c$the cost of one drafter call, in target calls
$\TV(p,q)$total variation distance, $\frac12\sum_x\abs{p(x)-q(x)}=1-\sum_x\min(p(x),q(x))$
outthe distribution of the token that a method actually writes

Colour code of the figures. target   drafter   accepted   relaxed, lossy   correction after a rejection   rejected. The full table of notation is on page 1.

Words. Exact and lossless are used as synonyms: the output follows the target's distribution (with greedy decoding: it is the target's text). Lossy and relaxed: it does not.

6. Where the content comes from

Each page ends with its sources.
