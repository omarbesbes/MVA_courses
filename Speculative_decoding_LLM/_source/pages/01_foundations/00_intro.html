What you will be able to do after this page
  1. Say where the time goes when a language model generates text, and why checking $\ga$ proposed tokens costs about as much as writing one.
  2. Run one round of speculative decoding by hand, with greedy decoding and with sampling.
  3. State the accept / resample rule and prove that its output follows exactly the distribution of the large model.
  4. Compute an acceptance rate, $\be=\sum_x\min(p(x),q(x))=1-\TV(p,q)$, and prove that no exact rule accepts more often.
  5. Derive the number of tokens produced per call of the large model, $\frac{1-\al^{\ga+1}}{1-\al}$, and the speed-up law; find the best draft length.
  6. Explain what the law hides (total compute, batch size, the latency of the drafter) and why exactness puts a ceiling on the speed-up — the reason lossy methods exist.

The story of the page in seven steps

A large model writes slowly but checks quickly; so a small model writes, the large one checks, and a well-chosen coin flip makes the result exactly the large model's own.

  1. The cost (§1). A language model produces one token per forward pass. The time to generate a text is the number of tokens times the time of a pass.
  2. The asymmetry (§1). One forward pass over a sequence returns a next-token distribution at every position. Scoring several proposed tokens is one pass; writing them is several.
  3. Guess and check (§2). A small, fast model writes a draft of $\ga$ tokens. The large model reads it in one pass and keeps the longest prefix it agrees with, plus one token of its own.
  4. The coin flip (§3). With sampling, “agrees” becomes: keep the drafted token $x$ with probability $\min(1,p(x)/q(x))$; otherwise draw a replacement from what $p$ has in excess of $q$. The output is an exact sample of the large model.
  5. The overlap (§3). A drafted token is kept with probability $\sum_x\min(p(x),q(x))$: the overlap of the two distributions, one minus their total variation. No exact rule can keep more.
  6. The speed-up (§4–§5). With an acceptance rate $\al$ and a drafter that costs a fraction $c$ of the large model, a round yields $\frac{1-\al^{\ga+1}}{1-\al}$ tokens for a time $\ga c+1$.
  7. The ceiling (§6). Exactness caps the acceptance rate at the overlap. To go faster one improves the draft (page 2) or stops asking for exactness (pages 3, 4 and 5).

What you need

  • A discrete probability distribution on a finite set (here the vocabulary), and what “draw $x$ from $p$” means.
  • Conditional probabilities and the chain rule $\P(x_1,\dots,x_n)=\prod_t\P(x_t\mid x_1,\dots,x_{t-1})$.
  • The sum of a geometric progression, $1+\al+\dots+\al^{\ga}=\frac{1-\al^{\ga+1}}{1-\al}$.
  • Total variation between two distributions. It is recalled in §3; if you followed the optimal transport course of this repository, you have already met it, with its transport interpretation, in Lesson 3, §5 — and you will see that speculative sampling is the optimal plan computed there.

Notation

The guide uses the notation of Leviathan, Kalman and Matias (2023). Beware when you open other papers: several of them swap the letters (the DeepMind paper of Chen et al. calls the target $q$ and the draft $p$).

SymbolMeaning
$\V$The vocabulary: the finite set of tokens (tens of thousands of them in a real model, 3 to 6 in our examples).
$x_{The prefix: the prompt and all the tokens generated so far.

$\ga$The number of tokens drafted before each check (draft length, lookahead).
$\be$The probability that a drafted token is accepted, for a given prefix. $\be$ depends on the prefix through $p$ and $q$.
$\al$The average of $\be$ over prefixes: the acceptance rate.
$c$The cost coefficient: time of one call of $\Mq$ divided by the time of one call of $\Mp$ (for instance $0.05$).

$\TV(p,q)$The total variation distance, $\frac12\sum_x\abs{p(x)-q(x)}$, a number between $0$ (same distribution) and $1$ (disjoint supports).
