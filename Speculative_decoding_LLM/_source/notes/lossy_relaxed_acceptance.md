# Lossy speculative decoding by relaxed token-level acceptance (statistical rules and thresholds, no learned judge) and the theory of the speed versus fidelity trade-off — state as of 22 November 2026

Conventions used in every section. **Common notation**: p = target (verifier) next-token distribution, q = draft distribution, x = drafted token, u ~ U(0,1); "lossless SD" = accept with probability min(1, p(x)/q(x)), on rejection resample from norm(max(0, p − q)). Several papers use the opposite letters (p = draft, q = target): the mentored decoding blog, Yin et al. 2024, CACTUS, LANTERN. Formulas are first given in the paper's own notation, then translated. **VERIFIED** = read in the primary source during this session (arXiv HTML/PDF, official repository file, official documentation), with a verbatim quote or a table/figure reference; **UNVERIFIED** = taken from a secondary source or a search-engine summary. "Not found" = looked for and not present in the passages read. All arXiv HTML pages were downloaded and searched locally on 22 Nov 2026; quotes keep the LaTeX of the arXiv HTML rendering. Scope boundary: learned judge classifiers (Judge Decoding, AutoJudge, SelfJudge, the learned part of DIVERSED) and step-level reasoning methods (RSD, SpecReason) are not covered; they appear only where a covered paper compares against them.

## 1. Lenience in Leviathan et al. 2023 ("Fast Inference from Transformers via Speculative Decoding")

### Takeaway
Lenience is a one-paragraph idea in Appendix A.5 only: multiply q(x) by a factor l ∈ [0,1] before the acceptance comparison, i.e. accept with probability min(1, p(x)/(l·q(x))); the only stated guarantee is an upper bound (no token is sampled with probability above p(x)/l), with an explicit warning that there is no lower bound. The paper reports acceptance rates and improvement factors (2.5X at l=1 up to 5X at l=0.1 for T5-small drafting T5-XXL on EnDe) but no quality measurement at all for lenient decoding.

### Inferences
- The greedy lenience condition is printed as "$p(x)\leq l\cdot max(p)$"; with l ≤ 1 this would accept only low-probability tokens, so it is almost certainly a typo for p(x) ≥ l·max(p) (accept x if its target probability is at least a fraction l of the top probability). The corrected form is exactly the "multiplicative relaxation" later used by SpecCascade TokenV3 (threshold 1−α), AdaptiveSpec (κ) and analysed by Sharma 2026; flag the typo rather than quoting the inequality as a rule.
- The greedy improvement factors (3.3X, 3.3X, 3.9X, 4.9X) are reproduced exactly by plugging α = 0.75, 0.75, 0.8, 0.87, γ = 8, c = 0.015 into Theorem 3.8 (my recomputation: 3.30, 3.30, 3.87, 4.91), so they are model-based expected factors, not measured walltimes. The T=1 factors (2.5X–5X) are 4–7% above what the same formula gives at its best γ with the rounded α of Table 5 (2.33, 2.94, 3.45, 4.79); the paper does not say how they were obtained. A writer should present all lenience speed figures as "expected improvement factors", not as measured speedups.
- Translating into a fidelity statement (my derivation, consistent with Eq. 10 of arXiv:2607.26627 and with Theorem 5 of Yin et al.): with acceptance min(1, p/(l q)) and the unchanged residual, P(reject) = Σ_x max(0, q(x) − p(x)/l) and TV(output, p) = Σ_x max(0, min(q(x), p(x)/l) − p(x)) = TV(p,q) − P(reject). So every unit of rejection probability removed by lenience is paid one-for-one in total variation; l → 0 gives output = q (pure draft model).
- The Appendix does not say whether the residual distribution is changed under lenience; all later users (DistillSpec Algorithm A.1, Narasimhan et al. with β=1, Hugging Face and vLLM-style implementations) keep norm(max(0, p − q)). Mentored decoding (Section 6) is the variant that changes the residual.

### Gaps
- No task-quality number of any kind (BLEU, ROUGE) is reported for lenient decoding in the paper; the only quality-related statement is the p(x)/l bound.
- γ used for the T=1 lenience improvement factors: not found. Whether those factors were measured or computed: not stated.
- No official code release was found in the paper.

## 2. DistillSpec (Zhou et al., ICLR 2024), lossy part: lenience functions and the quality versus latency trade-off

### Takeaway
DistillSpec generalises Leviathan's lenience to a "lenience function" f(p, ε) with acceptance min(1, f(p(y_t), ε)/q(y_t)) and studies three choices, f_lin = p/ε, f_sq = p/ε², f_exp = p^ε (ε = 1 lossless, ε < 1 lossy), keeping the standard residual; results are shown only as trade-off curves (Figure 5b and Appendix C.3.1), with the qualitative finding that f_lin/f_sq give the better curve, f_exp a worse one, and that a distilled draft gives a higher and flatter curve. The "6–10×" claim is not a lossy-SD result: it comes from distilling the target and then using a distilled draft across a T5 "model garden" (6.4× on XSum, 10.7× on GSM8K).

### Inferences
- Correction to the brief: the 6–10× figure does not "combine lossy speculative decoding with a model garden". The four model-garden configurations (raw, distilled, speculative, DistillSpec) contain no lenience; the gain comes from swapping the fine-tuned target for a smaller distilled target of equal quality and then running lossless SD with a distilled draft. It is "lossy" only in the sense that the final system no longer samples from the original large model.
- In common notation the three rules are: accept with probability min(1, p(x)/(ε q(x))), min(1, p(x)/(ε² q(x))), min(1, p(x)^ε / q(x)). f_lin and f_sq are the same family (a constant multiplier on p); f_sq with ε is f_lin with ε². Only f_lin/f_sq inherit Leviathan's bound P_out(x) ≤ p(x)/l (with l = ε or ε²). f_exp raises small target probabilities much more than large ones (p^ε → 1 as ε → 0 for every token with p > 0), so it has no multiplicative cap, which is a plausible reason for its worse curve; the paper itself gives no explanation.
- Because the residual is left as norm(max(0, p − q)), the output distribution of f_lin is the one given in Section 1 (Eq. 10 of arXiv:2607.26627).

### Gaps
- No numeric quality/latency pairs are printed for the lossy curves (figures only); the ε grid, the model pair of Figure 5(b) (presumably T5-XL/T5-Small, not stated in the caption) and the sampling temperature of Figure 6 were not found in the text read.
- No bound or characterisation of the output distribution for any lenience function.

## 3. Early relaxed verification: Spec-Verification in SpecDec (Xia et al., arXiv 2203.16487) and the approximate acceptance of Stern et al. 2018

### Takeaway
Both pre-LLM "draft-then-verify" papers relax a greedy exact-match test with a deterministic rule on the target's ranking. Stern et al. (2018) accept a proposal if it is in the target's top-k (or within distance ε of the argmax for pixel intensities); top-2/top-3 raises the mean accepted block size from 4.95 to 5.67/5.91 at k=10 but costs 0.6–0.9 BLEU. SpecDec (2022–2023) accepts a drafted token if it is in the target's top-β and its log-likelihood gap to the top-1 is at most τ; with β=3, τ=1.0 it raises mean accepted tokens from 6.41 to 7.89 and BLEU from 26.62 to 26.97 on the WMT14 EN→DE dev set, and the paper reports about 5× speedup with BLEU on par with beam search; over-relaxing (β=5, τ=5) reaches 11.01 tokens (6.8×) with BLEU falling to 26.58.

### Inferences
- Common-notation forms. Stern: accept x iff x ∈ top-k(p) (or |x − argmax p| ≤ ε). SpecDec: accept x iff rank_p(x) ≤ β and log(max_v p(v)) − log p(x) ≤ τ, i.e. p(x) ≥ e^{−τ}·max_v p(v) (τ = 1.0 means p(x) ≥ 0.368·max p) plus a top-3 cap. SpecDec's rule is therefore the conjunction of a top-k test and a multiplicative-margin test, the same shape as Leviathan's greedy lenience, SpecCascade TokenV3, AdaptiveSpec's margin rule and TensorRT-LLM's "relaxed_topk + relaxed_delta".
- Both are deterministic rules defined relative to greedy decoding; the "output distribution" is a point mass on a sequence that may differ from the greedy one, and neither paper characterises it. The quality evidence is BLEU only, on small supervised translation/summarisation Transformers with a specially trained, sequence-distilled drafter, so the "quality improves under mild relaxation" finding should not be transferred to general LLM drafters without the caveats of Section 13.
- Stern's own data show the classic pattern: on a fixed model, top-2/top-3 acceptance buys 15–19% more accepted tokens at k=10 for 0.6–0.9 BLEU, while SpecDec's "τ=1, top-3" buys 23% more tokens with +0.35 BLEU; the difference is that SpecDec adds the log-likelihood gap, which prevents accepting a rank-2/3 token when the target is confident.

### Gaps
- SpecDec: batch size used for the latency numbers and the venue pages (Findings of EMNLP 2023) were not opened; the venue comes from the arXiv comment field.
- Neither paper reports results under sampling (temperature > 0).

## 4. BiLD — Kim et al., "Speculative Decoding with Big Little Decoder" (NeurIPS 2023)

### Takeaway
BiLD is a deterministic, threshold-based lossy scheme with two knobs: the small model keeps generating until its own maximum probability falls below a fallback threshold α_FB, and the large model then rolls back to the first position whose distance between small and large predictions exceeds a rollback threshold α_RB, where the distance is the cross-entropy between the small model's hard label and the large model's soft label, i.e. the negative log-probability the large model assigns to the small model's token. On a T4 GPU with (m)T5-large/small it reports about 1.5× speedup with no quality loss and up to 2.12× within about 1 BLEU/ROUGE-L point, and claims a better trade-off than rejection-sampling SD; no guarantee on the output distribution is given.

### Inferences
- Common-notation rule: draft with q until max_v q(v) < α_FB (or 10 tokens); then accept each drafted token x iff −log p(x) ≤ α_RB, i.e. p(x) ≥ e^{−α_RB}; at the first violation replace the token by the large model's own choice. With the thresholds used, α_RB ∈ [1, 10] corresponds to a floor on the target probability between e^{−1} ≈ 0.37 and e^{−10} ≈ 4.5·10^{−5}; summarisation thresholds [2, 6] correspond to 0.135 down to 0.0025. This is a fixed absolute threshold on p(x), the simplest member of the "target-probability threshold" family (typical acceptance replaces the constant by min(ε, δ·exp(−H(p)))).
- Two separate sources of loss: accepted tokens with p(x) above the floor but different from what the large model would produce, and blocks that end without any verification because the fallback is never triggered (the caveat quoted from Narasimhan et al.).
- The reported gain over rejection-sampling SD comes mainly from far fewer rollbacks (1.56% versus 9.81% on IWSLT), i.e. from determinism of the test plus an adaptive draft length, not from a better drafter.

- The exact (α_FB, α_RB) pairs behind each row of Table 1 are not printed (sweeps are shown as curves in Figure 4 and Appendix 7.5.3).
- No experiments on decoder-only LLMs in the paper; later LLM-scale numbers for BiLD come only from other papers' re-implementations.

## 5. Typical acceptance in Medusa (Cai et al., ICML 2024) and its implementation in serving stacks (vLLM)

### Takeaway
Typical acceptance replaces the stochastic test by a deterministic threshold on the target's own probability of the candidate: accept iff p(x) > min(ε, δ·exp(−H(p))), with the first token of each step decoded greedily and always accepted. The paper motivates it by the fact that rejection sampling loses efficiency as temperature rises, sweeps ε from 0.01 to 0.25 with δ = √ε and shows the speed/quality trade-off only as a plot (Figure 5); the reference code defaults are posterior_threshold = 0.09 and posterior_alpha = 0.3. vLLM shipped the rule as `TypicalAcceptanceSampler` from v0.5.1 to v0.9.2 with the same defaults; the file is gone from v0.10.0 onwards (V0 speculative workers removed in July 2025) and the current vLLM exposes a different lossy verifier, FLy. Independent 2026 evaluations report that typical acceptance distorts the distribution and can lose several accuracy points on hard tasks.

### Inferences
- Common notation: accept x iff p(x) > min(ε, δ·e^{−H(p)}); defaults ε = 0.09, δ = 0.3. The threshold never exceeds ε = 0.09 and falls with entropy: at H(p) = 0 it is min(0.09, 0.3) = 0.09; it starts to drop below ε only when e^{−H} < 0.3, i.e. H(p) > 1.2 nats; at H = 3 nats it is about 0.015. So the rule is "any token with target probability above 9% is acceptable, and less than that when the target is very uncertain".
- The draft probability q(x) does not enter the test at all (vLLM even documents it as unused). Consequently the output is not a relaxation of p along q in the sense of lenience; it is "whatever the drafter proposes, provided it lies in the target's η-sampling allowed set", which is exactly the truncation-based characterisation of arXiv:2607.26627. With a strong drafter this is benign; with a weak one, every accepted token is a draft-model choice.
- In Medusa the drafter is a set of heads on the target itself and candidates are the heads' top predictions, which is the most favourable case; the degradations reported in 2026 are for separate small drafters or EAGLE-3 heads on hard reasoning/code/multilingual tasks. The paper's own evidence (MT-Bench writing and roleplay at T = 0.7) covers only open-ended generation.
- AASD (Section 12) uses the opposite entropy dependence (threshold rising with entropy, capped at max p), and FLy uses entropy as a gate for deferral; "entropy-adaptive acceptance" is therefore not one rule but three different design choices.

## 6. Mentored decoding (Vivien Tran-Thien, blog post, September 2023)

### Takeaway
Mentored decoding is the solution of an explicit optimisation problem: maximise the probability of accepting the draft token subject to KL(target ‖ resulting distribution) ≤ D. The optimum has a closed form: acceptance min(1, q_i/(α p_i)) in the blog's notation (p = draft, q = target), i.e. Leviathan's linear lenience with a per-position factor α found by binary search, together with a modified residual proportional to max(0, q_i/β − p_i). The only experiment is small (T5-large/T5-small, WMT15 EN→FR subset, 1000 examples, Tesla V100) and is reported as a scatter plot: higher tokens per second than speculative decoding, with BLEU unchanged for the smallest D and degrading as D grows.

### Inferences
- Common notation (p = target, q = draft): accept x with probability min(1, p(x)/(α q(x))), α ∈ (0,1] chosen per position so that KL(p ‖ π) = D; on rejection sample from norm(max(0, p/β − q)) with β ≥ 1 tied to α by Σ_v max(0, q(v) − p(v)/α) = Σ_v max(0, p(v)/β − q(v)). The output is π(v) = clip(q(v); p(v)/β, p(v)/α): the draft distribution clipped into a multiplicative band around the target. Leviathan's bound π ≤ p/l is the upper edge of that band (with l = α); mentored decoding adds the lower edge π ≥ p/β, which is precisely the "minimum probability" guarantee that Leviathan's Appendix says lenience lacks.
- Three differences from plain lenience: (1) α is adaptive, so the divergence budget D is spent where the two models disagree; positions with KL(p‖q) ≤ D are accepted outright; (2) the residual is flattened (p/β − q instead of p − q); (3) there is an explicit per-token KL guarantee. The "mentored_dec (β=1)" evaluated by Narasimhan et al., by Xia et al. and by Wang et al. drops (1) and (2): with a global α and β = 1 it is exactly Leviathan/DistillSpec linear lenience (acceptance min(1, p/((1−α)q)), residual norm(max(0, p − q))). Results quoted for "mentored decoding" in those three papers are therefore results for linear lenience.
- The KL-optimal residual is one particular member of the set of TV-optimal residuals of Yin et al. (Theorem 4): it vanishes where q > p and is below (p − q)/P(reject) where q ≤ p. So mentored decoding is simultaneously on the TV Pareto front and KL-optimal; the standard residual is TV-optimal but not KL-optimal.
- The per-token guarantee is on forward KL from the target and does not compose into a small sequence-level guarantee: over N lossy positions the bound is at most N·D by the chain rule (same structure as FSD's bound in Section 7).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed publication and no experiment on decoder-only LLMs by the author; no numeric results table (chart only). The computational overhead of the per-token binary search is not quantified in the post.
- The claim "provably optimal" rests on the linked proof.pdf, which was read but not independently checked line by line here.

## 7. Fuzzy Speculative Decoding (Holsman, Huang, Dhingra; Findings of ACL 2025, arXiv 2502.20704)

### Takeaway
FSD replaces the token-level probabilistic test by a position-level deterministic test on the two full distributions: accept the drafted token iff Div(P_target, P_draft) at that position is below a user threshold T (JS divergence by default; KL and TV also studied), otherwise sample from the target. A "reducible" variant (rFSD) also accepts when the ordinary SD test passes and reduces to SD at T = 0. The paper gives a sequence-level bound Div ≤ N·%M_D·T, and reports that FSD matches SD accuracy at about 1–4 tokens/s more and gains about 5 tokens/s for about 2 points of accuracy; the main weakness, acknowledged by the authors and confirmed by 2026 benchmarks, is that the threshold-to-quality relationship is not known in advance and that the rule leans entirely on the drafter being a good language model.

### Inferences
- Common notation: accept x iff D(p, q) < T, where D is computed on the whole next-token distributions and does not depend on which token x was drafted; on rejection draw from p (plain FSD) or from norm(max(0, p − q)) (rFSD). Position by position the sampled distribution is q when D(p,q) < T and p otherwise: a hard switch between the two models, i.e. a token-level cascade whose deferral signal is distributional agreement. This is the same structural family as BiLD* and SpecCascade (target (1−δ)q + δp with δ ∈ {0,1}); only the deferral predicate differs.
- Plain FSD is not on the rejection/bias Pareto front: when D(p,q) ≥ T it rejects with probability 1 and resamples from p even if the draft token would have passed the ordinary test; rFSD repairs this (same output distribution, rejection probability TV(p,q) instead of 1), which is the content of "Takeaway 5" above.
- In the paper's main experiments the draft tokens are greedy (argmax of q) while rejections are sampled from p, so the "q" that is accepted is a point mass; the bound N·%M_D·T is stated for distributions, and the reported accuracy changes are for this mixed greedy/sampled regime.
- The per-position guarantee Div(p, π) ≤ T is of the same type as mentored decoding's KL ≤ D and CACTUS's KL ≤ δ, but FSD spends the whole budget by switching to q, whereas mentored decoding and CACTUS move only part of the way from p toward q; this is consistent with the 2026 finding that methods "that tightly control deviation from p" degrade more gracefully than methods "that explicitly rely on q" (Section 13).

## 8. Speculative cascades: Narasimhan et al., "Faster Cascades via Speculative Decoding" (ICLR 2025, arXiv 2405.19261) and the Google Research blog post

### Takeaway
The paper turns lossy SD into "speculative sampling toward a user-chosen target distribution π = T(q, p)": accept with probability min(1, π(x)/q(x)) and resample from norm(max(0, π − q)), which samples exactly from π. Choosing π = (1−δ)·q + δ·p with a binary deferral decision δ(q,p) gives speculative cascades (Chow, Diff, OPT), and a token-specific variant keeps q only on tokens that pass a per-token test (TokenV1/V2/V3). The optimal deferral rule for a 0-1 or log loss with a rejection-rate budget compares the two models' expected losses with a cost proportional to TV(p,q) (Lemma 4), with a plug-in estimate and regret bound (Lemma 5). On T5 (WMT, XSum, CNN/DM) the OPT and Token rules give 1.2–2.6× latency reduction at the large model's quality, better than lossy SD and BiLD*; on Gemma 2B→27B the Token (V3) rule is best on most tasks. Later independent benchmarks find OPT fragile with weak drafters and TokenV3 the most conservative and accuracy-preserving of the training-free rules.

### Inferences
- The framework's main conceptual contribution for a writer: every "relax the acceptance" method can be re-expressed as "exact speculative sampling toward a different target π", and then the acceptance rate is exactly 1 − TV(q, π) and the bias is whatever separates π from p. Lenience/mentored decoding, BiLD*, FSD/r-fuzzy, the static ensemble, contrastive targets (Section 10) and CACTUS all fit Algorithm 4; typical acceptance, FLy, MARS and top-k rules are deterministic set-membership tests that fit it only with a point-mass drafter.
- Important difference of intent: Chow/Diff/OPT/Token do not try to stay close to p; they try to minimise loss against the ground truth under a rejection budget, so they will deliberately keep q's token when q looks more confident than p. This explains both the "better than the large model" curves on T5 and the failures with weak or MTP drafters reported in 2026: the plug-in estimates (max-probabilities) are only as good as the drafter's calibration.
- TokenV3 in common notation: the draft token x is accepted with probability 1 if p(x) ≥ (1−α)·max_v p(v) (since then π(x) ≥ q(x)); otherwise with probability min(1, p(x)·η/q(x)) where η is the draft mass outside the top set. For α → 1 everything is accepted; for α = 0 only the target argmax is in the top set, so the minimal setting is "accept the target's argmax, otherwise behave like SD toward η·p", which is not lossless SD (the point made by Xia et al.). The top set {v : p(v) ≥ (1−α) max p} is the min-p sampling allowed set with p_base = 1−α.
- "Latency reduction when matching the large model's quality" (Table 2) is a different metric from "speedup over the target" used elsewhere: lossless SD does not appear as a row, and the lossy-SD row (1.61×/1.10×/1.57×) is the closest reference point.

## 9. Theory of the trade-off: Yin et al. (NeurIPS 2024) and other formal treatments of lossy speculative decoding

### Takeaway
Yin et al. formalise lossy SD as a choice of an acceptance probability b(x) and a rejection (residual) distribution, and prove for a single token that (i) for a fixed b the minimal total-variation bias has a closed form and is attained by any residual that puts no mass where the draft over-proposes and at most A(x) elsewhere, and (ii) for every b between the lossless value min{1, q/p} and 1 (their notation: p = draft, q = target), rejection probability plus minimal TV bias equals TV(p, q): the Pareto front is a straight line. Later formal results refine this: the static mixture w·p + (1−w)·q attains that front (DIVERSED, AISTATS 2026); the standard residual is TV-bound-optimal whenever acceptance is only relaxed upward and earlier draft positions tolerate more relaxation than later ones (COOL-SD, AAAI 2026); KL-constrained versions give mentored decoding (per-distribution) and CACTUS (per drafted token); a per-position divergence threshold gives a linear-in-length sequence bound (FSD); and an approximate verifier with false-positive rate η_FP samples from (1−η_FP)·p + η_FP·q (SPRINTER). No sequence-level optimality result exists; all optimality statements are single-token.

### Inferences
- Translation of Yin et al. into the common notation (p = target, q = draft): for acceptance b(x) ∈ [min(1, p(x)/q(x)), 1] and an optimal residual, P(reject) + TV(output, p) = TV(p, q). Equivalently, TV(output, p) ≥ TV(p,q) − P(reject) for every such rule, with equality iff the residual puts no mass on tokens where q(x)·b(x) > p(x) and no more than (p(x) − b(x)q(x))/P(reject) elsewhere. Every percentage point of extra single-token acceptance costs at least one percentage point of total variation; no clever acceptance function can do better in TV.
- Which practical rules sit on this front (my check against Theorem 4): (i) linear lenience with the unchanged residual (residual is 0 where q > p and (p − q)/TV(p,q) ≤ (p − q)/P(reject) elsewhere); (ii) mentored decoding (residual ∝ max(0, p/β − q)); (iii) the static ensemble (DIVERSED Proposition 1; its residual normalises to the standard one, as the Hugging Face implementation notes: "the fallback [v-q]+ = w*[p-q]+ normalizes to the same distribution as [p-q]+"); (iv) r-fuzzy and binary speculative cascades at each position (either lossless SD or accept-all). Not on the front: plain FSD (rejects with probability 1 when the divergence test fails). Rules that replace a rejected token by the target's own sample or argmax (vLLM's typical acceptance sampler, FLy, MARS) use, when run with sampling, the residual that Yin et al. call sub-optimal ("Decoding-UNO"); under greedy decoding the TV framework is degenerate because both distributions are point masses.
- Consequence: total variation cannot rank the Pareto-optimal rules; they differ only in where the extra probability mass goes. Lenience caps over-proposed tokens at p/l and leaves moderately over-proposed tokens at q; the static ensemble inflates every over-proposed token proportionally; binary cascades switch wholesale to q. The 2026 empirical result that "overshoot suppression … matter[s] far more than the linear interpolation" (arXiv:2607.26627) is exactly a statement about this choice, which the TV theory is blind to. KL-based formulations (mentored decoding, CACTUS) do discriminate, because forward KL penalises removing mass from target-likely tokens and reverse KL penalises adding mass where the target has little.
- Yin et al.'s experiment is often read as "use the optimal residual"; in fact their Decoding-OPT residual [A]_+ with A ∝ max{q − p, −ε} (their notation) is proportional to the positive part of target minus draft, i.e. the standard SD residual, and the baseline is "resample from the target". The practical lesson is therefore: when relaxing acceptance, keep the standard residual rather than resampling from p (consistent with COOL-SD Proposition 1); the measured benefit is small (win rates 52.5–57.5%).
- Sequence level: FSD's N·%M_D·T and the chain rule for mentored decoding/CACTUS give bounds that grow linearly with the number of relaxed positions; COOL-SD's block-level TV bound and its "relax early positions more" result are the only position-aware statements found, and they are proved for two positions under small perturbations and evaluated on image models. No result links any of these divergences to task accuracy; the 2026 length-inflation findings show an effect (longer outputs) that none of the bounds capture.
- Reading aid for Yin et al.'s Figure 4: the sentence "$(0,{\sf TV}[p,q])$ stands for Speculative Decoding" uses coordinates (bias, rejection probability): lossless SD has zero bias and rejection probability TV; the other end of the line, $({\sf TV}[p,q],0)$, is "accept everything", whose output is the draft distribution.

### Gaps
- No theorem bounds KL or TV for typical acceptance, FLy, MARS, top-k or margin rules as a function of their thresholds (Sharma's certificates concern acceptance, not fidelity).
- No sequence-level Pareto characterisation; no formal treatment of length change or of error propagation after an accepted mismatch.
- ARC-Decode (ICML 2026 poster) claims "a risk-bounded acceptance criterion derived from an analytic upper bound on the potential distributional deviation" based on Jensen–Shannon divergence, but only the abstract was accessible; the bound itself was not found. No conformal or distribution-free risk-control method for token-level acceptance in LLM speculative decoding was found.
